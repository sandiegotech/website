Lectio · The First Project

Let any person read a great work. With genuine ease.

Lectio is a reading app for the great works of the Western canon — Homer, Plato, Dante, Darwin, Camus — built on an AI-produced modern edition reviewed by human scholars, a comprehension layer that lives beside the text, and a model that makes every word free to read and every printed volume a means of funding this institution.

It is the first project of the San Diego Institute of Technology. It is a large undertaking, and it is underway.

The First Project · iPad · In Development · MIT Licensed

Over 130 works. A custom Lectio Edition cover for each — from Homer to Woolf, Plato to Darwin.

The ProblemReading

The greatest books ever written are almost entirely unread.

Not because people aren't capable. Because the on-ramp is brutal — and no one has fixed it.

The great works are free, in the public domain, and contain some of the most important things ever written: The Iliad, Meditations, The Divine Comedy, Moby-Dick, On the Origin of Species, Democracy in America. And almost nobody reads them.

The reason is not laziness. It is the on-ramp: archaic translations that put even native English speakers at a remove, names and references that assume a classical education no one has anymore, dense walls of text with no context and no margin for the mind. Most reading apps reproduce the same forbidding 1909 file and wish you luck.

The research question Lectio investigates is what happens when you fix that. If you give a modern reader a clear, plain-English edition of The Iliad — synthesized from the full scholarly record, with context beside the text and every hard word a tap away — does the book become readable? The hypothesis is yes. And that the right instrument for this is not a summary, not a podcast, not an audiobook: it is the book itself, properly made for the reader it has now.

This is also a question about AI. Lectio holds that AI can either replace reading or deepen it, and that the design of every feature must choose between those two. A tool that reads for you removes the reader. A tool that removes the friction from stopping at an unknown word — or that gives you a plain-English edition of Dante instead of a nineteenth-century verse translation — leaves the reader intact while making the reader better. That distinction is the research.

The Lectio EditionThe flagship

A fresh, plain-modern-English edition of each work. Produced by deep research. Free in the app.

The thing only Lectio does: a new edition of each great work, synthesized from the full scholarly record and expressed in the language people actually read now.

Not a summary. Not a simplification. A real, faithful rendering of the work — the sense and force of the original, in contemporary English — produced by a rigorous process and read by a human before it ships. The claim the project is testing: a person who reads the Lectio Edition of The Iliad has read The Iliad.

How it is made. For each section of text, the research pipeline gathers everything available: every public-domain translation, the original Greek or Latin, and all available scholarship — modern translations as research input, academic commentaries, the ancient scholia. It synthesizes from the full record and produces a rendering that is faithful to the original, informed by every serious reading of it, and expressed in original language. Then a human reads and approves it before it ships. Copyrighted translations are research inputs, never reproduced text — what scholarship says about a passage can be synthesized; the text of a copyrighted translation cannot be included.

Provenance is a first-class feature. Every Lectio Edition carries a full provenance record: the AI model used, the date produced, the sources consulted, and a plain-language description of the method. The reading surface shows only "Lectio Edition." The full record is one tap away. It is never passed off as a historical translation.

Editions have vintages. As models improve, the edition can be regenerated — a 2026 edition, a 2028 edition, and so on. The current edition is always the default. Older vintages are archived with their provenance intact: a permanent, citable record. Students citing a passage can pin to a specific vintage.

The Lectio Edition is the default in the app. One tap switches to any public-domain translation or the original language. Accessibility by default, depth on demand.

The CorpusThe scope

Starting with three. The scope: the books that built the modern mind.

The corpus is the great works themselves — the books that built the modern mind, whatever shelf they happen to sit on. The inspiration for the scope is old: in 1909, Charles Eliot assembled the five-foot shelf — the Harvard Classics — 51 public-domain volumes meant to give any person a genuine education in fifteen minutes a day. That shelf is a map we admire, not a boundary we accept.

Lectio is being built to make these works readable — not just accessible, but readable: the kind of reading where you finish and feel the work was worth the time. Production began with three:

Meditations

Marcus Aurelius. Stoic philosophy written by a Roman emperor to himself. In the app now with the Lectio Edition, the George Long 1862 translation, Méric Casaubon 1634, and the original Greek.

The Iliad

Homer. In the app now — the showcase for multi-edition reading: the Lectio edition, Pope's heroic couplets, and Butler's plain prose, side by side with your place kept.

Hamlet

Shakespeare. The original text beside a Lectio edition in modern prose — the sternest test of the method, where the original's music has to stay audible.

The bundle has since grown to seventeen works — Homer to Hamlet, Gilgamesh to Genesis, the Tao Te Ching to the Dhammapada — and every one now carries a Lectio edition, with the human read-through that precedes final publication underway. Custom covers exist for over 130 works; the catalog scales as each edition proves out.

The MarginComprehension layer

Context beside the text. The reading column stays calm.

The signature UI of Lectio: a warm reading column on the left, a quiet right-hand rail — The Margin — carrying the living layer. Tap a word, see a person, follow a reference. All of it in the margin, none of it interrupting the text.

The comprehension layer is pre-generated, not live. For each work, the research pipeline produces an annotation dictionary: 300 to 400 entries covering proper nouns, recurring concepts, and the high-frequency references a modern reader will stumble on. Who is Achilles? What is xenia? What are the Furies? Each entry is generated once from the full scholarly record, human-reviewed, and committed to the content bundle as static, finished data. A reader tapping "xenia" gets a considered, edited definition — not whatever a model generates cold that afternoon.

The Docent. The margin also carries a reading companion. Ask it about the passage in front of you and it answers from the text and the curated editorial layer — not the open internet. It runs on-device: free, offline, and private, with nothing sent anywhere. AI is an instrument here, never the authority — the Docent's answers are labeled as its own, and the text remains the text.

Words not in the annotation dictionary fall through to Webster's 1913 — a complete, public-domain dictionary included in the app. No API call for ordinary vocabulary. On-device AI handles anything genuinely unexpected. The design goal is near-zero per-user cost: content is pre-generated and served static, so each additional reader costs essentially nothing.

Multi-edition reading. Every work carries multiple editions: the Lectio Edition, every public-domain translation available, and the original-language text. Switching editions keeps your place. Each edition carries a provenance card — translator and year for historical editions; model, date, and sources for Lectio editions — available in the switcher and in any exported PDF.

What's built The Margin, the Docent, tap-to-explore words, word save, notes, search, editorial enrichment per passage, and multi-edition reading are all running. Seventeen works are in the content bundle.
The Lectio Edition in PrintThe artifact

The app is free. The book is the product. The proceeds fund the Institute.

A well-made printed Lectio Edition is the natural physical form of this work — and the revenue model that makes everything else possible.

Lectio the app is free end-to-end: the public-domain texts, the original languages, The Margin, the annotation dictionary, and the Lectio Edition itself. No subscription, no paywall on the text. The near-zero AI cost architecture — content pre-generated and served static — makes free sustainable at any scale.

Revenue comes from the physical artifact. A printed Lectio Edition of each work — carefully designed, human-read and approved in full before printing, carrying a provenance colophon — is produced on demand. No inventory to carry. Whoever wants only to read pays nothing; whoever wants to keep a fine edition buys the book. Proceeds flow through SDIT and fund its work.

Every work and every language is another edition with nothing to warehouse, so the catalogue only grows. Spanish editions — fitting San Diego — come next. This is not a separate product from the app; it is another rendering of the same content, made permanent. The book is typeset directly from the same content bundle the app reads — text, provenance, divergences and all — so the page and the reader can never drift apart. The press already runs: finished interiors come off it today. The knowledge is never gated. Only the made object is sold.

A modern translation that tells you exactly how it was made — model, vintage, sources, human reviewer — is the selling point, not a disclaimer. Honesty is the product.
