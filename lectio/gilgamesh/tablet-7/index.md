# The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet VII

The Doom of Enkidu

When the day broke, Enkidu turned to Gilgamesh. “My friend,” he said, “why are the great gods sitting in council together? I dreamed it in the night. Anu and Enlil and Ea, and Shamash of the bright sky, sat down together to judge. Because the two of us killed Humbaba, and because we struck down the Bull of Heaven, one of us must die. So the gods had decided, and now they were deciding which. Enlil said, ‘Let Enkidu die. Gilgamesh shall not die.’ Then Shamash spoke up for me: ‘Was it not at my word that they killed the Bull of Heaven and killed Humbaba? Shall innocent Enkidu die for that?’ But Enlil turned on him in anger: ‘You went down with them, day after day, as if you were one of their company. That is why you speak for them.’ And the council held, and the doom stood, and then I woke.”

So Enkidu lay down, sick, and the sickness would not lift. He lay in front of Gilgamesh, and his tears ran down like rain, and he said: “My brother, my dear brother — they are letting me go in place of my brother.” And then a strange grief took him, and he turned on a thing that could not answer: the great door he had built with his own hands, the cedar door he had set up in Enlil's temple. He had gone twenty leagues to find the wood. He had searched the forest until he found the one cedar that towered over all the rest, a tree no man had ever matched. He had cut it and shaped it, the leaf of the door and the pivot and the post, all of it perfect — seventy-two cubits high and twenty-four across.

“Door,” he said, “you have no ears and no mind. There is no understanding in you. I searched twenty leagues for your wood, I found the highest cedar in the forest for you — and there is nothing in you to know it. If I had known what you would cost me, I would have raised my axe and split you into kindling, and hung up a gate of woven reeds in your place. Some king who comes after me will hang his name on you. He will take my name off and put his own. And you will stand in the temple, and I will be gone.” Gilgamesh heard what his friend said, and his tears ran down.

Then he cursed the trapper, the man who first found him at the watering place. “Trapper,” he said, “may you never have enough. May your game grow thin and your catch grow small. May the wild things slip out of your nets and your snares stand empty. May whatever you take be the leanest share, and may your hands go home empty before the others'.” So he laid his curse on the trapper, and his heart was not eased.

And then he turned on Shamhat, and cursed her too. “Come, Shamhat,” he said, “and I will set your fate — a fate that will not end as long as the world stands. I will curse you, and the curse will fall hard. You will have no house of your own. You will sit at the crossroads in the dust of the potters' yard. The shadow of the wall will be your shelter, and the dunghill your bed at night. Thorn and bramble will tear your feet. The drunk and the sober alike will strike your face. Because you drew me out of the wild — because of you I am lying here, and the fever is on my back.”

But Shamash heard him out of heaven, and he called down to him: “Enkidu — why do you curse Shamhat? She gave you bread, the food fit for the gods. She gave you beer, the drink fit for a king. She put a fine robe on your body. And she gave you Gilgamesh for a friend and a brother. Now Gilgamesh, your friend and your brother, will lay you on a great bed. He will lay you on a bed of honor and seat your memory at his left hand, and the princes of the earth will kiss your feet. He will set all of Uruk weeping for you. He will turn the whole people to mourning, and the proud men he will bend to your service. And when you are gone, he himself will let his hair grow wild and put on a lion's skin and wander the open country, grieving for you.”

Enkidu heard the words of Shamash, and the anger went out of him. He took back his curse, and in its place he blessed her, with as much weight as he had cursed. “Shamhat,” he said, “let me set your fate again. Let kings and princes and nobles love you. Let the lover comb out his hair for you and loosen his belt for you, and lay you down on a bed of lapis and gold. Let him heap his treasure on you — carnelian and gold and the blue stone out of the storehouse. Let no man's house come before yours. Let the wife, the mother of seven, be left for your sake.”

But the sickness only grew, and that night Enkidu had another dream, and it was the dream of his death. He lay alone, and the weight of it came down on him, and in the morning he told it to his friend. “Gilgamesh, hear the dream I had in the night. The sky cried out, and the earth answered, and I stood between them by myself. And a man came toward me. His face was dark, and his face was like the face of the Anzu bird, and his hands were the claws of a lion. He came at me and overpowered me. He pressed me down. He held me down under him.” [A few lines are lost.]

“He changed my arms,” Enkidu went on, “and made them into wings, into the wings of a bird. And he took me by the wing and led me down — down to the house of darkness, the house of Irkalla. To the house that no one leaves who once goes in. Down the road that runs only one way, that no one walks back up. To the house where the ones who live there sit without light, where the dust is their food and clay is what they eat. They wear feathers like birds, a coat of feathers, and they sit in the dark and never see the day. And on the door and the bolt, the dust lay thick.”

“I went into the house of dust,” he said, “and I saw the crowns brought low. I saw the ones who had worn crowns, who had ruled the land in the old days — they were the ones serving now, setting out the roasted meat for Anu and Enlil, carrying the cold water from the skins. In the house of dust I saw the high priest and his servant, the seer and the man of spells, the priest who anointed the great gods. I saw Etana there, and Sumuqan the god of the herds. And there sat the queen of the dead, Ereshkigal, and bowing before her was Belet-seri, who keeps the records of the underworld, reading the tablet aloud in her presence. She lifted her head, and she looked at me.” [The rest of the dream is lost.]

When Enkidu had told the dream, he could not rise from it. The fever took him in earnest, and he lay on his bed and grew weaker, and the days went by, and he did not get up. He lay there day after day, and the sickness fed on him, and Gilgamesh sat beside him and could do nothing.

And Enkidu lifted his eyes to his friend and said the thing that was hardest of all. “I am not dying the way a man wants to die. The man who falls in battle falls clean — his friends see it, and his name goes on, and people say of him, ‘There was a man.’ But I am dying in a bed. I will waste away and die of sickness, and there is no glory in it, and no name. Gilgamesh — I am dying like this, and not like a man.”
