# The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet X

The Ale-Wife, the Boatman, and the Waters of Death

At the edge of the world, where the sea begins, there was a tavern, and the woman who kept it was Siduri, the ale-wife of the gods. She had her own vats and her own brewing-vessels, and a veil over her face. And Gilgamesh came toward her along the shore — wrapped in the skin of a lion, his face burned dark by the sun and the weather, his cheeks fallen in, his look the look of a man who has come a very long way and means to do harm. There was grief set deep in his face, and the road of years was on it.

Siduri watched him coming and said to herself: “This is surely a killer. Where is he heading, with murder in his face?” And she was afraid. She went inside and barred her door, and she went up onto the roof and hid. But Gilgamesh heard the bolt slide home, and he lifted his chin and put his hand against the door. “Ale-wife,” he called, “what did you see, that you bar your door against me — that you bolt it and climb to your own roof? I will break the door. I will shatter the bolt.”

And Siduri said to him: “Who are you?” And Gilgamesh answered her: “I am Gilgamesh, the one who killed the guardian, who brought down Humbaba in the Cedar Forest, who killed the lions in the mountain passes, who seized the Bull of Heaven that came down out of the sky and cut it apart.”

“If you are that man,” said Siduri, “why are your cheeks so hollow and your face so fallen? Why this grief in your heart, this look of a long road, of cold and heat, of a man wandering the wild?” And Gilgamesh said to her: “How should my cheeks not be hollow, my face not fallen? How should there not be grief in my heart? My friend, whom I loved, who went through every danger at my side — my friend Enkidu, whom I loved — the fate of all people has come down on him. Six days I wept for him and seven nights. I would not give him up for burial until the worm fell out of his nose. Then I was afraid. I saw what death was, and I grew afraid of dying, and I took to the wild.

“The fate of my friend lies heavy on me. How can I keep still? How can I be quiet? The friend I loved has turned to clay; Enkidu, my friend, is dirt now. And will I not lie down like him, and never get up again, not for all time?” And then he said the thing that had driven him out of his city: “Ale-wife, now that I have looked on your face, do not let me look on the death that I am afraid of. Tell me the way to Uta-napishti, the one man who lives forever. Tell me the road, and I will go. If it can be crossed, I will cross the sea; and if it cannot, I will range the wild until I find him.”

Siduri said to him: “Gilgamesh, there is no crossing here, and there never has been. No one who has come to this shore since the first days has ever crossed the sea. The one who crosses it is Shamash, the sun, and no one else. The crossing is hard, the way is hard, and in the middle of it lie the Waters of Death, that close off the channel. And even if, somehow, you crossed the sea, when you came to the Waters of Death — what would you do then?

“But Gilgamesh — there is Ur-shanabi, the boatman of Uta-napishti. He is down in the wood with the Stone Ones; he is in among the trees, cutting evergreen. Go and let him see your face. If it can be done, cross with him. And if it cannot, then turn back the way you came.” And Gilgamesh heard her, and he took up his axe and drew the sword from his belt, and he went down into the wood and came among them.

He came on Ur-shanabi, and on the Stone Ones. And a blind fury came up in Gilgamesh. He fell on the Stone Ones and smashed them. He scattered them in the trees, and he did not stop until they were broken past mending. Then he came back and stood over Ur-shanabi, and looked him in the eye.

And Ur-shanabi said to him: “Tell me your name. I am Ur-shanabi, the boatman of Uta-napishti, the Distant.” And Gilgamesh answered him as he had answered the ale-wife — who he was, and what he had done, and how his friend Enkidu was dead and turned to clay, and how the fear of dying had driven him into the wild and out to the edge of the world. “Now show me Uta-napishti,” he said. “Show me the way.”

But Ur-shanabi said: “Gilgamesh, it is your own hands that have kept you from crossing. You have smashed the Stone Ones; you have scattered the very ones who carried the boat safe across the Waters of Death. The Stone Ones are broken; the evergreen is not gathered. But take up your axe, Gilgamesh, and go down into the wood and cut three hundred poles, each one sixty cubits long. Trim them, strip them, and fit each with a cap. Then bring them to me.”

Gilgamesh heard him and went down and cut three hundred poles, sixty cubits each, and trimmed them and stripped them and capped them, and brought them to the boat. And the two of them went aboard and pushed off into the sea, and in three days they ran a journey of a month and a half, and so Ur-shanabi came to the Waters of Death. And there he said: “Now, Gilgamesh, take a pole and push — but your hand must never touch the Waters of Death, or you are lost. Take a first pole, a second, a third. Take a hundred poles, two hundred, three hundred.” And so it was done. When the three hundred poles were spent, Gilgamesh stripped off his clothes, and with his own arms he raised them up for a sail, and the wind took the boat across.

Uta-napishti was watching from far off, and a thought troubled him, and he said in his heart: “Why are the Stone Ones of the boat smashed? Why does someone ride in it who is not its master? The man who comes is no man of mine. I look, and I do not understand him.” And when the boat had come to land, Gilgamesh stepped ashore and stood before Uta-napishti, the Distant — the one man who had passed out of the company of the dying and been given life that does not end.

And Uta-napishti looked at him and said: “Why are your cheeks so hollow, your face so fallen? Why this grief in your heart, this look of a long road — your face burned with cold and heat, ranging the wild for the wind? Who are you?” And Gilgamesh told him all of it again, every word — Enkidu his friend, the strongest in the land, who stood with him against every danger, brought down by the fate of all people; the six days and seven nights of weeping; the worm and the fear; the wandering. “I came by every hard road to reach you,” he said. “I wore myself out crossing the sea. My face is worn with grief. And for what? I have found no rest. I have only worn out my own body and filled myself with sorrow.”

Uta-napishti said to him: “Why do you stretch out your grief so long, Gilgamesh? The gods made you out of the flesh of gods and the flesh of men; they made you the way they made your father and your mother. You — you have worn yourself out and got nothing for it; you have filled your flesh with sorrow and only brought your far-off death a little nearer. Mankind is cut down like a reed in the canebrake. The handsome young man, the lovely young woman — death takes them while they are young. No one sees death. No one sees the face of death. No one hears the voice of death. But death is savage, and it cuts mankind down.

“Do we build a house to stand forever? Do we set a seal on a thing to last for all time? Do brothers divide a share and keep it forever? Does hatred last forever in the land? Does the river forever rise and flood? The dragonfly hatches, and looks once on the sun, and is gone. From the oldest days there is no permanence. Look — the sleeping and the dead, how alike they are. Both of them are the very picture of death. The slave and the master, when their time is come, they are the same. The Anunnaki, the great gods, gather, and Mammitum, who fixes fate, fixes it with them: they hand out death and they hand out life. But the day of death they do not tell. They have never told it, to anyone.”
