# The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet I

The Coming of Enkidu

Of the man who saw the deep, the country's foundation, who knew the proper way of all things — let me tell. Gilgamesh saw everything, traveled every road, learned the whole of wisdom. He looked on what was secret and uncovered what was hidden, and he carried home a story older than the Flood. He went a long road, came back worn out but at peace, and cut the whole of his labor into a slab of stone.

Look at the wall of Uruk, how it shines like bronze. Study its foundation; no later king will ever match it. Climb the wall and walk its length, test the brickwork: were these bricks not fired in the kiln? Did the Seven Sages themselves not lay the groundwork? A square mile of city, a square mile of date groves, a square mile of clay pits, and half a square mile for Ishtar's temple — three square miles and a half, and all of it is Uruk. Find the box of cedar, slip the bronze ring that locks it, lift the lid, and take out the tablet of lapis lazuli. Read aloud how Gilgamesh suffered, and all that he came through.

Two parts of him were god and one part was human. The Lady Ninsun bore him, and the great gods shaped his body. He was enormous and perfectly made, beautiful and terrible at once. Over the men of Uruk he towered like a wild bull, and there was no one who could stand against his weapons or his will.

But his strength ran without a rein. Day and night it drove him, and his arrogance only grew. He left no son to his father; he wore the young men out. He left no girl to her mother, no bride to her husband — not the soldier's daughter, not the young man's wife. The people of Uruk muttered in their houses, and at last their cry went up to the gods.

The gods of heaven heard the complaint, and they called to Aruru, the great mother who had first made mankind: “You made Gilgamesh. Now make his equal. Make someone to match the storm in his heart, and let the two of them strive together, so that Uruk can rest.”

Aruru heard, and in her mind she formed the image of the god of the sky. She washed her hands, pinched off a piece of clay, and threw it down in the wild. There, in the empty country, she made Enkidu — a fighter, born in silence, hard as the war-god Ninurta himself. Hair covered his whole body; the hair of his head was long as a woman's and thick as a field of grain. He knew nothing of people, nothing of settled land. He wore the skins of animals. He grazed on grass with the gazelles, he crowded with the wild beasts at the watering place, and his heart was glad in the water with them.

A trapper, a man who set snares, met him face to face at the watering place — once, and again, and a third time. When the trapper saw him his face went stiff with fear. He drove his own animals home, shaken and sick at heart, his face drawn like a man's who has come a long, hard road. For Enkidu filled in the pits the trapper dug, and tore out the traps he set, and pulled the wild beasts free of his hands, so that the trapper could no longer hunt.

He went to his father and said: “Father, a man has come down from the hills, the strongest in the land. His strength is like a rock fallen from the sky. He ranges the hills with the beasts and feeds with them, and he keeps crossing my ground, and I am afraid of him. He fills in my pits and pulls up my traps; he takes the game out of my hands.” His father answered: “Go to Uruk, to Gilgamesh, and tell him this. Then ask him for Shamhat, one of the women of the temple, and bring her back with you. When the animals come down to the water, let her take off her clothes and show him her body. He will go to her — and after that the beasts that grew up with him in the wild will turn away from him.”

So the trapper went to Uruk, and stood before Gilgamesh, and said the same words. And Gilgamesh said: “Go, trapper. Take Shamhat with you.”

They set out together, and on the third day they came to the watering place, and there they sat down to wait. One day they waited, and a second. Then the wild beasts came down to drink, and Enkidu was among them — the man born in the hills, who ate grass with the gazelles, who drank with the beasts and was happy in the water. Shamhat saw him: the wild man, the savage out of the deep country.

“There he is,” the trapper said. “Open yourself, Shamhat. Don't hold back. When he comes to you, take him in and show him your art. The animals that grew up with him will turn from him, once his body has lain against yours.” Shamhat loosened her clothing and opened herself, and he came to her, and she did not hold back. She showed him her art, and his desire lay on her for six days and seven nights, and Enkidu stayed with her until he had had his fill.

When he was satisfied he turned back to his animals. But the gazelles saw him and ran, and the wild beasts of the open country bolted from his body. Enkidu started after them and his legs would not carry him; his knees gave out, the wild thing had gone out of him. And yet his mind had widened; now he understood. He came back and sat down at the woman's feet, and he watched her face and listened. And Shamhat said to him: “You are handsome, Enkidu. You are like a god. Why should you run with the animals across the empty land? Come, let me take you to Uruk, to the holy temple where Anu and Ishtar are served, where Gilgamesh rules the people and lords it over them like a wild bull.”

Enkidu listened, and the words pleased him, for his heart wanted a friend, someone who would understand him. “Take me to the city,” he said. “I will go to Gilgamesh, the mighty one. I will challenge him to his face and cry out in Uruk, ‘I am the strongest here!’ I will change the way things have always been — the one born in the wild has power, and I will show it.” “Then come,” said Shamhat, “and let him see your face. I know where he is; I will show you Gilgamesh. Look hard at him, Enkidu: he is full of life and strength. The sun-god Shamash loves him, and Anu and Enlil and Ea have made his mind deep and wide. Even before you came down from the hills, he saw you coming in his dreams.”

For Gilgamesh had gotten up and gone to his mother to have his dreams read. “Mother,” he said, “in the night I felt strong, and I walked out proud among the young men, and the stars crowded the whole of the sky. Then something like a rock from the sky-god fell down in front of me. I tried to lift it and it was too heavy; I tried to roll it and could not. All of Uruk gathered around it; the people pressed close, and the men kissed its feet. I loved it the way a man loves his wife; I held it close. I leaned my forehead against it, and they helped me, and I lifted it at last and carried it to you — and you made it my equal.”

Ninsun was wise and knew all things, and she said to her son: “The thing like a star from heaven that fell beside you, that you could not lift — that is a strong companion who is coming to you, a friend who rescues his friend. He is the strongest in the land; his force is like a rock from the sky. You loved him the way a man loves his wife, and held him close, and he will never leave you. That is the meaning of your dream.”

“Mother, I dreamed again. In the street of Uruk an axe was lying, and a crowd stood around it. I saw it and was glad; I loved it the way a man loves his wife; I bent and picked it up and set it at my side — and you made it my equal.” And Ninsun answered: “The axe you saw is a man. You will love him as you love a wife, and I will make him your equal. A strong companion is coming, one who saves his friend, the strongest in the land, his strength like a rock from the sky.” And Gilgamesh said: “Then let it fall this way. Let me find a friend to stand beside me.” So Gilgamesh told his dreams.
