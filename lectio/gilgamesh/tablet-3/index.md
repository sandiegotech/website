# The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet III

The Mother's Prayer

The elders of Uruk came to Gilgamesh, the great men of the city, and they gave him their counsel for the road. “Gilgamesh,” they said, “do not lean on your own strength alone. Keep your eyes open, and make every blow land where you aim it. The one who goes in front keeps his companion safe. The one who knows the road brings his friend home. Let Enkidu walk ahead of you. He knows the way to the Cedar Forest; he has seen the fight that Humbaba gives. Let him guard your body and bring you back.”

Then they turned to Enkidu and laid the king in his hands. “In our assembly we have given the king into your keeping. When you return, give the king back to us. Bring Gilgamesh home.”

Gilgamesh said to Enkidu: “Come, my friend. Let us go to the Egalmah, to Ninsun, the great queen, my mother. Ninsun is wise; she knows all things. She will set our feet on a sure path.” So the two of them went together to the temple, to Ninsun.

Gilgamesh stood before his mother and said: “Ninsun, hear me. I am setting out on a long road, to the place where Humbaba is. I go to a fight I have never fought, down a road I have never traveled. From the day I leave until I come back, until I reach the Cedar Forest and cut down the evil that Shamash hates — pray for me to Shamash. Speak to him on my behalf.”

Ninsun heard the words of her son, and her face was troubled. She went into her chamber and washed herself with the purity plant. She put on a robe worthy of her body, and over her breast she fastened an ornament worthy of her breast. She wound her sash about her, and set her crown upon her head. Then she climbed the stairs and went up onto the roof of the temple.

On the roof she set a censer before Shamash and laid the incense down. She scattered the fragrant offering, and she raised her arms to the rising sun. Then she spoke to the sun-god, and her prayer was also a complaint.

“Shamash,” she said, “why did you do this to my son? Why have you given Gilgamesh a heart that will not rest? You laid this restlessness on him — you yourself — and now it drives him out the door, down a far road to Humbaba, into a fight he does not know and a country he has never seen. From the day he leaves until the day he comes home, watch over him. When you cross the sky by day, keep your eye on him. When you go down at night, do not let go of him.”

“And when you sink below the world and the dark comes,” she went on, “let Aya, your bride, remind you of him without fear. Hand him to the Watchmen of the Night, to the stars. And in the night let your father Sin keep him as well, so that there is no hour when no god is holding my son.”

“Rouse the great winds against Humbaba,” she prayed — “the south wind and the north, the east wind and the west, the whistling wind, the piercing wind, the blizzard, the evil wind, the wind of Simurru, the demon wind, the ice wind, the storm, the sandstorm — thirteen winds. Let them rise and darken Humbaba's face, so that the weapons of Gilgamesh can reach him.”

When she had finished her prayer, she put out the censer and came down from the roof.

Then Ninsun called Enkidu to her, and she spoke to him before the holy women of the temple, the votaries who served in the house. “Enkidu the strong,” she said, “you did not come out of my body. But here, now, I take you as my own, and I name you among the dedicated of Gilgamesh.” And she laid a token on Enkidu's neck, and she made him her son and the brother of the king.

“Enkidu,” she said, “you are my son now, and Gilgamesh is your brother. Go in front of him on the road. The companion who leads the way must bring his friend back whole. I lay the king on you, as the elders laid him on you. Whatever the Cedar Forest holds, come back to me with my son.”

[Some lines are lost here. When the text resumes, Gilgamesh is leaving instructions for the city while he is away.]

And once more the elders gave their charge, the same words they had spoken at the start: “In our assembly we have given the king into your keeping, Enkidu. When you return, give the king back to us.” So Gilgamesh and Enkidu turned their faces to the Cedar Forest and the long road.
