# The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet VI

Ishtar and the Bull of Heaven

When the fight was over, Gilgamesh washed the matted hair from his body and cleaned the filth of battle from his gear. He shook his hair down across his back, and he threw off his soiled clothes and put on clean ones, and wrapped a cloak around himself and tied it with a sash. Then he set the crown on his head, and he was beautiful again — a king come home.

And the Lady Ishtar lifted her eyes to the beauty of Gilgamesh, and desire moved in her. “Come to me, Gilgamesh,” she said. “Be my husband. Give me the fruit of your body, and you will be mine and I will be yours. I will harness you a chariot of lapis and gold, with wheels of gold and fittings of amber, and team it with lions and great mules. Come into my house, into the sweet smell of cedar. When you cross my threshold, the doorway and the footstool will kiss your feet. Kings and princes and nobles will kneel to you, and all that the hills and the lowlands grow they will bring to you as tribute. Your goats will drop triplets and your ewes twins; your loaded donkey will outrun the swiftest mule; your horse will run famous before the chariot, and no ox will stand against yours at the yoke.”

But Gilgamesh opened his mouth and answered her: “And what would I have to give you, if I took you for a wife? Oil for your skin, fine clothes for your body, bread fit for a god and beer fit for a king — and what would I get back? You are a brazier that goes cold the moment one needs its fire. A back door that keeps out neither wind nor storm. A palace that caves in on the soldiers inside it. An elephant that throws off its covering. Pitch that blackens the hands that carry it. A waterskin that soaks the man who hauls it. Limestone that crumbles the wall it was set in. A battering ram that turns on its own army. A shoe that bites the foot of the one who wears it. Where is the husband of yours who lasted? Where is the brave man of yours who is not crying?”

“Listen, and I will tell your loves out, one by one. There was Dumuzi, the husband of your girlhood — and you set on him a grief that comes back every year, and he is wept for still. You loved the bright little roller-bird, and then you struck it and broke its wing, and now it sits in the groves and cries kappi, kappi — my wing, my wing. You loved the lion, perfect in his strength — and you dug for him seven pits, and seven more. You loved the war-horse, glorious in the charge — and you gave him for his portion the whip and the spur and the lash; you decreed that he gallop seven leagues without rest, that he churn and drink his own muddied water, and you doomed his mother Silili to weep for him forever.

“You loved the shepherd, the herdsman, the keeper of the flock, who heaped up loaves baked in the ashes for you and butchered a kid for you every single day — and you struck him and turned him into a wolf, and now his own herd-boys drive him off, and his own dogs tear at his haunches. And you loved Ishullanu, your father's gardener, who carried you baskets of dates and made your table shine, day after day. You looked him over and you came to him: ‘Ishullanu, let me taste your strength. Put out your hand and touch my sex.’ And Ishullanu said to you, ‘What do you want with me? My mother bakes; I have eaten; am I to eat the bread of insult and shame instead, and lie under a heap of reeds in the cold?’ So when he refused you, you struck him too, and turned him into a stunted thing and stood him in the middle of his own garden, where he cannot climb up and cannot climb down. And me — would you love me the same way, and bring me to the same end?”

When Ishtar heard this, she went up to heaven in a fury, weeping. She came before Anu her father and Antu her mother, and the tears ran down. “Father,” she said, “Gilgamesh has heaped insult on me. He has stood there and told over every shameful thing I ever did.” But Anu said to her: “And did you not bring it on yourself? Did you not provoke the king, that he should tell over your shameful deeds?”

“Father,” she said, “give me the Bull of Heaven, so I can strike Gilgamesh down in his own house. Give me the Bull — and if you will not, I will smash the doors of the underworld and break them off their hinges. I will let the dead come up to eat the living, and I will make the dead more than the living.” And Anu answered her: “If I give you the Bull of Heaven, there will be seven years of empty husks in Uruk. Have you laid up grain enough for the people, and grown grass enough for the cattle, to last out the seven barren years?” “I have,” she said. “I have heaped up the grain; I have grown the grass. The seven lean years are provided for.” So when he had heard her out, Anu put the Bull's nose-rope into her hands.

Ishtar led the Bull of Heaven down to the earth, down to the land of Uruk. Where it came, the woods dried up and the marshes and the reed-beds went to dust, and it went down to the river and drank the level down by seven cubits. It snorted, and a pit opened in the ground, and a hundred men of Uruk fell in. It snorted again, and a second pit opened, and two hundred men fell in. It snorted a third time, and the pit opened under Enkidu — but he fell only to the waist, and he sprang straight back out and seized the Bull by the horns.

The Bull spat in Enkidu's face, and with its thick tail it flung its dung at him. And Enkidu called to Gilgamesh: “My friend, we two together can bring it down. I will get behind it and take it by the tail and hold it fast. Then you — like a butcher who knows his work — drive your sword in between the base of the horns and the back of the neck.” So Enkidu came round behind the Bull and gripped it by the tail and held on, and Gilgamesh struck like a butcher, sure and unafraid, and drove the sword in deep between the horns and the nape, and the Bull of Heaven went down. They cut out its heart and laid it before Shamash. Then they stepped back and bowed themselves to the ground before the sun-god, and sat down side by side.

Then Ishtar climbed up onto the wall of Uruk, of Uruk the sheepfold, and she stamped and wailed along the rampart: “Grief on Gilgamesh! He has shamed me, and now he has killed the Bull of Heaven!” Enkidu heard her, and he tore the haunch from the Bull and flung it up at her, into her face. “If I could catch you,” he shouted, “I would do the same to you. I would hang its guts on your arms.”

Ishtar called together the women of her temple, the courtesans and the singers and the dancers, and they made their mourning over the haunch of the Bull of Heaven. And Gilgamesh called the smiths and the craftsmen, and they came and marveled at the size of the horns. Each was thirty minas of lapis in the solid; the rim of each was two fingers thick; and the two of them together would hold six measures of oil. He gave them to his god Lugalbanda, to keep oil for the anointing, and he carried them in and hung them in his own bedchamber.

Then they washed their hands in the Euphrates, and took each other by the hand, and rode through the streets of Uruk, and the people of the city crowded out to look at them. And Gilgamesh put a question to the serving-women of the palace: “Who is the most splendid of men? Who is the most glorious of heroes?” And they answered: “Gilgamesh is the most splendid of men. Gilgamesh is the most glorious of heroes.”

There was a feast in the palace, and Gilgamesh made merry. And when the night came on, the men lay down on their beds to sleep. But Enkidu lay down and a dream came to him in the dark — a heavy dream. And he rose to tell the dream, and said to his friend:
