# The Epic of Gilgamesh

Anonymous · Sumer & Akkad

- 2100–1200 BCE
- Eastern tradition
- Epic poem
- Lectio Edition 2026

The oldest long story we have. Gilgamesh, king of Uruk, and Enkidu, a man raised among animals, become friends. When Enkidu dies, Gilgamesh refuses his own death and goes looking for a way to live forever. It was written in Akkadian on clay tablets and was lost for two thousand years.

## There is no old translation to fall back on.

Every other book here has a readable public-domain translation behind it. Gilgamesh does not. The tablets were dug up in the 1850s and are still being pieced together. The first English versions, by Smith and Thompson, are free to use and are halting and incomplete. Every complete, readable English Gilgamesh is modern and under copyright.

So for this work the Lectio Edition is the primary text and not a modernizing of an older one. It follows the Standard Babylonian version in eleven tablets, drawn from the public-domain scholarly record.

This also makes it the edition with the least to check against. No comparison text is in the bundle. The review says so, and so should this page.

## How it begins.

> **Tablet I · The Coming of Enkidu**

> Of the man who saw the deep, the country's foundation, who knew the proper way of all things — let me tell. Gilgamesh saw everything, traveled every road, learned the whole of wisdom. He looked on what was secret and uncovered what was hidden, and he carried home a story older than the Flood. He went a long road, came back worn out but at peace, and cut the whole of his labor into a slab of stone.

> Look at the wall of Uruk, how it shines like bronze. Study its foundation; no later king will ever match it. Climb the wall and walk its length, test the brickwork: were these bricks not fired in the kiln? Did the Seven Sages themselves not lay the groundwork? A square mile of city, a square mile of date groves, a square mile of clay pits, and half a square mile for Ishtar's temple — three square miles and a half, and all of it is Uruk. Find the box of cedar, slip the bronze ring that locks it, lift the lid, and take out the tablet of lapis lazuli. Read aloud how Gilgamesh suffered, and all that he came through.

The first 198 words of the Lectio Edition, as they stand in the bundle the app reads.

## What this edition decided.

The first draft added to the story. It explained, moralized and supplied feelings the tablets do not give. Most of the review's work was taking that out.

- A sentence that explains, interprets or moralizes is cut, not reworded. So is a feeling the tablet does not state.
- A gap in the tablets is reported once, in square brackets, outside the story's voice: what is lost, and where the text resumes. The narrator never comments on the edition.
- Where the tablet gives a name the draft had dropped, the name is restored: Ninurta, Ishhara, Anzu, Belet-ili.
- The edition follows the Standard Babylonian version and does not import passages from the older one, however famous.
- Shamhat is named, and her office is stated plainly. She is not "the harlot".

### Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 33 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 7. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

### *sha naqba imuru*

Tablet I, line 1

**Older:** "who saw everything" (early translators)

**Lectio:** who saw the deep, the country's foundation

*Naqbu* is the deep water under the earth and, by extension, the whole of things. "Everything" keeps the breadth and loses the depth Gilgamesh later goes down to.

### Shamhat

Tablet I

**Older:** "the harlot", "the temple-harlot"

**Lectio:** Shamhat, one of the women of the temple

Shamhat is a name. A woman of Ishtar's temple held a recognized office. She is the one who makes Enkidu a human being.

### Enkidu's change

Tablet I

**Older:** stress on his having "grown weak"

**Lectio:** his mind had widened; now he understood

When the gazelles run from him, the Akkadian says he had gained reason. The scene is a loss, and before that it is a birth.

### *melammu*

Tablet IV

**Older:** "seven splendors", "seven coats of armor"

**Lectio:** seven cloaks of terror

Humbaba's *melammu* are an aura that spreads dread. They are not cloth or metal. The phrase keeps a thing worn and what it gives off.

### *kima kallati*

Tablet VIII

**Older:** "he covered the face of his friend"

**Lectio:** he covered his friend's face, the way a man veils the face of a bride

The Akkadian says Gilgamesh veiled Enkidu like a bride. Older versions sometimes give the act and drop the comparison. The strangeness is the meaning.

### Siduri's counsel

Tablet X

**Older:** the well-known speech telling Gilgamesh to eat, be merry and cherish his wife and child

**Lectio:** Siduri gives no such speech in this edition

That speech belongs to the Old Babylonian version. The Standard Babylonian text, which this edition follows, does not have it. Here Siduri questions him and sends him on to the boatman.

### *kikkish kikkish, igar igar*

Tablet XI

**Older:** "O reed-hut, reed-hut! O wall, wall!" (Smith and after)

**Lectio:** Reed wall, reed wall!

Ea has sworn with the other gods not to warn anyone. So he speaks to the wall of the house while the man listens behind it. The edition keeps the oath and the cry together so the trick shows.

## This is the only edition in the bundle.

No older translation of Gilgamesh is in the bundle. The public-domain versions are fragmentary, and the complete modern ones are under copyright and are not reproduced.

### Still open

What the first review left for the editor to decide or restore.

- The editor's own read of all eleven tablets, with a printed scholarly edition in hand. This work needs it more than any other.
- Passages the tablets preserve and the edition skips or shortens: the hymn to the king in Tablet I, the forging of the weapons in Tablet II, the fourth dream in Tablet IV, Gilgamesh's answer to Enkidu in Tablet VII, the list of grave goods in Tablet VIII, and Ea's cover story in Tablet XI. Restoring them is new writing.
- Shamash's warning in Tablet IV stands in the wrong place. Moving it means swapping two segments.
- One segment in Tablet VIII was an editor's summary and is set aside. It needs the tablet's own text or a bracketed note in its place.
- A note on the killing of Humbaba describes his speech differently from how the text now has it.
