# The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet V

Humbaba and the Cedars

They stood at the edge of the forest and could not speak. They looked up at the tops of the cedars, where the trees climbed out of sight, and they looked at the dark mouth of the wood where the path ran in. There was a track worn smooth where Humbaba walked, and the road ran straight and the way was good. And there before them was the cedar mountain, the home of the gods, the throne of Irnini. Down the whole face of the mountain the cedars poured their green; their shade was deep and good and full of rest. The thornbushes were knotted together; the undergrowth was a thick mat; the boxwood and the cedar stood close. A ravine ran around the wood, two leagues across, and again two-thirds of that, and there was no easy way in.

And the forest was loud. It was loud all day long. A pigeon was calling and a turtledove answered it; the wood rang with the cry of the stork and rippled with the chatter of the francolin; the cicadas kept up their high singing without rest. High in the branches the mother monkeys called and the young monkeys squealed, and the whole green mountain was a kind of music — a band playing, a drum kept up day in and day out, before the face of Humbaba.

Gilgamesh and Enkidu went in among the trees, and the going was slow, and their courage rose and fell as they walked. They steadied each other on the bad ground. “A slippery path holds no fear for two who hold each other up,” they said to each other. “Two will not be pulled down. A rope of three strands is not easily cut. Even the great lion — two cubs can roll him over.” So they kept on, deeper, until they came to where the guardian was.

And Humbaba saw them, and he mocked them. His voice was not afraid. “So the fool and the half-wit come to give each other advice,” he said. “But you, Gilgamesh — why have you come to me at all? Go on, Enkidu, you son of a fish that never knew its father, you turtle that never sucked its mother's milk — go on and counsel him. I watched you when you were small and running with the beasts, and I did not bother to come down to you then. And now you have brought Gilgamesh up into my forest, and you stand here, an enemy, a stranger.” His face was a horror, and it would not hold still; it shifted and changed as they watched. “Gilgamesh,” he said, “I will cut your throat and your neck. I will feed your flesh to the screaming vulture, to the eagle and the carrion bird.”

Gilgamesh's heart turned over in him, and he said low to his friend: “Enkidu — his face keeps changing. I cannot look at it.” And Enkidu answered him hard, to put the iron back in him: “Why are you whimpering like that, my friend? Why are you hiding behind your own small voice? We have come too far for this. There is nothing left to do but fall on him — strike, and strike harder, and do not lift your foot to run. The smith who would shape the metal blows the bellows again and again until the iron glows. Blow on it. Do not turn your back. Bring down the flood on him; crack the whip.”

So they fell on Humbaba, and the fight was a thing that shook the world. The ground split under the heels of their feet. They wheeled and grappled, and the mountains cracked with it — Sirion split, and Lebanon split. The bright clouds went black overhead, and death came raining down on them like a cold fog. Then Shamash, who loved Gilgamesh, loosed out of heaven the great winds against Humbaba — the south wind and the north, the east wind and the west, the whistling wind, the piercing wind, the blizzard, the evil wind, the wind of Simurru, the demon wind, the ice wind, the storm, the sandstorm — thirteen winds, and they rose up all at once and closed over Humbaba's face. He could not push forward through them; he could not scramble back out behind. The winds held him fast, and he could neither fight nor run. And Gilgamesh's weapon was at his throat.

Then Humbaba, pinned and beaten, begged for his life. “You are young yet, Gilgamesh,” he said. “Your mother bore you, and you are the child of Ninsun, the wild cow of the byre. It was Shamash, the lord of the mountain, who roused you to come up here — this was never your own doing. O Gilgamesh, king, true son of Uruk — let me go, and I will be your servant. I will cut down as many of these trees as you tell me to cut. I will guard the fine timber for you, the wood good enough for your own palace. Only let me live.”

But Enkidu said: “Do not listen to him, my friend.” And Humbaba turned then to Enkidu. “You know the law of my forest,” he said. “You above all — you who lived here among the beasts, you know what Enlil set down. I should have caught you at the gate and killed you the moment you set foot among my branches. I should have fed your flesh to the vulture and the eagle. And now, Enkidu, the mercy is in your hands. Speak to Gilgamesh. Tell him to spare my life.”

Enkidu would not be moved. “My friend,” he said to Gilgamesh, “Humbaba, the guardian of the Cedar Forest — finish him. Grind him down, kill him, leave nothing. Do it now, before word of this reaches Enlil where he sits in Nippur, before the great gods hear and turn their anger on us. Enlil is in Nippur; Shamash is in Sippar. Set up a monument that will stand forever, and let it say how Gilgamesh killed Humbaba.”

When Humbaba understood that he would die, he spent his last breath on a curse. He laid it on the two of them where they stood: “May the two of you never grow old. And may Enkidu find no one to bury him but his friend Gilgamesh.” And Enkidu, hearing it, said: “My friend, I have been talking and talking to you, and you have not been listening to me. You have been listening to the curse of Humbaba.” But the thing was already decided. They closed on him, and they cut him open, and they tore out his insides and his tongue, and Humbaba the guardian was dead. And abundance rained down on the mountain.

Then they took up the axes against the cedars. Gilgamesh felled the great trees, and Enkidu went among them clearing the stumps and the brush. And as they worked, Enkidu looked at the tallest cedar of all, the one whose crown scraped the sky, and a thought came to him. “My friend,” he said, “we have brought down the highest cedar there is, the tree whose top touches the heavens. Let us make a door out of it — a door seventy-two cubits high and twenty-four wide and a cubit thick, its frame and both its pivots cut whole from the one tree. Let us carry it to Nippur. The Euphrates itself will carry it down for us, and Nippur will be glad of it.”

So they lashed the great timber into a raft. Enkidu took the steering, and the river took the load, and the cedars went down the Euphrates toward home. And Gilgamesh stood on the raft and held in his hands the head of Humbaba.
