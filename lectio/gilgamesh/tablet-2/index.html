The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet II

Bread, Beer, and the Wrestling at the Gate

Tablet II2 of 11

Shamhat took Enkidu by the hand and led him like a child. She brought him to a camp of shepherds out where the sheepfolds stood, and the shepherds gathered around to look at him. They said to one another: “This man is built like Gilgamesh — shorter, maybe, but heavier in the bone. He was raised in the open country; he was suckled on the milk of wild things.”

They set bread before him and he did not know what it was. All his life he had drawn the milk of wild animals; now they put bread in front of him, and he squinted at it and stared. Enkidu did not know how to eat bread. He had not been taught how to drink beer.

So Shamhat opened her mouth and said to him: “Eat the bread, Enkidu. It is what life is made of. Drink the beer. It is the custom of the land.” Enkidu ate the bread until he was full. He drank the beer — seven jugs of it — and the heart in him lifted, and his face shone, and he sang for the gladness of it.

Then they took water and washed the matted hair from his body, and they rubbed him with oil, and he became a man. They put clothes on him, and he was dressed like a husband. He took up a weapon and went out against the lions, so the shepherds could lie down and sleep. He ran off the wolves; he drove away the lions. He kept watch over the flock in the dark of the night, and the men of the camp lay easy, and Enkidu was their guard — a man awake while everyone else slept.

One day a stranger came along the road, hurrying toward Uruk. Enkidu and Shamhat were sitting there, and Enkidu raised his eyes and saw the man, and called to him. “You there — where are you running in such a hurry? What hard road are you on?”

The man caught his breath and answered: “They have called me to a wedding. That is the custom here — a man chooses a wife and the city comes to feast. I have piled the wedding table high with good things to eat. But before the bridegroom touches her, the king touches her first. For Gilgamesh, the king of Uruk, the bride is opened to him first of all. The gods decreed it; from the day his birth-cord was cut, it has been his by right. He goes in to the bride before the husband does.”

When Enkidu heard the man's words, his face went pale, then dark with anger. “Take me to Uruk,” he said. “Take me to where Gilgamesh is. I will stand in front of him. I will shout in his face, ‘I am the strongest here!’ I will change the way things have always been. The one born in the wild country is the stronger man — and I will prove it.”

He went on ahead, and Shamhat came behind him, and they entered Uruk. The people crowded around him in the street. They gathered at his feet and looked him up and down. “He is built like Gilgamesh,” they said. “The very image of him — shorter, but heavier in the bone. Here is one born in the open country, raised on the milk of wild beasts.” And the men rejoiced: “At last, a man to stand against Gilgamesh. At last, someone has come who is his match.”

That night the bed was laid for the goddess Ishhara; Gilgamesh was coming to lie with the new wife as he always did. But Enkidu stepped into the street and planted himself at the door of the wedding house. He set his foot against the door and would not let Gilgamesh pass.

They came together at the door of the wedding house. They grabbed each other and locked, two great oxen lowering their heads to gore. They smashed the doorpost. They cracked the wall. They wrestled through the street, and the whole of Uruk shook with it — doorframes splitting, the houses trembling on their foundations, the people pressed back against the walls to watch two men try to break each other.

Gilgamesh bent his knee, one foot still planted on the ground — and with that turn his fury broke and ran out of him. He stepped back. And when he had drawn back, Enkidu spoke to him, and the heat went out of both of them. Enkidu said: “Your mother bore one man like you and no other. She is the wild cow of the byre, Ninsun, and she lifted your head higher than any man's. Enlil himself has given you the kingship over the people.”

They threw their arms around each other and kissed. The anger was gone as if it had never been. They took each other by the hand, and from that hour Gilgamesh and Enkidu were one heart.

Time passed, and Enkidu grew heavy and quiet. He sat down and his eyes filled, and his arms hung loose, and the strength in him had no work to do. Gilgamesh saw it and asked him: “My friend, why are your eyes full of tears? Why are your shoulders down, and your heart sick inside you?” And Enkidu answered: “Grief has closed my throat, my friend. My arms have gone slack; my strength has turned to weakness.”

Then Gilgamesh said: “There is a forest far off, the Cedar Forest, and a thing lives in it called Humbaba, the terror set to guard it. Let us go there, the two of us, and cut down the cedar, and kill the guardian. Whatever is evil in the land, let us drive it out. I am set on this. A man does not live forever; only the gods do. Whatever a man does, it is wind in the end. But a name — a name stands. Let us go and cut our names into the world, so that when I am gone the people will say, ‘Gilgamesh did this. He went into the Cedar Forest and he did not turn back.’”

Enkidu had known that forest once, when he ranged the hills with the beasts, and the thought of it stopped his breath. “My friend,” he said, “that country is no place for men. I knew it when I lived among the animals. The forest runs sixty leagues in every direction; who would go down into that? Humbaba — his roar is the floodwater coming, his mouth is fire, and his breath is death itself. He hears the wild cattle stir a half-day off, deep in his trees. Enlil set him there to keep men out, and laid a weakness on whoever goes in. Why do you want this thing? No one comes back from a fight with Humbaba.”

But Gilgamesh would not be turned, and when the elders of Uruk heard what he meant to do they came and stood before him, troubled. “You are young, Gilgamesh,” they said. “Your heart runs ahead of you, and you do not yet know what you are reaching for. We have heard of Humbaba. His roar is a flood. His mouth is fire. His breath is death. Why do you want this? No one stands against him and lives.” But Gilgamesh only laughed, and looked at his friend, and set his face toward the cedar and the long road out of the city.
