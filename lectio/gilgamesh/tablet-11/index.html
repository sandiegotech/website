The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet XI

The Flood, the Plant, and the Walls of Uruk

Tablet XI11 of 11

Gilgamesh looked at him — at Uta-napishti, the Distant — and said: “I look at you, and you are no different from me. You are nothing strange. You are made the way I am made. I had thought to find you a hard man, a fighter, ready to be fought. But here you lie at your ease on your back, doing nothing. Tell me — how did it happen? How did you come to stand in the assembly of the gods, and find the life that does not end?”

And Uta-napishti said to him: “Gilgamesh, I will tell you a thing that is hidden. I will tell you a secret of the gods. You know the city Shuruppak, the old city that stands on the bank of the Euphrates. The city was old, and the gods who lived in it were old. And it came into their hearts, the great gods, to send down the Flood. Anu their father swore to it, and the warrior Enlil, who drove the thing forward, and Ninurta their helper, and Ennugi who keeps the dikes and the canals. And Ea, the clever one, had sworn the oath with them, but he repeated their words to the reed wall of my house.

“‘Reed wall, reed wall! Fence, fence! Hear me, reed wall; understand me, fence! Man of Shuruppak, son of Ubar-Tutu — tear down your house and build a boat. Give up your goods and look for life. Let your possessions go, and save what is alive. Take up into the boat the seed of every living thing. The boat you build, let its measure be measured: let its length and its width be the same; roof it over the way the deep is roofed.’ And I understood, and I said to Ea, my lord: ‘I hear you, and I will do it. But what shall I say to the city — to the elders and the people, when they ask me why?’ And Ea taught me what to tell them.

“So I built the boat. The whole city came to help me. On the fifth day I laid out the frame of it. Its floor was an acre square; its walls rose a hundred and twenty cubits high, and each side of its deck ran a hundred and twenty cubits: as high as it was long and wide. I gave it six decks below and divided it into seven, and each of those into nine, and I drove the water-plugs into it. I laid in the poles and stored what was needed. I melted pitch in the kilns, and I poured the pitch over the inside and the outside, until the seams were sealed and the water could not come in. Every day I slaughtered oxen and sheep for the workers, and I gave them beer and wine to drink, red wine and white, as if it were river water, so that they feasted as men feast at the New Year. And on the seventh day the boat was finished.

“The launching was hard; they had to shift the ballast above and below until two-thirds of it was in the water. Everything I had I loaded into her — all my silver, all my gold, all the living seed I owned. I put on board my family and my kin. I put on the beasts of the field, the wild things and the tame, and all the craftsmen, every one. Shamash had set me a time: ‘When the one who sends the storms rains down ruin in the evening, go into the boat and seal your door.’ That time came. I watched the look of the weather, and the weather was a terror to look at. I went into the boat and sealed the door behind me, and to the boatman, to Puzur-Enlil who had caulked her, I gave over the boat and all that was in it.

“As soon as the first light of morning came, a black cloud rose up out of the horizon. Adad the storm-god thundered inside it, and before him went Shullat and Hanish, the heralds, moving over hill and plain. Erragal tore out the mooring-poles of the world; Ninurta came and made the dikes overflow. The Anunnaki lifted their torches, and the whole land blazed with their flare. The stillness of Adad swept up to the sky, and everything that had been light was turned to dark. The land was shattered like a pot. For one whole day the south wind blew, gathering speed as it came, and it drove the water up over the mountains and fell on the people like a battle. No one could see anyone else. No one in the sky could make out the people below. Even the gods were afraid of the Flood. They drew back; they went up to the heaven of Anu and cowered there against the wall, crouched like dogs.

“And Ishtar cried out like a woman in childbirth, sweet-voiced Belet-ili, the Lady of the gods, wailed aloud: ‘The old days have turned to clay, because I spoke for evil in the assembly of the gods! How could I speak for evil in the assembly of the gods, and call for a war to destroy my own people? It was I who gave birth to them, my people — and now they fill the sea like the spawn of fish.’ And the Anunnaki, the great gods, wept with her. The gods sat bowed and weeping, their lips drawn tight, all of them together.

“Six days and seven nights the wind went on, the storm and the Flood flattening the land. When the seventh day came, the storm broke off, and the Flood that had fought like an army grew quiet. The sea fell still. The wind dropped, the Flood held off. I opened a hatch, and the light fell on my face, and I looked out at the weather. Everything was still. And all of mankind had turned to clay. The floodplain lay flat as a roof. I sank down. I sat and I wept; the tears ran down my face. I looked for the shoreline at the rim of the sea, and at twelve places, far off, an island showed.

“The boat ran aground on the mountain called Nimush. Mount Nimush held the boat fast and would not let it shift. One day, and a second day, Mount Nimush held the boat and would not let it shift. A third day, and a fourth, Mount Nimush held the boat fast. A fifth day, and a sixth, Mount Nimush held it, and would not let it shift.

“When the seventh day came, I brought out a dove and let it go. The dove flew off, and it came back; there was no place to rest, and it turned and came home to me. I brought out a swallow and let it go. The swallow flew off, and it came back; there was no place to rest, and it turned and came home to me. Then I brought out a raven and let it go. The raven flew off, and it saw that the water had gone down; it ate, and it bobbed its head, and it walked about, and it did not come back. So I knew the land was dry.

“Then I brought everything out to the four winds, and I made a sacrifice. I poured out a libation on the peak of the mountain. I set out seven and seven cult-vessels, and into the bowls I heaped reed and cedar and myrtle. The gods smelled the smell of it. The gods smelled the sweet smell, and they gathered like flies over the man making the offering. And when the great Lady came, she lifted up the great jeweled flies that Anu had made for her courtship, and she said: ‘You gods here — as surely as I will never forget this necklace at my throat, I will remember these days, and never forget them. Let all the gods come to the offering — but let Enlil not come, for he, without thinking, brought on the Flood and gave my people over to ruin.’

“And then Enlil came. And when he saw the boat, Enlil was filled with rage. He turned on the gods, on the host of heaven: ‘Has some living soul escaped? No man was meant to live through the destruction!’ Ninurta opened his mouth and spoke to the warrior Enlil: ‘Who but Ea could have done such a thing? It is Ea who knows every craft.’ And Ea opened his mouth and spoke to the warrior Enlil: ‘You are the wise one of the gods, the warrior. How could you, without thinking, have brought on the Flood? Lay the man's sin on the man who sins; lay the wrong on the wrongdoer. But ease your hand, before he is wiped out; bear with him, before he is cut off. Rather than send the Flood, better a lion had come and thinned the people; rather than send the Flood, better a wolf, better a famine, better a plague had come and laid the land low. As for me — I did not give away the secret of the great gods. I let Atra-hasis the wise see a dream, and so he heard the secret of the gods. Now — decide what is to be done with him.’

“Then Enlil came up into the boat. He took me by the hand and led me out, and he led my wife out, and made her kneel beside me. He stood between us and touched our foreheads to bless us: ‘Until now Uta-napishti was a man. From this hour on, Uta-napishti and his wife shall be like us, like the gods. Uta-napishti shall live far off, at the mouth of the rivers.’ And they took me, and they settled me far away, at the mouth of the rivers.

“But you, Gilgamesh — who will call the gods to assembly for you, so that you can find the life you are looking for? Come — see if you can go without sleep for six days and seven nights.” And Gilgamesh sat down on his haunches, and no sooner had he sat than a mist of sleep came breathing over him, the way fog comes off the sea, and he slept. Uta-napishti said to his wife: “Look at the strong man who wants to live forever — sleep is already breathing over him like a fog.” And his wife said: “Touch him, wake him. Let him go home in peace the way he came, back through the gate he came out of, back to his own land.” But Uta-napishti said: “Mankind is a deceitful thing; he will try to deceive you. Come — bake a loaf of bread for each day, and set it by his head, and mark the wall with the days he sleeps.”

So she baked the loaves and set them by his head, and she marked the wall for each day he slept. The first loaf had gone hard as a stone. The second was like leather. The third was soggy. The crust of the fourth had turned white. The fifth was gray with mold. The sixth was still fresh. And the seventh — he was still asleep when Uta-napishti reached out and touched him, and Gilgamesh woke. And Gilgamesh said to him: “Sleep had hardly poured over me, and you reached out and touched me and woke me!” But Uta-napishti said: “Look at the loaves, Gilgamesh. Count them. Your first loaf is a stone; your second is leather; the third is soggy; the fourth has gone white; the fifth is gray with mold; the sixth is still fresh — and the seventh, you were sleeping when I touched you.” And Gilgamesh said: “Uta-napishti, what am I to do now? Where am I to go? A thief has taken hold of my body. Death is sitting in my bedroom, and wherever I set my foot, there death is.”

Uta-napishti turned to Ur-shanabi the boatman. “Ur-shanabi,” he said, “the harbor has cast you out, and the boat has cast you out. You who used to walk this shore — you are barred from it now. As for the man you brought here, his body is matted with hair, and the skins he wears have spoiled the beauty of him. Take him, Ur-shanabi, and bring him to the washing-place. Let him wash his hair clean in water, until it is white as snow. Throw away the skins, and let the sea carry them off. Let his body be wetted till it shines. Bind a fresh cloth on his head, and put on him a robe to cover his nakedness — a robe that will stay new until he comes to his own city, that will not wear out, that will stay fresh the whole way home.” So Ur-shanabi took him and washed him, and threw the skins into the sea, and put the fresh robe on him, and the two of them went down and boarded the boat to start for home.

But the wife of Uta-napishti said to him: “Gilgamesh came here worn out with the journey. What will you give him to carry home?” And Gilgamesh heard, and lifted up his pole and brought the boat back to the bank. So Uta-napishti said to him: “Gilgamesh, you came worn out with the journey. What shall I give you to carry home? Let me tell you a hidden thing, Gilgamesh — a secret of the gods I will tell you. There is a plant. It grows down under the water, and it has a thorn like a rose, and it will prick your hand like a bramble. But if you can lay your hands on it, you will have life again — it makes the old man young.” When Gilgamesh heard it, he opened the channel and tied heavy stones to his feet, and they pulled him down into the deep water. He took the plant, though it cut his hands. He cut the stones loose from his feet, and the sea threw him back up onto its shore.

And Gilgamesh said to Ur-shanabi the boatman: “This is the plant of the heartbeat, the cure by which a man gets back his life. I will carry it home to Uruk of the strong walls. I will give it to an old man to eat, and watch — and so I will test the plant. Its name is The-Old-Man-Grows-Young. And when it has proved itself, I will eat of it myself, and have my own lost youth again.” At twenty leagues they broke bread. At thirty leagues they stopped for the night. And Gilgamesh came on a pool whose water was cool, and he went down into it and bathed. But a snake smelled the sweetness of the plant. It came up out of the water without a sound and carried the plant away, and as it went it shed its skin. And Gilgamesh sat down and wept. The tears ran down his face, and he said to Ur-shanabi: “For whom, Ur-shanabi, have my arms worn themselves out? For whom has the blood of my heart been spent? I have done myself no good at all. I have done a good turn for a snake. And now the tide has carried it twenty leagues away, and I cannot find the channel again, and I have left the boat on the shore, and there is nothing here for me.”

So they went on, and at twenty leagues they broke bread, and at thirty leagues they stopped for the night, and at last they came to Uruk of the strong walls. And Gilgamesh said to Ur-shanabi the boatman: “Climb up, Ur-shanabi, onto the wall of Uruk. Walk along it. Look at the foundation; study the brickwork. Were these bricks not fired in the kiln? Did the Seven Sages themselves not lay the groundwork? A square mile of city, a square mile of date groves, a square mile of clay pits, and half a square mile for Ishtar's temple — three square miles and a half, and all of it is Uruk.”
