The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet IV

The Road to the Cedars

Tablet IV4 of 11

At twenty leagues they broke bread. At thirty leagues they stopped for the night. In a single day they walked what an ordinary man would walk in a month and a half. They were going toward the Cedar Forest, toward Humbaba and the road that no one returns from, and they made nothing of the distance. They dug a well before the setting sun, so that there would be fresh water in it.

Gilgamesh climbed the hill, and he poured out a handful of flour on the ground, and he said: “Mountain, bring me a dream. Let me see a good sign.” Enkidu fixed the place where Gilgamesh would sleep, like a man making a shelter against a storm wind. Gilgamesh lay down with his chin on his knees, and sleep came over him, the way sleep falls on all people.

But in the middle of the night his sleep broke off, and he started up and said to his friend: “Enkidu — did you wake me? Why am I awake? Did you call out, or did a god pass by? My flesh is crawling. Enkidu, I had a dream, and it was a hard one. We were standing in a gorge between two mountains, and the mountains came down. The two of us were like little flies in the reed-beds.” And Enkidu said to him: “My friend, your dream is good. The dream is a piece of luck. The mountain you saw is Humbaba. We will take Humbaba, we will throw him down, and his great body will lie out flat on the ground.”

Let me see a good sign.” Enkidu fixed the place where Gilgamesh would sleep, and Gilgamesh lay down with his chin on his knees, and sleep came over him, the way sleep falls on all people.

But in the middle of the night his sleep broke off, and he started up and said to his friend: “Enkidu, I had a second dream, and it was worse than the first. A mountain stood before me. It threw me down and pinned my feet, and a great light grew brighter and brighter overhead. Then a man stepped out of it — the most handsome man in the land — and he pulled me out from under the mountain. He gave me water to drink, and my heart grew steady, and he set my feet back on the ground.” And Enkidu said: “My friend, your dream is good. The dream is a piece of luck. The mountain is Humbaba. Even if he throws you down, you will not stay down. The one who pulled you free and gave you water — that is the sun-god Shamash, who loves you, and Lugalbanda, your own father, the god who watches over you. The two of them will lift you out of every danger, and we will do a thing the world will remember.”

At twenty leagues they broke bread. At thirty leagues they stopped for the night. In a single day they walked what an ordinary man would walk in a month and a half. They dug a well before the setting sun, so that there would be fresh water in it.

Gilgamesh climbed the hill and poured out a handful of flour on the ground, and he said: “Mountain, bring me a dream. Let me see a good sign.” Enkidu fixed the place where Gilgamesh would sleep, and Gilgamesh lay down with his chin on his knees, and sleep came over him, the way sleep falls on all people.

But in the middle of the night his sleep broke off, and he started up and said to his friend: “Enkidu, I had a third dream, and the whole earth was in an uproar. The ground cracked and split. The sky went black as pitch, and lightning struck across it, and fire broke loose. The flames stood up tall — and then death came raining down. The light died out, the fire went out, and everything that had fallen turned to ash.” But Enkidu turned the dream over in his mind and said: “My friend, this dream too is good. We will come down on Humbaba like that storm. Take heart. We will go up against him, and he will not stand.”

And Gilgamesh wept before Shamash, and Shamash heard him out of heaven, and a voice came to him in warning. “Hurry,” the god said. “Stand against him now, before he reaches the deep wood and goes down among the trees and wraps himself in his power. Humbaba wears seven cloaks of terror. He has put on only one; the other six are laid aside. Strike while he wears the one. Once he is armored in all seven, no one alive can come near him.”

[More dreams follow on the road, but the clay is broken, and they cannot be read whole.]

At last they came to the edge of the Cedar Forest, and they stood still. The trees rose up before them, taller than any trees they had ever seen, and the green of them was deep and dark and went on without end.

And there was the gate of the forest, broad and high. Enkidu reached out and put his hand against it — and the moment he touched it his hand went heavy and his arm went numb, and the strength drained out of it for a while. He stood there, his hand hanging useless, afraid; and the tears ran down his face, and he could not lift his arm.

Gilgamesh said to him: “Enkidu, my friend — why are you crying like a coward? You who were never afraid, you who came down out of the hills, who killed lions at the watering place and never flinched — why has your courage gone? Take my hand. We will go on together. Forget death; think of life. A man who walks in front of his friend keeps him safe, and his name lives after him. Two people will not drown — a boat with two ropes does not sink, and a rope braided three ways is not easily cut. You hold me up, and I will hold you, and let whatever comes, come. We have come this far. There is no turning back now.”

So they steeled themselves at the gate of the wood. The strength came back into Enkidu's arm. They looked once more at the dark of the trees and the height of the cedars, and they breathed deep, and they went in.
