# The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet IX

The Road of the Sun

Gilgamesh wept for Enkidu, his friend, and the bitterness of it drove him out into the wild country. “I will die too,” he said to himself, “and will I not be like Enkidu? Grief has come into my heart. I am afraid of death, and so I roam the open land. I will take the road to Uta-napishti, the son of Ubar-Tutu, and I will go quickly.”

He kept on, out past the edge of the settled world, until he came to the passes of the mountains. There were lions in those passes, and at night he was afraid. He lifted his eyes to the moon-god Sin and prayed: keep me alive. He took up his axe in his hand and drew the sword from his belt, and he fell on the lions the way an arrow falls, and scattered them, and killed them, and made nothing of them. [About two dozen lines are lost.]

And he set his face to the road, however long it was, and went.

He came at last to the twin mountain, the mountain called Mashu. Day after day Mashu keeps the watch over the going-out and the coming-in of the sun. Its two peaks lean up against the floor of heaven; its roots reach down to the underworld below. At its gate the sun comes up in the morning, and into its gate the sun goes down at night. It was the door the sun passed through, and there was no other like it.

The Scorpion-people kept the gate of it — a man and his wife. The terror of them was a thing that could kill; the dread they shed went out over the mountains. When the sun rose and when it set, it was they who watched over it. Gilgamesh saw them, and the fear of it covered his face and went dark over him; but he gathered himself, and bowed his head, and went on toward them.

The Scorpion-man called to his wife: “The one coming toward us — the flesh of him is the flesh of the gods.” And the woman answered him: “Two parts of him are god, and one part is human.” Then the Scorpion-man spoke out to Gilgamesh, to the offspring of the gods: “Why have you come this long road? Why have you traveled all this way to me, across waters that are hard to cross? Tell me the reason you have come.” [About sixteen lines are lost.]

Gilgamesh answered him: “I came for Uta-napishti, my forefather — the one who stood in the assembly of the gods and was given life that does not end. I came to ask him about death, and about life.” The Scorpion-man opened his mouth and said to Gilgamesh: “There has never been a man, Gilgamesh, who could do what you ask. No one has gone through the heart of the mountain. For twelve double-hours the way runs on inside it — twelve double-hours of darkness, thick darkness, and no light anywhere. At the rising of the sun the gate is opened, and at the setting of the sun the gate is shut, and between them is only the dark. No one has ever gone in there and come out.” [About sixty lines are lost: the rest of the warning, and Gilgamesh's answer.]

“My heart is heavy,” Gilgamesh said, “with sorrow and with cold dread. In the heat and in the frost, weeping or weariness, I will go. Open the gate of the mountain to me.” And the Scorpion-man heard him out, and was moved, and said: “Go, then, Gilgamesh. Go, and do not be afraid. The mountain of Mashu I open to you. The high places and the ranges of it — cross them safely. May your feet carry you where you mean to go.” And he opened the gate of the mountain to him.

So Gilgamesh went in, and took the Road of the Sun. At one double-hour the dark was thick, and there was no light: it would not let him see ahead or behind. At two double-hours the dark was thick, and there was no light: it would not let him see ahead or behind. He kept on going.

At three double-hours the dark was thick, and there was no light: it would not let him see ahead or behind. At four double-hours the dark was thick, and there was no light: it would not let him see ahead or behind. He kept on going. At five double-hours the dark was thick, and there was no light: it would not let him see ahead or behind. At six double-hours the dark was thick, and there was no light: it would not let him see ahead or behind. He kept on going.

At seven double-hours the dark was thick, and there was no light: it would not let him see ahead or behind. At eight double-hours he pressed forward, and he cried out, for the dark was thick and there was no light, before him or behind. At nine double-hours — he felt the north wind on his face. The dark was still thick, and still there was no light, but the air had moved, and he kept on.

At ten double-hours the way was nearly through. It was only two double-hours more. At eleven double-hours he came out just before the rising of the sun, and ahead of him was the first gray of the light. And at twelve double-hours it had grown bright — the darkness was behind him, and he stepped out into the full of the day.

He came out into a garden, and it ran down to the sea. He looked, and stood still. The trees of the garden were the trees of the gods, and what they bore was not fruit but stones. There stood a tree hung with carnelian, red as ripe fruit, heavy with it, beautiful to look at. Another tree carried lapis lazuli for its leaves, deep blue, and it too bore its fruit, a thing good to see.

And everywhere through the garden the gems grew like a thicket. Where another wood would have thorns and briars, here there was carnelian and the red stone; where another would have vines, here were gems hanging down. There was cedar, and agate, and the pearl out of the sea, and lapis lazuli grew where brambles would be. Whatever a garden by the shore should be — leaf and branch and bush and thorn — here it was all cut from jewels, and it shone, and the sea lay beyond it. [The end of the tablet is broken, and a few lines are lost.]
