The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet VIII

The Mourning of Gilgamesh

Tablet VIII8 of 11

As soon as the first light broke, Gilgamesh opened his mouth and made his lament over Enkidu. “Enkidu, my friend — your mother was a gazelle, your father a wild donkey of the steppe, and the wild asses raised you on their milk, and the herds of the open country taught you every pasture and watering place. Now I weep for you, Enkidu, and I cannot stop.”

“Let the roads weep for you — the roads we walked together to the Cedar Forest. Let them mourn you day and night and never fall silent. Let the elders of broad-streeted Uruk mourn you, the ones who stood behind us with their blessing when we set out. Let the high country mourn you, the ridges we climbed side by side. Let the open land cry out for you as if it were your mother.”

“Let the cypress and the cedar mourn you, the great trees we cut our way through in our anger. Let the bear mourn you, and the hyena, the panther and the leopard, the stag and the ibex, the lion and the wild bull, and every creature of the steppe that ran beside you when you were one of them. Let the holy river Ulaya mourn you, the river along whose bank we used to walk in our pride. Let the pure Euphrates mourn you, the water we poured out from our skins. Let the young men of Uruk mourn you, who watched us strike down the Bull of Heaven. Let the plowman mourn you at his furrow, the one who sang your name into his work; let the herdsman who mixed butter and beer for your mouth mourn you; let the brewer who poured you the good beer mourn you; let the woman of the temple, who rubbed your body with sweet oil, mourn you; let everyone who set a wedding feast and saw you mourn you. Let them all weep for you, Enkidu, my brother.”

“Hear me, men of Uruk; hear me, you elders. I weep for Enkidu, my friend. I cry out like a woman who is keening over the dead. He was the axe at my side, the bow in my hand, the dagger at my belt, the shield that went before me. He was my finest robe, the broad sash around my waist. And an evil thing has risen up and stolen him from me.”

“My friend, you ran with me over the hills; together we caught the Bull of Heaven and killed it; together we threw down Humbaba, who lived in the Cedar Forest. What is this sleep that has taken hold of you now? You have gone dark, and you do not hear me.”

But Enkidu did not lift his eyes. Gilgamesh reached for his heart and laid his hand against it, and it was still; there was no beat under his hand. Then he covered his friend's face, the way a man veils the face of a bride. He circled the body again and again, back and forth, like an eagle wheeling overhead — like a lioness whose cubs have been taken from the pit, turning and turning with nowhere to go.

He tore out his hair in fistfuls and let it fall. He ripped off his fine clothes and flung them down on the ground as if they were a filthy thing, a thing he could not bear to touch.

Again, as soon as the first light broke, Gilgamesh cried out across the land. He called to the coppersmith and the goldsmith, the stonecutter and the jeweler. “Make a likeness of my friend. Make a statue of Enkidu.” And he laid out for them what they were to make: the body of gold, the chest of lapis lazuli, the skin laid over with precious stone — a figure such as no craftsman had ever shaped before, so that his friend would be honored and not forgotten.

“I will lay you down on a great bed,” he said to Enkidu. “I will lay you on a bed of honor. I will give you the seat of rest, the seat at my left hand, and the rulers of the earth will kiss your feet. I will make all of Uruk weep for you and turn the whole glad people to mourning, and when you are gone I will let my own hair grow wild, and put on a lion's skin, and walk out into the open country.”

When the next day's light came, Gilgamesh broke the seal on his treasure-house. He brought out a great table cut from precious wood and set it where Shamash, the sun-god, could see it. He filled a bowl of carnelian with honey, and he filled a bowl of lapis lazuli with butter, and he set them out and lifted them up to Shamash.

He brought out his finest things — the rich gear and the worked metal and the bright stone laid up in the treasury — and he displayed them one by one, gifts to be carried down to the powers of the underworld. He set out a gift for the queen of the dead, and for the gods who govern the house of dust, and showed each one to Shamash, so that they would welcome his friend and walk at his side.

[Here the tablet is badly broken. What follows is the heart of the funeral — the long inventory of the grave goods Gilgamesh sends down with Enkidu, named gift by named gift, god by god of the underworld. Parts of it survive: a flask, a throwing-stick of fine wood, vessels of carnelian and lapis, weapons and ornaments, each presented to a named netherworld power. The full list cannot be read.]

[The close of Tablet VIII, the last of the funeral rites, is lost.]
