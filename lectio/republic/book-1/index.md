# The Republic, Book I

What Is Justice?

I went down to the Piraeus yesterday with Glaucon, the son of Ariston, to offer my prayers to the goddess, and to see how they would put on the festival, this being the first time it was held. I thought the procession our own people made was beautiful, though the one the Thracians marched in looked every bit as good. We said our prayers, watched the spectacle, and set off back toward the city. And just then Polemarchus, the son of Cephalus, caught sight of us from a distance, starting on the road home, and sent his slave running ahead to tell us to wait for him. The boy caught my cloak from behind and said: Polemarchus asks you to wait.

I turned around and asked where his master was.

There he is, said the boy — coming up behind you. Just wait.

Of course we'll wait, said Glaucon; and in a few minutes Polemarchus came up, with Adeimantus, Glaucon's brother, Niceratus the son of Nicias, and several others, all on their way from the procession.

Polemarchus said: Socrates, it looks to me as if you two are heading back to the city.

A fair guess, I said.

But do you see how many of us there are? he said.

Of course.

Well then, he said, either prove yourselves stronger than all of us, or stay where you are.

Isn't there one option left, I said — that we persuade you to let us go?

And could you persuade us, he said, if we refused to listen?

Not a chance, said Glaucon.

Then you'd better make up your minds to it: we won't be listening.

Adeimantus added: Hasn't anyone told you there's a torch-race on horseback this evening, in honor of the goddess?

On horseback! I said. That's something new. You mean riders carrying torches and handing them on to one another as they race?

Exactly, said Polemarchus — and there's an all-night celebration besides, which will be worth watching. We'll go out after dinner and see it. There will be crowds of young men there, and we'll talk. So stay — don't be difficult.

It looks as if we'll have to, said Glaucon.

Then it's settled, I said.

So we went home with Polemarchus, and there we found his brothers Lysias and Euthydemus, and with them Thrasymachus of Chalcedon, Charmantides of Paeania, and Cleitophon the son of Aristonymus. Polemarchus's father Cephalus was there too. I hadn't seen him in a long while, and he seemed to me to have grown very old. He was sitting on a cushioned chair with a garland on his head — he had just been sacrificing in the courtyard — and other chairs stood around the room in a half-circle, so we sat down beside him. The moment he saw me, Cephalus greeted me warmly, and then he said:

Socrates, you don't come down to see us as often as you should. If I were still strong enough to make the trip up to town easily, you wouldn't need to come here — I would come to you. But as things are, you must visit the Piraeus more often. I want you to know: in my case, as the body's pleasures wither away, the appetite for conversation and the delight I take in it grow in exact proportion. So don't refuse me. Keep company with these young men, and come here to us as to old friends — as to your own family.

And I enjoy it, Cephalus, I said. I like talking with the very old. They have gone ahead of us on a road that we too will probably have to travel, and I think we ought to ask them what it's like — rough and hard going, or smooth and easy. You have reached the point the poets call 'the threshold of old age,' and I would especially like to hear what you make of it. Is it a hard stretch of life? What is your report?

I'll tell you, Socrates, he said, exactly how it looks to me. Several of us of about the same age often gather together — birds of a feather, as the old proverb has it. And when we meet, most of the company do nothing but grieve. They miss the pleasures of their youth — the sex, the drinking, the feasts and everything that goes with them — and they carry on as though they had been robbed of great things: life was good then, and what they have now is no life at all. Some of them complain of the insults an old man suffers from his own family, and on that theme they recite the whole litany of miseries old age has caused them. But in my view, Socrates, they are blaming the wrong thing. If old age were the cause, it would have done the same to me and to every other man who has grown old. In fact I have known others who don't feel that way at all — above all the poet Sophocles. I was with him once when someone asked him: How is it with you and sex, Sophocles? Can you still be with a woman? Quiet, man, he said. It is the sweetest relief to have escaped all that — like escaping from a raging, savage master. I thought it a good answer then, and I think so still. For in old age comes a deep peace and freedom from such things. When the appetites slacken and loosen their grip, then what Sophocles said comes true exactly: we are set free from a whole crowd of raving masters. But of all these complaints, including the grievances about family, there is one true cause — not old age, Socrates, but a man's character. For someone with an orderly and contented nature, old age weighs only moderately. For the other sort, youth is as heavy a burden as age.

I was delighted with him, and because I wanted to keep him talking, I prodded: Cephalus, I suspect most people don't believe a word of this. They think you carry old age lightly not because of your character but because of your money. The rich, they say, have many consolations.

You're right, he said; they don't believe me. And there is something in what they say — only not as much as they think. Themistocles' answer fits here. A man from Seriphus was needling him, saying his fame came from his city and not from himself; and he replied that he himself would never have become famous had he been born in Seriphus — but neither would the Seriphian, had he been born in Athens. The same answer fits men who aren't rich and find old age hard to bear: a decent man wouldn't carry old age easily in poverty, but a bad man, however rich he got, would never be at peace with himself.

Cephalus, I said, tell me — did you inherit most of what you have, or did you make it yourself?

Make it, Socrates? he said. As a man of business I've fallen somewhere between my grandfather and my father. My grandfather — whose name I carry — inherited about the fortune I hold now and multiplied it many times over; my father, Lysanias, drew it down below what it is today. As for me, I'll be content to leave these boys here not less than I received — and a little more.

The reason I asked, I said, is that you never seemed to me to care much for money, and that is generally the mark of a man who didn't make it himself. The self-made are twice as attached to it as anyone else. They love their money the way poets love their poems and fathers love their children — as their own creation — on top of loving it for what it can do, the way the rest of us do. It makes them hard company: they have no good word for anything but wealth.

That's true, he said.

It certainly is, I said. But tell me this much more: what do you think is the greatest good you have enjoyed from having a large fortune?

Something, he said, that most people would never take my word for. Understand this, Socrates: when a man begins to face the thought that he will die, fear and worry come over him about things that never touched him before. The stories told about the world below — how the man who did wrong here must pay for it there — stories he once laughed at, begin to torture his soul with the thought that they might be true. And whether from the weakness of age, or because he now stands closer to that other place and sees it more distinctly, he fills up with doubt and dread, and starts running his accounts, asking whether he has wronged anyone. The man who finds a life full of injustices in his ledger wakes from his sleep in terror, again and again, the way children do, and lives in dark foreboding. But the man who knows he has done no wrong has sweet hope always at his side — as Pindar so charmingly says, the kind nurse of his old age. His words are these:

'Hope,' he says, 'keeps warm the heart of the man who lives in justice and holiness — hope, the nurse of his old age, the companion of his road; hope, which more than anything steers the veering, restless mind of mortals.'

Marvelously well said — wonderfully well. And it is just here that I set the great worth of having money — not for every man, but for the decent one. Wealth does a great deal to keep a man from cheating or lying to anyone, even unintentionally, and from leaving for that other world afraid — still owing sacrifices to a god, or money to a man. It has many other uses too. But weighing one thing against another, Socrates, I would say that for a man of sense, this is what wealth is best for.

You put it beautifully, Cephalus, I said. But this very thing, justice — what shall we say it is? Simply telling the truth and giving back whatever you have taken from anyone? Or can those very acts be sometimes just and sometimes unjust? Here is what I mean. Everyone would surely agree that if you borrowed weapons from a friend while he was in his right mind, and he went mad and demanded them back, you shouldn't return them — the man who returned them would not be doing right, nor would a man determined to tell the whole truth to someone in that state.

You're right, he said.

Then telling the truth and returning what you have taken, I said, is not the definition of justice.

Oh yes it is, Socrates, Polemarchus broke in — at least if we are to believe Simonides.

And on that note, said Cephalus, I hand the argument down to you all — it's time I saw to the sacrifices.

Then isn't Polemarchus your heir? I said.

Certainly, he said, laughing — and off he went to his sacrifices.

Then tell me, I said, you heir of the argument: what is it Simonides said about justice that you think he got right?

That it is just to give every man what he is owed. That's what he said, and I think he said it well.

Well, it isn't easy to doubt Simonides — a wise man, an inspired one. But what he means by it, you may understand, Polemarchus; I confess I don't. He obviously doesn't mean what we were saying a moment ago — returning something a man left in your keeping when he asks for it back out of his mind. And yet what is left in your keeping is owed, surely, isn't it?

Yes.

But it must on no account be returned when the man asking for it back has lost his mind?

Certainly not.

So when Simonides said that giving back what is owed is just, he wasn't including that case?

Certainly not — because he thinks a friend owes a friend good treatment, never harm.

I see, I said. Then a man who returns gold left in his keeping, when the return would hurt the receiver and the two are friends, isn't giving back what is owed — that's what you take Simonides to mean?

Yes.

And what about enemies — should they too be given whatever we happen to owe them?

Absolutely, he said — what is owed to them; and what an enemy owes an enemy, I take it, is exactly what suits him: something bad.

Then Simonides, it seems, was speaking in riddles, the way poets do, about what justice is. What he really meant, apparently, is that justice is giving each man what suits him — only he called that his due.

That must have been his meaning, he said.

Then by Zeus, I said, suppose someone asked him: Simonides, the art we call medicine — what does it give that is due and fitting, and to whom? What do you think he would answer?

Obviously, he'd say it gives drugs and food and drink to bodies.

And the art we call cooking — what does it give that is due and fitting, and to what?

Seasoning, to dishes.

Good. Then the art we would call justice — what does it give, and to whom?

If we are to follow the pattern, Socrates, it is the art that gives benefit to friends and harm to enemies.

So his meaning is that justice is helping your friends and harming your enemies?

I think so.

Now, when it comes to sickness and health, who is best equipped to do good to friends and harm to enemies?

A doctor.

And on a voyage, against the dangers of the sea?

A ship's captain.

And the just man — in what action, and for what purpose, is he the one best able to help friends and hurt enemies?

In war, I'd say: fighting against the one and allying with the other.

Very well. But to people who aren't sick, my dear Polemarchus, a doctor is no use?

True.

And a man who stays on land has no need of a captain?

No.

So to people not at war, the just man is useless?

I'm far from thinking that.

Then justice is useful in peace as well as in war?

Yes.

The way farming is useful — for getting grain?

Yes.

Or shoemaking — for getting shoes?

Yes.

Then what about justice? For what use, for getting what, would you say it is useful in peacetime?

For contracts, Socrates — that's where justice is useful.

And by contracts you mean partnerships — joint undertakings?

Exactly.

Now, in a game of checkers, who is the good and useful partner — the just man, or the expert player?

The expert player.

And in laying bricks and stones, is the just man a better and more useful partner than the builder?

Not at all — quite the opposite.

Then what is the partnership in which the just man is the better partner — better than the harp-player, the way the harp-player is the better partner when there are notes to be struck?

A money partnership, I think.

Except, Polemarchus, presumably not when the money is being used. When there's a horse to be bought or sold with the common funds, it's the man who knows horses you want as a partner, isn't it?

Certainly.

And with a ship, the shipwright or the captain?

True.

Then when is it — for what joint use of silver or gold — that the just man is more useful than anyone else?

When you want it deposited and kept safe, Socrates.

In other words, when the money isn't to be used at all, but to sit idle?

Precisely.

So justice is useful with money exactly when the money is useless?

It looks that way.

Likewise, when a pruning-hook has to be kept safe, justice is the useful thing, for partnerships and for individuals alike; but when it has to be used, the vine-grower's art takes over?

Clearly.

And when a shield or a lyre has to be kept and not used, justice is useful; but when they are to be used, it's the soldier's art or the musician's?

Certainly.

And so with everything else: justice is useless while things are in use, and useful while they lie useless?

So it seems.

Then justice, my friend, can't be worth very much, if it is only useful for things out of use. But let's look at it this way. The man who is cleverest at landing a blow in a fight — boxing or any other kind — is also the cleverest at guarding against one, isn't he?

Certainly.

And the man best at warding off disease is also the one best able to slip it in unnoticed?

True.

And the best guard of an army camp is the very man who is best at stealing the enemy's plans and operations?

Certainly.

So whatever a man is a good guardian of, he is a good thief of as well?

So the argument suggests, at least.

Then if the just man is good at guarding money, he is good at stealing it.

That is what the argument implies, at any rate.

So the just man stands revealed as a kind of thief. You probably learned this from Homer. He is fond of Autolycus, Odysseus's grandfather on his mother's side, and says of him that

'he surpassed all mankind in thievery and the swearing of oaths.'

So justice, by your account and Homer's and Simonides', is a kind of art of stealing — practiced, of course, for the benefit of friends and the harm of enemies. Isn't that what you meant?

No, by Zeus, he said. Though I no longer know what I did mean. But this much I still hold: justice is helping your friends and harming your enemies.

One more question, then. When you say friends and enemies, do you mean those who really are so, or those who only seem to be?

Well, he said, a man is bound to love those he considers good and hate those he considers bad.

But don't people make mistakes about that — thinking many a man good who isn't, and the reverse?

They do.

Then for people who have made that mistake, the good are their enemies and the bad their friends? True.

And in that case it is just for them to help the bad and harm the good?

Apparently.

And yet the good are just — the last people to do wrong?

True.

Then by your argument it is just to injure people who have done nothing wrong.

No, no, Socrates, he said — the argument must be a bad one.

Then it's the unjust it is just to harm, I said, and the just we should help?

That sounds better.

But watch what follows, Polemarchus. For the many men who misjudge people, it will turn out just to harm their friends — since their friends are actually bad — and to help their enemies — since their enemies are actually good. And then we will be asserting the exact opposite of what we said Simonides meant.

That's exactly what follows, he said. Let's go back and change our definition — it looks as though we didn't define 'friend' and 'enemy' correctly.

Where did we go wrong, Polemarchus? I asked.

We said a friend is whoever seems good.

And how should we change it now?

Say that a friend is the man who both seems good and is good, he said. The one who seems good but isn't only seems a friend, and isn't one. And the same for an enemy.

On this account, then, the good man will be a friend and the bad man an enemy?

Yes.

So you would have us add something to what we first said about the just. At first we said it was just to help a friend and harm an enemy; now we are to say further: it is just to help a friend if he is good, and to harm an enemy if he is bad?

Exactly, he said. Put that way, I think it's right.

But is it really the part of a just man, I said, to injure any human being at all?

Certainly, he said — bad men, enemies: those he ought to harm.

When horses are injured, do they become better or worse?

Worse.

Worse, that is, by the excellence proper to horses — not the excellence proper to dogs?

As horses, yes.

And dogs, injured, become worse as dogs — by the excellence proper to dogs, not to horses?

Of course.

Then shouldn't we say the same of men, my friend — that when they are injured, they become worse by the excellence proper to a human being?

Certainly.

And isn't justice the human excellence?

To be sure.

Then men who are injured, my friend, necessarily become more unjust.

So it appears.

Well — can musicians, by their music, make men unmusical?

Certainly not.

Or horsemen, by their horsemanship, make men worse riders?

Impossible.

Then can the just, by justice, make men unjust? In short, can the good, by their goodness, make men bad?

Surely not.

No more than heat can make things cold — that isn't its work, but its opposite's?

It cannot.

Or dryness make things wet?

Clearly not.

Then harming is not the work of the good man at all?

Impossible.

And the just man is good?

Certainly.

Then, Polemarchus, it is not the work of a just man to harm anyone — friend or anyone else. It is the work of his opposite: the unjust man.

I think you are entirely right, Socrates, he said.

So if anyone says justice is giving each man what is owed to him, and what he means by it is that the just man owes harm to his enemies and help to his friends — the man who said it was no sage. He simply wasn't telling the truth; for it has become clear to us that it is never just to harm anyone.

Agreed, said Polemarchus.

Then you and I will stand together in arms, I said, against anyone who claims that Simonides said this — or Bias, or Pittacus, or any other of the wise and blessed men?

Count me in, he said — I am ready to fight at your side.

Shall I tell you, I said, whose saying I think it really is — that it is just to help friends and harm enemies?

Whose?

I think it belongs to Periander, or Perdiccas, or Xerxes, or Ismenias the Theban, or some other rich man convinced of his own great power. That is the sort who first said that justice is 'helping your friends and harming your enemies.'

Very true, he said.

Well then, I said: since this account of justice and the just has also broken down, what else might someone say it is?

Now, all through the discussion Thrasymachus had been trying repeatedly to seize control of the argument, and each time he had been held back by the people sitting near him, who wanted to hear it out. But when we paused, and I had asked this question, he could contain himself no longer. He gathered himself like a wild animal and sprang at us as if to tear us to pieces. Polemarchus and I froze in fright.

He bellowed into the middle of the room: What is this drivel, Socrates, that has had hold of you two all this time? Why are you playing the fool, bowing and deferring to each other? If you really want to know what justice is, stop asking questions and preening yourself on refuting whatever anyone answers — you know perfectly well it is easier to ask than to answer. Answer yourself, for once: say what you think justice is. And don't you dare tell me it's the needful, or the beneficial, or the profitable, or the gainful, or the advantageous — whatever you say, say it clearly and precisely, because I won't take that kind of mush from you.

At this I was stunned, and I looked at him with fear. I think if I hadn't seen him before he saw me, I would have lost my voice altogether. As it was, I had looked at him first, just as the argument began to drive him wild, so I was able to get an answer out.

Thrasymachus, I said, trembling a little, don't be hard on us. If Polemarchus and I have gone astray somewhere in examining the argument, I promise you the error wasn't willing. If we were hunting for gold, you would never suppose we were 'deferring to each other' and spoiling our own chances of finding it; justice is a thing worth more than a great deal of gold, so don't imagine we would give way to one another like fools instead of straining as hard as we can to bring it to light. We are straining, my friend, believe me. What we lack, it seems, is the power. So the far likelier response from you clever people is pity — not anger.

He heard that and gave a great sardonic laugh. By Heracles, he said — there it is, the famous irony of Socrates! I knew it. I told these people in advance that you would refuse to answer, that you would put on your mock-modesty, that you would do anything sooner than answer a question.

That's because you are a wise man, Thrasymachus, I said. You knew perfectly well that if you asked someone what twelve is, and prefaced the question with: 'And don't you dare tell me twelve is twice six, or three times four, or six times two, or four times three — I won't take that kind of nonsense from you' — then obviously no one could answer a question put that way. But suppose he said back to you: What do you mean, Thrasymachus — I'm to give none of the answers you have listed? Even if one of them happens to be true? Am I supposed to say something false instead — or what do you want me to do? What would you answer him?

Hah, he said — as if the two cases were alike!

Why shouldn't they be? I said. But even if they aren't alike, and the person being asked thinks they are — do you suppose he'll be any less likely to answer what seems right to him, whether we forbid it or not?

Is that what you're planning, then? You are going to give one of the forbidden answers?

I wouldn't be surprised, I said — if on reflection one of them seemed right to me, danger or no danger.

And what if I show you a different answer about justice, he said — better than all of those? What penalty do you name for yourself then?

What else, I said, but the penalty that fits the ignorant? And what fits the ignorant is to learn from the man who knows. That is the penalty I propose: I'll learn.

Aren't you sweet, he said. But no — on top of the learning, you pay cash.

Certainly — when I have some, I said.

The money is there, said Glaucon. Speak up, Thrasymachus — don't worry about the fee. We will all chip in for Socrates.

Oh, I'm sure you will, he said — so that Socrates can pull his usual trick: never answer himself, and when someone else answers, seize the argument and tear it to shreds.

But my dear man, I said, how could anyone answer, when in the first place he doesn't know and doesn't claim to know, and in the second place, even if he has some notion of the matter, a man of no small authority has forbidden him to say anything he actually thinks? No — you are the one who should speak. You are the one claiming to know and to have something to say. So do it: give me the pleasure of your answer, and don't grudge your teaching to Glaucon here and the rest of them.

When I had said this, Glaucon and the others begged him to go ahead. Thrasymachus obviously wanted to speak — he thought he had a magnificent answer and was hungry for the applause — but he kept up the pretense of insisting that I be the one to answer. In the end he gave in, and then: There you have it, he said — the wisdom of Socrates. He won't teach anybody anything himself; he goes around learning from others and never so much as pays thanks.

That I learn from others, I said, is true enough, Thrasymachus; but that I never pay is a lie. I pay what I can. And what I can pay is praise, since money I don't have. How willingly I do it, when someone seems to me to speak well, you will find out the moment you answer — for I expect you to speak well.

Then listen, he said. I say that justice is nothing other than the advantage of the stronger. Well? Where is the praise? You won't give it — you refuse.

First let me understand you, I said. Justice, you say, is the advantage of the stronger. What do you mean by that, Thrasymachus? Surely not something like this: Polydamas the prize-fighter is stronger than we are, and a diet of beef serves his body's advantage — so the same diet is advantageous, and therefore just, for us weaker men too?

You're disgusting, Socrates, he said. You grab the argument by whatever handle does it the most damage.

Not at all, my friend, I said. I only want you to say more clearly what you mean.

All right, he said. You know, don't you, that some cities are ruled by tyrants, some by the people, some by aristocracies?

Of course.

And in each city the ruling element is the one that holds power?

Certainly.

And each government makes its laws to its own advantage: a democracy makes democratic laws, a tyranny tyrannical ones, and so on with the rest. In making those laws, they declare that this — their own advantage — is what is just for their subjects, and whoever steps outside it they punish as a lawbreaker and a criminal. So this, my good man, is what I say justice is, the same thing in every city: the advantage of the established government. And since the government holds the power, the conclusion, for anyone who reasons rightly, is that everywhere justice is one and the same thing — the advantage of the stronger.

Now I understand what you mean, I said. Whether it's true is another matter — that I will try to find out. But notice, Thrasymachus: you have defined justice as an advantage yourself — the very sort of answer you forbade me to give. You have merely added 'of the stronger' to it.

A trivial addition, no doubt, he said.

Whether it's trivial or important isn't clear yet. What is clear is that we must examine whether it's true. I agree that justice is some kind of advantage — but you add that it is the advantage of the stronger, and that I don't know yet. It has to be looked into.

Look into it, then.

I will, I said. Tell me: you also say, don't you, that it is just for subjects to obey their rulers?

I do.

And are the rulers in every city infallible, or are they capable of making mistakes?

They are certainly capable of making mistakes, he said.

So when they set about making laws, they make some correctly and some incorrectly?

True.

Where 'correctly' means to their own advantage, and 'incorrectly' against their own advantage? Is that your position?

Yes.

But whatever they enact, their subjects must do — and that is what is just?

Doubtless.

Then by your argument it is just to do not only what is to the advantage of the stronger, but the opposite as well — what is to their disadvantage.

What's that you're saying? he asked.

Only what you are saying, I believe. Let's look at it more closely. Haven't we agreed that the rulers, in ordering their subjects to act, sometimes mistake what is best for themselves — and that whatever the rulers order, it is just for the subjects to do? Wasn't that agreed?

Yes.

Then consider, I said: you have also agreed that it is just to do what is to the disadvantage of the rulers — the stronger — whenever they unintentionally give orders that are bad for themselves; since you say it is just for subjects to do whatever the rulers command. In that case, most wise Thrasymachus, doesn't it follow inescapably that it is just to do the very opposite of what you said? The weaker are then commanded to do what is to the disadvantage of the stronger.

Yes, by Zeus, Socrates, said Polemarchus — nothing could be plainer.

No doubt, Cleitophon broke in — if you're testifying for him.

Who needs a witness? said Polemarchus. Thrasymachus himself admits that rulers sometimes give orders that are bad for themselves, and that it is just for subjects to obey them.

Yes, Polemarchus — doing what the rulers command: that is what Thrasymachus laid down as just.

Yes, Cleitophon, and he also laid down that justice is the advantage of the stronger. And having laid down both, he then admitted that the stronger sometimes order the weaker — their subjects — to do what is against the stronger's own advantage. From those admissions it follows that what is to the advantage of the stronger is no more just than what is to their disadvantage.

But, said Cleitophon, by 'the advantage of the stronger' he meant what the stronger believed to be his advantage. That is what the weaker must do — that is what he laid down as the just.

That is not what he said, replied Polemarchus.

It makes no difference, Polemarchus, I said. If that is Thrasymachus's position now, let's take it from him that way. Tell me, Thrasymachus: was this how you meant to define justice — what the stronger believes to be his advantage, whether it really is or not?

Absolutely not, he said. Do you suppose I call a man 'the stronger' at the very moment he is making a mistake?

I did think that was what you meant, I said, when you agreed that rulers are not infallible and sometimes get things wrong.

That's because you argue like an informer, Socrates — out to trap a man on his words. Tell me: when a doctor makes a mistake about his patients, do you call him a doctor in respect of that very mistake? Or an accountant who gets a sum wrong — is he an accountant at the moment of the error, in respect of the error? We do say, as a manner of speaking, that the doctor erred, the accountant erred, the writing-master erred. But in fact each of them, insofar as he is what we call him, never errs at all. Speaking strictly — since you are such a stickler for strictness — no craftsman errs. A man errs when his knowledge deserts him, and in that moment he is no craftsman. So no craftsman, no expert, no ruler errs at the moment he is ruling, though everyone would say 'the doctor erred,' 'the ruler erred.' Take my earlier answer in that loose sense. But the strictest answer is this: the ruler, insofar as he is a ruler, does not err; unerring, he lays down what is best for himself; and that is what the subject must do. So, as I said from the beginning: the just is doing what is to the advantage of the stronger.

Well then, Thrasymachus, I said: you think I argue like an informer?

I certainly do.

You think these questions were a plot — that I asked them in order to sabotage you in the argument?

I don't think it, he said — I know it. And it will do you no good. You can't hide your tricks from me, and out in the open you will never overpower me in argument.

I wouldn't dream of trying, my dear man, I said. But so that nothing of this sort comes between us again, mark out your terms: when you speak of the ruler — the stronger whose advantage it is just for the weaker to serve — do you mean the ruler in the loose, everyday sense, or in that strict sense you were describing just now?

I mean the ruler in the strictest possible sense, he said. Now do your worst — trick that, twist that, if you can. I ask no mercy from you. Not that you have a chance.

Do you think I'm mad enough, I said, to try to shave a lion — to play the trickster with Thrasymachus?

You just tried, he said — though you're no good at that either.

Enough of this, I said. Answer me instead: the doctor in the strict sense, the one you were just describing — is he a money-maker or a healer of the sick? And speak of the man who really is a doctor.

A healer of the sick, he said.

And a ship's captain — the true captain — is he a ruler of sailors, or just another man aboard?

A ruler of sailors.

The fact that he happens to be sailing in the ship doesn't count; that is not why we call him captain. It is not the sailing that gives him his title, but his art and his authority over the crew.

True, he said.

Now, I said, doesn't each of these have its own interest — something advantageous to it?

Certainly.

An interest which the art exists to look after and provide for?

Yes — that is exactly what each art is for.

And is there any interest an art itself has, beyond being as perfect as it can possibly be — that and nothing more?

What do you mean by that?

This, I said. Suppose you asked me whether the body is sufficient in itself, or has needs. I would answer: it certainly has needs. That is exactly why the art of medicine was invented — because the body is defective, not sufficient to itself, and something was required to provide for its interests. Does that sound right to you or not?

It sounds right, he said.

But what about medicine itself — is it defective? Does any art, in general, stand in need of some further excellence, the way eyes need sight and ears need hearing, so that over the eyes and ears you need another art to study and supply what serves their seeing and hearing? Is there some flaw in the art itself, so that each art needs another art to look after its interests, and the looker-after needs yet another in its turn, and so on without end? Or does each art see to its own interest? Or does it need neither itself nor another to study what serves it — because there is no flaw or defect in any art at all, and no interest for an art to serve except its subject's? An art, so long as it is strictly and wholly what it is, is correct, and so remains unblemished and pure. Consider this with that strictness of yours: is it so or not?

It appears to be so, he said.

Then medicine considers not the interest of medicine, but the interest of the body?

Yes, he said.

And horsemanship the interest not of horsemanship but of horses. No art considers its own interest — it has no needs — but the interest of the thing it is set over?

So it appears, he said.

And yet, Thrasymachus, the arts do rule their subjects — they are the stronger, they govern the thing they work on?

He conceded this — very grudgingly.

Then no science or art studies or prescribes the interest of the stronger, but the interest of the weaker, its own subject?

He tried to fight this one too, but in the end he conceded it.

So, I said, no doctor, insofar as he is a doctor, studies or prescribes the doctor's own interest, but the patient's? We agreed that a doctor in the strict sense is a ruler of bodies, not a money-maker. Wasn't that agreed?

Yes.

And the ship's captain, in the strict sense, is a ruler of sailors, not just a man aboard?

That was agreed.

Then a captain of that kind — a ruler — will study and prescribe the interest of the sailor, the subject, and not his own?

He conceded it — barely.

So then, Thrasymachus, I said, no one in any position of rule, insofar as he is a ruler, studies or commands what is to his own advantage — only what is to the advantage of his subject, the one his craft works upon. Everything he says and everything he does is said and done with that one in view: with a view to what is good and fitting for the subject.

By the time we had reached this point in the argument, it was obvious to everyone that his account of justice had been turned inside out. Instead of replying, Thrasymachus said: Tell me, Socrates — do you still have a wet nurse?

What? I said. Shouldn't you be answering, rather than asking things like that?

Because she's neglecting you, he said. She lets you go around sniveling and never wipes your nose — thanks to her, you can't even tell sheep from shepherd.

And why exactly do you say that? I asked.

Because you imagine that shepherds and cowherds are studying the good of their sheep and cattle — fattening and tending them with some other end in view than their masters' profit and their own. Worse, you think that the rulers of cities — those who truly rule — feel toward their subjects any differently than a man feels toward his sheep, or ponder anything else, night and day, than how to draw profit from them. You are so far gone about the just and justice, the unjust and injustice, that you don't even know this: justice and the just are in reality someone else's good — the advantage of the stronger, the ruler — and the personal loss of the subject who obeys and serves. Injustice is the opposite: it rules the truly simple-minded — the just — and the ruled do what is to the advantage of the stronger, and by serving him they make him happy, and themselves not remotely so. Look at it this way, most innocent Socrates: a just man everywhere comes off worse than an unjust one. First, in contracts, wherever the two go into partnership: when the partnership is dissolved, you will never find the just man with more than the unjust — always less. Then in dealings with the city: when taxes fall due, the just man pays more and the unjust less on equal property; when there is money to be handed out, the one gains nothing and the other much. And when each of them holds office: the just man, even if he suffers no other loss, watches his own affairs decay from neglect while his justice bars him from taking anything from the public purse — and on top of that he is hated by his relatives and friends when he refuses to bend the rules for them. With the unjust man, every bit of this is reversed. I mean the man I described a moment ago, the one with the power to take and keep taking on a grand scale. Watch him, if you want to judge how much more profitable it is, for oneself, privately, to be unjust rather than just. And you will learn it most easily of all if you go to the most complete injustice — the kind that makes the wrongdoer supremely happy, and makes those he wrongs, the ones who refuse to touch injustice, supremely wretched. That kind is tyranny. It takes what belongs to others not little by little but at one stroke, by stealth and by force — the sacred and the holy, the private and the public together. When a man is caught committing any one of these crimes singly, he is punished and utterly disgraced: temple-robber, kidnapper, burglar, swindler, thief — that is what they call the ones who commit such wrongs piecemeal. But when a man seizes not only the citizens' money but the citizens themselves, and makes slaves of them — then, instead of those ugly names, he is called happy and blessed, not only by his own people but by everyone who hears that he has achieved injustice whole and entire. For those who denounce injustice denounce it not because they are afraid of doing it, but because they are afraid of suffering it. So there it is, Socrates: injustice, when it comes on a sufficient scale, is a stronger, freer, and more masterful thing than justice; and, as I said from the start, the just is the advantage of the stronger, and the unjust is profit and advantage for oneself.

Having emptied this over our ears all at once, like a bath attendant tipping his bucket, Thrasymachus intended to leave. But the company wouldn't let him; they made him stay and answer for what he had said. And I begged him myself: Thrasymachus, you astonishing man — you fling a speech like that at us, and then you mean to walk out before adequately teaching us, or finding out for yourself, whether it is true or not? Do you think it a small thing you are trying to settle — and not the conduct of a whole life, the rule by which each of us would live most profitably?

And do I think otherwise? said Thrasymachus — do I deny that it matters?

You seemed to, I said — or else to care nothing for us, and to be quite indifferent whether we live better or worse in our ignorance of what you claim to know. Come, my friend: share it with the rest of us. Whatever good you do to a company this size will not go unrewarded. And I will tell you my own view: I am not convinced. I do not believe injustice is more profitable than justice — not even if you give it free rein and never hinder it from doing what it wants. No, my friend: let a man be unjust, let him have full power to do wrong, by stealth or by open force — he still does not convince me that injustice pays better than justice. And perhaps I am not the only one of us who feels this way. So convince us properly, you blessed man, that we are wrong to value justice above injustice.

And how am I supposed to convince you? he said. If what I have just said hasn't done it, what more can I do for you? Shall I take the argument and spoon-feed it into your soul?

No, by Zeus, I said — not that. But first, stand by what you say — or if you change your position, change it openly and don't try to deceive us. As it is, Thrasymachus — let's keep examining what has been said — you see that having first defined the true doctor, you didn't think it necessary to keep the same strictness later, about the true shepherd. You think that insofar as he is a shepherd he fattens his sheep not with an eye to what is best for the sheep, but like some banqueter with an eye to the feast, or else to the sale — like a money-maker, not a shepherd. Yet surely the shepherd's art cares for nothing but the good of what it is set over — providing the best for it; as for the art's own affairs, they are provided for already, so long as it lacks nothing of being the shepherd's art. That is why I supposed just now that we must agree that every rule, insofar as it is rule, studies what is best for nothing else than the thing it rules and cares for — in politics and in private life alike. But you — do you really think that the rulers of cities, the true rulers, rule willingly, gladly?

Think it? he said. By Zeus, I know it.

Really, Thrasymachus? I said. Then haven't you noticed that no one takes the other kinds of office — the lesser ones — willingly? They demand pay for them, on the understanding that the benefit of ruling will go not to themselves but to the ruled. And tell me this much: don't we distinguish one art from another by its having a different power — a different work it can do? And answer what you actually think, my dear illustrious man, so we can get somewhere.

Yes, he said — that is what makes them different.

And each art gives us its own distinct benefit, not one benefit in common — medicine gives health, navigation gives safety at sea, and so on?

Certainly.

And the wage-earner's art gives wages? That is its power. You wouldn't call medicine and navigation the same art, would you? If a ship's captain gets healthy because the sea air agrees with him, you don't for that reason call what he practices medicine — not if we are using words strictly, as you proposed?

Certainly not, he said.

Nor call wage-earning medicine, I suppose, if a man gets healthy while earning a wage?

No.

Nor call medicine wage-earning, if a man earns fees while he heals?

Certainly not.

Then we are agreed that each art has its own particular benefit?

Yes.

Then any benefit that all these craftsmen enjoy in common clearly comes from some further thing they all make use of in common — the same thing added on to each art?

It seems so, he said.

And we say that when craftsmen earn wages and are benefited by it, that comes from their use of the additional art of wage-earning?

He agreed — reluctantly.

Then this benefit, the receiving of pay, does not come to any of them from his own art. Looked at strictly, medicine produces health and wage-earning produces the wage; house-building produces a house, and wage-earning, following along beside it, produces the pay; and so with all the rest — each art does its own work and benefits the thing it is set over. But if no pay is added, does the craftsman get any benefit from his art?

Apparently not, he said.

But does he therefore confer no benefit either, when he works for nothing?

No — he confers it, I'd say.

Then this much is clear by now, Thrasymachus: no art and no rule provides for its own benefit. As we said long ago, it provides for and commands the benefit of its subject — the advantage of the weaker, not of the stronger. That, my friend, is why I said just now that nobody willingly chooses to hold office and take other people's troubles in hand to set them straight; everyone demands pay, because the man who intends to practice his art well never does or commands what is best for himself — he commands what is best for the subject, so long as he acts within the art. For that reason, it seems, there must be pay for those who are to consent to rule — money, or honor, or a penalty for refusing.

What do you mean by that, Socrates? said Glaucon. The first two kinds of pay I recognize; but the penalty — what it is, and how it can count as pay — that I don't understand.

Then you don't understand the pay of the best men, I said — the pay for which the most decent people rule, when they consent to rule at all. Don't you know that love of honor and love of money are considered — and are — things to be ashamed of?

I know that, he said.

Well, that is why the good will not consent to rule for money or for honor, I said. They don't want to be called hirelings for openly charging a fee for the work, or thieves for quietly helping themselves out of the public funds; and honor won't move them, since they are not ambitious. So if they are going to consent to rule, there must be compulsion on them, and a penalty — which may be why it has been thought shameful to go after office instead of waiting to be forced to it. And the greatest penalty is this: to be ruled by a worse man, if you will not rule yourself. That fear, it seems to me, is what makes decent men take office when they do — and they go to it then not as to something good, not expecting to enjoy themselves in it, but as to a necessity, because they cannot hand it over to anyone better than themselves, or even as good. In a city of good men, if such a thing existed, people would fight to escape ruling the way they now fight to obtain it; and there it would become plain that the true ruler really does not exist to serve his own advantage but his subjects' — so that every man of understanding would choose to be benefited by another rather than take the trouble of benefiting others. On this point, then, I flatly refuse to grant Thrasymachus his claim that justice is the advantage of the stronger. But we will come back to that another time. What Thrasymachus is saying now seems to me a far bigger matter — his claim that the life of the unjust man is better than the life of the just. Which do you choose, Glaucon? Which side seems to you closer to the truth?

I say the life of the just man pays better, he answered.

Did you hear, I said, the whole catalogue of goods that Thrasymachus just assigned to the unjust life?

I heard it, he said. I'm not persuaded.

Then shall we persuade him back, if we can find a way to show that what he says isn't true?

Of course we should, he said.

Now, I said, if we answer him with a set speech of our own — matching his, reciting all the goods that come with being just, and then he replies, and then we do — we will end up counting and measuring the goods claimed on either side, and soon we will need jurors to decide between us. But if we examine the question as we were doing just now, by coming to agreements with each other, then we ourselves will be jurors and advocates at once.

Exactly, he said.

Then which way do you prefer? I said.

Yours, he said.

Come then, Thrasymachus, I said, answer us from the beginning. You say that complete injustice is more profitable than complete justice?

I certainly do, he said — and I have told you why.

Then how do you speak of the two of them in these terms: one of them, presumably, you call a virtue and the other a vice?

Obviously.

Justice the virtue, injustice the vice?

Very likely, my sweet friend, he said — when I am telling you that injustice pays and justice doesn't.

Then what do you say?

The opposite, he replied.

That justice is a vice?

No — a very noble simple-mindedness.

Then injustice you call bad character?

No, he said — I call it good judgment.

So the unjust, in your eyes, are intelligent and good, Thrasymachus?

The ones who can carry injustice all the way, he said — who have the power to bring cities and whole nations under them. You probably think I mean purse-snatchers. Even that pays, he said, so long as you don't get caught; but it isn't worth mentioning next to what I am describing.

I do understand what you mean, I said. What amazed me is this: that you file injustice under virtue and wisdom, and justice under their opposites.

That is exactly where I file them.

Now that, my friend, is a harder position — and it is no longer easy to know what to say. If you had laid it down that injustice pays but conceded, as some others do, that it is nonetheless a vice and shameful, we would have something to answer along the usual lines. But now you will obviously say that injustice is fine and strong, and drape it with everything we used to attribute to the just — seeing that you had the nerve to rank it with virtue and wisdom.

A true prophecy, he said.

Still, I said, I must not flinch from following the argument through, so long as I take you to be saying what you actually think. Because I believe you are genuinely not joking now, Thrasymachus — you are saying what you take to be the truth.

What difference does it make to you, he said, whether I believe it or not? Refute the position.

None at all, I said. But try to answer me this one thing more: does the just man want to get the better of another just man?

Not at all, he said. Otherwise he wouldn't be the charming innocent he is.

And would he try to go beyond what a just action allows?

No.

And getting the better of the unjust man — would he think that just, or unjust?

He would think it just, he said, and he would try — but he wouldn't have it in him.

That is not my question, I said. My question is whether the just man refuses to get the better of his like, another just man, while thinking it right to get the better of his unlike, the unjust?

Yes — that's how it is.

And the unjust man? Does he claim to get the better of the just man and the just action?

Of course, he said — he claims to get the better of everyone.

So the unjust man will also strive to get the better of another unjust man and his action — he will fight to take the most of everything for himself?

True.

Then let's put it this way, I said: the just man does not try to get the better of his like, but of his unlike; the unjust man tries to get the better of both his like and his unlike.

Nothing could be better put, he said.

And the unjust man is intelligent and good, and the just man neither?

Good again, he said.

And doesn't the unjust man also resemble the intelligent and the good, while the just man does not resemble them?

Naturally, he said — being the kind of man he is, he resembles others of that kind; the just man doesn't.

Fine. Then each of them is of the same character as those he resembles?

Certainly, he said.

Very good, Thrasymachus. Now, do you call one man musical and another unmusical?

I do.

Which of the two is intelligent, and which is not?

The musical man is intelligent, obviously, and the unmusical one is not.

And where he is intelligent, he is good at it; where he is unintelligent, bad?

Yes.

And the same for a doctor?

The same.

Then tell me, my friend: does a musician tuning a lyre want to get the better of another musician — to outdo him in the tightening and loosening of the strings — or claim to deserve more than another musician?

I don't think so.

But the better of the unmusical man — yes?

Of course.

And a doctor — in prescribing food and drink, would he want to get the better of another doctor, or of the medical art itself?

He would not.

But the better of the non-doctor — yes?

Yes.

Now look at knowledge and ignorance in general. Does any man who knows — whatever his field — want to choose to do or say more than another man who knows? Wouldn't he rather do the very same as his like in the same situation?

Well — perhaps that must be so, he said.

And the ignorant man? Wouldn't he try to get the better of the knower and the ignorant alike?

Perhaps.

And the man who knows is wise?

Yes.

And the wise man is good?

True.

Then the good and wise man will not want to get the better of his like — only of his unlike, his opposite?

So it seems, he said.

While the bad and ignorant man will try to get the better of both?

Apparently.

Now, Thrasymachus, I said: didn't we find that the unjust man tries to get the better of both his like and his unlike? Weren't those your words?

They were, he said.

And the just man will get the better of his unlike, but not of his like?

Yes.

Then the just man resembles the wise and good, I said, and the unjust man the bad and ignorant.

It looks that way.

But we agreed that each of them is of the same character as the one he resembles.

We did.

Then the just man stands revealed to us as good and wise, and the unjust as ignorant and bad.

Thrasymachus agreed to all of this, but not the easy way I'm telling it now — he was dragged to it, step by step, sweating tremendously, for it was a summer's day. And then I saw something I had never seen before: Thrasymachus blushing. Anyway, once we had agreed that justice is virtue and wisdom, and injustice vice and ignorance, I moved on:

Fine, I said; let's call that settled. But we also said that injustice is strong — powerful. Do you remember, Thrasymachus?

I remember, he said. But I'm not satisfied with what you're saying now either, and I have plenty to say about it. Of course, if I said it, I know perfectly well you would accuse me of making speeches. So either let me talk as long as I like, or, if you insist on asking questions, ask away — and I will do for you what we do for old women telling their tales: I'll say 'very good,' and nod, and shake my head.

Only never against your real opinion, I said.

To please you, he said — since you won't let me speak. What more do you want?

Nothing, by Zeus, I said. If that's what you're going to do, do it. I'll ask my questions.

Ask, then.

Then I ask what I asked before, so that our examination of justice and injustice can proceed in order — what each of them really is, compared with the other. It was said at some point that injustice is more powerful and stronger than justice. But now, I said, if justice is wisdom and virtue, it will easily be shown, I think, to be stronger than injustice, since injustice is ignorance — no one could still miss that. But I don't want it settled so flatly, Thrasymachus; I want to look at it another way. You would say that a city can be unjust — that it can try to enslave other cities unjustly, and succeed, and hold many of them enslaved under itself?

Of course, he said. And the best city — the one most completely unjust — is precisely the one that will do it.

I understand, I said — that was your argument. But here is what I am examining: will the city that becomes master of another hold that power without justice, or will it need justice to do it?

If it is as you were just saying, he said — justice is wisdom — then with justice. But if it is as I said, with injustice.

I'm delighted, Thrasymachus, I said — you are not just nodding and shaking your head; you are giving beautiful answers.

I'm indulging you, he said.

Kind of you. Then indulge me this far too, and tell me: do you think a city, or an army, or a gang of bandits or thieves, or any other group that goes after some common purpose unjustly, could accomplish anything at all if its members wronged one another?

Certainly not, he said.

And if they didn't wrong one another? They would do better?

Certainly.

Because injustice, Thrasymachus, breeds factions and hatreds and fighting among them, while justice brings concord and friendship. Isn't that so?

Let it be so, he said — I don't want to quarrel with you.

Very generous of you, my friend. Now tell me this: if the work of injustice is to plant hatred wherever it goes, then whenever it turns up — among free men or among slaves — won't it make them hate one another and split into factions, until they are incapable of doing anything together?

Certainly.

And if it turns up in two people? Won't they quarrel, and hate each other, and be enemies to each other and to the just alike?

They will.

And if injustice takes up residence in a single man, my remarkable friend — will it lose its power, or keep it undiminished?

Let's say it keeps it, he said.

Then its power appears to be something like this: whatever it enters — a city, a family, an army, anything at all — it makes that thing, first, incapable of acting as one, because of the factions and quarrels it stirs up, and second, an enemy — to itself, to everything that opposes it, and to the just. Isn't that so?

Certainly.

Then in a single man too, I suppose, it will do everything it is its nature to do. First it will make him incapable of action, at faction and war with himself, divided against himself; and then it will make him an enemy — to himself and to the just. Yes?

Yes.

And the gods too, my friend, are just?

Let's say so, he said.

Then the unjust man will be an enemy of the gods, Thrasymachus, and the just man their friend.

Feast yourself on the argument, he said — enjoy it, don't hold back. I won't cross you; I don't want these people turning against me.

Then come, I said — serve me the rest of the banquet by answering as you have been. We have shown that the just are wiser and better and more capable of action, and that the unjust can't act together at all. In fact, when we speak of unjust men ever pulling off some vigorous piece of work together, we aren't speaking quite truly: they could never have kept their hands off each other if they were wholly unjust. Clearly there was some justice in them — enough to stop them from wronging each other at the same time as their victims — and it was by that justice that they did what they did. They set out on their crimes only half-corrupted by injustice; for those wholly corrupted, the perfectly unjust, are also perfectly incapable of getting anything done. That, I take it, is how things stand — not as you laid it down at first. But there remains the question we set aside for later: whether the just also live better than the unjust, and are happier. To me they obviously are, even from what we have said so far. Still, we must look at it more closely. This is no idle topic — it is how a man ought to live.

Look, then, he said.

I am looking, I said. Tell me: would you say a horse has a function?

I would.

And would you take the function of a horse — or of anything else — to be the work that can be done only with it, or done best with it?

I don't follow, he said.

Try this: can you see with anything except eyes?

Certainly not.

Or hear with anything except ears?

No.

Then we would rightly call these their functions?

We would.

Now, you could cut a vine-shoot with a carving knife, or a chisel, or plenty of other tools?

Of course.

But with nothing so well, I think, as the pruning-hook made for the job?

True.

Then shall we set that down as its function?

We shall.

Now I think you will see better what I was asking before: whether the function of each thing is the work it alone can do, or the work it does more finely than anything else.

I see it, he said — and I think that is a thing's function.

Good, I said. And doesn't everything that has a function assigned to it also have an excellence — a virtue of its own? Back to the same examples: eyes have a function, we say?

They do.

So eyes have an excellence as well?

They have.

And ears too have a function and an excellence?

True.

And the same for everything else? Each thing has a function and an excellence of its own?

That is so.

Hold on, then: could eyes ever perform their function well if they lacked their own proper excellence and had, instead of it, the corresponding defect?

How could they? he said. You mean blindness instead of sight, I suppose.

Whatever their excellence may be, I said — I'm not asking about that yet. I'm asking whether it is by their own proper excellence that things perform their function well, and by the defect that they perform it badly.

That much is true, he said.

So ears too, deprived of their excellence, will do their work badly?

Certainly.

And we may bring everything else under the same rule?

So it seems to me.

Then next, consider this: does the soul have a function — something you could not accomplish with anything else in the world? Take managing, ruling, deliberating, and everything of that kind: is there anything but the soul to which we could rightly assign this work and say it is proper to it?

Nothing else.

And what about living? Shall we count that a function of the soul?

Very much so, he said.

And so the soul has an excellence too?

It has.

And will the soul ever accomplish its functions well, Thrasymachus, if it is deprived of its own excellence — or is that impossible?

Impossible.

Then a bad soul necessarily rules and manages badly, and a good soul does all these things well.

Necessarily.

Now, we agreed that justice is the soul's excellence, and injustice its defect?

We did.

So the just soul — the just man — will live well, and the unjust man will live badly?

Apparently, he said — by your argument.

And whoever lives well is blessed and happy, and whoever does not is the opposite?

Of course.

So the just man is happy, and the unjust man miserable?

So be it.

But it doesn't pay to be miserable. It pays to be happy.

Obviously.

Then never, blessed Thrasymachus, is injustice more profitable than justice.

Enjoy it, Socrates, he said — consider it your feast at the festival of Bendis.

A feast you provided, Thrasymachus, I said — once you turned gentle with me and stopped being fierce. And yet I have not feasted well — my own fault, not yours. I have behaved like one of those gluttons who snatch a taste of every dish as it is carried past, before they have properly enjoyed the one before. Before we found the thing we were looking for first — what justice actually is — I dropped that and rushed off to ask whether it is vice and ignorance or wisdom and virtue; and when the claim fell in our laps later that injustice is more profitable than justice, I couldn't keep myself from that one either. So the result of the whole conversation, for me, is that I know nothing. For as long as I don't know what justice is, I am hardly going to know whether it is a virtue or not — or whether the man who has it is unhappy or happy.
