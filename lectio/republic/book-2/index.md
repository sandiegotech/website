# The Republic, Book II

The Ring of Gyges

With that, I thought I was free of the argument. But it turned out, apparently, to be only a prelude. For Glaucon, who is the boldest of men in everything, wouldn't accept Thrasymachus's surrender; he wanted the fight fought to a finish. Socrates, he said, do you want to really convince us, or just to seem to have convinced us, that it is better in every way to be just than unjust?

Really, I said — if it were up to me.

Well then, you're not doing what you want. Tell me: do you think there is a kind of good we would welcome not for what comes of it, but for its own sake — glad simply to have it? Joy, for instance, and the harmless pleasures that lead to nothing afterward beyond the delight of the moment we have them.

Yes, I said, I think there is a good of that kind.

And a second kind, that we love both for itself and for what comes of it — knowing, say, and seeing, and being healthy? Goods like that we welcome on both counts, I suppose.

Certainly, I said.

And do you also see a third kind — exercise, say, and medical treatment when you're sick, and doctoring itself, and moneymaking in general? These we would call burdensome but good for us; nobody would choose them for their own sake, only for the pay and the other things that come out of them.

There is that third kind too, I said. But what's your point?

In which of the three do you place justice?

In the finest one, I'd say — the class of goods that anyone who means to be truly happy must love both for their own sake and for what comes of them.

That's not where most people put it, he said. They file justice under the burdensome kind — the goods a man practices for the pay, and for the standing a good reputation brings, but which in themselves are hard, and to be avoided.

I know they think that, I said; Thrasymachus has been running justice down on exactly those grounds for a while now, and singing the praises of injustice. But it seems I'm a slow learner.

Then listen to me too, he said, and see whether you still feel the same. Thrasymachus, it seems to me, gave in too soon — charmed by you like a snake. But to my mind neither justice nor injustice has been shown for what it is. What is each of them? What power does each have, all by itself, when it's in the soul? Never mind the pay and the consequences — that's what I want to know. So here is what I'll do, if you agree: I'll bring Thrasymachus's argument back to life. First I'll tell you what people say justice is, and where it comes from. Second, that everyone who practices it practices it unwillingly — as a necessity, not as a good. Third, that this is reasonable of them, since the unjust man's life, they say, is far better than the just man's. None of it is what I think, Socrates. But I'm at a loss. My ears are ringing with Thrasymachus and ten thousand voices like his, and the case for justice — that it is better than injustice — I have never yet heard made the way I want to hear it. I want to hear justice praised for what it is, in itself; and you are the man I most expect to hear that from. So I am going to praise the unjust life with everything I have, and my performance will show you exactly how I want to hear you, in your turn, condemn injustice and praise justice. See whether the plan suits you.

Nothing could suit me better, I said. What subject would a man with any sense rather return to, again and again, than this one?

Wonderful, he said. Then hear the first point — what people say justice is, and where it comes from.

By nature, they say, doing wrong is good and suffering wrong is bad — but the bad of suffering it outweighs the good of doing it. So once men have tasted both — dealing out wrong and taking it — those who lack the power to seize the one and dodge the other decide it pays to strike a deal: no one wrongs anyone. From there they begin laying down laws and contracts with one another, and whatever the law commands they call lawful and just. That, they say, is the origin of justice, and its very being: a compromise, halfway between the best case — doing wrong without paying for it — and the worst — being wronged without the power to hit back. Justice sits in the middle, and men settle for it not as a good but as the lesser evil; it gets its honor from weakness, from men's inability to do wrong and get away with it. For any real man, one who actually had the power, would never strike such a deal with anyone — never agree to neither wrong nor be wronged. He'd be out of his mind. There, Socrates, is the going account of the nature of justice, and of how it came into the world.

Now, that those who practice justice practice it unwillingly — for lack of the power to do wrong — we would see most plainly if we tried something like this in imagination. Give each of them, the just man and the unjust alike, license to do whatever he pleases, then follow along and watch where desire leads each one. We would catch the just man red-handed, headed down the very road the unjust man travels, driven by the craving to get ahead of others — the thing every nature naturally pursues as its good, until law drags it by force to honor equality. The license I mean would be most complete if they had the kind of power they say once came to the ancestor of Gyges the Lydian. He was a shepherd, the story goes, serving the man who then ruled Lydia. One day a great storm broke, and an earthquake tore the earth open at the very spot where he grazed his flock. Astonished at the sight, he climbed down into the chasm — and there, among other wonders the story tells of, he saw a hollow bronze horse fitted with little doors. Stooping to look in, he saw a corpse inside, larger, it seemed, than any man, and wearing nothing but a gold ring on its finger. He slipped the ring off and climbed back out. Now, when the shepherds held their usual monthly meeting, to send the king their report on the flocks, he came wearing it; and as he sat among the others, he happened to twist its bezel around toward himself, into his palm — and the instant he did, he became invisible to the men beside him, and they began talking about him as if he had left. He couldn't believe it. Feeling for the ring again, he twisted the bezel outward — and reappeared. After that he put the thing to the test, to see whether the ring really held this power, and it came out the same every time: bezel in, invisible; bezel out, visible. The moment he was sure, he arranged to be one of the messengers sent to the king; and once there, he seduced the king's wife, plotted with her against him, killed him, and took the throne. Suppose, then, there were two such rings — the just man wearing one, the unjust man the other. No one, it would seem, could be made of such iron that he would hold to justice. No one would keep his hands off what wasn't his when he could lift whatever he liked out of the market without fear, walk into any house and sleep with anyone he chose, kill anyone or spring anyone from prison at will — moving through the world, in short, like a god among men. And in all of it he would do exactly as the other man does; the two of them would be headed for the same place. Surely this is powerful evidence that no one is just willingly — only under compulsion, because justice is no private good to him: wherever a man believes he can get away with wrong, he does it. For every man believes, in his heart, that injustice pays far better than justice — and believes it rightly, says the maker of this argument. Because if a man got hold of a license like that and refused ever to do wrong, refused to lay a finger on other men's things, those who noticed would think him the most pathetic fool alive — though they'd praise him to each other's faces, lying to one another out of fear of being wronged themselves. So much for that.

As for the judgment itself — the actual lives of the two men we're discussing — we can only make it if we set the perfectly just man and the perfectly unjust man side by side; there's no other way. And how do we manage that? Like this. Subtract nothing from either: let the unjust man be perfectly unjust and the just man perfectly just, each of them complete at his own pursuit. First, then, the unjust man: have him operate the way the masters of a craft do — a first-rate pilot, say, or a doctor, who knows exactly what his art can and cannot do, attempts the one and lets the other alone, and can recover himself if he ever slips. Just so, the unjust man attempting his crimes in the right way must go undetected, if he's to be great at injustice; the one who gets caught is a bungler. For the summit of injustice is to seem just without being it. So to the perfectly unjust man we must give the most perfect injustice — nothing deducted — and allow him, while committing the greatest wrongs, to have secured the greatest reputation for justice. If he ever slips, he can recover: he can talk his way out, persuasively, should any of his wrongdoing come to light, and force his way through where force is called for — he has the courage and strength for it, and a good supply of friends and money. So there he stands. Now set beside him, in the argument, the just man: simple and noble, a man who wants, as Aeschylus says, not to seem good but to be it. So strip away the seeming. If he seems just, honors and rewards come with the seeming, and then no one could tell whether he's just for justice's sake or for the honors and rewards. Strip him bare of everything but justice, and make his condition the exact reverse of the other man's: let him do no wrong and carry the greatest reputation for wrongdoing — that way his justice is tested, proof against ill fame and everything it brings — and let him walk on unchanged until death, seeming unjust his whole life long while being just. Then both men have reached the extremes, one of justice, one of injustice, and we can judge which of the two is the happier.

Good heavens, my dear Glaucon, I said — how vigorously you scour your two men clean for the judging, first one and then the other, like a pair of statues.

I'm doing the best I can, he said. And with the two of them as I've described, it should be no trouble now to trace out the life that waits for each. Let me say it — and if it comes out coarsely, Socrates, remember that it isn't me speaking, but the men who praise injustice over justice. This is what they'll say: the just man in that condition will be whipped, stretched on the rack, chained, both his eyes burned out — and at the end, when he has suffered everything there is to suffer, he will be impaled on a stake, and learn at last that a man should want not to be just but to seem it. So that line of Aeschylus turns out to apply far more truly to the unjust man. He's the one, they'll say, pursuing the real thing; he's the one who refuses to live for appearances — who wants not to seem unjust but to be it,

'harvesting the deep furrow of his mind, where sound counsel takes root and grows.'

And the seeming wins him everything: first, rule in the city, since he's thought just; then a wife from whatever house he likes, and his children married into whatever house he likes; deals and partnerships with whomever he pleases — and profit from all of it, because cheating doesn't turn his stomach. Enter any contest against him, private or public, and he comes out on top, ahead at his rivals' expense. Being ahead, he's rich; being rich, he does his friends good and his enemies harm; and to the gods he offers sacrifice and dedicates gifts on the grand scale, magnificently, serving heaven — and whatever men he cares to serve — far better than the just man can. So the odds are, they say, that the gods love him better too. And that is how, Socrates, gods and men alike are said to conspire in making the unjust man's life better than the just man's.

I had an answer for Glaucon on the tip of my tongue, but his brother Adeimantus got in first: Socrates, he said, surely you don't think the case has been fully stated?

Why — what's missing? I said.

The very thing that most needed saying, he replied, hasn't been said.

Well then, I said, as the proverb goes — let brother stand by brother. If Glaucon has fallen short anywhere, back him up. Though for my part, what he has said already is enough to flatten me and leave me helpless to come to justice's rescue.

Nonsense, he said. Hear this much more. To bring out what I take to be Glaucon's real meaning, we have to go through the opposite speeches too — the ones that praise justice and condemn injustice. When fathers lecture their sons — when anyone with someone in his charge does it — they urge them to be just; but it isn't justice itself they praise. It's the respectability that comes of it: the hope that a reputation for being just will bring the offices, the marriages, all the advantages Glaucon just counted out as coming to the unjust man from his good name. And this crowd runs the reputation argument even further: they throw in good standing with the gods, and can list blessings by the armful, which heaven, they say, showers on the pious — just as the good Hesiod tells it, and Homer too. For the one says the gods make the oak trees, for men who are just,

'bear acorns at the crown and bees at the middle, while the wool-heavy sheep sag under their fleeces,'

with plenty of other good things in the same vein. And the other sings much the same song — a man whose fame is

'like some flawless king who, godfearing, upholds what is just: for him the black earth bears wheat and barley, his trees hang heavy with fruit, his flocks lamb without fail, and the sea yields him fish.'

Musaeus and his son promise the just even headier rewards than these, on heaven's behalf: in their account they lead them down into Hades, recline them on couches, lay on a banquet of the blessed, and leave them there garlanded and drunk for all eternity — as if the finest wage virtue could earn were an everlasting drinking party. Others stretch the gods' wages further still: the pious man who keeps his oaths, they say, leaves children's children and a whole line running on behind him. That is the style in which they praise justice — that, and more like it. But the godless and unjust get the other treatment: these they bury in mud down in Hades, and set them hauling water in a sieve; they drag them through infamy while they're still alive; and every torment Glaucon described falling on the just man who seems unjust, they assign to the unjust — that's their whole list; they have nothing else. So there you have it: the praising of the one, and the blaming of the other.

Beyond all that, Socrates, consider yet another way of talking about justice and injustice — you meet it in ordinary speech and in the poets alike. With one voice they all chant that self-command and justice are fine things, yes — but hard, and full of drudgery, while indulgence and injustice are sweet, easy to come by, and shameful only in reputation and by convention. Wrongdoing pays better than justice, they say, on the whole; and they're perfectly ready to call bad men happy and to honor them, in public and in private, provided they're rich or otherwise powerful — while the weak and the poor they slight and look past, even while admitting these are the better men. But the most astonishing of all is what they say about the gods and virtue: that the gods themselves deal misfortune and misery to many good men, and hand the opposite lot to the opposite sort. And begging priests and prophets come to rich men's doors and persuade them that they hold a power from the gods, procured through sacrifices and incantations, to heal — with feasting and pleasure — any wrong the man or his ancestors have done; and if he wants an enemy hurt, then for a small fee they will injure just and unjust alike, with certain summonings and binding-spells; for the gods, so they claim, can be persuaded to do their bidding. And for all of it they call the poets as witnesses. Some, on the ease of vice, quote Hesiod —

'Vice you can have in heaps, and easily: smooth is the road, and she lives very near. But in front of virtue the gods have set sweat,'

and a road that is long and steep. Others call Homer to witness that men can bend the gods, since he too said —

'Even the gods themselves can be moved by prayer: with sacrifice and gentle vows, with libations and the smoke of burnt fat, men turn them, praying, whenever someone has crossed the line and done wrong.'

And they produce a whole stack of books by Musaeus and Orpheus — offspring, so they say, of the Moon and of the Muses — and by these books they run their rites, persuading not only private men but whole cities that there is release and cleansing from injustice, through sacrifices and the pleasures of festival, for the living and for the dead alike. These last they call initiations — rites that deliver us from the evils of the world below; and terrible things wait, they say, for those who die without them.

Now, Socrates, he went on: when the young hear all this said about virtue and vice, and about how gods and men regard them, what do we suppose it does to their souls? I mean the gifted ones — the quick ones, who light like bees on everything they hear, gathering from it their conclusions about what kind of man to be and which road to take through life. A young man like that would most likely put Pindar's question to himself:

'By justice, or by crooked deceit, do I climb the higher wall' — and live my life fenced safe behind it?

For what the world tells me is this: if I am just but don't seem it, the being profits me nothing — there is only toil in it, and losses in plain view. But unjust, with a reputation for justice secured — that, they say, is a life fit for the gods. Well then: since the wise keep showing me that seeming overpowers even the truth, and is what happiness hangs on, I should hand myself over to it entirely. I should paint a facade of virtue around me — a shadow-picture of it, for a porch and a frontage — and haul along behind it the fox of Archilochus, wisest of sages: greedy, shifty, quick. 'But it's hard to be wicked and stay hidden forever,' someone objects. Nothing great was ever easy, we'll answer; and either way, if we're to be happy, this is the road that everything we've heard points down. To stay hidden, we'll form conspiracies and clubs; and there are teachers of persuasion for hire, selling the wisdom of the assembly and the courtroom — so partly by persuasion and partly by force, we will keep taking more than our share and never pay for it. 'But the gods can't be fooled, and can't be forced.' Come now: if there are no gods, or if human affairs are beneath their notice, why should we trouble to hide at all? And if they exist and do notice — well, everything we know of them we know from the stories and from the poets who traced their genealogies; and those same poets tell us the gods can be swayed and brought around 'by sacrifice and gentle vows and offerings.' Believe the poets in both things or in neither. If we believe them: do the injustice first, and sacrifice out of the proceeds. Stay just, and we merely escape heaven's punishment while pushing away injustice's profits. Go unjust, and we keep the profits — and by praying our way through every trespass and transgression, we talk the gods around and get off free. 'Ah, but in Hades we'll pay for what we did here — we, or our children's children.' My friend, the calculating young man will say, the initiations can do a great deal, and so can the gods of absolution. The greatest cities say so; and the children of the gods — the poets, heaven's own prophets — reveal that it is so.

On what ground, then, should we still prefer justice to the greatest injustice? If we wear a counterfeit front of decency over it, we shall do exactly as we intend, with gods and with men, in this life and after it — so runs the account of the many, and of the highest authorities. Given all of that, Socrates, by what device could a man be brought to honor justice — any man with real resources of mind or money, of body or of birth — rather than laugh out loud when he hears it praised? Truly: even a man who can refute everything I've said, and who is fully satisfied that justice is best, is remarkably forgiving toward the unjust; he feels no anger at them. He knows that apart from the rare man whom some divine instinct turns sick at wrongdoing, or the one who has won real knowledge and holds back, no one is just of his own free will. Men denounce injustice out of cowardice, or old age, or some other weakness — because they cannot manage it. The proof is plain: the first of them who comes into the power to do wrong does it — precisely as much of it as he can.

And the cause of all this, Socrates, is nothing but the fact my brother and I set before you at the start, when we told you how astonished we were: of all of you who call yourselves champions of justice — from the heroes of old, wherever their words survive, down to the men of today — not one has ever condemned injustice or praised justice except for the reputations, the honors, and the rewards that flow from them. What each of them is in itself, by its own power, inside the soul of the man who has it — hidden from gods and hidden from men — no one has ever adequately worked out, in verse or in prose: that injustice is the greatest of the evils a soul can carry within it, and justice the greatest good. If you had all been saying that from the beginning — if you had persuaded us of it from our childhood up — we would not be standing guard over one another against injustice; each man would be his own best guard, afraid that in doing wrong he'd be taking the worst of housemates into his own soul. All this, Socrates, and perhaps more besides, is what Thrasymachus — or anyone else — might say about justice and injustice, crudely turning their true powers upside down, as I see it. And I — I'll hide nothing from you — have argued it at the top of my strength precisely because I want to hear the opposite from your lips. So don't merely demonstrate to us that justice is stronger than injustice: tell us what each of them does, all by itself, to the man who has it, that makes the one an evil and the other a good. And strip out the reputations, as Glaucon asked you to. For unless you take the true reputation from each and hand each the false one, we'll say you are not praising justice but the appearance of it, not blaming injustice but the look of it — that your real advice is to be unjust and stay hidden, and that at bottom you agree with Thrasymachus: justice is another man's good, the advantage of the stronger, and injustice one's own advantage and profit, won at the weaker man's expense. You have agreed that justice belongs among the greatest goods — the ones worth having for what comes of them, but worth far more for their own sake: sight, hearing, understanding, health, all the goods that are fruitful by their own nature and not by repute. Then praise that in justice: the good it does the man who has it, by itself, and the harm injustice does him, by itself. The wages and the reputations — leave those to others to praise. From other men I could stomach that kind of case, hymning the reputations and the pay; from you I cannot — unless you order me to — because you have spent your whole life examining this one question and nothing else. So do not merely show us that justice is stronger than injustice: show us what each one does to the man who carries it — whether gods and men see it or not — that makes the one a good and the other an evil.

Now, I had always thought well of the natures of Glaucon and Adeimantus, but hearing all this I was purely delighted, and I said: Sons of that great man — Glaucon's admirer opened his elegy for you well, after you had distinguished yourselves in the battle at Megara, when he wrote:

'Sons of Ariston: godlike line of a famous man.'

That seems exactly right, my friends. There is something truly godlike in your condition if you can argue like that for injustice and remain unconvinced that it is better than justice. And I believe you really are unconvinced — my evidence is the whole rest of your character; from the speeches alone, I admit, I would have doubted you. But the more I trust you, the more I am at a loss what to do. I cannot defend her — I don't think I'm equal to it; and my proof is that I thought what I said to Thrasymachus had shown that justice is better than injustice, and you two would not accept it from me. Yet I cannot refuse to defend her either. I am afraid it would be a kind of impiety to stand by while justice is slandered — to give up, and not come to her aid while I still have breath in me and a voice. So the best course is to help her in whatever way I can.

At that, Glaucon and the others begged me to come to her aid every way I could — not to let the argument drop, but to track the matter down: what each of the two really is, and where the truth lies about their benefits. So I told them what seemed true to me: The inquiry we're attempting is no small thing; it wants sharp eyes, in my judgment. And since we are no great geniuses, I said, I think we should search the way men with weak eyesight would, if they were told to read small letters at a distance and then one of them noticed that the same letters existed somewhere else — larger, on a larger field. It would look like a godsend, surely, to read the big ones first, and then check the small ones against them, to see whether they really are the same.

Certainly, said Adeimantus. But Socrates, what's the parallel in our search for justice?

I'll tell you, I said. There is a justice of one man, we say; but there is also, surely, a justice of a whole city?

Certainly.

And a city is bigger than one man?

It is.

Then justice may well exist on a larger scale in the larger thing, and be easier to read there. So, if you're all willing, let's first search out what justice is in cities; then let's examine it in the individual too, looking in the shape of the smaller for the likeness of the larger.

That seems a fine proposal, he said.

And if we watched a city coming into being — in speech — wouldn't we also see its justice coming into being, and its injustice?

We probably would.

And once the city is complete, we can hope to spot what we're hunting more easily?

Yes — much more easily.

Then should we attempt it? I warn you, I said, it's no small job. So think it over.

It's been thought over, said Adeimantus. Go on — don't hold back.

Well then, I said: a city comes into being, I'd say, because none of us is self-sufficient. Each of us falls short of that — we all need many things. Or can you think of any other root for a city to grow from?

None.

So one man calls in a second for one need, and a third for another; and since we need many things, many of us gather into a single settlement as partners and helpers — and to that shared settlement we give the name city.

True, he said.

And each gives to another, or takes from him, whenever they trade — each believing the exchange leaves him better off.

Very true.

Come, then, I said — let's build a city in speech, from its beginning. Though what really builds it, it seems, is our need.

Of course.

And the first and greatest of needs is providing food — the thing our existence and our life depend on.

Absolutely.

The second is shelter; the third, clothing and things of that kind.

True.

Well then, I said: how will our city keep up with all that supply? Won't it take one man who's a farmer, another the housebuilder, and someone else a weaver? And should we add a shoemaker, or somebody else who tends to the body's needs?

We should.

So the city at its barest minimum would come to four or five men.

So it appears.

Now, how should they work? Should each man put his labor into the common pool — the one farmer, say, raising food for all four, spending four times the hours and four times the sweat on food, and sharing it out with the others? Or should he ignore them and produce a quarter of that food, for himself alone, in a quarter of the time — and spend the other three quarters getting himself a house, a coat, and a pair of shoes, troubling himself with no one, seeing to his own needs with his own hands?

Adeimantus's view was that he should keep to raising food for everyone, rather than try to produce everything himself.

Probably right, I said. Because the moment you say it, it strikes me that, to begin with, no two of us are born quite alike. Our natures differ; different natures suit different work. Don't you think?

I do.

Well: when does a man do finer work — when he spreads himself across many trades, or when he keeps to one?

When he keeps to one, he said.

And this, I think, is clear as well: let the right moment for a piece of work slip past, and the work is spoiled.

Clear enough.

Because the work won't wait on the worker's leisure. The worker has to follow the work's own schedule, and not treat it as a thing for his spare time.

He has to.

So it comes to this: everything is produced more plentifully, more easily, and better, when one man does one job — the job his nature fits him for — at the right moment, with his hands free of all the rest.

No question.

Then we'll need more than four citizens, Adeimantus, to cover the supplies we named. The farmer, it seems, won't be forging his own plough — not if it's to be a good one — or his hoe, or the rest of his field tools. Neither will the builder make all of his, and he needs plenty too; and the same goes for the weaver and the shoemaker.

True.

So carpenters and smiths and many other craftsmen of that kind will come to share our little city, and start to crowd it.

True.

Still, it wouldn't be very large yet — not even once we add the cowherds and shepherds and the rest of the herdsmen, so that the farmers have oxen for the plough, the builders and farmers both have teams for hauling, and the weavers and shoemakers have fleeces and hides.

Not very small either, he said, with all of those in it.

And then there's the site itself: to plant the city in some spot where it will need no imports at all — that's close to impossible.

Impossible.

So it will need still more people, to fetch in what it needs from other cities.

It will.

But if our agent goes out empty-handed — carrying nothing that the people he needs things from happen to need themselves — then empty-handed he'll come home. Won't he?

So it seems.

Then what they make at home must be not just enough for themselves, but of the kind, and in the quantity, to serve the people they'll be trading with.

It must.

So our city needs more farmers, and more of the other craftsmen.

More of them, yes.

And more agents too, presumably — the men to handle each import and export. And those men are merchants, aren't they?

Yes.

Then we'll need merchants as well.

Certainly.

And if the trading runs over the sea, we'll need a whole crowd more — men who know the work of ships.

A whole crowd of them, yes.

Now, inside the city itself: how will they share out what each of them makes? That sharing, remember, was the very reason we formed a partnership and founded a city at all.

Obviously, he said, by buying and selling.

That gives us a marketplace, then — and money, a token that stands in for goods, for the sake of exchange.

Certainly.

But suppose the farmer, or one of the other craftsmen, hauls what he's made to market and doesn't arrive at the same moment as the people who want to trade with him. Is he to sit idle in the marketplace, while his own work goes wanting?

Not at all, he said — there are men who see that opening and station themselves to fill it. In well-run cities they're generally the weakest in body, useless for work of any other kind. Their business is to stay planted in the market, paying money for goods to those who come wanting to sell, and handing over goods for money to those who come wanting to buy.

So that need, I said, brings retailers into our city. Isn't that what we call the men who sit fixed in the marketplace to serve the buying and the selling — retailers — while the ones who rove from city to city are merchants?

Just so.

And there are still other workers, I think — men whose minds don't quite earn them a place as partners, but whose bodies are strong enough for heavy labor. They sell the use of their strength; the price they get for it is called a wage; and so, if I'm not mistaken, they're called wage-earners. True?

True.

Then wage-earners, too, help fill out our city.

I think so.

Well then, Adeimantus — has our city grown to its full size now?

Perhaps it has.

Then where in it is justice? And where is injustice? At what point did they come in, among all the things we've been moving in?

I can't see it, Socrates, he said — unless it's somewhere in the dealings of these people with one another. I can't imagine where else they'd be found.

You may well be right, I said. We'd better look straight at it, then, and not flinch.

First, let's consider how people provided for in this way will live. Won't they raise grain and make wine, weave clothes, cobble shoes? They'll build their houses; in summer they'll work mostly stripped down and barefoot, in winter clothed and shod well enough. For food they'll grind their barley and their wheat — kneading the one, baking the other — and heap their handsome flatcakes and loaves on reeds or on clean leaves. They'll dine reclining on beds strewn with bindweed and myrtle, they and their children with them, drinking their wine after, wearing garlands, singing hymns to the gods, delighting in one another's company; and they'll have no more children than their means can carry, keeping a wary eye on poverty and on war.

But Glaucon broke in: You've served these men their dinner, it seems, with nothing to put on the bread.

True enough, I said — I forgot. They'll have relish too, of course: salt, obviously, and olives and cheese, and the bulbs and greens that country people boil into their pots; and for dessert we'll set them figs and chickpeas and beans, and they'll roast myrtle-berries and acorns at the fire, sipping wine in moderation as they go. And so, in peace and in health, they'll live out their lives to a good old age, as you'd expect, and hand down the same kind of life to their children after them.

And if you were fitting out a city of pigs, Socrates, he said — isn't that exactly the feed you'd fatten them on?

Well, how should it be done, Glaucon? I said.

The way it's usually done, he said. If they're not to live like animals, people recline on couches, dine off tables, and eat the cooked dishes and sweets that people eat today.

Ah, I said — now I understand. So we're examining not just how a city comes into being, but how a luxurious city does. Maybe that's no bad thing: watching that kind, we might spot sooner where justice and injustice take root in cities. Still, the true city — the healthy one, so to speak — is, to my mind, the one we've just described. But if you all want to look at a city with a fever, nothing stands in our way. Plainly the simple life won't satisfy some people. There will have to be couches, and tables, and the rest of the furniture; cooked delicacies, naturally, and perfume and incense, courtesans and pastries — and every variety of each. And the things we first counted as bare necessities — houses, clothes, shoes — plain versions will no longer do: now painting and embroidery must be set in motion, and gold and ivory and everything of that sort must be procured. Yes?

Yes, he said.

Then the city has to swell again; the healthy one is too small now. It must be stuffed with a mass and multitude of occupations that answer to no natural need — the whole tribe of hunters, to start, and all the imitators: the many who work in shapes and colors, and the many who work in music — the poets and their train of servants, reciters, actors, dancers, producers; and the makers of gear of every description, the gear of women's finery included. And we'll need many more servants besides. Won't there be a call for tutors, wet-nurses, nannies, ladies' maids, barbers — and then for sauce-cooks and chefs? And swineherds too: we had none in our earlier city — there was no need — but in this one we'll need them; and herds upon herds of every other animal, if people are going to eat them. Won't we?

Certainly.

And living this way, we'll need doctors far more often than we did before?

Far more.

And the land, which was enough to feed everyone then, will now be too small. True?

Quite true.

Then we'll have to carve ourselves a slice of our neighbors' land, if we're to have pasture and plough-land enough — and they'll want to carve a slice of ours, if they too have let themselves slip past the limit of the necessary, into the endless getting of money.

There's no avoiding it, Socrates, he said.

And after that we go to war, Glaucon. Or how does it play out?

We go to war, he said.

Now, let's say nothing yet about whether war works harm or good, I said — only this much: we have found where war is born. It grows out of the same soil that most of a city's evils grow from, private evils and public ones alike.

No doubt of it.

Then, my friend, the city must be bigger yet again — and not by a little, but by a whole army: one that will march out and fight the invaders for all the city's wealth, and for everything and everyone we were just describing.

Why? he said. Aren't the citizens enough by themselves?

Not if the bargain we all struck was sound, I said, when we were shaping the city. And we agreed, if you remember, that one man cannot practice many crafts well.

True, he said.

Well then — doesn't fighting a war look to you like a craft?

Very much so.

One deserving as much attention as shoemaking?

It certainly does.

But we stopped the shoemaker from trying to be a farmer at the same time, or a weaver, or a builder; a shoemaker he stayed, so that the shoemaking would be done well. To every other worker, the same way, we assigned one job — the job his nature fitted him for, the one he'd work at all his life, kept clear of the others, never letting the right moments slip — and so do fine work. Now, is anything more important than that the work of war be done well? Or is war so undemanding a business that a farmer can be a soldier on the side — or a shoemaker, or the practitioner of any trade you like — when nobody on earth can so much as become a decent player of checkers or dice by taking the game up as a pastime instead of working at it from childhood? Does a man just pick up a shield, or whatever the weapon or tool of war may be, and turn that same day into a competent fighter in heavy armor, or in any other kind of combat — when no other tool in the world makes a craftsman or an athlete of the man who merely grabs hold of it, and is worth nothing to anyone who hasn't gained the knowledge of it and put in the practice?

Tools that could teach a man their own use, he said, would be beyond price.

Then the greater the guardians' work is, I said, the more it demands: the most freedom from every other business, and the greatest skill and dedication in its own.

I'd certainly think so, he replied.

And a nature, too, fitted to that very calling?

Of course.

Then it would seem to be our job — if we can manage it — to pick out which natures, and what kind of natures, are fit to guard a city.

Our job indeed.

By Zeus, I said — then it's no light thing we've shouldered. Still, no flinching: we do what our strength allows.

No flinching, he agreed.

Now — do you think, I said, that when it comes to keeping guard, the nature of a well-bred pup differs much from the nature of a well-born young man?

How do you mean?

I mean each of them needs senses sharp enough to spot the enemy, speed enough to run down what he's spotted, and strength enough, if he catches it, to fight it out.

They'll need all of that, yes.

And courage, surely — if he's going to fight well.

Of course.

And will any creature be brave without spirit — a horse, a dog, anything alive? Have you never noticed what an irresistible, unbeatable thing spirit is? Let a soul have it, and that soul stands fearless before anything, and gives way to nothing.

I have noticed.

So what our guardian has to be in body is clear enough.

Yes.

And in soul as well: he must be spirited.

That too.

Then here's the problem, Glaucon, I said. With natures like that, what is to keep them from turning savage — against one another, and against the rest of the citizens?

By Zeus, he said — it won't be easy.

And yet they must be gentle to their own people and hard on the enemy. Otherwise they won't wait for somebody else to destroy the city; they'll get there first themselves.

True, he said.

So what do we do? I said. Where do we find a character at once gentle and great-spirited? A gentle nature is the opposite of a spirited one, after all.

So it appears.

And yet take away either quality, and a good guardian is out of the question. The combination looks impossible — from which it follows, apparently, that a good guardian is impossible too.

I'm afraid so, he said.

Stuck there, I looked back over what we'd been saying. We deserve to be stuck, my friend, I said — we've wandered off from the very image we set up.

Meaning what?

Meaning we failed to notice that there really are natures with these opposites in them — natures we were assuming couldn't exist.

And where would one find them?

In plenty of animals, actually — but best of all in the very one we matched our guardian with. You know how it is with well-bred dogs: their disposition is to be as gentle as can be with the people they know and are used to, and the very opposite with strangers.

I know it well.

So the thing is possible, I said — and in looking for a guardian built this way, we're not looking for anything against nature.

Apparently not.

Now, would you say our future guardian needs one thing more — that along with the spirit, he needs something of the philosopher in his nature?

How so? he said. I don't follow.

It's another thing you'll see in dogs, I said — something to wonder at in a mere animal.

What thing?

That the sight of a stranger angers a dog before the man has done him any harm at all, while the sight of someone he knows delights him, though the man has never done him a moment's good. Has that never struck you as strange?

I'd never paid it much attention before, he said. But that's certainly what they do.

Well, it's an exquisite touch in his nature, that — genuinely philosophic, in fact.

How so?

Because the one test he uses, I said, to tell a friendly face from a hostile one is that he knows the first and doesn't know the second. Now, how could a creature be anything but a lover of learning, when it defines what is its own and what is foreign by knowledge and by ignorance?

How indeed.

And the love of learning and the love of wisdom — philosophy — are the same thing?

The same, he said.

Then may we say it of a man too, and say it with confidence: if he's to be gentle toward his own and toward the people he knows, he must be by nature a lover of wisdom and of learning?

We may.

So there is our man: philosophic, spirited, swift, and strong by nature — the one who is going to make a fine and good guardian of our city?

Nothing surer.

That is what he'd be to start with, then. But now, how do we rear these men, and how do we educate them? And will working through that question move us toward the goal this whole inquiry is aimed at — how justice and injustice come to be in a city? I don't want us to skip what matters, or to drag through what doesn't.

Adeimantus answered: I fully expect the question will serve us well.

Then by Zeus, my dear Adeimantus, I said, we mustn't drop it — even if it proves a long road.

No, we mustn't.

Come then — like men with time on their hands, telling stories to pass it — let's educate these men of ours in speech.

By all means.

So what will the education be? Or is it hard to find better than the one long time has already worked out — physical training for the body, and for the soul, music: everything that belongs to the Muses?

It would be hard.

And we'll begin the education with music, before physical training?

Of course.

And under music, I said, you count stories, don't you?

I do.

And stories come in two kinds — the true and the false?

Yes.

And our men must be educated in both — but in the false ones first?

I don't see what you mean, he said.

You don't see, I said, that we start our children on myths before anything else? And a myth, taken as a whole, is false — though there's truth in it too. And we set children to myths before we ever set them to physical training.

That's so.

That's what I meant by taking up music before physical training.

Quite right, he said.

And you know that in any work the beginning is what matters most — above all with anything young and tender. That's when a creature is most easily molded; that's when whatever stamp you choose to press into it sinks in and holds.

Exactly.

Then shall we carelessly let our children hear whatever myths whoever happens along has happened to make up — and take into their souls opinions that are, for the most part, the very opposite of the ones we'll think they should hold when they're grown?

We will not allow that at all.

So our first job, it seems, is to stand watch over the makers of stories: to approve any story they make well, and to reject the rest. The approved ones we'll persuade the nurses and mothers to tell the children — shaping their souls with stories with far more care than they give to shaping their bodies with their hands. Most of the stories they tell now will have to be thrown out.

Which ones do you mean? he said.

The great stories will show us the pattern for the smaller ones too, I said. Great and small are necessarily cut from the same cloth, and carry the same stamp. Don't you think so?

Very likely, he replied — but I still don't know which ones you're calling great.

The ones Hesiod and Homer told us, I said — those two, and the rest of the poets. They have always been mankind's great storytellers; and the tales they composed were false, and they told them to men, and men tell them still.

But which stories? he said. What is the fault you find in them?

The fault that deserves the first and gravest blame, I said: telling lies — and what's more, telling them badly.

And when does that happen?

Whenever a story draws the nature of gods and heroes falsely — the way a painter fails whose portrait carries no likeness at all to the thing he set out to paint.

Yes, he said — that kind of thing certainly deserves blame. But how do they do it? What sort of thing do you mean?

First, I said, the greatest lie of all, told about the greatest beings — and an ugly lie at that: Hesiod's tale of what Uranus did, and how Cronus took his revenge on him. As for Cronus's own deeds, and what his son did to him in turn — even if they were true, I wouldn't think they should be told so freely to the young and unformed. Far the best would be silence; and if they simply had to be told, then let a chosen few hear them in secret rite, after sacrificing not a pig but some great victim, hard to procure — so that the circle of hearers stays as small as it possibly can.

You're right, he said — those stories are hard ones.

And they are not to be told in our city, Adeimantus, I said. No young listener is to be told that in committing the most extreme crimes he'd be doing nothing shocking — or in punishing an unjust father by whatever means came to hand — that he'd only be doing what the first and greatest of the gods have done before him.

No, by Zeus, he said. I don't think those things are fit for telling either.

Nor a word about gods warring on gods — the plots, the pitched battles — none of which is true anyway — not if the future guardians of our city are to believe that hating one another over trifles is the ugliest thing there is. The last thing we should do is tell them tales of the battles of the giants, or work those battles into embroidered robes — or all the other quarrels, so many and so various, of gods and heroes against their own kith and kin. No: if we mean to persuade them that no citizen has ever been at odds with another citizen, and that such a thing is unholy, then that is the note the old men and old women must strike from the children's first days; and as the children grow, the poets must be made to compose along the same lines for them. But Hera bound by her own son; Hephaestus flung from heaven by his father for trying to shield his mother from a beating; and every battle of the gods that Homer composed — these must not be admitted into our city, whether they were meant as allegory or not. The young can't judge what is allegory and what isn't; whatever opinions get in at that age tend to set — hard to wash out, and immovable ever after. Which is exactly why the first stories they hear should be, perhaps, the finest things they could possibly hear — stories composed to turn them toward virtue.

That stands to reason, he replied. But if someone pressed us — which stories are those, exactly? — what would we say?

Adeimantus, I said, you and I are not poets, not today; we are founders of a city. Founders need to know the patterns their poets must build stories on, and the lines past which those poets can't be allowed to go. But composing the stories is not the founders' work.

Fair enough, he said. But that's just it: these patterns for speaking of the gods — what would they be?

Something like this, I said. However the god actually is, that is how he must always be rendered — whether the rendering is done in epic, in song, or in tragedy.

So he must.

Well then: the god is truly good, and must be spoken of as such?

Certainly.

And nothing good is harmful, is it?

I think not.

Does what isn't harmful do harm?

Never.

And what does no harm — does it do any evil?

No again.

And what does no evil could hardly be the cause of any evil?

How could it be?

Go on: the good is beneficial?

Yes.

The cause, then, of well-being?

Yes.

Then the good is not the cause of everything. Of what goes well it is the cause; of what goes badly it is blameless.

Entirely so, he said.

Then neither can the god, since he is good, be the cause of everything, as most people say. Of what befalls human beings he is the cause of only a little; for the greater part he bears no blame — for our goods are far fewer than our evils. The goods we must credit to no one but the god; but for the evils we have to hunt out some other cause — anything but him.

That seems to me, he said, as true as it gets.

Then we must not accept it — not from Homer, not from any other poet — when they blunder about the gods, foolishly, and tell us that two jars

'stand on Zeus's floor, filled with fates — one jar of good, the other of evil,'

and that the man to whom Zeus deals a mixture of the two

'meets now with evil, and now again with good,'

while the man who gets the second jar unmixed —

'gnawing hunger drives him across the shining earth.'

Nor this, either:

'Zeus, who deals out the good and the evil to us.'

And whoever says that the breaking of the oaths and the truce — which was Pandarus's doing — was brought about by Athena and Zeus, will not have our approval; nor the strife and the trial of the gods, laid to Themis and Zeus. Nor may the young be allowed to hear what Aeschylus says, that

'god plants the guilt in mortal men when he wills to ruin a house, root and branch.'

And if anyone composes the sufferings of Niobe — the play those lines come from — or the house of Pelops, or the Trojan war, or anything in that vein, then either he must not be allowed to call these things the work of a god; or, if a god's work they are, he must find in them some such account as we are seeking now: he must say that what the god did was just and good, and that the punished were the better for their punishment. What the poet cannot be permitted to say is that those who paid the penalty were miserable, and that a god was the cause. If he said that bad men were miserable because they needed punishing, and that the god's punishment did them good — that we would allow. But that a god, who is good, becomes the cause of evil to anyone — that claim must be fought with everything we have: no one is to say it in his own city, if that city is to be well governed, and no one is to hear it, young or old, in verse or out of it. Said aloud, such things are impieties; they profit no one; and they do not even agree with themselves.

You have my vote for that law, he said. It suits me well.

Then let this stand as the first of our laws and patterns for the gods, which all speaking and all composing will have to fit: the god is not the cause of everything — only of the good.

Quite satisfactory, he said.

Now for a second one — see what you think of this. Is the god a sorcerer? Would he appear by design now in one shape and now in another — sometimes actually changing, trading his own form for a crowd of others, sometimes only fooling us into thinking he had? Or is he single and simple — of all beings the least likely to step out of his own form?

I can't say, he replied — not on the spot.

Then try it this way. If a thing steps out of its own form, mustn't the change come either from itself or from something else?

It must.

And things in the best condition are the ones least changed or shifted by anything outside them. Take the body: the healthiest and strongest is least altered by food and drink and hard labor; take any plant: the most vigorous is least altered by sun and wind and everything of that kind. Isn't it so?

Of course.

And the braver and wiser a soul is, the less any outside force can shake it or warp it?

Yes.

And the same holds, I'd suppose, even for made things — furniture, houses, clothes: when they're well made and in good condition, time and wear alter them least.

That's so.

So whatever is in a fine state — whether nature made it so, or craft, or both — admits the least change from outside.

So it seems.

But the god, and everything that belongs to the god, is in every way in the finest state?

Of course.

Then from outside, at least, the god is the last thing in the world to be forced into many shapes.

The very last.

But might he change and transform himself?

Clearly he must, he said — if he changes at all.

Then which way? Does he make himself better and more beautiful by the change, or worse and uglier?

Worse — it could only be worse, if he's altered at all. For surely we won't say the god is lacking in beauty or in goodness.

Exactly right, I said. And that being so, Adeimantus, do you think anyone — god or man — willingly makes himself worse in any way at all?

Impossible.

Then it is impossible that a god should even want to change himself. Each of them, it seems — being as beautiful and as good as it is possible to be — remains forever, simply, in his own form.

That has to follow, he said — in my judgment at least.

Then let none of the poets tell us, my friend, that

'the gods, disguised as strangers out of far countries, take every shape, and pass through the cities of men;'

and let no one lie to us about Proteus and Thetis, or bring Hera on — in a tragedy or in any other kind of poem — transformed into a priestess collecting alms

'for the life-giving children of Inachus, the river of Argos.'

No more lies of that sort, please. And no mothers, either, taking the poets at their word and terrifying their little children with the bad version of these tales — how certain gods, mind you, go roaming by night, got up as strangers of every description. That way they blaspheme the gods and turn their children into cowards at a single stroke.

Heaven forbid, he said.

But maybe, I said, though the gods themselves are incapable of changing, they make us think they appear in all these different forms — tricking us, bewitching us?

Maybe, he replied.

What! I said. Would a god be willing to lie — to pass off falsehood in word or in deed, by holding up a phantom of himself?

I don't know, he replied.

Don't you know, I said, that the true lie — if that phrase can be spoken — is a thing every god and every man hates?

What do you mean? he said.

I mean this, I said: no one willingly accepts a lie in the most sovereign part of himself, about the most sovereign things. There, above everywhere else, everyone dreads to harbor it.

I still don't follow, he said.

That's because you think I'm saying something deep, I said. All I mean is this: to be lied to in one's own soul about the things that are — to be deceived there, to be left ignorant there, to have the lie and to hold it there — that is what no one would ever stand for. In that place, human beings hate the lie beyond everything.

Beyond everything — yes.

And this is what most exactly deserves the name I gave it just now — the true lie: that ignorance in the soul of the man who has been deceived. For the lie in words is only a copy of that condition in the soul, an image that comes after it — not the pure, unmixed lie itself. Isn't that so?

Perfectly so.

And the real lie is hated by men just as much as by gods?

Yes.

The lie in words, on the other hand — there are times when it's useful, and earns no hatred. Against enemies, for one. Or when the people we call friends, in some fit of madness or folly, are set on doing harm: then the lie becomes a kind of medicine — a way of turning them back. And in the storytelling we were discussing a moment ago: since we don't know how the truth stands about those ancient things, we make the false resemble the true as closely as we can, and so put it to use.

That's exactly how it is, he said.

Then in which of these ways would a lie be useful to the god? Would he falsify for lack of knowledge — making his lie resemble a truth about ancient days he doesn't know?

That would be laughable, he said.

So there is no lying poet in the god.

I should say not.

Would he lie, then, out of fear of enemies?

Out of the question.

Because those close to him are fools, then, or madmen?

But no fool or madman is a friend of the gods.

Then there is nothing left — no motive at all for a god to lie.

None whatever.

So everything divine, everything of god and spirit, is free of falsehood through and through.

Through and through.

Then the god is altogether simple and true, in deed and in word. He does not change in himself, and he does not deceive others — not by apparitions, not by speeches, not by the sending of signs, to the waking or to the dreaming.

That is how it looks to me too, he said, as you say it.

Then you grant our second pattern, I said, for all speaking and all composing about the gods: they are no shape-shifting sorcerers, and they do not mislead us with lies, in word or in deed.

I grant it.

So, though there is much in Homer we praise, this one thing we will not praise: Zeus sending the lying dream to Agamemnon. Nor will we praise Aeschylus, where Thetis tells how Apollo sang at her wedding, dwelling on the happiness of the child she would bear:

'how he would know no sickness, how his days would be long, and every blessing would be his; and over my fortunes, so favored of heaven, he raised the victory-song, and gladdened my heart. And I believed the divine mouth of Phoebus could not lie — brimming as it was with the prophet's art. But the very god who sang, the guest at that very table, the god who promised all of it — he is the one who has killed my son.'

When a man says things like that about the gods, we will be angry with him; we will refuse him a chorus; and we won't allow teachers to use his lines in the education of the young — not if our guardians are to grow up god-revering and godlike, as far as that lies in human power.

I am in complete agreement with these patterns, he said — and I would make them my laws.
