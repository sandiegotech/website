# The Republic, Book V

The Three Waves

A city like this, then — a constitution like this — I call good and right, and so is a man of the same pattern. And if this one is right, the others are bad and mistaken: bad in how they run their cities, bad in how they build the souls of the people who live in them. Their badness comes in four forms.

What are they? he asked.

I was about to describe the four, in the order they seemed to me to grow out of one another, when Polemarchus — he was sitting a little way from Adeimantus — reached out his hand, caught his cloak up by the shoulder, pulled him close, and leaned in to whisper something in his ear. All I caught of it was: Do we let him off, or what do we do?

Certainly not, said Adeimantus — out loud now.

Who is it, I said, that you're not letting off?

You, he said.

And why me, I asked, in particular?

Because we think you're slacking, he said — cheating us out of a whole branch of the argument, and not the smallest one, so you won't have to go through it. You thought we wouldn't notice when you tossed it off lightly — as if it went without saying — that where women and children are concerned, 'friends will hold everything in common.'

And wasn't that right, Adeimantus?

Yes, he said. But this 'right,' like everything else, needs an explanation — what kind of common holding? There could be many. So don't skip past the one you mean. We've been sitting here a long time expecting you to say something about how our citizens will produce children, how they'll rear them once they're born, this whole community of women and children you mentioned. We think it matters enormously — whether it's handled rightly or wrongly makes all the difference to the city. So now, when you're starting in on another constitution before settling these questions, we've resolved — as you heard — not to let you go until you've gone through all of it the way you went through everything else.

Put me down, said Glaucon, as voting for that resolution.

In fact, Socrates, said Thrasymachus, take it as carried unanimously.

You don't know what you're doing, I said, ambushing me like this. Just as I was finishing with the constitution, glad to have that question laid to rest, content that you'd accepted what was said — now you're starting the whole argument over from the beginning. What a swarm of arguments you're stirring up! I saw it coming, and steered around it at the time to spare us the ordeal.

And what do you think we came here for, said Thrasymachus — to prospect for gold, or to listen to an argument?

An argument, I said — one with a limit.

For anyone with sense, Socrates, said Glaucon, the limit for listening to arguments like these is a whole lifetime. So never mind us. Just don't you get tired of answering the question your own way: what will this community be like, this sharing of women and children among our guardians — and the rearing of the children while they're still young, in the stretch between birth and schooling, which people consider the most demanding of all? Try to tell us how it's supposed to go.

It's not an easy subject to go through, my innocent friend, I said. It invites even more disbelief than everything we covered before. People will doubt that what I say is possible; and even if it came fully to pass, they'll doubt it would be for the best. That's why I'm reluctant to touch it, my dear comrade — for fear the whole thing looks like a daydream.

Don't be, he said. Your audience isn't unfeeling, or skeptical, or hostile.

My good friend, I said — are you saying that to encourage me?

I am, he said.

Then you're achieving the opposite, I said. If I trusted myself to know what I'm talking about, the encouragement would be fine. Among intelligent friends who love you, a man who knows the truth about the greatest and dearest things can speak it securely. But to make the argument while you're still unsure, still searching — which is what I'm doing — is a frightening, slippery business. Not because I might get laughed at; that's childish. But if I slip on the truth just where a man most needs solid footing, I won't fall alone — I'll drag my friends down with me. So I bow to Nemesis before I say what I'm going to say. Because I honestly believe it's a lesser crime to kill a man by accident than to deceive people about what is beautiful and good and just in the laws. That's a risk better run among enemies than among friends — so thanks very much for the encouragement.

Glaucon laughed. Socrates, he said, if the argument does us any harm, we release you as in a homicide case: acquitted in advance, your hands clean of deceiving us. So take heart and speak.

Well, I said, the man acquitted in court is clean — the law says so. And if there, then probably here too.

Then on that score, he said, speak.

Now I have to go back, I said, to something that maybe should have been said in its place earlier. Though perhaps this is the right way after all: the men's drama has been played to its end, so the women's takes the stage — especially with you calling for it.

In my opinion, for people born and educated as we described, there's no right acquisition and use of children and women except along the road we started them down at the beginning. In the argument, remember, we set out to make the men watchdogs guarding a flock.

Yes.

Then let's stay with that, and give the women a birth and rearing to match, near enough, and see whether it suits us or not.

How?

Like this. Do we think the females of our watchdogs should stand guard alongside the males, and hunt with them, and share everything else they do? Or should they stay inside, on the theory that bearing and nursing the puppies leaves them unfit, while the males do the work and carry the whole care of the flock?

They share everything, he said — except that we treat the females as weaker and the males as stronger.

And can you use any animal for the same work as another, I said, if you don't give it the same rearing and training?

You can't.

So if we're going to use the women for the same tasks as the men, we have to teach them the same things.

Yes.

The men were given music and physical training.

Yes.

Then the women get those same two arts, and the work of war as well — and are used the same way.

It follows from what you're saying, I suppose.

Probably, I said, next to what we're used to, a lot of what we're proposing would look laughable if it were carried out as described.

It certainly would.

And what's the funniest thing in the picture? Isn't it obviously the women exercising naked in the wrestling schools alongside the men — and not just the young ones, but the older women too, like the old men in the gyms who keep at their exercise wrinkled and none too pleasant to look at?

By Zeus, he said — that would look ridiculous, at least by present standards.

Well, I said, now that we've launched into it, we can't fear the jokes of the clever set — all the kinds of things they'll say about a change like this in physical training, and in music, and not least in the bearing of arms and the riding of horses.

You're right, he said.

But since we've set out, we have to march into the rough country of the law — after asking these wits to drop their own business and try being serious for once, and reminding them that it wasn't so long ago that Greeks found it shameful and ridiculous, as most of the barbarian world still does, for men to be seen naked; and that when first the Cretans and then the Spartans opened their gymnasiums, the sophisticates of the day were free to make a comedy of it all. Don't you think so?

I do.

But once experience showed, I think, that stripping is better than covering all such things up, then what was laughable to the eye melted away before what argument revealed as best. And that showed something more: it's a fool who finds anything ridiculous but what's bad — who tries to raise a laugh looking at any spectacle but the spectacle of folly and vice, or who aims seriously at any mark of beauty but the good.

Absolutely.

First, then, we have to come to an agreement about whether these proposals are workable or not — and give the challenge its opening, whether it comes in jest or in earnest: is the female of the human species capable of sharing in all the work of the male, or in none of it, or in some things and not others? And the business of war — where does that fall? Isn't that the best beginning, and likely to lead to the best conclusion?

Much the best.

Then shall we make the argument against ourselves, on the objectors' behalf, so the other side of the case doesn't sit under siege undefended?

Why not? he said.

Then let's say this on their behalf: 'Socrates and Glaucon, you need no outside opponent — you convicted yourselves. At the founding of your city, you agreed that each person must do the one job nature suits him for.' We did agree, I think — of course. 'And doesn't a woman differ from a man by nature — enormously?' We'll have to admit the difference. 'Then isn't it proper to assign each a different job, matched to that nature?' Certainly. 'Then how are you not wrong — how are you not contradicting yourselves — when you now say that men and women must do the same jobs, their natures being poles apart?' There's the charge. Can you make any defense to it, my remarkable friend?

It's not so easy on the spot, he said. So I'll ask you — I'm asking now — to be the voice for our side of the case too, whatever that case is.

This, Glaucon, I said — this and a lot more like it — is what I saw coming all along. That's why I was afraid and reluctant to touch the law about how wives and children are to be gotten and reared.

By Zeus, he said, it doesn't look like an easy problem.

It isn't, I said. But it's like this: fall into a small swimming pool or into the middle of the widest sea — either way, you swim.

You do.

Then we swim too, and try to come out of this argument alive — hoping some dolphin takes us on its back, or some other rescue past reasonable hope.

It seems we'd better, he said.

Come on, then, I said — let's find the way out, if we can. We've agreed that different natures should have different pursuits, and that a woman's nature and a man's are different. And now we're saying that these different natures should have the same pursuits. That's the indictment, isn't it?

Exactly.

Glaucon, I said, the power of the art of contradiction — what a noble thing it is!

Why do you say that?

Because so many people seem to fall into it even against their will, I said. They think they're reasoning when they're really just scoring points — because they can't examine a statement by dividing it into its kinds, they chase the contradiction in the mere words. It's wrangling they practice on each other, not dialectic.

That happens to plenty of people, he said. But surely not to us, here and now?

It absolutely does, I said. We're in real danger of doing contradiction-work without meaning to.

How?

We keep insisting, bravely, combatively, on the letter of it — that unlike natures must not have the same pursuits — but we never once examined what kind of unlikeness and what kind of likeness we meant, or what our rule was aiming at when we assigned different pursuits to different natures and the same to the same.

No, he said, we never looked at that.

Then by that logic, I said, we're free to ask ourselves whether the bald and the long-haired have the same nature or opposite ones — and once we agree they're opposite, then if bald men make shoes, forbid the long-haired to make them, and if the long-haired do, forbid the bald.

That would be a joke, he said.

A joke for one reason only, I said: back then we didn't mean 'same nature' and 'different nature' absolutely, in every sense. We had our eye on the one kind of unlikeness and likeness that bears on the work itself. We meant, for instance, that a man with a doctor's mind and a woman with a doctor's mind have the same nature. Don't you think?

I do.

But a doctor and a carpenter — different natures?

Completely different.

So with the two sexes, I said: if one of them shows itself different from the other for some art or practice, we'll say that work should go to the one it suits. But if they turn out to differ only in this — that the female bears the young and the male begets them — we'll say it hasn't been shown at all that a woman differs from a man for the purposes we're talking about, and we'll keep thinking our guardians and their women should do the same work.

And rightly, he said.

Next, don't we tell the man who says otherwise to instruct us: for which art, which pursuit that goes into the framing of a city, is a woman's nature different from a man's?

That's fair.

And maybe he'll say what you said a moment ago — that it's hard to answer well on the instant, but not hard after some thought.

He might.

Then shall we ask him to follow us, and see if we can show him there's no pursuit in the management of a city that is peculiar to women?

By all means.

Come then, we'll say to him, answer us this: when you say one person is naturally gifted for something and another isn't, do you mean the one learns it easily, the other with difficulty? That the one, from a short course of study, discovers far beyond what he was taught, while the other, after long study and practice, can't even keep hold of what he learned? That in the one, the body serves the mind well; in the other, it fights it? Are these the marks — or are there others — by which you define the naturally gifted for each pursuit, and the ungifted?

Nobody will claim there are others, he said.

Now: do you know of any human occupation at which the male sex doesn't beat the female on all these counts? Or should we drag it out — talking about weaving, and minding the cakes and the stews, where the female sex is thought to be really something, and where getting beaten is the most laughable defeat of all?

It's true, he said — the one sex is bested by the other across the board, you could say. Plenty of individual women are better than plenty of individual men at plenty of things — but on the whole it's as you say.

Then, my friend, there is no pursuit in the running of a city that belongs to a woman because she's a woman, or to a man because he's a man. Natural gifts are scattered through both alike: by nature the woman shares in every pursuit, and the man in every one — only in all of them the woman is weaker than the man.

True.

So shall we assign everything to men, and nothing to a woman?

How can we?

No — because, as we'll say, one woman is born for medicine and another isn't; one is born musical, another unmusical.

Of course.

And one fit for physical training and war, while another is unwarlike and has no love of exercise?

I'd say so.

And one loves wisdom, another hates it? One is spirited, another spiritless?

That too.

Then one woman has a guardian's nature and another doesn't. Wasn't that the very nature we picked our male guardians for?

The very one.

So for guarding a city, the nature of woman and man is the same — only weaker in her, stronger in him.

So it appears.

Then women of that kind must be chosen to live with men of that kind and stand guard with them — they're up to it, and their natures are kin.

Certainly.

And the same natures get the same pursuits?

The same.

Then we've circled back to where we began, and we agree: it's nothing against nature to give the guardians' wives music and physical training.

No — nothing.

So the law we were laying down wasn't impossible, wasn't wishful — it stands in line with nature. It's the present arrangement, it seems, that stands against it.

So it seems.

Now, our task was to check whether our proposals were possible, and whether they were best?

It was.

That they're possible stands agreed?

Yes.

Then next we need agreement that they're best?

Clearly.

Well — for making a woman fit to guard, we won't have one education that makes guardian men and another that makes guardian women, will we? Especially when it receives the same nature to work on?

No — the same for both.

Then here's a question I'd like your opinion on.

What?

Do you hold that one man is better and another worse? Or do you think all men are equal?

Hardly equal.

Then in the city we were founding, who do you think turned out better men — the guardians, given the education we described, or the shoemakers, educated in shoemaking?

A ridiculous question, he said.

I take your point, I said. So — the guardians are the best of the citizens?

By far.

And these women will be the best of the women?

That too — by far.

And is anything better for a city than to have in it the best possible women and the best possible men?

Nothing.

And that is what music and physical training, applied as we described, will bring about?

Of course.

Then the arrangement we've laid down is not only possible; it's the best thing for the city.

It is.

Then the guardians' women must strip — since they'll wear excellence in place of robes — and take their share of war and the whole guarding of the city, and do nothing else; though within all that, the women get the lighter duties, because of the weakness of their sex. And the man who laughs at naked women exercising for the sake of what's best — his laughter plucks

'the unripe fruit of wisdom,'

and he doesn't know, it seems, what he's laughing at or what he's doing. For it is, and always will be, the finest of sayings: what helps is beautiful, and what harms is ugly.

Absolutely.

Say, then, that we've escaped one wave of our law about women — and weren't altogether drowned for decreeing that our guardian men and guardian women must share every pursuit. The argument stayed in agreement with itself: what it proposes is possible, and beneficial too.

It was no small wave you escaped, either.

You won't call it big, I said, once you see the one after it.

Go on, then — show me.

This law follows from that one, I said, and from everything that came before. Here it is: these women are to belong to all these men in common; no woman is to live privately with any man; and the children are to be common too — no parent to know his own child, no child his parent.

That's a far bigger wave than the other, he said. Whether it's even possible — and whether it would do any good — both invite real disbelief.

On the good of it, I said, I don't think anyone would argue — deny that sharing the women and sharing the children would be the greatest of goods — if it were possible. But whether it's possible or not: that, I expect, is where the fight will be.

Both points, he said, could be fought over quite well.

You mean I face the arguments in coalition, I said. I thought I'd slip away from one of them — if you agreed the thing was beneficial, I'd be left with only the question of possibility.

You were spotted making your getaway, he said. Defend both.

Then I'll take my punishment, I said. But do me this one kindness: let me have a holiday of the mind, the way lazy-minded men feast on their own thoughts when they walk alone. Men like that don't wait to discover how their desires might actually come true. They skip all that, to spare themselves the labor of deliberating over what's possible and what isn't; they assume they have what they want, and go straight to arranging everything else — happily rehearsing all they'll do when it's real — making an idle soul idler still. Well, I'm going soft the same way. I want to put off the question of whether it's possible until later; for now, assuming it's possible — with your permission — I'll examine how the rulers will arrange it all when it comes about, and show that nothing could be more beneficial to the city and its guardians than these arrangements in practice. That's what we'll examine together first, and the other question after — if you allow it.

Permission granted, he said — proceed.

I think, I said, that if our rulers are worthy of the name — and their auxiliaries likewise — the auxiliaries will be willing to carry out orders, and the rulers willing to give them: obeying the laws themselves in some things, and imitating their spirit in whatever we leave to their judgment.

That's fitting, he said.

Then you, their lawgiver, I said, will do with the women just what you did with the men: select them — as near as possible of the same nature — and hand them over to them. And since their houses and mess-tables are common, and no one owns anything of that private kind, they'll be together — mingled in the gymnasiums and in the whole of their upbringing — and driven by an inborn necessity, I think, toward coupling with one another. Or doesn't 'necessity' sound right to you?

Not a geometer's necessity, he said, but a lover's — which is probably sharper than the other at compelling and persuading the run of mankind.

Much sharper, I said. But next point, Glaucon: disorder in the couplings, or in anything else, is an unholy thing in a city of the blessed, and the rulers won't allow it.

No, he said — because it isn't just.

Then clearly our next task is to make the marriages as sacred as we can. And the sacred ones will be the ones that do the most good.

Absolutely.

So how will they do the most good? Tell me this, Glaucon — I see hunting dogs at your house, and a whole yard of fine game-birds. Have you, by Zeus, paid attention to their matings and their breeding?

What about them? he said.

First: among them, though they're all fine stock, aren't there some that are, and prove to be, the best?

There are.

And do you breed from all of them alike — or take care to breed from the best?

From the best.

And from the youngest? The oldest? Or, as far as you can, from those in their prime?

From those in their prime.

And if they weren't bred that way, you'd expect your birds and your dogs to turn out far worse stock?

I would.

And horses, I said, and the other animals? Any different?

It would be strange if it were.

Good heavens, my dear friend, I said — then what surpassingly skilled rulers we're going to need, if the same holds for the human race.

It does hold, he said. But why the surpassing skill?

Because, I said, they'll be forced to use a great deal of medicine. With bodies, you know, when they don't need drugs and will submit to a regimen, we think an ordinary doctor will do. But when drugs are required, we know a bolder doctor is called for.

True. But what are you driving at?

This, I said: it looks as if our rulers will have to use falsehood and deception on a large scale — for the benefit of the ruled. And we said, remember, that all such things are useful in the way a drug is useful.

And we were right.

Well, in the marriages and the making of children, this 'right use' turns out to be no small thing.

How so?

It follows from what we've agreed, I said, that the best men must lie with the best women as often as possible, and the worst men with the worst women as rarely as possible; and the children of the best must be reared, and the children of the worst must not — if the flock is to stay at the peak of its quality. And all this has to happen with no one knowing but the rulers themselves — if the guardians' herd is also to stay as free of infighting as possible.

Completely right, he said.

Then we'll have festivals established by law, where we'll bring the brides and grooms together — with sacrifices, and hymns our poets will compose to fit the weddings. The number of the marriages we'll put in the rulers' hands, so that — reckoning in wars and disease and everything of the kind — they can hold the number of men as steady as they can, and our city, as far as possible, grows neither great nor small.

Right, he said.

Then I think we'll have to invent an ingenious kind of lottery, so that at every pairing our inferior man blames his luck, and not the rulers.

Very much so, he said.

And the young men who prove themselves in war or elsewhere — among the honors and prizes we give them, I think, should be a freer license with the women; that way, under cover of the prize, we get the most children sown from men of that sort.

Right.

And the officers appointed over these matters — men or women or both, since offices, I take it, are open to women and men alike —

Yes —

— will take the children of the good, I think, and carry them into the pen, to nurses who live apart in a certain quarter of the city. The children of the inferior — and any child of the others born defective — they'll hide away in a secret and unspoken place, as is fitting.

Yes, he said — if the guardians' breed is to stay pure.

And they'll take charge of the feeding too — bringing the mothers to the pen when their milk comes in, while contriving every contrivance so that no mother knows her own child; and supplying other women with milk if the mothers run short. They'll watch that the mothers don't nurse too long; and the sleepless nights, and all the rest of the labor, they'll hand to the nurses and attendants.

You're describing a very easy sort of child-bearing, he said, for the guardians' wives.

As is proper, I said. Now let's take up the next point of our plan. We said the children should come from parents in their prime.

True.

And do you agree with me that the prime lasts, at a fair estimate, twenty years for a woman and thirty for a man?

Which years?

A woman, I said, bears for the city from twenty to forty. A man begets for the city from the time he's past his fastest years as a runner until he's fifty-five.

In both, at least, he said, that's the prime of body and of mind.

Then if a man older or younger than that puts his hand to the making of children for the commonwealth, we'll call it an offense — unholy and unjust. He is raising up for the city a child that, if it escapes notice and gets born, will have been conceived without the sacrifices and without the prayers that priestesses and priests and the whole city will offer at every wedding festival — that from good parents, better children may be born, and from useful parents, more useful ones. This child is begotten in darkness instead, the fruit of a terrible failure of self-command.

Right, he said.

The same law holds, I said, if a man still of begetting age touches a woman of bearing age without a ruler joining their hands. We'll say he's foisting a bastard on the city — unsanctioned and unconsecrated.

Completely right, he said.

But when the women and the men pass beyond the age of breeding, I imagine we'll set them free to couple with whomever they please — except a daughter or a mother, a daughter's daughters or a mother's line before her; and the women likewise, except a son or a father and their line down or up — and all this only after we've charged them to be careful, above all, that not a single conception, if they can help it, ever sees the light; and if one forces its way through, to dispose of it on the understanding that there is no rearing for such a child.

That's all reasonably said, he replied. But fathers and daughters, and the kin you just named — how will they tell each other apart?

They won't, I said. Instead: counting from the day a man becomes a bridegroom, whatever children are born in the tenth month after — or the seventh — he will call his sons and daughters, boys and girls, and they will call him father; their children in turn he'll call grandchildren, and they'll call his generation grandfathers and grandmothers. And all the children born in the season when their mothers and fathers were making children will call one another brothers and sisters — which is exactly what will keep them from touching one another, as we just said. Though the law will let brothers and sisters live together, if the lot falls that way and the oracle at Delphi confirms it besides.

Completely right, he said.

This, then, Glaucon, is how the guardians of your city hold their women and children in common. That it's consistent with the rest of the constitution — and by far the best arrangement — is what the argument has to establish next, isn't it? Or how shall we proceed?

That way, by Zeus, he said.

Then isn't this the first step toward agreement — asking ourselves what we can name as the greatest good in the framing of a city, the thing a lawgiver aims at when he lays down the laws, and what as the greatest evil; and then examining whether what we've just gone through walks in the tracks of the good, and out of the tracks of the evil?

By all means.

Well then: do we know a greater evil for a city than whatever tears it apart and makes it many instead of one? Or a greater good than what binds it together and makes it one?

We don't.

And the sharing of pleasure and pain binds it — when, as far as may be, all the citizens rejoice and grieve alike at the same gains and the same losses?

Absolutely, he said.

While making such feelings private dissolves it — when at the same event, striking the city or its people, some are in agony and others celebrate?

Of course.

And doesn't that begin here — when the citizens no longer say the words 'mine' and 'not mine' together, about the same things? And the same with 'somebody else's'?

Exactly so.

Then the best-run city is the one in which the most people say 'mine' and 'not mine' about the same things, in the same way?

By far.

And such a city stands closest to the condition of a single human being. Think of what happens when one of our fingers is hurt. The whole partnership stretched from body to soul, ordered into one system under its ruling part, feels the hurt — the whole of it aches together with the aching part — and so we say the man has a pain in his finger. And the same account holds for any part of a man: pain when a part suffers, pleasure when it mends.

The same, he said. And to your question: the best-governed city lives closest to that condition.

Then whenever one citizen meets with anything good or bad, a city like that will above all call the experience its own — and the whole of it will rejoice with him, or hurt with him.

Necessarily, he said — in a well-governed one.

Time now, I said, to go back to our own city and check it against the argument's terms — whether it fits them best, or some other city fits them better.

Let's.

Well: in our city, as in every other, there are rulers and there are the people?

There are.

And they all call one another citizens?

Of course.

But besides 'citizens,' what do people in other cities call their rulers?

In most, masters; in the democracies, exactly this name — rulers.

And the people in ours? Besides citizens, what will they call the rulers?

Saviors, he said, and helpers.

And the rulers — what will they call the people?

Their paymasters and providers.

And in other cities, what do rulers call the people?

Slaves.

And rulers there — what do they call each other?

Fellow rulers.

And ours?

Fellow guardians.

Now, in other cities: can you name a ruler who addresses one colleague as family and another as an outsider?

Yes — plenty do.

And the family one he thinks of and speaks of as his own; the outsider as not his own?

Just so.

And your guardians? Could any of them think of a fellow guardian as an outsider — or address him as one?

Never, he said. In everyone he meets he'll see a brother or a sister, a father or a mother, a son or a daughter — or the children or the parents of these.

Beautifully said, I replied. But tell me this too: will your laws give them only the names of kinship, or every act that goes with the names? Toward the fathers, everything custom commands toward fathers — the respect, the care, the obedience owed to parents — on pain of standing badly with gods and with men, since anything else would be neither holy nor just? Will these be the sayings that ring in the children's ears from the whole city, from earliest childhood, about the men pointed out as their fathers, and about the rest of their kin — or something else?

These, he said. It would be laughable if kinship names were only mouthed, with none of the acts behind them.

Then in our city more than any other, when a single citizen fares well or badly, they'll all say the words together — the concord we named just now: 'mine is doing well,' or 'mine is doing badly.'

Completely true, he said.

And didn't we say that shared pleasures and pains follow along with this conviction, and with this way of speaking?

We did — and rightly.

Then our citizens, more than any others, will hold one and the same thing in common, the thing they call 'mine' — and holding it so, they'll share, more than any others, one pain and one pleasure?

Much more.

And the cause of it — over and above the rest of the constitution — is the guardians' having women and children in common?

The chief cause by far, he said.

But we agreed this sharing is the greatest good for a city — comparing a well-run city to a body, and the way it stands toward the pain and pleasure of its parts.

And rightly agreed, he said.

Then the cause of the greatest good to our city has shown itself: the community of children and women among its defenders.

Completely, he said.

And notice — this agrees with what we settled before. We said, didn't we, that they must have no private houses, no land, no possessions at all; that they'd take their keep from the other citizens as wages for their guarding, and spend it all in common — if they were going to be guardians in fact.

Right, he said.

Then isn't it as I say: the earlier rules and these new ones together make them even more truly guardians — keeping them from tearing the city to pieces by calling 'mine' not the same thing but each a different thing, one man hauling into his own private house whatever he can get apart from the others, another into his different house, to a different wife and different children, breeding private pleasures and private griefs over private things? Instead, sharing one conviction about what is their own, and straining toward the same mark, they'll all feel — as far as human beings can — one pain and one pleasure together.

Exactly so, he said.

And what about lawsuits and prosecutions? Won't they all but vanish among them, since they own nothing but their bodies and hold everything else in common? Every quarrel people fall into over money or children or kin — our guardians will be clean of all of it.

They'll be well rid of it.

Nor could suits for violence or assault fairly arise among them. Between men of an age, we'll say, defending yourself is fine and just — imposing on them the necessity of keeping their bodies fit.

Right, he said.

And there's another good in the law: a man whose temper flares and is satisfied on the spot, one on one, is less likely to push the quarrel on to something bigger.

Certainly.

The older, meanwhile, will be charged with ruling and disciplining all the younger.

Clearly.

And a younger man will never lay hands on an elder or strike him — unless the rulers command it — nor, I think, dishonor him any other way. Two guards stand in his way, and they're enough: shame and fear. Shame bars him from touching anyone who might be his parent; fear tells him the others will come to the victim's aid — some as his sons, some as his brothers, some as his fathers.

That's how it works out, he said.

Then under these laws, every way you look at it, the men will keep the peace with one another?

Deep peace.

And with no quarrel among the guardians themselves, there's no fear that the rest of the city will ever split against them, or against itself.

No fear at all.

As for the pettiest of the evils they'd be rid of, I hesitate even to mention them — they're so unseemly. The poor flattering the rich. All the strain and ache of raising children and scraping up the money a household devours: borrowing here, defaulting there, getting it any way they can and handing it over to their wives and slaves to manage. Everything men suffer on that front, my friend — it's obvious, and ignoble, and not worth the words.

Obvious to a blind man, he said.

Rid of all that, they'll live a life more blessed than the life of Olympic victors.

How so?

The Olympic victor is counted happy for a small part of what our guardians have. Their victory is finer, and their keep at the public expense more complete. The victory they win is the preservation of the entire city; the crown they wear — they and their children too — is food and everything else a life requires. They take honors from their own city while they live, and when they die, a worthy burial.

Fine prizes, he said.

Do you remember, I said, that earlier in the argument somebody's objection — never mind whose — hit us: that we weren't making our guardians happy, since they could have everything the citizens have and yet had nothing? And we answered, didn't we, that we'd look into that later if occasion arose, but for now we were making our guardians guardians and the city as happy as we could — we weren't shaping one class in it for happiness at the expense of the whole.

I remember, he said.

Well then — now that the life of our defenders shows itself far finer and better than any Olympic victor's, is there any comparing it with the life of the shoemakers, or the other craftsmen, or the farmers?

I'd say not, he said.

Still, it's fair to repeat here what I said back then: if a guardian ever tries to be happy in a way that unmakes him as a guardian — if this measured, steady life, the best there is by our account, doesn't satisfy him, and some senseless, boyish notion of happiness gets into his head and drives him to use his strength to grab everything in the city for himself — he'll learn that Hesiod was truly wise when he said the half is somehow more than the whole.

If he asks my advice, he said, he'll stay with this life.

Then you accept it, I said — this partnership of the women with the men that we've gone through: education, children, and the guarding of the other citizens; that whether they stay in the city or go out to war, they guard together and hunt together like hounds, and share everything in every way they can; and that in doing all this they'll be doing what's best, and nothing contrary to the nature of female and male, nothing against the way the two are naturally made to partner with each other.

I accept it, he said.

Then what remains, I said, is to settle whether it's actually possible — among human beings as among the other animals — for this partnership to come about; and if so, how.

You beat me to it, he said — I was about to say exactly that.

As for their conduct of war, I said, I think the way of it is plain to see.

How?

They'll campaign together, men and women alike — and take the children with them to war, the sturdy ones, so that like every other craftsman's children they can watch the work they'll do themselves when they're grown. And beyond watching, they'll fetch and serve in everything the war requires, and attend on their fathers and their mothers. Haven't you seen how it goes in the trades — how the potters' children spend a long time watching and helping before they ever put hand to a pot?

I have.

And should potters train their children more carefully than the guardians train theirs — with real experience and a full view of what their work will be?

That would be laughable, he said.

Besides, every living thing fights harder with its young looking on.

So it does. But there's no small risk, Socrates. If they're beaten — and war is full of defeats — they'll lose their children's lives along with their own, and the rest of the city will never recover.

True, I said. But tell me first: do you think our aim should be to arrange that they never risk anything at all?

Not at all.

Well — if they're ever to take risks, shouldn't it be the kind where success makes them better?

Clearly.

And you think it makes little difference — not worth a risk — whether boys who are to be men of war see war as boys, or don't?

No — it matters, in the way you mean.

Then start from this: the children are to be spectators of war, and we're to contrive their safety — and then all's well, isn't it?

Yes.

Now first, I said, their fathers won't be ignorant of war; they'll know, as far as human beings can, which campaigns are dangerous and which are not.

Likely so, he said.

So they'll bring the children to the safe ones, and think hard before bringing them to the others.

Right.

And they'll set commanders over them — not the most expendable men, but those fit by experience and age to lead children and look after them.

As is proper.

Still, we'll say — the unexpected has happened to many men, many times.

Very much so.

Then against such chances, my friend, we should wing the children from the first — so that when it comes to it, they can fly away and escape.

What do you mean? he said.

We must mount them on horses, I said, as young as possible; and once they're taught to ride, bring them to the watching on horseback — not on spirited or warlike animals, but on the fastest and most obedient we have. That way they'll get the best look at their own future work, and, if it's needed, the surest escape — following their older leaders to safety.

I think you're right, he said.

Now to the war itself, I said. How should your soldiers deal with one another, and with the enemy? Tell me if I see it rightly. Any of them who deserts his post, or throws away his weapons, or does anything else of that cowardly kind — shouldn't he be demoted to craftsman or farmer?

By all means.

And a man taken alive by the enemy — shouldn't we make a present of him to his captors, to do with their catch whatever they please?

Exactly.

But the man who wins distinction — who proves his worth — don't you think that first, on campaign itself, he should be crowned by his comrades in arms, by each of the young men and boys in turn? Or no?

I do.

And greeted with the right hand?

That too.

But this next one, I said, I don't think you'll go along with.

What?

That he kiss, and be kissed by, each of them.

That above all, he said — and I'll add my own clause to the law: for as long as that campaign lasts, no one he wants to kiss may refuse him; so that any soldier who happens to be in love, with a boy or with a girl, will be all the hungrier to carry off the prize for valor.

Good, I said. And we've already said that the brave man will have more marriages ready for him than the others, and be chosen for them oftener than the others, so that the most children possible come from such a man.

We did say that.

And by Homer's rule too, it's just to honor the brave among the young. Homer says that when Ajax distinguished himself in the war, he 'was honored with the long cut off the chine' — the proper honor for a brave man in his prime, being tribute and added strength in one.

Completely right, he said.

Then in this at least we'll follow Homer, I said. At the sacrifices and every occasion like them, we'll honor the brave — in the measure that they've proved brave, women as well as men — with hymns, and with the privileges we just named, and beyond these with

'the seat of honor, the best of the meat, and the cup kept full,'

so that in honoring our brave men and women, we're training them at the same time.

Excellent, he said.

Good. And of those who die on campaign — whoever falls with glory, won't we say, first, that he belongs to the golden race?

Above all.

And won't we believe Hesiod, that when men of that race die,

'they become pure spirits dwelling upon the earth, noble beings, protectors against evil, guardians of mortal men'?

We'll believe him.

Then we'll ask the god how the dead of that divine and golden kind ought to be buried, and with what distinctions — and bury them exactly as he directs?

Of course.

And for all time after, we'll tend their graves and kneel at them as at the tombs of divine spirits. And we'll keep the same customs for any who die of old age, or in any other way, when their lives are judged extraordinarily good.

That's only just, he said.

Next: how will our soldiers deal with their enemies?

Meaning what?

First, enslavement. Does it seem just for Greeks to enslave Greek cities? Or should they refuse — as far as it's in their power — and hold the other cities to the same custom: to spare the Greek kind, and be on guard against the whole race falling enslaved to the barbarians?

Sparing them, he said, is better — wholly and entirely.

Then they'll own no Greek slave themselves, and counsel the other Greeks the same?

By all means, he said. That way they'd turn their force outward, against the barbarians, and keep their hands off one another.

And stripping the dead, I said — is it right, after a victory, to strip the fallen of anything besides their weapons? Doesn't it give cowards an excuse not to close with the enemy still fighting — as if crouching over a corpse were the performance of a duty? Many an army has been lost to that kind of plunder.

Many indeed.

And doesn't it seem grubby and money-grasping — the mark of a small and womanish mind — to strip a corpse: to treat the dead body as your enemy when the real foe has flown off and left behind the tools he fought with? Is it any different from the dog that bites the stone that hit it and never touches the man who threw it?

Not a bit different, he said.

So — no robbing of corpses; and no preventing the enemy from taking up their dead?

None, by Zeus, he said.

Nor will we carry weapons to the temples as offerings — least of all the weapons of Greeks — not if we care at all for the goodwill of the other Greeks. Sooner, we'll fear pollution in bringing to a shrine spoils stripped from our own kind — unless, of course, the god says otherwise.

Completely right, he said.

And the ravaging of Greek land, the burning of Greek houses — what will your soldiers do to their enemies on that score?

I'd enjoy hearing your opinion, he said.

In mine, I said, they do neither. They take the year's harvest — nothing more. Do you want to know why?

Very much.

It looks to me like this: we have these two names, war and civil strife — two names, and two things, sitting on two different kinds of quarrel. The two I mean are the kindred and one's own, on one side; the foreign and alien on the other. Hatred inside the kin is called civil strife; hatred toward the alien is called war.

Nothing off the mark in that, he said.

Then see if this is on the mark as well. I say the Greek race is its own kin — one household — and to the barbarian world it is foreign and alien.

Well said, he replied.

Then when Greeks fight barbarians and barbarians fight Greeks, we'll say they're at war — enemies by nature — and that hatred is to be called war. But when Greeks fight Greeks, we'll say they're friends by nature; that Greece, in such a case, is sick and torn against itself; and that hatred is to be called civil strife.

I'm content, he said, to see it that way.

Then look at what we now agree to call civil strife, I said — wherever a city splits and each side wastes the other's fields and burns the other's houses, how abominable the strife seems, and how little either side loves its city. Otherwise they would never have dared shear their own nurse and mother to the bone. The measured thing is for the winners to take the losers' harvest — and to remember that they'll be reconciled someday, that they won't be at war forever.

That thought belongs to far gentler men, he said, than the other.

Well then, I said — this city you're founding: won't it be Greek?

It has to be, he said.

So its people will be good, and gentle?

Very much so.

And lovers of Greece? They'll count Greece their own, and share in the shrines all Greeks share?

Very much that too.

Then any quarrel with Greeks — their own kind — they'll treat as civil strife, and won't even give it the name of war?

They won't.

So they'll quarrel like people who expect to be reconciled?

Certainly.

They'll correct their opponents with goodwill — chastening them, not punishing them with slavery and destruction; correctors, not enemies.

Just so, he said.

Then, being Greeks, they won't ravage Greece or burn its houses; and they won't accept that everyone in a city is their enemy — men, women, children alike — but only the few who caused the quarrel, always a minority. On all these counts they'll refuse to waste the land or raze the houses of a people who are mostly their friends; and they'll press the conflict only until the guilty are forced to pay by the innocent, who are the ones suffering.

I agree, he said — that's how our citizens should deal with their opponents; and with the barbarians, the way Greeks now deal with each other.

Then we lay down this law too for the guardians: no ravaging of land and no burning of houses.

Laid down, he said. It's good — and so is everything we enacted before it.

But Socrates — I think if we let you go on like this, you'll never remember the question you shoved aside to say all of it: whether this constitution can come into being, and how it possibly could. Because if it existed, everything would be splendid for the city that had it. I'll even add advantages you've left out: fighting their enemies they'd be the best of soldiers, because they'd never desert each other — these men who know one another as brothers and fathers and sons and call each other by those names. And if the women campaigned alongside, whether in the line itself or arrayed behind as a terror to the enemy and a reserve for whenever it's needed — I know all this would make them unbeatable outright. And at home I see all the goods they'd have that you haven't mentioned. Take it that I grant it all — these advantages and ten thousand others, if this constitution came to be — and say no more about the thing itself. Let's just try now to convince ourselves of this one point: that it's possible, and how — and let the rest go.

That was sudden, I said — this raid you've launched on my argument, with no mercy for a man dragging his feet. Maybe you don't understand: I barely got out from under the first two waves, and now you're bringing on the third — the monster of the set, the biggest and hardest of the three. When you've seen it and heard it, you'll forgive me entirely: no wonder I hesitated, no wonder I was afraid to state so paradoxical a proposal, let alone undertake to examine it.

The more of this we hear, he said, the less any of us will release you from saying how this constitution can come to be. Speak — and no more stalling.

Then first let's remember, I said, that we arrived here searching for what justice is, and what injustice is.

We did, he said. What of it?

Nothing — only this: if we find what justice is, will we insist that the just man differ from it in nothing — match justice itself exactly, in every respect? Or will we be satisfied if he comes as close to it as can be, and has more of it in him than anyone else?

That will satisfy us.

Then it was for the sake of a model, I said, that we were searching for what justice itself is, and for the perfectly just man — whether he could exist, and what he'd be like if he did — and likewise injustice and the perfectly unjust man. We wanted to look at them and see how they stood with respect to happiness and its opposite, so that we'd be forced to agree about ourselves: whoever comes nearest to them will have the portion nearest theirs. We were not out to prove that these things could exist.

That's true, he said.

Do you think a painter is any less good, then, who paints a model of what the most beautiful human being would be — rendering everything in the picture as it should be — but can't prove that such a man could exist?

By Zeus, no, he said.

Well — weren't we, by our own account, making a model in words of a good city?

We were.

Then is our account any less good, do you think, because we can't prove that a city can be governed as we described?

Surely not, he said.

Then that's the truth of it, I said. But if, to please you, I must push on and show how, exactly, and on what terms it comes nearest to being possible — then for that demonstration, grant me the same admissions over again.

Which?

Can anything be carried out exactly as it's spoken? Or is it the nature of things that doing grips truth less firmly than talking does — whatever some people think? Do you agree, or not?

I agree.

Then don't force me to exhibit the thing realized in deed, precisely as we spelled it out in words. If we can find how a city might be run closest to what we've said, then say that we've found what you demanded — that it's possible. Won't you be content with that? I know I would.

So would I, he said.

Then next, it seems, we should try to find and show what's done badly in cities today — the thing that keeps them from being run our way — and the smallest change that would carry a city over to our kind of constitution. One change, for preference; failing that, two; failing that, the fewest in number and the mildest in force.

By all means, he said.

Well, there is one change, I said, that I think would carry it over. Not a small change, and not an easy one — but possible.

What is it? he said.

Here I stand, I said, at the thing we likened to the greatest wave. But it shall be said, even if the wave breaks over me — a real breaker of laughter and disgrace — and washes me away. Mark what I'm about to say.

Say it.

Unless philosophers become kings in our cities, I said, or the men now called kings and rulers become philosophers — genuinely, thoroughly — so that political power and philosophy meet in the same hands, while the many natures that now chase one without the other are barred from both by force, there will be no rest from evils for our cities, my dear Glaucon, nor, I think, for the human race. And until then, this constitution we've been building in words will never grow into what it could be, never see the light of the sun. This is what made me so slow to speak: I could see how far it runs against belief. It's hard to accept that there is no other road to happiness — for a city, or for a single life.

Socrates, he said — you've hurled out a sentence, an argument, after which you can expect a great many men — and no negligible men either — to fling off their coats, so to speak, right where they stand, snatch up whatever weapon is nearest, and come at you at a dead run, ready to do wonders. If you don't build your defense in argument and make your escape, you'll pay the penalty — jeered at in earnest.

And whose doing is that, I said, if not yours?

And a fine thing I did, he said. But I won't betray you: I'll defend you with what I have. What I have is goodwill and encouragement — and maybe I'd answer your questions more carefully than another man would. So with a helper like that, try to show the doubters that it is as you say.

I must try, I said, since you offer so great an alliance. If we're going to slip past the men you describe, it seems to me essential to define for them which philosophers we mean when we dare to say that philosophers must rule. Once that's clear, a defense is possible: we can show that some people are fitted by nature to take hold of philosophy and to lead in a city, and the rest are made to leave philosophy alone and follow the leader.

This would be the moment to define them, he said.

Come along, then — let's see if we can somehow lay it out adequately.

Lead on.

Will you need reminding, I said, or do you remember: when we say a man loves something — if the word is used rightly — he can't love part of it and not the rest. He has to cherish the whole of it.

You'll have to remind me, it seems, he said. I don't quite follow.

That answer, Glaucon, would suit somebody else, I said. A man of your amorous history has no business forgetting that every boy in his bloom finds some way to sting and stir a lover of boys — a man of your sort — and every one of them seems worth his devotion and attention. Isn't that how you people deal with the beautiful? One boy has a snub nose: you'll call it charming and praise it. Another's hook of a nose is regal, you say. The one in between has perfect proportions. The dark ones look manly; the pale ones are children of the gods. And 'honey-pale' — who do you think coined that phrase but a lover talking baby-talk, happily excusing a sallow cheek as long as youth is on it? In one word: there's no excuse you won't invent, no sound you won't make, to avoid losing a single boy in the flower of his spring.

If you insist on using me to make your point about lovers, he said — fine; for the argument's sake, I'll go along.

And the wine-lovers? Don't you see them doing the same — welcoming every wine, on any pretext?

I do.

And the lovers of honor — you've watched them, I think. If they can't command an army, they'll command a battalion; if they can't be honored by the great and important, they'll settle for being honored by the small and negligible. Honor, of one size or another, they must have.

Exactly so.

Then tell me yes or no: when we say a man has an appetite for something, do we mean he has an appetite for the whole kind — or for one part of it and not another?

The whole, he said.

So the philosopher too — the lover of wisdom — we'll say has an appetite for wisdom: not for this piece and not that, but for the whole of it?

True.

Then someone who's picky about his studies — especially while he's young and can't yet tell what's useful from what isn't — we won't call a lover of learning, or a philosopher. As we don't call the picky eater hungry: he has no appetite for food. He's a bad eater, not a good one.

And we'll be right.

But the one who's game to taste every kind of learning, who goes to his studying gladly and can't get his fill — him we'll rightly call a philosopher, won't we?

Then you'll have a strange crowd on your hands, said Glaucon. All the lovers of spectacles are what they are, I suppose, because they delight in learning things. And the lovers of listening would be the oddest people to count among philosophers — men who'd never willingly come near a discussion or any such pastime, yet who run around to every chorus at the festivals of Dionysus as if their ears were under contract, never missing one, in town or village. Are we to call all these people philosophers — them, and the students of every other petty craft?

Not at all, I said — though they do have a look of philosophers about them.

And the true ones? he said. Who do you mean by those?

The lovers of the sight of truth, I said.

Right enough, he said — but what does it mean?

It wouldn't be easy to explain to just anyone, I said. But you, I think, will grant me the starting point.

Which is?

That since beautiful is the opposite of ugly, they are two things.

Of course.

And being two, each of them is one?

That too.

And the same account holds of just and unjust, good and bad, and all the forms: each of them is, in itself, one thing; but because they show up everywhere in company with actions and bodies and one another, each appears to be many things.

That's right, he said.

Well then, this is my dividing line, I said. On one side I put the lovers of spectacles you just mentioned — the lovers of crafts, the practical men. On the other, the people our argument is about — the only ones rightly called philosophers.

How do you mean? he said.

The lovers of sounds and sights, I said, delight in beautiful voices and colors and shapes and everything crafted from them. But the beautiful itself — their thought can't see its nature, can't delight in it.

That is how it is, he said.

And the ones able to approach the beautiful itself and see it as it is, by itself — won't they be rare?

Very rare.

Then a man who believes in beautiful things but not in beauty itself — who couldn't follow if someone tried to lead him to the knowledge of it — do you think he's living awake, or dreaming? Look at it this way. What is dreaming, if not this: that a man, asleep or awake, takes the likeness of a thing not for a likeness, but for the thing itself?

I'd certainly call that man a dreamer, he said.

And the opposite man — the one who holds that there is a beautiful itself, and can look upon both it and the things that share in it, and doesn't mistake the sharers for it or it for the sharers — is he awake, or dreaming?

Wide awake, he said.

Then this man's state of mind, since he knows, we'd rightly call knowledge — and the other's opinion, since he opines?

Certainly.

And what if the man we say opines gets rough with us and disputes the claim? Is there any way to soothe him — to talk him around gently, while keeping quiet the fact that he isn't in his right mind?

We'd better find one, he said.

Come, then — think what we'll say to him. Or shall we question him like this? We'll tell him first that nobody begrudges him anything he knows — we'd be delighted to see that he knows something. Then we'll say: tell us this, though. Does the man who knows, know something — or nothing? You answer for him.

I'll answer that he knows something.

Something that is, or something that is not?

That is. How could a thing that is not be known?

Then are we sure of this — from however many angles we look at it: what fully is, is fully knowable; what in no way is, is in every way unknowable?

Completely sure.

Good. Now if something is so placed that it both is and is not — won't it lie between what purely is and what wholly is not?

Between them.

Then since knowledge is set over what is, and ignorance, of necessity, over what is not — over this in-between we'd have to look for something between ignorance and knowledge, if there is any such thing?

Certainly.

Well — do we say opinion is something?

Of course.

A power the same as knowledge, or a different one?

A different one.

So opinion is stationed over one thing and knowledge over another — each according to its own power.

Yes.

And knowledge is naturally set over what is, to know it as it is? — though hold on: first I think we need to draw a distinction.

What distinction?

Powers, I said. We'll say powers are a class of things that are — the things by which we can do whatever we can do; we, and everything else that can do anything. Sight and hearing, I mean, belong among the powers — if you follow the kind I want to describe.

I follow, he said.

Then hear how they strike me. In a power I see no color, no shape, none of the features so many other things have — the ones I can look at to tell one thing from another for myself. In a power I look at one thing only: what it is set over, and what it accomplishes. That's how I came to call each of them a power. The one set over the same thing, accomplishing the same thing, I call the same power; the one set over something different, accomplishing something different, I call a different one. What about you — how do you do it?

The same way, he said.

Then back to you, my excellent friend, I said. Knowledge — do you say it's a power, or what class do you put it in?

A power, he said — the strongest of them all.

And opinion — a power, or shall we file it under some other kind?

A power, he said. Opining is exactly what opinion enables us to do.

But a little while ago you agreed that knowledge and opinion are not the same.

Of course, he said. How could anyone with any sense count the infallible and the fallible as one thing?

Well said, I answered — plainly we're agreed that opinion is one thing and knowledge another.

Another thing entirely.

So each of them, being a different power, is naturally set over a different object?

Necessarily.

Knowledge over what is — to know what is, as it is?

Yes.

And opinion, we say, opines?

Yes.

The same object knowledge knows? So the knowable and the opinable will be the same thing? Or is that impossible?

Impossible, he said — by what we've agreed. If different powers are naturally set over different things, and both opinion and knowledge are powers — each different from the other, as we said — then it follows: the knowable and the opinable can't be the same.

Then if what is, is the knowable, the opinable has to be something other than what is?

Something other.

Then does opinion opine what is not? Or is even that impossible — to opine what is not? Think about it: doesn't the man who opines bring his opinion to bear on something? Or is it possible to opine — but opine nothing?

It's impossible.

No — the man who opines, opines some one thing?

Yes.

But what is not isn't 'some one thing.' Speaking strictly, it's nothing at all.

Quite.

And we assigned ignorance, of necessity, to what is not — and to what is, knowledge?

And rightly, he said.

Then opinion opines neither what is, nor what is not?

Neither.

So opinion can be neither ignorance nor knowledge?

So it seems.

Then does it lie somewhere beyond them — outdoing knowledge in clarity, or ignorance in darkness?

It does neither.

Then opinion looks to you, I said, like something darker than knowledge and brighter than ignorance?

Very much so, he said.

Lying inside the two boundaries?

Yes.

So opinion would be between the two?

Exactly that.

Now, didn't we say earlier that if something turned up which at once is and is not, it would lie between what purely is and what altogether is not — and that neither knowledge nor ignorance would be set over it, but whatever turned up, in its turn, between ignorance and knowledge?

Right.

And what has turned up between them is the thing we call opinion?

It has.

Then what remains to be found, it seems, is the thing that shares in both — in being and in not-being — and can't rightly be called either one, pure and simple. If it comes to light, we can justly call it the opinable: the extremes matched to the extremes, the in-between to the in-between. Isn't that so?

It is.

With all that laid down, I'll say: let him step up and answer me — that good fellow who denies there is any beautiful itself, any form of beauty itself that stays forever the same in all respects, but who does believe in the many beautiful things — the spectacle-lover, who won't stand it if anyone says the beautiful is one, and the just is one, and so on. My friend, we'll say to him: of all these many beautiful things, is there one that won't ever show itself ugly? Of the just things, one that won't show itself unjust? Of the holy, one that won't show unholy?

No, he said — it's inevitable that they show up beautiful from one angle and ugly from another; and so with everything else you're asking about.

And the many doubles? Do they show up as halves any less than as doubles?

No less.

And bigs and smalls, and lights and heavies — will those names hold to them any more firmly than the opposite names?

No, he said — each of them hangs on to both, always.

Then is each of the many what somebody says it is — any more than it is not what he says it is?

They're like the double-edged jokes at a banquet, he said, or the children's riddle about the eunuch and his throw at the bat — the one that riddles what he hit it with, and what the bat was sitting on. These many things equivocate the same way, and you can't fix any of them firmly in your mind as being, or as not being, or as both, or as neither.

Then can you do anything better with them, I said, than set them between being and not-being? Surely they can't turn up darker than what is not — by not-being more than it — or brighter than what is, by being more.

Completely true, he said.

So we seem to have found it: the many conventional beliefs of the many, about the beautiful and the rest, are tumbling about somewhere between what is not and what purely is.

We have.

And we agreed in advance that whatever turned up like this would have to be called the opinable, not the knowable — the wanderer in between, caught by the power in between.

We did.

Then as for those who gaze on the many beautiful things and don't see the beautiful itself — and can't follow another man who'd lead them to it — who see the many just things and not the just itself, and so on all the way through: we'll say they opine everything, but know nothing of what they opine.

That's what we have to say.

And what about those who gaze on each thing itself — the things that stay forever the same in every respect? Won't we say they know, and don't merely opine?

That follows too, necessarily.

And won't we say these men embrace and love the things knowledge is set over, and the others the things opinion is set over? We do remember, don't we, saying of the latter that they love and gaze at beautiful sounds and colors and the rest, but the beautiful itself — they won't even allow that it's anything at all?

We remember.

Then we won't be off-key, will we, calling these people lovers of opinion rather than lovers of wisdom? Will they be very angry with us for saying it?

Not if they listen to me, he said. Nobody has a right to be angry at the truth.

But those who embrace, in each case, the thing itself that is — those we must call lovers of wisdom, not lovers of opinion?

Absolutely.
