The Republic, Book IX

The Tyrant's Soul

Book IX9 of 10

Last comes the tyrannical man himself, I said — and we still have to ask how he emerges out of the democratic man, and, once he exists, what kind of man he is, and what kind of life he leads: miserable or happy.

Yes, he said — he is the one still left.

But you know, I said, there is something I feel we have left out.

What?

The desires. I don't think we have adequately settled what they are and how many kinds there are — and as long as that is missing, the whole inquiry will stay blurred.

Well, he said, it's not too late to fix it.

Quite right, I said. And here is what I want to see about them. Among the unnecessary pleasures and desires, some strike me as outright lawless. They are probably born in every one of us; but in some people, disciplined by the laws and by the better desires working alongside reason, they are either rooted out entirely or left few and weak — while in others they stay stronger and more numerous.

Which desires do you mean?

The ones that wake while we sleep, I said — when the rest of the soul is dozing, the reasoning, gentle, ruling part of it, and the beast in us, wild and gorged with food or drink, shakes off sleep, springs up, and goes looking for a way to satisfy its own nature. In that state, you know, it will dare anything, loosed from every shame and every scruple. It does not shrink, in fantasy, from trying to sleep with a mother — or with anyone else, man, god, or beast; it is ready for any bloodshed, and there is no food it will not eat. In a word, there is no folly and no outrage it will not commit.

Perfectly true, he said.

But take a man who keeps himself healthy and in command of himself, and who goes to sleep only after he has wakened his reasoning part and feasted it on good arguments and good questions, coming to a quiet agreement with himself; who has given the appetitive part neither too little nor too much, just enough for it to sleep without disturbing the best part with its pleasure or its pain, leaving that part alone, pure, by itself, free to reach out and grasp at something it does not yet know, in the past, the present, or the future; a man who has soothed the spirited part in the same way and does not fall asleep with his temper up against anyone — when he has quieted those two parts and stirred the third, where thinking lives, and only then takes his rest, you know that in that state he touches truth most nearly, and the visions of his dreams are least likely to be lawless.

I agree entirely.

We have wandered rather far to say all this. But the point I want is this: some terrible, wild, lawless class of desires is in every one of us — even in those of us who look thoroughly measured — and sleep is where it shows itself. Consider whether I'm talking sense, and whether you agree.

I do agree.

Now recall the democratic man and how we said he was formed. He was raised from childhood by a thrifty father who honored only the money-making desires and had no use for the unnecessary ones, the ones that exist for amusement and show. Yes?

True.

Then he fell in with more polished company, men full of the desires we have just been describing, and out of loathing for his father's stinginess he ran to their kind of excess and every sort of indulgence. But because his nature was better than his corrupters', he was pulled in both directions and settled in the middle, tasting each life in what he told himself was moderation — a life neither slavish nor lawless, as he saw it. And so the oligarchic man became democratic.

Yes — that was our view of him, and it still is.

Now imagine, I said, that this man has grown older in his turn, and has a young son brought up in his father's habits.

I'm imagining him.

Then imagine the same thing happening to the son that happened to the father. He is pulled toward total lawlessness — which the men doing the pulling call total freedom. His father and the rest of the household throw their weight behind the middle desires; the others push from the opposite side. And when these terrible sorcerers and tyrant-makers feel their grip on the young man slipping, they contrive to plant one master passion in him, a standing champion for the idle desires that drain whatever he has — a great winged drone. What else could you call the passion of such men?

Nothing else, he said. That is exactly what it is.

And when the rest of the desires come buzzing around it, steeped in incense and perfume, garlands and wine, all the loose pleasures of that kind of company, and feed the drone and fatten it to its full size, then they plant in it the sting of longing. From that moment this champion of the soul takes Madness for its bodyguard and is stung into frenzy; and if it finds in the man any opinions or desires still counted decent, anything still capable of shame, it kills them and throws them out, until it has purged him of self-command and filled the emptiness with madness brought in from outside.

That, he said, is a complete picture of how the tyrannical man is made.

And is this why love has been called a tyrant since the old days?

It may well be.

And a drunk man, my friend, I said — doesn't he too have something of the tyrant's mind in him?

He does.

And the man who is truly out of his mind, deranged, undertakes to rule not only men but gods, and fully expects to manage it?

He certainly does.

Then the strictly tyrannical man, my friend, I said, appears when nature, or habit, or both together, make a man drunken, lustful, and mad. Isn't that so?

Exactly.

So that is how this man is made, and that is what he is. Now — how does he live?

As the joke goes, he said: you tell me.

I will, I said. From then on, I think, it is feasts and revels and parties and mistresses and everything of that sort, once Love the tyrant has moved in and steers everything in his soul.

Inevitably.

And day and night, desires shoot up beside it — many of them, and fierce, and every one of them demanding.

They do indeed.

So whatever income he has is quickly spent.

Of course.

After that comes borrowing, and carving into the estate itself.

Naturally.

And when everything is gone, won't the desires, packed close and crying in him like a nest of young birds, begin screaming for food? And won't he — goaded on by them, and above all by Love, whose bodyguard the rest of them are — go half-mad, hunting for anyone who has anything he can take, by fraud or by force?

Absolutely.

Money has to come from every side, or his life turns to agony and torment.

It must.

And just as the new pleasures inside him beat out the old ones and stole what was theirs, so the man himself, young as he is, will claim more than his father and mother — and once his own share is spent, he will start taking a slice of theirs.

No doubt about it.

And if they won't hand it over, first he'll try theft and fraud on his own parents?

Certainly.

And when that fails, he'll move on to seizing it by force?

Probably so.

And if the old man and the old woman hold their ground and fight back — what then, my friend? Will he hesitate, and hold back from anything a tyrant would do?

I wouldn't feel very easy, he said, about that man's parents.

But by Zeus, Adeimantus — for the sake of a mistress he took up last week, someone he has no necessary tie to at all, do you believe he would strike his mother, his oldest friend, whose tie to him nature itself made necessary? Or, for the sake of some blooming boy, the newest and least necessary of loves, raise his hand against his worn old father, the first and most necessary friend he has — and put his parents under such creatures' rule if he brought them into the same house?

Yes, by Zeus, he said, I believe he would.

What a blessing, then, I said, to give birth to a tyrannical son.

A real blessing, he replied.

And when his father's and mother's money runs out, and the swarm of pleasures packed inside him keeps clamoring, he starts by breaking into someone's house, or lifting the cloak off a man walking home late; after that he'll strip a temple clean. And all the while the old opinions he held since childhood about what is honorable and what is shameful — the ones we count as just — are overthrown by opinions newly freed from slavery, Love's bodyguard, ruling at its side. In his democratic days, while he still lived under the laws and under his father, those desires broke loose only in sleep, in dreams. But now, under Love's tyranny, he has become, awake and permanently, the man he used to be only rarely and in dream: he will stop at no murder, no forbidden food, no unspeakable act. The passion that lives in him as a tyrant, in total lawlessness and anarchy, sole ruler now, drives the man it possesses, as a tyrant drives a city, into any recklessness that will feed it and the noisy mob around it — the desires that bad company brought in from outside, and the ones his own bad nature loosed and set free from within. Isn't that this man's life?

It is, he said.

And if there are only a few men like this in a city, I said, and the mass of the people are sound, they leave and hire on as bodyguards to some foreign tyrant, or as paid soldiers in whatever war is running. If there is no war and everything is quiet, they stay home and commit a stream of small evils right in the city.

Such as?

They steal; they break into houses; they cut purses and strip travelers of their clothes; they rob temples; they sell people into slavery. Sometimes, if they can talk, they turn informer — they bear false witness, and they take bribes.

Small evils, he said — as long as there are only a few of them.

Small is small next to great, I said; and all of it together, set against the tyrant, doesn't come within a mile — as the saying goes — of the misery and rot he brings a city. Because when a city fills up with men of this kind, and with the others who follow them, and they realize how many they are, that is when they breed the tyrant — helped along by the foolishness of the people — and the man they breed is whichever of them carries the greatest and strongest tyrant in his own soul.

Naturally, he said — that man would be the most tyrannical already.

If the city yields willingly, well and good. But if it resists, then — just as he once punished his own mother and father — he will punish his fatherland now, if he can: he'll bring in his new companions, and hold his once-dear motherland, as the Cretans call it, his fatherland, in slavery to them and keep it so. And that is where this man's desires finally arrive.

That is it exactly.

Now, in private life, I said, before they ever hold power, isn't this what such men are like? Their company is flatterers, men ready to serve them in anything; or, if they need something from someone, they do the groveling themselves — no pose of devotion is beneath them — and once they've got what they wanted, strangers again.

Very much so.

So through the whole of their lives they are never friends with anyone: always someone's master, or someone's slave. Real freedom and real friendship the tyrannical nature never tastes.

Never.

Wouldn't we be right, then, to call such men faithless?

Of course.

And unjust — as unjust as men come, if we were right earlier about what justice is.

And we were right, he said.

Then let us sum up the worst man in a sentence: he is, awake, what we described in the dream.

Exactly.

And that is what the man becomes who is most tyrannical by nature and wins sole power — and the longer he lives as tyrant, the more like this he grows.

Inevitably, said Glaucon, taking over the answering.

And now, I said, won't the man who shows himself the most wicked also show himself the most wretched? And the man who is tyrant longest and hardest — won't he in truth be wretched longest and hardest, whatever the crowd may think?

That much is certain, he said.

And the tyrannical man corresponds to the tyrannized city, the democratic man to the democratic city, and so on down the line?

Of course.

And as city stands to city in virtue and in happiness, so man stands to man?

Naturally.

Then in virtue, how does the city under a tyrant stand against the city under kings — the one we described at the start?

They are opposite extremes, he said: one is the best of cities, the other the worst.

I won't ask which is which, I said — that's obvious. But happiness and misery: is your judgment the same there? And let's not be dazzled into panic by staring at one man, the tyrant, or at the handful around him. We ought to go in and look at the whole city, slip down into every corner of it and see everything — and only then declare what we think.

A fair challenge, he said. And it's plain to everyone that no city is more wretched than one under a tyrant, and none happier than one under kings.

And in judging the men, wouldn't I be fair to make the same demand — that the verdict belong to someone who can enter a man's character in thought and see through it, not a child gawking from outside, dazzled by the front tyrants dress themselves in for the world, but someone whose sight goes all the way in? Suppose I say we should all listen to the man qualified to judge — one who has lived under the same roof with a tyrant, watched him at home, seen how he deals with his own household, where he stands most stripped of his stage costume, and watched him again in public dangers. Shouldn't we ask the man who has seen all that to report how the tyrant stands, for happiness and misery, against everyone else?

That would be the fairest demand of all.

Then shall we claim that we ourselves are among those who can judge — that we have met such men before — so that we'll have somebody to answer our questions?

By all means.

Then look at it this way, I said. Keep the likeness of city and man in front of you, take each in turn, and tell me the condition of each.

What conditions? he asked.

Start with the city, I said. A city under a tyrant — do you call it free or enslaved?

As enslaved as a city can be.

And yet you can see masters in it, and free men.

I see a few, he said. But the whole of it, near enough — and certainly the most decent part of it — is degraded and enslaved, and without honor.

Then if the man resembles the city, I said, the same order must hold in him: his soul loaded with slavishness and meanness, the most decent parts of it enslaved, while a small part — the maddest and most vicious — sits as master.

It must be so.

So what will you call such a soul — slave or free?

A slave, surely.

And the enslaved, tyrannized city least does what it actually wants?

Far the least.

Then the tyrannized soul will also least do what it wishes — I mean the soul taken as a whole. Dragged always by the sting of the gadfly, it will be full of turmoil and regret.

Of course.

And the tyrannized city — must it be rich or poor?

Poor.

Then the tyrannical soul, too, must always be needy and never filled.

So it must.

And such a city, and such a man — mustn't they be full of fear?

Very much so.

Do you expect to find more wailing and groaning, more lament and grief, in any other city?

Nowhere.

And in any man more than in this tyrannical one, maddened by his desires and his passions?

There couldn't be.

It was looking at all this, I think, that made you judge the tyrannized city the most wretched of cities.

And wasn't I right? he said.

Completely, I said. But looking at the same facts in the tyrannical man — what do you say now?

That he is by far the most wretched of all men.

There, I said, you may be going wrong.

How?

I don't think this man has quite reached the extreme of wretchedness yet.

Then who has?

There is one you will probably think more wretched still.

Who?

The man who is tyrannical by nature, I said, and does not live out his life as a private citizen — the unlucky one whose bad luck runs so far that he is made an actual tyrant.

Going by everything we've said, he said, I'd guess you're right.

Yes, I said, but guessing isn't good enough here. A question like this wants the hardest scrutiny we can give it — for what we are examining is the greatest question there is: the good life and the bad.

Quite right.

Then see whether there is something in this. Here, I think, is where we have to look.

Where?

At the private rich men in our cities who own many slaves. They resemble the tyrant in this: they rule over many. The difference is the size of his crowd.

Yes, that's the difference.

Well — you know that these men live without fear. They aren't afraid of their household slaves.

What would they be afraid of?

Nothing, I said. But do you see why?

Yes: because the whole city stands ready to defend each private citizen.

Well said, I replied. But now suppose some god lifted one of these men — a man with fifty slaves or more — clean out of the city, him and his wife and children, and set him down in a wilderness with his property and his whole household, where no free man could come to his aid. What kind of fear, and how much of it, do you think he'd feel that he and his children and his wife would be killed by the slaves?

Total fear, I should think.

Wouldn't he be forced, from that moment, to fawn on some of his own slaves — to promise them all sorts of things and set them free with nothing requiring it? Wouldn't he stand revealed as a flatterer of his own servants?

He'd have to, he said, or die.

And now suppose the god planted neighbors all around him — a ring of them, many, who will not tolerate one man claiming mastery over another, and who, if they catch such a man, punish him with the extreme penalty.

Then his case is worse still, I suppose — ringed on every side by nothing but enemies.

And isn't that exactly the prison the tyrant is chained in? His nature is what we described, packed with fears and passions of every kind; and greedy as his soul is, he alone of the men in the city can never travel anywhere, never go to see any of the things every other free man is eager to see. He burrows into his house and lives there mostly like a woman kept indoors, envying every other citizen who goes abroad and sees anything worth seeing.

Exactly so.

Such is the harvest of evils, then, for the man badly governed within himself — the tyrannical man you just judged most wretched — when he does not live out his life in private but is forced by some accident to become an actual tyrant: unable to rule himself, he undertakes to rule others. It is as if a man sick and helpless in his own body were forced to spend his life not resting at home but competing and fighting against other bodies.

The likeness is perfect, Socrates, he said — that's it exactly.

So isn't his condition wretched through and through, dear Glaucon? Doesn't the reigning tyrant live a harder life than the life you judged hardest of all?

He does.

In truth, then, whatever anyone may think, the real tyrant is the real slave — one long career of flattery and groveling, a courtier to the vilest men alive. His desires he never satisfies at all; he is needier than anyone, and genuinely poor, if you know how to look at a soul whole; loaded with fear his whole life long, full of convulsions and pains — if his condition really matches the city he rules. And it does match. Doesn't it?

It does.

And on top of all this, add what we said before: power must make him — and does make him — more envious, more faithless, more unjust, more friendless, more impious than he was; host and feeder of every vice. And for all these reasons he is supremely unhappy himself, and then makes everyone near him just as unhappy.

No one with any sense will contradict you.

Come then, I said. Like the judge who decides the whole competition, give your verdict now across the field: who, in your judgment, is first in happiness, who second, and so on through the five — the kingly man, the timocratic, the oligarchic, the democratic, the tyrannical.

The judging is easy, he said. I rank them like choruses, in the order they came on stage — by virtue and vice, by happiness and its opposite.

Shall we hire a herald, then, I said, or shall I make the announcement myself? The son of Ariston has judged: the best and most just man is the happiest — and he is the most kingly man, the one who is king over himself; the worst and most unjust is the most wretched — and he, in turn, is the man who, being the greatest tyrant over himself, is the greatest tyrant over his city.

Announce it yourself, he said.

And shall I add: whether or not gods and men see them for what they are?

Add it.

Good, I said. That would be our first proof. Now the second — see if there's anything in it.

What is it?

Since the soul, like the city, divides into three parts, I think it gives us a second demonstration.

What is that?

This. Three parts — and, it seems to me, three pleasures, one proper to each part; and likewise three kinds of desire, and three kinds of rule.

Explain.

One part, we said, is what a man learns with; another is what he gets angry with; the third had so many faces that we couldn't give it one name of its own, so we named it for its biggest and strongest member. We called it the appetitive part, for the intensity of its appetites for food and drink and sex and everything that goes with them — and the money-loving part too, because money, more than anything, is how appetites like these get satisfied.

And rightly.

So if we said its pleasure and its love are for profit, that would gather it best under a single head for the argument — so we know what we mean whenever we speak of this part of the soul. Call it money-loving, profit-loving: is that fair?

It seems right to me.

And the spirited part — don't we say it drives, whole and always, toward power and victory and a great name?

Very much.

So victory-loving and honor-loving would suit it well?

Extremely well.

But the part we learn with — everyone can see that its whole effort, always, is to know the truth as it stands, and that of the three it cares least for money and reputation.

Far the least.

Learning-loving, wisdom-loving — those are the names that fit it?

Of course.

And in some souls this part rules, I said, while in others one of the other two does — whichever way it falls out?

Just so.

That is why we say there are three primary kinds of human being: the lover of wisdom, the lover of victory, the lover of gain.

Exactly.

And three forms of pleasure, one underlying each?

Certainly.

Now, I said, if you asked these three men in turn which of their lives is the pleasantest, each would praise his own the loudest. The money-maker will tell you that next to making a profit, the pleasures of being honored or of learning are worth nothing — unless they bring in money.

True.

And the lover of honor? Doesn't he count the pleasure of money as vulgar, and the pleasure of learning — except where learning brings distinction — as smoke and nonsense?

That is exactly how it is.

And the philosopher — what do we suppose he thinks the other pleasures are worth, next to knowing the truth and how it stands, and living always in some such state while he learns? Doesn't he hold them far from real pleasure, and call them necessary in the strict sense — meaning he would want nothing to do with them if necessity didn't force them on him?

We can be sure of it, he replied.

So when the pleasures of each kind, and the lives themselves, are in dispute this way, I said — not over which life is nobler or more shameful, or worse or better, but simply over which is more pleasant and freer of pain — how do we know which of the three men speaks truest?

I honestly can't say, he said.

Well, look at it this way. What should things be judged with, if they are to be judged well? Is there anything better than experience, and intelligence, and reasoned argument? Could you find a better standard than these?

Hardly, he said.

Then look. Of our three men, which has the widest experience of all the pleasures we named? Do you think the lover of gain, by studying what truth itself is, gains more experience of the pleasure of knowing than the philosopher has of the pleasure of gain?

There's no comparison, he replied. The philosopher had no choice: from childhood on he has tasted the other pleasures. But the lover of gain — not only is there nothing forcing him to learn how things really are and taste how sweet that pleasure is; even if he were eager to, he'd find it hard going.

Then in experience of both kinds of pleasure, the lover of wisdom stands far above the lover of gain.

Far above.

And against the lover of honor? Has the philosopher less experience of the pleasure of being honored than that man has of the pleasure of knowing?

No, he said. Honor comes to all three of them, if each accomplishes what he set out to do — the rich man is honored by plenty of people, and so is the brave man, and so is the wise one — so the pleasure of being honored is one they've all tasted. But the pleasure held in the sight of what really is — no one but the philosopher can have tasted that.

Then as far as experience goes, I said, he is the finest judge of the three.

By far.

And he alone will have experience joined to intelligence.

Certainly.

Further: the very instrument the judging must be done with belongs not to the lover of gain or the lover of honor, but to the philosopher.

What instrument?

Reasoned argument. We said the judging must be done through arguments, didn't we?

Yes.

And argument is, above all others, the philosopher's own instrument.

Of course.

Now, if wealth and profit were the best standard for judging things, then whatever the lover of gain praised or condemned would necessarily be truest.

Necessarily.

And if honor and victory and courage were the standard, then the verdict of the honor-lover and the victory-lover?

Clearly.

But since the standard is experience and intelligence and argument —

Then necessarily, he said, the truest pleasures are the ones praised by the lover of wisdom and of argument.

Then of the three pleasures, the pleasantest belongs to the part of the soul we learn with; and the man in whom that part rules has the pleasantest life.

How could it be otherwise? he said. The man of understanding praises his own life with full authority.

And which life and which pleasure does our judge put second?

Obviously the fighter's, the honor-lover's — it stands closer to his own than the money-maker's does.

And the lover of gain comes last, it seems.

Of course, he said.

That makes two in a row, then: twice running the just man has thrown the unjust. Now the third fall — wrestled Olympic style, for Olympian Zeus the Savior. Watch: I once heard a wise man say that only the pleasure of the understanding is fully true and pure, and all the others are shadow-painting. And this fall would be the greatest and most decisive of all.

Much the greatest. But what do you mean?

I'll trace it out, I said, if you answer while I ask.

Ask away.

Then tell me: don't we say pain is the opposite of pleasure?

We do.

And there is such a thing as feeling neither enjoyment nor pain?

There is.

A state midway between the two — a kind of stillness of the soul with respect to both. Is that what you'd call it?

It is.

Do you remember what sick people say when they're sick?

What?

That nothing is pleasanter than being healthy — but that they never noticed it was pleasantest until they fell ill.

I remember.

And people gripped by some violent pain — you've heard them say nothing is pleasanter than for the pain to stop?

I have.

And in many other situations like these, I think, you find people in pain praising not enjoyment but the absence of pain — the rest from it — as the pleasantest thing there is.

Yes, he said. Because at those moments the rest itself becomes pleasant — welcome enough to satisfy them.

And when a man's enjoyment stops, that rest from pleasure will be painful.

I suppose it will.

So the rest we just called a state between the two will at different times be both — pain and pleasure.

So it seems.

But can what is neither of two things become both of them?

I don't see how.

And pleasure arising in the soul is a kind of motion, and so is pain. Yes?

Yes.

But didn't we just see that feeling neither pain nor pleasure is a stillness — a midpoint between the two?

We did.

Then how can it be right to think the absence of pain is pleasant, or the absence of enjoyment painful?

It can't be.

So the stillness isn't really pleasant or painful — it only appears so: beside what hurts, the still state looks pleasant; beside what pleases, painful. And there is nothing sound in any of these appearances, measured against true pleasure — only a kind of conjuring trick.

That is what the argument says, anyway.

Then look at pleasures that don't come out of pains, I said, so you don't keep thinking — as you may be thinking now — that pleasure is by nature the stopping of pain, and pain the stopping of pleasure.

Where should I look? he said. Which pleasures do you mean?

There are plenty, I said, but the clearest case is the pleasures of smell. Out of nowhere, with no pain beforehand, they arrive at extraordinary strength; and when they end, they leave no pain behind them.

Very true.

So let no one persuade us that pure pleasure is relief from pain, or pain relief from pleasure.

No.

And yet, I said, the so-called pleasures that reach the soul through the body — the biggest crowd of them, and the most intense — are exactly this kind: some form of release from pain.

They are.

And the pleasures and pains of anticipation, the ones that run ahead of the real thing — the same kind?

The same.

Do you know what they are like, then, I said — what they most resemble?

What?

You believe there is, in nature, an up and a down and a middle?

I do.

Then wouldn't a man carried from below toward the middle be sure he was moving up? And standing in the middle, looking back down at where he came from, wouldn't he be certain he was at the top — never having seen the true top?

By Zeus, he said, I don't see how such a man could think anything else.

And if he were carried back down, he'd suppose he was moving down — and this time he'd be right?

Of course.

And all this would happen to him because he has no experience of what is truly up, in the middle, and down?

Obviously.

Then is it any wonder that people with no experience of truth hold unsound opinions about pleasure and pain and the state in between, the way they do about so much else? When they're carried toward the painful, they judge truly — they really are in pain. But when they're carried from pain back to the middle state, they are firmly convinced they have arrived at fullness, at pleasure. Never having seen true pleasure, they compare pain against the painless and are taken in — like men who, never having seen white, compare gray against black. Can any of that surprise you?

No indeed — I'd be far more surprised if it weren't so.

Then think of it this way, I said. Hunger and thirst and everything like them — aren't they emptyings of the body's condition?

Certainly.

And ignorance and folly — emptyings of the soul's condition?

Very much so.

And a man fills the one when he takes in food, the other when he comes to understand?

Of course.

Now, which filling is the truer: filling with what has less being, or with what has more?

With what has more, obviously.

Then which class of things do you think has the greater share of pure being — things like bread and drink and meat and nourishment in general, or the class of true opinion and knowledge and understanding and, in a word, all excellence? Judge it this way: what keeps company with the unchanging and immortal and true, is itself of that kind, and comes to be in what is of that kind — does that seem to you to be more, or what keeps company with the changing and mortal, is itself of that kind, and comes to be in what is of that kind?

What holds to the unchanging, he replied — by far.

And does the being of the unchanging share any less in knowledge than it does in being?

Not at all — in knowledge equally.

And in truth equally?

Equally.

And conversely: what has less of truth has less of being as well?

Necessarily.

So as a whole, the kinds of thing that serve the care of the body have less share of truth and of being than the kinds that serve the care of the soul?

Much less.

And the body itself, compared with the soul — the same?

Yes.

Then what is filled with things that have more being, and itself has more being, is more really filled than what is filled with things that have less being, and itself has less?

Obviously.

So if being filled with what suits our nature is pleasant, then what is more really filled, and with things that are more real, would enjoy true pleasure more really and more truly; while what takes in things with less being is filled less truly and less securely, and gets a pleasure less trustworthy and less true.

Inescapably.

Then those who have no experience of wisdom and virtue, who spend their whole time at feasts and everything of that sort, are carried down, it seems, and back up as far as the middle, and wander that road their whole lives. They never cross beyond it: they have never looked up toward the true top, never been carried there, never been really filled with what really is, never tasted a pleasure that is solid and pure. Like cattle they graze, eyes forever down, heads bent to the ground — to the table, that is — fattening themselves and mounting each other; and in their greed for more of these things they kick and butt one another with horns and hooves of iron, and kill one another, and can never be filled — because the part of themselves they are filling is not the real part, and holds nothing, and what they are filling it with is not real either.

Socrates, said Glaucon, you describe the life of most men like an oracle.

And doesn't it follow that the pleasures they live among are mixed with pains — mere images of the true pleasure, shadow-paintings that take their color from being set side by side, so that both look intense; pleasures that breed insane desires for themselves in fools, and get fought over, the way Stesichorus says the shadow of Helen was fought over at Troy by men who did not know the truth?

Something exactly like that must be the case.

And the spirited part — mustn't the same story hold wherever a man works to fill it: envy driving his love of honor, violence his love of victory, temper his sullenness, as he chases his fill of honor and victory and anger without calculation and without sense?

The same story must hold there too.

Then may we say this with confidence, I said? Even the desires of the gain-loving and honor-loving parts — whenever they follow knowledge and argument, and pursue with their help the pleasures that understanding prescribes, they will get the truest pleasures they are capable of getting, because they are following truth, and the pleasures that are properly their own — if indeed what is best for each thing is also what is most its own.

And it certainly is its own.

So when the whole soul follows the wisdom-loving part and there is no faction in it, each part can do its own work and be just in everything — and, in particular, each reaps its own pleasures, the best pleasures and, as far as possible, the truest.

Exactly.

But when one of the other two takes control, it fails to find even its own pleasure — and it forces the other parts to chase a pleasure that is alien to them and false.

True.

And the farther a thing stands from philosophy and argument, the more surely it produces effects like these?

Much the farther.

And what stands farthest from argument is what stands farthest from law and order?

Clearly.

And the desires that stand farthest, as we saw, are the lustful and tyrannical ones? — Yes.

And the kingly, orderly ones stand nearest?

Yes.

Then the tyrant, I think, will stand at the greatest distance from true pleasure — pleasure that is a man's own — and the king at the least.

Necessarily.

So the tyrant will live the least pleasant life, I said, and the king the most.

Absolutely.

And do you know, I said, just how much less pleasantly the tyrant lives than the king?

Tell me.

There are three pleasures, it seems — one legitimate, two bastard. The tyrant has fled beyond even the bastard ones. He has run from law and argument out past the border, and lives with a bodyguard of slave pleasures; and how far he has sunk is not easy to state — except perhaps like this.

How?

The tyrant stood third from the oligarch, counting down — the democrat sat between them.

Yes.

Then if what we said earlier is true, the pleasure he lives with is a phantom three times removed from the oligarch's, as truth goes.

It is.

And the oligarch in turn stands third from the king, if we count kingly and aristocratic as one.

Third, yes.

So the tyrant is removed from true pleasure, I said, by a number that is three times three.

Apparently.

Then the phantom of tyrannical pleasure, taken by the number of its length, is a plane figure.

Quite.

But take the square, and then the cube, and it becomes plain how great the distance is that separates them.

Plain, at least, he said, to a man who can calculate.

Then turn it around: if you want to say how far the king stands from the tyrant in truth of pleasure, finish the multiplication and you will find the king lives seven hundred and twenty-nine times more pleasantly, and the tyrant, by the same interval, more painfully.

You have poured out a prodigious calculation, he said, of the distance between the two men — the just and the unjust — in pleasure and pain!

And yet the number is true, I said, and it fits human lives too — if days and nights and months and years fit human lives.

They certainly do fit.

Then if the good and just man beats the bad and unjust man by that much in pleasure, how incalculably further will he outstrip him in grace of life, in beauty, in virtue?

Incalculably indeed, he said.

Good, I said. And now that we have come this far in the argument, let us go back to the claims that started it. Wasn't it said that injustice pays for the completely unjust man who is reputed to be just?

It was.

Now, then, is the moment to have a word with the man who says so — now that we have settled what doing injustice and doing justice each accomplish.

What shall we say to him?

Let us mold an image of the soul in words, so that the man who says such things can see what he is actually saying.

What kind of image?

One like those natures the old myths tell of, I said — the Chimera, Scylla, Cerberus, and all the others where many shapes are said to have grown together into one.

So the stories go.

Then mold a single creature: a beast of many colors and many heads, wearing a ring of heads of animals tame and wild, able to change them and sprout new ones out of itself at will.

That would take a clever sculptor, he said. Still — since words are easier to shape than wax, consider it molded.

Now a second form, a lion; and a third, a man. Make the first by far the biggest, and the second next.

That's easier, he said. Done.

Now join the three into one, so that they somehow grow together.

Joined.

And around the outside of them mold the likeness of one of the three — the man — so that anyone who cannot see inside, who sees only the outer shell, takes it for a single animal: a human being.

It is molded.

Now, to the man who says it pays this creature to do injustice, and that doing justice brings no advantage, let us say: he is claiming nothing more than that it pays the creature to feast the many-formed beast and make it strong, and the lion and everything lion along with it, while starving the man inside and weakening him, until the other two drag him wherever either of them pleases — and never to get any of them used to the others or make them friends, but to leave them to bite and fight and devour one another.

That, he said, is exactly what the praiser of injustice is saying.

And the one who says justice pays is saying that a man should do and say only what will give the man inside the fullest mastery over the whole human creature — that he should tend the many-headed beast like a farmer, feeding and taming the gentle heads and blocking the growth of the wild ones, making the lion's nature his ally, and, caring for all of them in common, bring them up to be friends with one another and with himself.

Yes — that is exactly what the defender of justice means.

From every angle, then, the man who praises justice speaks the truth, and the man who praises injustice speaks falsely. Judge it by pleasure, by reputation, by advantage: the praiser of justice is right, and the fault-finder has nothing sound to say — he doesn't even know what it is he is condemning.

Nothing sound at all, I'd say.

Then let us persuade him gently — he isn't wrong on purpose — and ask him: 'Friend, wouldn't we say this is what the customary standards of noble and shameful come from? The noble is what brings the beast in our nature under the human — or better, perhaps, under the divine; the shameful is what enslaves the tame in us to the wild.' Will he agree, or not?

He will, if he takes my advice.

Then on this argument, can it pay any man to take gold unjustly, when it works out like this: in the act of taking the gold, he hands the best part of himself into slavery under the worst? If it would pay no one to sell a son or a daughter into slavery for gold — and slavery under savage, evil men at that — no matter how enormous the price, then what happens when a man enslaves the most divine thing in him to the most godless and polluted thing, and feels no pity for it? Isn't he wretched — bribed with gold toward a ruin more terrible than Eriphyle's, when she took the necklace as the price of her husband's life?

Far more terrible, said Glaucon — I'll answer for him.

And why do you think self-indulgence has been condemned from the old days? Because in such a man that terrible thing — the great, many-formed beast — is let loose further than it should be.

Clearly.

And willfulness and bad temper are condemned when the lion-and-snake part of us grows and strains out of all proportion?

Certainly.

And luxury and softness are condemned because they slacken and unstring that same part, and breed cowardice in it?

Of course.

And flattery and servility, when a man puts this same spirited part under the mob of a beast, and for the sake of money and the beast's bottomless appetite trains the lion, from youth up, to take abuse — until it becomes a monkey instead of a lion?

Just so.

And why do you think menial and manual work carries a stigma? Shall we say it is only this: when the best part in a man is naturally so weak that it cannot rule the creatures inside him, but has to wait on them instead, and can learn nothing except how to flatter them?

It seems so, he said.

Then in order that such a man may be ruled the way the best man is ruled, we say he ought to be the servant of that best man — the man with the divine ruler inside him. Not that the servant should be ruled to his own harm, as Thrasymachus thought about the ruled; but because it is better for everyone to be ruled by what is divine and intelligent — best of all by his own, living inside him; failing that, set over him from outside — so that as far as possible we may all be alike, and all friends, steered by the same pilot.

Yes, he said — and rightly.

And the law shows the same intention — the law that is every citizen's ally. So does our rule over children: we do not allow them to be free until we have set up a constitution inside them, as inside a city — until, by tending the best in them with the best in us, we have installed in the child a guardian and ruler like our own. Then, and only then, we let them go free.

Yes — that is what the law shows.

On what ground, then, Glaucon, and by what argument, can we say it pays a man to be unjust or self-indulgent or to do anything shameful that makes him a worse man, even if it brings him more money or more power?

On no ground at all.

And how can it pay to do injustice and escape — to go unpunished? Doesn't the man who escapes simply become worse? While in the man who is caught and punished, the beast in him is calmed and tamed and the gentle part set free; the whole soul, restored toward its best nature, wins self-command and justice together with understanding — a condition more precious to it than strength and beauty and health are to the body, exactly as the soul is more precious than the body.

Absolutely.

Then a man with any sense will bend every effort of his life toward this end. First, he will honor the studies that shape his soul this way, and despise the rest.

Clearly.

Next, I said, his bodily condition and training: he will not hand them over to beastly, irrational pleasure and point his life that way. He won't even make health his object — won't rank being strong or healthy or handsome as things that matter — unless self-command comes with them. You will find him always tuning the harmony of his body for the sake of the concord in his soul.

He certainly will, if he means to be truly musical.

And in acquiring money, the same order and concord? He won't be dazzled by what the crowd congratulates itself on, and pile up wealth without limit, and bring limitless troubles on himself.

I think not.

He will keep his eye instead on the constitution inside him, I said, and stand guard against disturbing anything there through having too much or too little; steering by that, he will add to his wealth, or spend it, as far as he is able.

Exactly so.

And honors — the same rule. He will taste, even gladly, the ones he judges will make him better; but the ones he thinks would break down his settled condition he will avoid, in private and in public alike.

Then if that is what he cares about, he said, he won't be willing to enter politics.

By the dog, I said, he will — very much so, in his own city. Though perhaps not in the city of his birth, unless some divine stroke of luck intervenes.

I understand, he said. You mean the city we have just founded and described — the one that exists in words. I don't think it exists anywhere on earth.

But perhaps, I said, there is a pattern of it laid up in heaven for the man who wants to see it — and, seeing it, to found that city in himself. Whether it exists anywhere, or ever will, makes no difference. He will act in the politics of that city, and of no other.

Most likely, he said.
