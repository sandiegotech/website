# The Republic, Book III

The Noble Lie

So that, I said, is where we stand on the gods — the kinds of stories men should and should not hear, from childhood on, if they are going to honor the gods and their parents, and take their friendships with one another seriously.

Yes, he said — and I think we have it right.

Now, what if they are also to be brave? Don't they need, on top of this, the kind of stories that will make them fear death as little as possible? Or do you think anyone can ever be brave with the fear of death inside him?

No one could, he said.

And a man who believes the underworld is real and full of horrors — will he be unafraid of death? Will he choose to die in battle rather than lose and be enslaved?

Impossible.

Then it seems we must supervise the tellers of these stories too, and ask them not to run down the underworld wholesale but rather to speak well of it — because what they say now is not true, and not useful to men who are going to have to fight.

We must, he said.

Then we'll strike out everything of that sort, I said, beginning with these lines:

'I would rather work the fields, hired out to some landless man with barely enough to live on, than be king over all the dead who have gone down to nothing.'

And this must go too — the lines where Hades himself panics,

'terrified that the halls of the dead would lie open to mortals and immortals — ghastly, moldering places that even the gods shudder at.'

And again:

'So it's true — even in the house of Hades something survives: a soul, a shadow-image; but there is no mind left in it at all.'

And this, about Tiresias:

'To him alone, even dead, Persephone has granted mind; the others flit about, mere shadows.'

Or this:

'His soul fled from his limbs and went down to Hades, wailing its fate, leaving behind its manhood and its youth.'

And this:

'and the soul, with a thin cry, vanished under the earth like smoke.'

And:

'As bats in the depths of an eerie cave fly squeaking when one drops from the chain of them clinging to the rock, and they hold fast to one another — so the souls went down together, squeaking.'

We'll ask Homer and the other poets not to take it personally when we strike these lines and everything like them. It isn't that they aren't poetry, or that most people don't enjoy hearing them. Just the opposite: the more poetic they are, the more dangerous they are for boys and men who are meant to be free — who should fear slavery more than they fear death.

Absolutely.

And we must also throw out all the dreadful, shuddering names of that world — Cocytus and Styx; 'those under the earth'; 'the sapless dead' — every phrase of the kind that makes the listener's flesh crawl. Perhaps such horrors have a use of some sort; but we are afraid for our guardians — afraid the shudder will leave them more feverish and softer than they ought to be.

And that fear is justified, he said.

So they must go.

They must.

And we should tell stories, and sing, in exactly the opposite key.

Clearly.

Then do we also cut the weeping and wailing of famous men?

That has to follow, he said, from everything so far.

But look — are we right to cut it? Think about it. We hold that a good man will not consider death a terrible thing for another good man — for his friend.

We do hold that.

Then he won't mourn for him as though something terrible had happened to him.

No.

And we also say that such a man is the most self-sufficient of anyone when it comes to living well — that he, of all men, has the least need of others.

True, he said.

So for him, least of all men, is it a catastrophe to lose a son, or a brother, or his money, or anything else of the kind.

Least of all.

So he, least of anyone, will collapse in grief when such a loss comes; he will bear it more calmly than any other man.

Far more calmly.

Then we're right to cut the laments of famous men. Hand them over to women — and not to the serious ones, at that — and to the cowardly sort of men, so that the men we say we are raising to guard this country will be disgusted at the thought of carrying on like that.

Quite right.

So once again we'll ask Homer and the other poets not to show us Achilles, a goddess's son, lying now on his side, now on his back, now face down; then on his feet, pacing the shore of the barren sea, out of his mind with grief; or scooping black ash in both hands and pouring it over his head, weeping and wailing in all the ways Homer wrote it. And not to show Priam, near kin of the gods, begging and groveling,

'rolling in the dung, calling out to each man by name.'

And we'll beg him harder still not to make the gods grieve out loud, crying,

'Ah, my misery — I bore the best of men, and bore him for sorrow.'

And if he must have grieving gods, at least let him not dare to travesty the greatest of them — not make Zeus say,

'What a sight for my eyes — a man I love hunted around the city walls, and my heart grieving with him.'

Or:

'Ah me — Sarpedon, whom I love best of men, is fated to fall to Patroclus, son of Menoetius.'

Because, my dear Adeimantus, if our young men took things like that seriously — instead of laughing them off as unworthy of the gods — hardly one of them would think such behavior beneath him. He is only a man, after all. He would never catch and rebuke the impulse to say and do the same. Instead, at the smallest setback, with no shame and no endurance, he would be singing dirges and wailing his heart out.

That's completely true, he said.

And it must not happen — the argument just showed us so, and we should trust that argument until someone persuades us with a better one.

No, it must not.

And they shouldn't be lovers of laughter, either. When a man surrenders to a violent fit of laughter, it tends to bring on just as violent a swing in him.

So I believe.

So we can't accept poets showing men of worth helpless with laughter — and gods, even less.

Even less indeed, he said.

Then we won't accept lines like Homer's about the gods, where

'unquenchable laughter broke out among the blessed gods as they watched Hephaestus puffing up and down the hall.'

If we follow your argument, he said, we can't accept them.

Mine — if you want to call it mine, I said. Either way, we can't accept them.

And truth must be prized above almost everything. If we were right before — if a lie really is useless to the gods, and useful to men only as a kind of medicine — then clearly medicine like that belongs in the hands of doctors. Ordinary people have no business touching it.

None, he said.

So if lying is a privilege at all, it belongs to the rulers of the city — to be used against enemies, or on their own citizens, for the city's good. No one else may touch it. And for a private citizen to lie to these rulers, we'll say, is a worse offense than for a patient to lie to his doctor, or an athlete to his trainer, about the state of his own body — or for a sailor to hide from the helmsman how things actually stand with the ship and the crew, what he and his shipmates are doing.

Very true, he said.

So if the ruler catches anyone else in the city lying —

'any of the craftsmen who serve the people — prophet, or healer of sickness, or worker in wood' —

he will punish him for bringing in a practice that can capsize and sink a city as surely as a ship.

He certainly will, he said — if actions ever follow our words.

Next: won't our young men need self-command?

Of course.

And for most people, doesn't self-command come down mainly to this — obey those in command, and be in command of yourself, of the pleasures of drink and sex and food?

I'd say so.

Then we'll approve of lines like the one Homer gives Diomedes,

'Sit down, friend. Be quiet, and do as I say,'

and the lines near it,

'the Greeks came on breathing fury, silent, in fear of their commanders,'

and everything else in that spirit.

We will.

But what about this one —

'You wine-sack — with a dog's eyes and a deer's heart' —

and what comes after it? Are these good things to hear — all the insolent speeches, in verse or prose, that ordinary men have flung at their rulers?

They are not.

They may be entertaining, I don't doubt it. But they do nothing for a young man's self-command, and that makes them dangerous. Do you agree?

Yes.

Then again — having the wisest of men say that the finest thing he knows is when

'the tables are loaded with bread and meat, and the steward draws wine from the bowl and carries it round and fills the cups' —

is that the right thing for a young man to hear, if he is learning to rule himself? Or this:

'the most pitiful death of all is to die of hunger.'

And what about the story of Zeus — how, while the other gods and men slept and he alone lay awake making plans, he forgot them all in one rush of desire; how the sight of Hera struck him so hard that he wouldn't even go inside, but wanted to take her right there on the ground, saying no craving had ever seized him like this — not even when they first went to each other,

'behind their parents' backs.'

Or the one where Hephaestus throws a chain around Ares and Aphrodite over the same kind of business?

No, by Zeus, he said — none of that strikes me as fit for them to hear.

But acts of endurance against all odds, done and spoken of by famous men — those they should see and hear. Like this:

'He struck his chest and rebuked his own heart: Bear up, my heart. You have borne worse than this.'

Absolutely.

And we can't allow our men to take bribes, or to love money.

Certainly not.

So no singing to them that

'gifts persuade the gods, and gifts persuade the mightiest kings.'

And Phoenix, Achilles' old tutor, can't be praised as giving sound advice when he tells him to defend the Greeks if they bring gifts, but without gifts to stay angry. Nor will we believe it of Achilles himself — we won't grant that he was ever so greedy that he took Agamemnon's presents, or that he gave back Hector's body for a ransom and would not have done it otherwise.

No, he said — that kind of thing deserves no praise.

I love Homer, I said, so I hesitate to say it: but it is downright impious to say these things about Achilles, or to believe them when others say them. The same goes for the passage where he tells Apollo,

'You have crossed me, Far-shooter, deadliest of all the gods; and I would pay you back, if I had the power,'

and where he defies the river — a god — and is ready to fight him; and where he offers his hair, already vowed to the other river, Spercheius, saying, 'let the hero Patroclus carry these locks with him' — a corpse — and we're told he did it. None of it is true. And the dragging of Hector around Patroclus's tomb, and the slaughter of living captives on the pyre — we will say all of that is false. We will not let our citizens believe that Achilles — son of a goddess and of Peleus, that most self-possessed of men, third in line from Zeus, raised by the wise Cheiron — was such a chaos inside that he carried two diseases that contradict each other: a money-grubber's stinginess, and arrogant contempt for gods and men.

You're right, he said.

Then let's not believe it either — or let anyone repeat it — that Theseus, son of Poseidon, and Peirithous, son of Zeus, set out to commit those monstrous abductions, or that any other hero and child of a god would dare the terrible, impious deeds now falsely pinned on them. Let's force the poets to say either that the deeds weren't theirs, or that they weren't children of gods — but not both at once. And let them stop trying to convince our young men that gods breed evil, and that heroes are no better than ordinary men. As we said before, that is neither pious nor true — for we proved that evil cannot come from gods.

Impossible.

And such stories do real damage to those who hear them. Every rotten man will forgive himself his rottenness once he's convinced that the same things are done, and always were done, by

'the close-sown kin of the gods, near to Zeus, whose ancestral altar stands high in the open air on Ida's peak,'

men in whom

'the blood of the gods has not yet died out.'

So let's shut these stories down, before they breed in our young men an easy conscience about being bad.

By all means.

Now then, I said — what kind of speech is left, while we're marking out what may and may not be said? We've covered how to speak of gods, and demigods, and heroes, and the world of the dead.

We have.

What remains would be speech about men, wouldn't it?

Obviously.

But it's impossible for us to settle that one right now, my friend.

Why not?

Because, if I'm not mistaken, we're going to say that poets and storytellers get the most important things about men wrong. They tell us that plenty of unjust men are happy, and plenty of just men miserable; that injustice pays, so long as no one catches you; that justice is another man's gain and your own loss. We'll forbid them to say any of that, and order them to sing and tell the opposite — won't we?

We certainly will, he said.

But if you grant me that, I'll say you've already granted the very thing we've been hunting all along.

You're right, he said — I see the inference.

So we can't settle what should be said about men until we've discovered what justice is, and whether by its nature it profits the one who has it — whether he looks just to the world or not.

Very true.

That's enough, then, about the content of stories. Now we should look at the style — the how of the telling — and then we'll have covered both what should be said and the way of saying it.

Adeimantus said: I don't understand what you mean.

Well, you'd better, I said. Maybe this will make it clearer. Everything the storytellers and poets tell is a narrative of things past, present, or to come. Isn't it?

What else could it be?

And don't they carry it off either by plain narration, or by imitation — impersonating their characters — or by a mix of the two?

That, he said, I still don't follow.

Then I must be a ridiculous teacher, I said — I can't make myself clear. Like a man who's no good at speaking, I'll give up on the whole and break off a piece to show you what I mean. You know the opening of the Iliad, where the poet tells how Chryses begged Agamemnon to release his daughter; how Agamemnon exploded at him; and how Chryses, having failed, prayed the god down on the Greeks. Now, as far as these lines —

'and he begged all the Greeks, but above all the two sons of Atreus, marshals of the armies' —

the poet speaks in his own voice; he never tries to make us think the speaker is anyone but himself. But after this he speaks as Chryses — he does everything he can to make us believe it isn't Homer talking but the old priest himself. And that's how he built virtually the whole narrative: the events at Troy, the events in Ithaca, the whole of the Odyssey.

So he did.

And it's all narrative, isn't it — both the speeches he delivers and the stretches in between?

Of course.

But when he delivers a speech in someone else's character, doesn't he make his own voice as much as possible like the person he has announced as the speaker?

He does.

And to make yourself like someone else in voice or in bearing — that is imitating the person you become?

Yes.

Then in passages like that, it seems, he and the other poets build the narrative through imitation.

Exactly.

But if the poet never hid himself anywhere, his whole poem would be narration without imitation. So that you don't tell me again you don't understand, I'll show you how that would go. If Homer had said that Chryses came bringing his daughter's ransom, as a suppliant to the Greeks and above all to their kings — and had then gone on speaking not as Chryses but still as Homer — there would be no imitation in it, just plain narration. It would run something like this (I'll drop the meter; I'm no poet): The priest came and prayed that the gods let them take Troy and come home safe, and asked them to accept the ransom and free his daughter, out of reverence for the god. When he finished, the others were respectful and ready to consent; but Agamemnon flew into a rage and ordered him to leave now and never come back — or the staff and the god's garlands might not protect him. Before he would free the daughter, he said, she would grow old beside him in Argos. He told the old man to go and stop provoking him, if he wanted to get home in one piece. Hearing this, the old man was afraid, and left in silence; and once clear of the camp he prayed and prayed to Apollo, calling the god by all his names, reminding him of every gift that had ever pleased him — temples built, offerings burned — and asking one favor in return: that his tears fall back on the Achaeans, paid for by the god's arrows. That, my friend, is how it becomes plain narration, without imitation.

I understand, he said.

Then you can also see the reverse of it, I said — cut everything the poet says between the speeches, and leave only the exchanges.

I see that too, he said. That's what tragedy is.

You've got it exactly, I said. And now I think I can make plain what I couldn't before: one kind of poetry and storytelling works wholly through imitation — tragedy, as you say, and comedy; another through the poet's own report — you'll find that above all in the dithyramb; and a third uses both, as epic does, and plenty of other forms too. Am I making sense?

Yes, he said — now I see what you were getting at.

Then remember what came before all this: we had settled what should be said, and still had to examine how.

I remember.

What I meant was just this: we need to decide whether we'll allow the poets to build their narratives through imitation, or partly through imitation and partly not — and in that case, where each belongs — or whether they're not to imitate at all.

I can guess what you're weighing, he said: whether we'll admit tragedy and comedy into the city, or not.

Perhaps, I said — and perhaps something bigger still. I honestly don't know yet. But wherever the argument carries us, like a wind, there we must go.

Well said, he replied.

Then here's the question, Adeimantus: should our guardians be imitators, or not? Or does the answer follow from what we said earlier — that one man does one job well, not many; and that if he tries his hand at many, he'll grasp at all of them and distinguish himself in none?

It follows.

And doesn't the same argument hold for imitation — one man can't imitate many things as well as he imitates one?

He can't.

Then he'll hardly manage to practice a calling worth anything and at the same time imitate many things — be an imitator by trade. Even two forms of imitation that look like next-door neighbors — comedy and tragedy — can't be written well by the same people. You did just call those two imitations?

I did — and you're right, the same men can't do both.

Any more than a man can recite epic and act on stage at once.

True.

Even tragic and comic actors are different people — and all of these are imitations, aren't they?

They are.

And it seems to me, Adeimantus, that human nature is minted in even smaller coin than this — too small to imitate many things well, or to do well the many real things the imitations copy.

Quite true, he said.

Then if we're to keep our first principle — that our guardians must be released from every other craft to be craftsmen of the city's freedom, precise ones, practicing nothing that doesn't lead to that end — they should neither do nor imitate anything else. And if they do imitate, let them imitate, from childhood on, what fits their calling: men who are brave, self-commanded, pious, free — all that family of qualities. But what is servile and shameful they should neither do nor be good at imitating — nor anything else that is vicious — or from the imitation they'll catch the reality. Haven't you noticed how imitations, kept up from youth onward, settle into habits and a second nature — in the body, in the voice, in the mind itself?

I certainly have, he said.

Then we won't allow the men we care for — men who have to become good — to imitate a woman, young or old: berating her husband, or trading defiance with the gods and bragging because she thinks herself blessed, or drowning in misfortune and grief and lament. Much less one who is sick, or in love, or in labor.

Quite right.

And no imitating slaves, women or men, doing what slaves do.

No.

Nor bad men, it seems — cowards, and men doing the opposite of everything we just prescribed: mocking and jeering at one another, trading filth, drunk or sober, and all the other ways such men wrong themselves and other people in word and deed. Nor should they get into the habit of making themselves over into madmen, in speech or action. They must recognize madness and badness, in men and in women — but do none of it, and imitate none of it.

Very true.

What about smiths at their trade, or any other craftsmen, or the rowers of warships, or the men who call the stroke for them — should they imitate any of that?

How could they, he said, when they're not even permitted to give those trades their attention?

And horses neighing, bulls bellowing, rivers rushing, the sea booming, thunder — will they imitate things like that?

They've already been forbidden, he said, to be mad or to act like madmen.

If I follow you, I said, you mean there's one style of speaking and narrating that a genuinely good man uses when he has something to say, and another style — nothing like it — that the man of the opposite nature and upbringing will always favor.

And what are they? he asked.

This, I said. When a decent man comes, in the course of a story, to a speech or an action of another good man, I think he'll be willing to deliver it as if he were that man, and feel no shame in the imitation — most of all when the good man is acting steadily and wisely; less so, and less willingly, when he has been shaken by sickness or desire or drink or some other stumble. But when he comes to a character unworthy of him, he'll refuse to shape himself in earnest after his inferior — except briefly, perhaps, when the character does something good. He'll be ashamed: partly because he has no practice imitating such people, partly out of disgust at stamping and fitting himself into the molds of worse men. In his mind he holds the whole business beneath him, unless it's done as a joke.

That sounds likely, he said.

So he'll use the kind of narration we walked through a moment ago with Homer's lines: his style will draw on both imitation and plain narrative, but with a small share of imitation in a long stretch of story. Or am I talking nonsense?

It's exactly right, he said. That has to be the pattern for such a speaker.

Then take the other kind of narrator. The worse he is, the more he'll imitate everything and count nothing beneath him — in dead earnest, in front of a crowd, he'll try to do it all. What I mentioned just now: thunder, the racket of wind and hail, of axles and pulleys, the voices of trumpets and flutes and pan-pipes and every instrument there is — even the sounds of dogs and sheep and birds. His whole style will be imitation in voice and gesture, with maybe a thin thread of narration through it.

That has to be the other kind, he said.

So those, I said, are the two styles I meant.

So they are.

Now, the first of the two involves only small variations. Give the speech the musical mode and rhythm that fit it, and a speaker who does it right stays close to a single mode from start to finish — the changes are small — and keeps, likewise, to much the same rhythm.

That's exactly so.

But the other style needs the opposite: every mode and every rhythm, if it's to be delivered as it demands — because it holds every possible shape of change.

Emphatically so.

And every poet, every speaker of anything, hits one of these two patterns of style, or the other, or some blend of the two. There's nowhere else to land.

Nowhere, he said.

Then what do we do? Admit all of them into our city, or one of the two unmixed styles, or the blend?

If my vote carries, he said: the unmixed imitator of the good man.

And yet, Adeimantus, the blended style is delicious too. In fact, the style children and their tutors love best — and the crowd, by a wide margin — is the exact opposite of the one you chose.

It is what they love.

But maybe you'd say it doesn't fit our constitution — because with us no man is double or multiple. Every man does one job.

It doesn't fit.

And isn't this why ours is the only city where we'll find the shoemaker a shoemaker, and not a ship's pilot on the side; the farmer a farmer, and not a juror on the side; the soldier a soldier, and not a businessman on the side — and so on all the way down?

True, he said.

So then: suppose a man arrives in our city who is clever enough to become anything — to imitate everything under the sun — and he wants to show himself off, poems and all. We'll bow down to him as someone sacred and wonderful and delightful; and then we'll tell him that there is no man like him in our city, and no law that permits there to be one. We'll pour myrrh on his head, crown him with a wreath of wool, and escort him to another town. For our own souls' health we'll settle for the plainer, less delightful poet and storyteller — the one who imitates the way a decent man speaks, and keeps to the patterns we laid down at the start, when we first set out to educate our soldiers.

We certainly will, he said — if it's up to us.

Then, my friend, I said, the part of music that deals with stories and legends looks finished. We've said what is to be told, and how to tell it.

So it seems to me too.

What's left, then, is the manner of song and melody.

Obviously.

And by now anyone could work out what we'll have to say about those — if we're going to stay consistent with ourselves.

Glaucon laughed. Then I'm afraid, Socrates, he said, that 'anyone' leaves me out. I can't quite work out, right at this moment, what we ought to say — though I have my suspicions.

You can say this much at least, I said: a song is made of three things — words, mode, and rhythm.

That much, he said, yes.

Well, as far as the words go, a song's words are no different from words unsung, are they? They should follow the same patterns we laid down just now, and in the same spirit.

True.

And the mode and the rhythm have to follow the words.

Of course.

Now, we said we have no use for dirges and lamentation in our stories.

We did.

So which are the mourning modes? Tell me — you're the musician.

The Mixolydian, he said, and the taut Lydian, and others in that vein.

Out they go, I said. They're useless even to women — the ones expected to be decent — let alone to men.

Agreed.

Next: drunkenness, softness, and laziness are about the last things that suit a guardian.

The very last.

Then which modes are soft — the drinking-party modes?

The Ionian, he said, and the Lydian again — the ones they call 'loose.'

And will you find any use for those, my friend, in men of war?

None at all, he said. Which seems to leave you the Dorian and the Phrygian.

I don't know the modes, I said. Just leave me the one that would rightly imitate the voice and cadence of a brave man in war, or in any forced and dangerous work — a man who, when it goes against him, when he's headed into wounds or death or has fallen into some other disaster, in every one of those moments stands his ground in good order and endures whatever fortune sends. And leave me one more, for a man at peaceful work — work not forced on him but freely chosen — persuading someone or asking for something, whether praying to a god or teaching and correcting a man; or the reverse, holding himself open to another's request or instruction or persuasion; a man acting as his own mind sees fit, never arrogant, moving with self-command and measure through all of it, and at peace with the outcome. These two modes — the forced and the free, the mode of the unlucky and the mode of the lucky, the brave man's and the self-commanded man's — leave me those.

Then you're asking, he said, for exactly the two I named: the Dorian and the Phrygian.

In that case, I said, our songs and melodies won't need a forest of strings, or every scale at once.

Apparently not.

So we won't be supporting the craftsmen who build triangular harps and Lydian zithers and every many-stringed, many-scaled contraption.

It appears not.

And flute-makers, flute-players — will you take those into the city? Isn't the flute the most 'many-stringed' instrument of them all — isn't the every-scale instrument itself just an imitation of the flute?

Clearly it is, he said.

That leaves you the lyre and the cithara as useful in town, I said; and out in the fields, the herdsmen can have some kind of pipe.

So the argument tells us, at least.

We're doing nothing radical, my friend, I said — just choosing Apollo and Apollo's instruments over Marsyas and his.

By Zeus, he said, no — nothing radical at all.

And look at that — by the dog! — without noticing, we've been purging the city we called bloated with luxury a little while ago.

And very sensibly too, he said.

Then let's finish the purge, I said. After the modes comes rhythm, and the same rule holds: we shouldn't chase intricate rhythms or every kind of foot, but look for the rhythms that belong to an orderly and courageous life — and once we've found them, make the foot and the melody follow that kind of speech, not the speech follow the foot and the melody. Which rhythms they are is your job to say, as it was with the modes.

Honestly, he said, I can't. That there are three basic forms out of which the meters are woven — the way four notes underlie all the modes — that much I've observed and can tell you. But which kind of life each one imitates, I cannot say.

Then on that, I said, we'll consult Damon: which feet fit meanness and insolence, or madness and the rest of what's bad, and which rhythms are left for their opposites. I have a hazy memory of hearing him name a composite war-march rhythm, and a dactyl — the heroic foot — which he arranged in some way I don't quite follow, rise and fall made equal, resolving into short and long; and, I think, an iambus, and a trochee too, with their longs and shorts assigned. And with some of these I believe he praised or blamed the tempo of the foot as much as the rhythm itself — or the two together; I can't say exactly. As I said, all of this can go to Damon. Settling it would take us more than a little talk. Or do you think otherwise?

By Zeus, no — I don't.

But this much you can settle now: that gracefulness and gracelessness follow good rhythm and bad.

Of course.

And good rhythm follows on beautiful speech, shaped to match it, and bad rhythm on the opposite kind; and the same goes for what sounds harmonious and what sounds off — since, as we just said, rhythm and mode follow the words, and not the words them.

Yes, he said — they have to follow the speech.

And the manner of the speech, I said, and the speech itself — don't they follow the character of the speaker's soul?

Of course.

And everything else follows the speech?

Yes.

Then fine speech, fine harmony, fine grace, and fine rhythm all follow from simplicity of soul — not the simple-mindedness we politely excuse with that name, but the real thing: a mind whose character is genuinely good and fine.

Entirely so.

Then if our young men are going to do their own work, mustn't they chase these qualities everywhere?

They must.

And they are everywhere. Painting is full of them, and every craft like it; weaving is full of them, and embroidery, and architecture, and the making of every kind of furnishing; so is the nature of our bodies, and of every growing thing. In all of these there is grace or gracelessness. And gracelessness, bad rhythm, disharmony — these are sisters of bad speech and bad character; while their opposites are sisters, and imitations, of the opposite — a self-commanded and good character.

Completely, he said.

Then is it only the poets we have to stand over, forcing them to plant the image of good character in their poems or else not write among us at all? Or do we stand over the other craftsmen too, and stop them from putting bad character — undisciplined, mean, graceless — into their images of living things, or into their buildings, or into anything else they make? And the one who can't comply is barred from working here — so that our guardians aren't raised among images of vice like cattle in a poisoned pasture, cropping and grazing a little from many places, day after day, until without noticing it they've collected one great evil in their souls. Shouldn't we look instead for the craftsmen with a native gift for tracking down the nature of the beautiful and the graceful — so that our young men live as if in a healthy country, where everything does them good, and from every side beautiful sights and sounds strike their eyes and ears like a breeze bringing health from good places, tilting them from childhood on, unawares, into likeness and friendship and harmony with the beauty of reason?

There could be no finer upbringing, he said.

And isn't that, Glaucon, I said, why the upbringing in music matters most? Rhythm and mode sink deeper into the inmost soul than anything else; they take hold of it with the strongest grip, bringing gracefulness with them; and they make a man graceful — if he's raised rightly; if not, the opposite. And the man properly raised on music will feel it sharpest when something has been botched or stunted by craft or by nature — his disgust will be the right disgust. He'll praise beautiful things, take joy in them, receive them into his soul, feed on them, and become noble and good; the ugly he'll rightly condemn and hate while he is still young, before he can grasp the reason why. And when reason arrives, the man raised this way will recognize it and embrace it — no one will greet it like family the way he does.

Yes, he said — that's my view too: those are the reasons for an education in music.

Then think of reading, I said. We only had it down when we stopped missing the letters — few as they are — in all the combinations where they circulate; when we never dismissed one as beneath notice in a small word or a large one, but strained to make it out everywhere, knowing we wouldn't be readers until we did.

True.

And if letters showed up reflected in water or in a mirror, we wouldn't recognize them there before we knew the letters themselves — it's one and the same skill and practice.

Exactly.

Then by the gods, I say it's the same here: we will never be musical either — nor will the guardians we say we must educate — until we recognize the forms of self-command, courage, generosity, greatness of spirit, and all their siblings, and their opposites too, everywhere they circulate; until we notice them wherever they turn up, themselves and their images, and never dismiss them in small things or great, but hold it all one and the same skill and practice.

It must be so, he said.

So when a beautiful character in the soul and a matching, harmonizing beauty in the body are cast from the same mold — wouldn't that be the fairest sight there is, for anyone who has eyes to see it?

By far.

And what is fairest is what we love most?

Of course.

Then the truly musical man would love people like that most of all — and where the harmony was missing, he would not love.

Not if the flaw were in the soul, he said. If it were something in the body, he'd bear with it and love anyway.

I understand, I said — you have, or once had, a beloved like that. Fine; I'll grant it. But tell me this: does extravagant pleasure have anything in common with self-command?

How could it? he said. Pleasure like that unhinges the mind as thoroughly as pain does.

Anything in common with the rest of virtue?

Nothing.

With outrage and dissipation, then?

More than with anything else.

And can you name a pleasure bigger or sharper than sex?

I can't, he said — nor a madder one.

But the right kind of love is loving what is orderly and beautiful — a disciplined, musical love?

Very much so.

Then nothing manic, nothing that borders on dissipation, may come near the right kind of love?

It may not.

Then that pleasure may come nowhere near it, and lover and beloved who love rightly can have no share in it?

No, by Zeus, Socrates, he said — nowhere near.

So it seems you'll lay down a law in the city we're founding: a lover may kiss his favorite, keep his company, touch him as a father would a son — for beauty's sake, and only if he consents. Beyond that, his dealings with anyone he cares for must never appear to go further; otherwise let him bear the reproach of a man with no music and no taste in him.

Just so, he said.

Well then, I said — do you see our account of music coming to an end? It ended where an ending belongs: music should end in the love of beauty.

I agree, he said.

After music, our young men get physical training.

Naturally.

Here too the training must be strict, starting in childhood and running through the whole of life. The truth of it, I believe, is this — see what you think. I don't believe that a sound body makes the soul good by its own goodness. It's the other way around: a good soul, by its own goodness, makes the body as good as a body can be. What do you say?

I think so too.

Then if we tend the mind properly and hand over to it the detailed care of the body — keeping ourselves to the general patterns, so as not to run long — we'd be doing it right?

Quite right.

Drunkenness we've already ruled out. A guardian is the last man on earth who can afford to be drunk and not know where he is.

It would be absurd, he said — a guard who needs a guard.

What about food, then? These men are athletes in the greatest contest there is. Aren't they?

They are.

Then would the conditioning of our professional athletes suit them?

Perhaps.

It's a sleepy sort of conditioning, I said, and it makes health a fragile thing. Haven't you noticed? These athletes sleep their lives away, and if they step even slightly outside their prescribed routine they come down with serious, violent illnesses.

I've noticed.

Our war-athletes need something subtler: wakeful as watchdogs, seeing and hearing at the keenest pitch, and able to take all the changes a campaign throws at them — strange water, strange food, blazing heat, winter storms — without their health giving way.

So it seems to me.

Then wouldn't the best physical training be a close sister of the music we described a moment ago — the simple kind?

Meaning what?

A simple, honest training — and above all, the training for war.

How does that go?

You could learn it from Homer, I said. You know that when his heroes are on campaign he doesn't feast them on fish — though they're camped on the Hellespont, right beside the sea — or on boiled meat, only roasted, which is what soldiers can manage most easily: a fire is nearly always to hand, and it beats hauling pots and pans around.

True.

And Homer, I think I'm right in saying, never once mentions sweet sauces. Every athlete in training knows that much, in fact: a body that means to be in condition keeps away from all such things.

They know it, he said, and they're right to keep away.

Then you won't approve of Syracusan dining, my friend, or Sicilian fancy cooking, if all this seems right to you.

I won't.

Then you'll also frown on a Corinthian mistress for a man who means to keep his body in shape.

Absolutely.

And on those famous delights of Athenian pastry.

Necessarily.

Because all this kind of eating and living, I think, we'd rightly compare to the songs written in every scale and every rhythm at once.

Exactly.

There, elaborateness bred indiscipline; here, it breeds disease. Plainness in music breeds self-command in souls; plainness in physical training breeds health in bodies.

Very true, he said.

And when indiscipline and disease multiply in a city, don't the law courts and the clinics throw their doors wide? Don't law and medicine start to swagger, once even large numbers of free men are taking them terribly seriously?

It can hardly go otherwise.

And can you find better proof that a city's education is bad and shameful than this — that first-rate doctors and judges are needed not just by tradesmen and the poor, but by the very people who boast they were raised as free men? Isn't it disgraceful — doesn't it argue a stunning lack of cultivation — to be forced to import your justice from other men, as your lords and judges, because you're bankrupt of your own?

The most disgraceful thing of all, he said.

Are you sure it's the most? I said. What about this, which is worse: a man who not only spends most of his life in the courts as defendant and plaintiff, but is vulgar enough to preen himself on it — proud of being slick at wrongdoing, able to twist through every turn, to wriggle out through every loophole, bending like a willow shoot to slip past justice — and all for the sake of small, worthless stakes. He has no idea how much finer and better it is to arrange your life so that you never need a drowsing juror at all.

You're right, he said — that is worse.

And to need medicine, I said — not for a wound, or one of the seasonal sicknesses that visit everyone, but because idleness and the regimen we described have filled a man with gases and humors like a swamp, forcing the clever sons of Asclepius to invent names like 'flatulence' and 'catarrh' for his diseases — doesn't that strike you as a disgrace?

It does, he said. Those really are strange, newfangled names for diseases.

The kind of thing, I said, that didn't exist, I suspect, in Asclepius's own day. I take my evidence from Troy: when Eurypylus was wounded, and a woman gave him Pramnian wine thickly sprinkled with barley meal and grated cheese — a recipe you'd call inflammatory — the sons of Asclepius found no fault with the girl who served it, and none with Patroclus, who was managing his treatment.

It certainly was a strange drink, he said, for a man in that state.

Not if you remember, I said, that the old guild of Asclepius — before Herodicus, as they say — didn't practice today's medicine, this system that chaperones diseases through life. Herodicus was a trainer who turned sickly, so he blended training with doctoring — and wore down first and worst himself, and then plenty of others after him.

How? he said.

By making his dying long, I said. He had a fatal disease, and he waited on it hand and foot. Recovery was impossible, so living became nursing: no time for anything else, tortured the moment he strayed from his routine. His expertise made death slow for him — he struggled all the way to old age.

A fine prize his art won him, he said.

The prize you'd expect, I said, for a man who never understood why Asclepius left this branch of medicine untaught to his heirs. It wasn't ignorance or inexperience. It was knowing that in every well-run city each man has one job to do — and no one has the leisure to spend his life being sick and doctoring himself. We see this clearly enough in the working man; absurdly, we can't see it in the rich and supposedly fortunate.

How so? he said.

When a carpenter falls sick, I said, he expects his doctor to hand him a drug to vomit the illness up or purge it out below, or to reach for cautery or the knife and be done with it. If someone instead prescribes him a long regimen — felt caps for his head and all the rest of it — he says straight out that he has no time to be sick, and that there's no profit in living like that, minding a disease while his proper work goes undone. Then he waves that sort of doctor goodbye, goes back to his usual routine, and either recovers and gets on with his business or, if his body can't carry it, dies and is quit of his troubles.

And for a man like that, he said, that seems the right way to use medicine.

Because he has work, I said — and if he can't do it, life doesn't pay?

Obviously.

But the rich man, as we tell it, has no work assigned to him that would make life unlivable if it were taken out of his hands.

None that anyone mentions.

Then you haven't heard Phocylides' saying — that once a man has a living, he should practice virtue.

Even sooner, I'd have thought, he said.

Let's not fight him over that, I said. Let's settle it for ourselves: is this practice the rich man's work, so that life without it is no life at all? Or does nursing a disease, which blocks a man from applying himself to carpentry and the other trades, block the road to Phocylides' advice just as thoroughly?

By Zeus, it does, he said — there's hardly a worse obstacle than this over-tending of the body, this fussing that goes beyond plain physical training.

And it's just as bad, I said, for running a household, an army, or a public office. Worst of all, it makes any kind of study or thought or work on one's own mind nearly impossible. The man is forever suspecting headaches and dizziness and blaming philosophy for causing them — so that wherever virtue is being practiced and tested, this body-coddling blocks the way. It keeps him convinced he is perpetually ill; it keeps him perpetually in labor over his own body.

It would.

So shouldn't we say Asclepius knew all this — that for people whose constitutions and habits were healthy but who came down with some specific ailment, he showed what medicine could do: drugs and surgery to drive the disease out, then the usual regimen, so the city's business wouldn't suffer. But bodies riddled through and through with disease he wouldn't touch. He wouldn't drain and dose by slow degrees to give a man a long, bad life, and let him father children in all likelihood just like himself. A man who couldn't live in his ordinary rounds, Asclepius judged, wasn't worth treating — no profit in him, to himself or to the city.

Asclepius the statesman, he said.

Clearly, I said. And because he was, look at his sons: at Troy they showed themselves good men in the fighting, and they practiced medicine exactly the way I've described. Remember — when Pandarus's arrow wounded Menelaus, they

'sucked out the blood, and sprinkled gentle remedies on the wound,'

but what he should eat or drink afterward they no more prescribed for him than for Eurypylus. The remedies were enough, they judged, to heal men who had been healthy and orderly in their lives before the wound — whatever they happened to be drinking at the moment. But a man diseased in his constitution and undisciplined in his habits — his life was no profit to himself or anyone else; the art was not made for men like that, and they wouldn't treat them if they were richer than Midas.

Statesmen too, he said, these sons of Asclepius — the way you tell it.

As they should be, I said. And yet the tragedians and Pindar won't listen to us: they call Asclepius Apollo's son and then claim that gold bribed him into healing a rich man who was already dying — for which the thunderbolt struck him down. We, keeping to our earlier rule, won't believe them on both counts. If he was a god's son, we'll say, he was not greedy; and if he was greedy, he was no son of a god.

On all that, Socrates, he said, you're right. But here's a question. Doesn't a city need good doctors? And wouldn't the best be the ones who've handled the most bodies, healthy and diseased alike — just as the best judges would be men who've dealt with natures of every kind?

Good ones — I certainly want them, I said. But do you know which ones I count good?

If you'll tell me, he said.

I'll try, I said. But your one question bundled together two things that aren't alike.

How so? he asked.

Doctors, I said, would become most skilled like this: starting in childhood, along with learning their art, they should handle as many bodies as possible, and the most diseased ones — and ideally suffer every illness themselves, and come to the work with less than iron constitutions. Because it isn't body that heals body — if it were, we could never allow a doctor's body to be sick or to fall sick — it's soul that heals body, and a soul that has gone bad, or is bad, can't heal anything well.

Right, he said.

But a judge, my friend, rules soul with soul. And a soul must not be reared from youth among vicious souls, keeping their company, running the whole catalogue of crimes itself so that it can sharply infer other men's wrongdoing from its own — the way a doctor infers from his own body. If a soul is to be fine and good, and judge soundly of what is healthy, it must come to the work with no experience of bad character, uncontaminated by it while it was young. That's exactly why decent people look naive when they're young — easy marks for the unjust: they carry no working models of evil inside themselves.

It's true, he said — it happens to them constantly.

That's why a good judge can't be young, I said. He must be old — a late learner in what injustice is, who studied it not as a resident in his own soul but as a foreign thing in the souls of others, over long years, until he knows exactly what kind of evil it is — by knowledge, not by personal acquaintance.

A judge like that, he said, would be the noblest kind there is.

And a good one, I said — which was your question. A man with a good soul is good. Whereas that other type — the clever, suspicious one, the veteran of his own many crimes, who fancies himself shrewd and wise: among his own kind he looks formidable, because the patterns inside him keep him on guard. But when he comes among good men, older men, he looks a fool instead — suspicious out of season, unable to recognize a sound character, since he has no sound pattern of his own to check it against. Still, since he runs into the bad more often than the good, he passes for wise rather than foolish — in his own eyes, and in the eyes of most other people.

Completely true, he said.

Then that's not the man we want for a good and wise judge — it's the first one. Vice can never know both virtue and itself; but virtue, in a nature educated by time, can come to knowledge of itself and of vice together. The virtuous man, not the bad one — he's the one who becomes wise, as I see it.

As I see it too.

Then this is the medicine, and this the judging, you'll write into law in our city: arts that will care for the citizens who are sound in body and soul. As for the rest — those unsound in body they will let die; those with evil-natured, incurable souls the judges themselves will put to death.

Well, he said, that's clearly best for the ones it happens to — and for the city.

As for your young men, then — trained on that simple music which we said breeds self-command — they'll obviously be wary of ever needing a courtroom.

Clearly.

And the man educated in music, hunting fitness along the same track, will choose to need no medicine except in a real emergency.

So I believe.

Even his exercises and his exertions aim at the spirited part of his nature — that is what he'll be rousing as he trains — not at raw strength. He won't spend his food and toil on muscle like the ordinary athletes.

Exactly right, he said.

Then, Glaucon, the men who established education in music and physical training didn't do it for the reason people think — the one to tend the body, the other the soul.

Then why? he said.

It's likely, I said, that both were established chiefly for the soul.

How so?

Haven't you noticed, I said, what happens to the mind itself when a man spends his whole life on physical training and never touches music? Or when he does the opposite?

Noticed what, exactly?

Savagery and hardness in the one case, I said; softness and over-tameness in the other.

I have noticed, he said. Pure athletes come out cruder than they should be, and pure musicians softer than is good for them.

And yet the savage part is the spirited part of our nature: rightly reared, it would be courage; strained past its pitch, it hardens, as you'd expect, into brutality and bad temper.

So it seems to me.

And gentleness — doesn't that belong to the philosophic nature? Slacken it too far and it goes softer than it should; rear it beautifully and it stays gentle and orderly.

Just so.

Now, we say our guardians need both natures.

They do.

And the two must be tuned to each other?

Beyond question.

And where they are tuned, the soul is self-commanded and brave?

Certainly.

And where they aren't, it is cowardly and coarse?

Very much so.

So: when a man hands himself over to music, and lets it pour into his soul through his ears as through a funnel — those sweet, soft, mournful modes we described a moment ago — and spends his whole life humming, besotted with song, then at first whatever spirit is in him is tempered like iron: made useful, where it had been brittle and useless. But if he keeps at it, never letting up, spellbound — after a while he begins to melt and run, until the spirit has dissolved out of him and the sinews of his soul are cut away, and he is left 'a feeble spearman.'

Very true.

If he started with little spirit by nature, this happens fast. If with a lot, the music weakens his spirit until it's quick-tempered: flaring at trifles, then guttering out just as fast. Instead of spirited, such men come out hot-tempered and irritable — brimming with bad temper.

Exactly so.

Now the reverse: a man who works his body hard and feasts well, and never touches music or philosophy. At first, with his body in high condition, he fills up with confidence and spirit, and becomes braver than he was.

He certainly does.

But what if he does nothing else — never exchanges a word with any Muse? Whatever love of learning was in his soul gets no taste of study or inquiry, no share in reasoned talk or in the rest of music. Doesn't it grow feeble, and deaf, and blind — never wakened, never fed, its perceptions never cleared?

It does, he said.

And such a man ends up hating argument, I think, with no music in him at all. He gives up persuading with words altogether and drives at everything like an animal, with violence and savagery; he lives in ignorance and clumsiness, without rhythm and without grace.

That's exactly how it is.

So for these two parts of us, it seems, I would say some god gave men the two arts — music and physical training — for the spirited part and the philosophic. Not for soul and body, except incidentally, but for those two: so they could be tuned to each other, slackened and tightened until each holds its proper pitch.

So it appears.

Then the man who blends physical training with music in the finest proportion, and brings them to bear on his soul in the truest measure — he's the one we should call the complete musician, the real master of harmony, far more than the fellow who tunes strings against each other.

And with reason, Socrates, he said.

And won't our city always need a supervisor of that kind, if its constitution is going to survive?

It will — as much as it needs anything.

So those are the patterns of their education and upbringing. Why should we spell out their dances too, their hunts with and without hounds, their athletic games and horse races? It's fairly obvious that the details follow the patterns; finding them won't be hard anymore.

Probably not, he said.

Good, I said. Then what do we settle next? Isn't it which of these same people will rule, and which be ruled?

Obviously.

Well, it's plain the rulers should be older and the ruled younger.

Plain enough.

And that the rulers should be the best of them.

That too.

Now, the best farmers — aren't they the ones with the most farming in them?

Yes.

Then since these must be the best of the guardians, won't they be the ones with the most guarding in them?

Yes.

For that, they'll need to be intelligent, and capable, and — above all — to care about the city.

So they will.

And a man cares most for what he loves.

Necessarily.

And he'd love most whatever he believes shares his own interests — the thing whose flourishing he takes for his own flourishing, and whose failure for his own failure.

Just so, he said.

Then we must select, out of all the guardians, the men who strike us most, over a whole lifetime of watching, as doing wholeheartedly whatever serves the city — and as refusing, under any conditions, to do what harms it.

They're the ones we want.

And I think we have to watch them at every age, to see that they guard this conviction — that no spell and no force can make them forget it, or throw overboard the belief that they must do what is best for the city.

What do you mean, throw overboard? he said.

I'll tell you, I said. It seems to me a belief leaves the mind either willingly or unwillingly: willingly, when the belief is false and the man learns better; unwillingly, whenever the belief is true.

The willing kind I understand, he said. The unwilling I still need explained.

Really? I said. Don't you agree that men part with good things unwillingly, and with bad things willingly? And isn't being cheated of the truth a bad thing, and holding the truth a good one? Or don't you count believing things as they are as holding the truth?

You're right, he said — men do lose a true belief against their will.

And when it happens, isn't it by theft, by force, or by bewitchment?

Now I'm lost again, he said.

I must be talking like a tragic chorus, I said — in riddles. By 'the robbed' I just mean men who get argued out of a belief, or who forget it: time lifts it from the one, argument from the other, and they never notice. Now do you see?

Yes.

By 'the forced' I mean the ones that pain or grief drives into changing their minds.

That I understand too, he said. You're right.

And 'the bewitched' — I think you'd say so yourself — are the ones who change their opinion under the sweet spell of pleasure, or the pressure of some fear.

Yes, he said. Everything that deceives, bewitches.

So, as I was just saying, we must find out who are the best guardians of this conviction inside themselves — that at every moment they must do whatever they judge best for the city. We have to watch them from childhood on: set them the tasks in which a man is most likely to forget such a conviction or be tricked out of it, and then select the one who keeps it in memory and stays hard to deceive, and reject the one who doesn't. Agreed?

Yes.

And we should set them labors and pains and contests too, and watch for the same things there.

Rightly so, he said.

Then, I said, we must hold a third kind of trial — against bewitchment — and watch them under it. Like men who lead colts into noise and uproar to find out if they spook, we must take our guardians young into terrors, then plunge them straight into pleasures — testing them harder than gold in the fire — to see whether a man resists the spell, keeps his composure through everything, guards himself well and the music he was raised on, and bears himself with rhythm and harmony in all of it: the kind of man who would be most useful to himself and to the city. And whoever is tested as a boy, as a youth, and as a man, and comes out clean, should be made a ruler of the city and a guardian; he should be given honors while he lives and in death, receiving the greatest prizes we have in burial and remembrance. The one who doesn't come out clean, we set aside. That, Glaucon, I said, is roughly how I think rulers and guardians must be selected and installed — an outline, not the fine print.

I see it much the same way, he said.

And these men are truly what we should call guardians, in the fullest sense: guarding against enemies outside and friends inside — so that the ones will lack the will to do harm, and the others the strength. The young men we've been calling guardians until now are better named auxiliaries, who help carry out what the rulers decide.

I think so too, he said.

Then how, I said, might we contrive one of those lies we spoke of earlier — the lies told at need? One noble lie, to persuade, in the best case, the rulers themselves; and failing that, the rest of the city?

What kind of lie? he said.

Nothing novel, I said — a Phoenician thing. It has happened before in many places, or so the poets say, and the world has believed them; but it hasn't happened in our time, and I doubt it could. It would take a great deal of persuading.

You sound like a man very reluctant to say it, he said.

Once I've said it, I replied, you'll understand the reluctance.

Out with it, he said. Don't be afraid.

Then I'll speak — though I don't know where I'll find the nerve, or the words. I will try to persuade first the rulers themselves and the soldiers, and then the rest of the city, that all the rearing and education we gave them — everything they thought was happening to them — was a kind of dream. In truth, all that time, they were under the ground, being formed and nourished inside the earth — they, their weapons, and the rest of their gear being worked and made. When they were finished, the earth, their mother, sent them up. And so they must treat the land they live on as their mother and their nurse: defend her if anyone attacks, and plan for her good; and they must treat the other citizens as their brothers, born of the same earth.

No wonder, he said, you took so long to bring yourself to tell your lie.

There was good reason, I said. All the same, hear the rest of the story. 'All of you in the city are brothers,' we'll tell them, telling the tale, 'but the god who formed you mixed gold into those of you fit to rule — which is why they carry the greatest honor. Into the auxiliaries he mixed silver, and iron and bronze into the farmers and the other craftsmen. Since you are all one family, you will mostly breed children like yourselves; but sometimes a golden parent will have a silver child, or a silver parent a golden one, and so on, any from any. So the god commands the rulers, first and above all, to keep watch over nothing so carefully as the children — over which metal is mixed into their souls. If their own child is born with bronze or iron in him, they must show no pity: they must assign him the honor his nature is owed, and push him out among the craftsmen or the farmers. And if a child born to those classes carries gold or silver, they must honor that, and raise the one to guard and the other to assist. For there is an oracle that the city is destroyed when a man of iron or bronze stands guard over it.' That's the story. Can you think of any device to make them believe it?

None at all, he said — not these men themselves. But their sons might, and their sons' sons, and the rest of the people who come after.

Even that, I said, would do some good — it would make them care more for the city and for one another. I take your point about how it might spread. But enough: let the story go wherever rumor carries it. Our job now is to arm these earth-born men and march them out with the rulers at their head. Let them come and look for the best campsite in the city — the spot from which they can best control anyone inside who refuses to obey the laws, and beat back anyone outside who comes down on the fold like a wolf. Once camped, let them sacrifice to the right gods and build their sleeping quarters. Yes?

Yes, he said.

Quarters that will keep out winter cold and summer heat, presumably.

Of course, he said. You mean houses.

Yes, I said — soldiers' houses, not businessmen's.

What's the difference? he said.

I'll try to tell you, I said. Surely the most dreadful and shameful thing a shepherd can do is to raise sheepdogs to help with the flock that turn — through indiscipline, or hunger, or some other bad habit — and try their teeth on the sheep themselves, behaving like wolves instead of dogs.

Dreadful, he said. Of course.

Then we must guard by every means against our auxiliaries doing that to the citizens — since they are the stronger — and becoming savage masters instead of friendly allies.

We must.

And wouldn't the best insurance be a truly good education?

But they have that already, he said.

Let's not swear to that yet, dear Glaucon, I said. What we can swear to is what we were just saying: that they must have the right education, whatever it turns out to be, if they're to have the thing that matters most for being gentle with each other and with the people they guard.

That's right.

And besides the education, a thinking man would add this: their housing, and their property in general, should be set up so as never to blunt their excellence as guardians — and never to tempt them into preying on the other citizens.

He'd be right.

Then consider, I said, whether something like this is the way they must live and be housed, if that is what they're to be. First: no private property, none, beyond the barest necessity. Second: no house, no storeroom, that isn't open to anyone who cares to walk in. Their food — everything men need who are athletes of war, self-commanded and brave — they'll receive from the other citizens as a fixed wage for their guarding, measured so that a year leaves them neither surplus nor shortfall. They'll live in common, eating at shared mess tables like soldiers in camp. As for gold and silver, we'll tell them they have the divine kind from the gods, always, in their souls — they have no need of the human kind on top of it; and it would be sacrilege to pollute the divine possession by mixing it with mortal gold, because the coin of the crowd has bred many unholy deeds, and the coin inside them is clean. Alone of the city's people, they are forbidden to handle or touch gold and silver, to be under one roof with it, to wear it, or to drink from it. This is how they'd save themselves and save the city. The moment they own private land and houses and money, they'll be householders and farmers instead of guardians — hostile masters of the other citizens instead of their allies. Hating and hated, plotting and plotted against, they'll spend their whole lives far more afraid of the enemies inside the walls than of the ones outside — and by then they and the rest of the city are already racing toward destruction. For all these reasons, I said, let's say this is how the guardians must be provided for in housing and everything else, and write it into law. Shall we?

By all means, said Glaucon.
