# The Republic, Book X

Poetry and the Myth of Er

And really, I said, among the many things about our city that convince me we built it right, none pleases me more, when I think it over, than our rule about poetry.

Which rule do you mean?

Refusing to admit any poetry that works by imitation. Now that we have distinguished the parts of the soul, I see more clearly than ever that such poetry must not be let in.

What do you mean?

Between ourselves — you won't report me to the tragedians and the rest of the imitative tribe — all that kind of thing seems to me to maim the minds of those who hear it, unless they carry the antidote: knowing what it actually is.

What are you thinking of?

I'll tell you, I said, though a certain love and reverence for Homer, which I have carried since I was a boy, makes the words stick in my throat. He seems to have been the first teacher and captain of this whole splendid tragic company. But no man is to be honored above the truth. So I'll say it.

By all means, he said.

Then listen — or better, answer.

Ask away.

Can you tell me what imitation is, altogether? I don't entirely grasp it myself — what it's supposed to be.

Then what chance, he said, do I have of grasping it?

None too strange a thing. Dim eyes often spot what sharp ones miss.

So they do, he said. But with you here I couldn't bring myself to speak up, even if I did see something. Look for yourself.

Then shall we start our inquiry the usual way? Our practice, you remember, is to posit a single form for each set of many things that we call by the same name. Do you follow me?

I do.

Then take any example you like. There are many beds in the world, and many tables — plenty of them, no?

Yes.

But only two forms for them — one form of the bed, one of the table.

True.

And we usually say that the craftsman who makes either one looks toward the form while he makes the beds or tables we use, and the same with everything else. For no craftsman makes the form itself. How could he?

He couldn't.

Now consider another kind of maker, and tell me what you would call him.

Who is he?

The one who makes everything — every single thing that each of the other craftsmen makes separately.

That's a formidable, marvelous man you're describing.

Wait — you'll say so with better reason in a moment. This same craftsman can make not only furniture of every kind but everything that grows from the earth; he produces every living thing, himself included; and on top of that he makes earth and sky and the gods, everything in heaven and everything under the earth.

An utterly amazing wizard, he said.

You don't believe me? Tell me — do you think there is no such maker at all, or that in one sense a person could make all these things, and in another sense not? Don't you see that there is a way you could make them all yourself?

What way?

An easy one — or rather many, and quick. Quickest of all: take a mirror and carry it around with you everywhere. In no time you'll make the sun and everything in the sky, the earth, yourself, the other animals and plants and furniture — everything we were just talking about.

Yes, he said — appearances, though nothing that really is.

Good, I said — you've come straight to the point. For the painter, I take it, is another maker of just this kind. Isn't he?

Of course.

But you'll say, I suppose, that what he makes isn't real. And yet in a certain way the painter, too, makes a bed. Doesn't he?

Yes, he said — the appearance of one.

And what about the carpenter? Weren't you just saying that he doesn't make the form — which is what we say a bed really is — but only a particular bed?

I was.

Then if he doesn't make what a bed is, he isn't making the real thing — only something like the real thing, which isn't quite it. So if anyone claimed that the work of the carpenter, or of any other craftsman, is completely real, he would probably not be telling the truth.

So it would seem, he said — at least to people who spend their time on arguments like ours.

Then we shouldn't be surprised if the carpenter's bed, too, turns out to be a dim thing next to the truth.

No.

Then shall we use these examples to hunt down what this imitator really is?

If you like.

We have three beds, then. One exists in nature — the bed itself — and I suppose we would say the god made it. Who else could?

No one.

A second is the work of the carpenter.

Yes.

And the painter's is a third?

Yes.

So three beds, and three masters set over them: the god, the carpenter, the painter.

Yes — three.

Now the god, whether by choice or because some necessity was on him, made only that one bed in nature — the one that is what a bed is. Two such beds, or more, he never produced and never will.

Why not?

Because if he made even two, a third would show up behind them — the one whose form both of the others would share — and that one, not the two, would be what a bed is.

Right, he said.

The god knew this, I think, and wanting to be the real maker of the real bed — not some carpenter making some bed — he made it one in nature, and one only.

So it seems.

Shall we call him, then, its maker in nature — something like that?

That would be just, he said, since by nature he has made this and everything else.

And the carpenter? Shall we call him the craftsman of a bed?

Yes.

And the painter — is he a craftsman and maker of a bed too?

Not at all.

Then what will you say he is, in relation to the bed?

The fairest name for him, I think, he said, is imitator — an imitator of what the others make.

Very well, I said. So the man whose product stands third from nature — him you call an imitator?

Exactly, he said.

Then the tragedian too, if he is an imitator, will be someone third in line from the king and from the truth — and so will every other imitator.

It looks that way.

Then we are agreed about the imitator. Now tell me this about the painter. Do you think he tries to imitate the thing itself, there in nature — or the works of the craftsmen?

The works of the craftsmen.

As they are, or as they appear? Draw that distinction too.

How do you mean?

This: a bed, looked at from the side, or head-on, or from any angle you like, doesn't differ from itself at all — it only appears different. And so with everything else.

Just so, he said. It appears different, but isn't.

Then consider this very point. What is painting aimed at, in each case — imitating what is, as it is, or imitating what appears, as it appears? Is it imitation of appearance, or of truth?

Of appearance, he said.

So the art of imitation is a long way from the truth. And that, it seems, is how it can make everything — because it touches only a small part of each thing, and that part an image. The painter, we say, will paint us a cobbler, a carpenter, any craftsman at all, though he understands nothing of their crafts; and yet, if he is a good painter, he might paint a carpenter, show it from a distance, and fool children and foolish men into thinking it is a real carpenter.

Of course.

Then here, my friend, is how we should think about all such cases. When someone tells us he has met a man who knows every craft, and everything else that anyone knows, each thing more precisely than anybody else — the only answer to give is that he is a simple fellow. It seems he has run into some wizard or impersonator and been taken in, and thought the man all-wise because he himself cannot tell knowledge from ignorance from imitation.

Absolutely true, he said.

Then next, I said, we have to examine tragedy and its captain, Homer — since some people tell us these poets know every craft, everything human that touches on virtue and vice, and everything divine besides. A good poet, they say, must know his subject if he is to compose about it well, or he could not compose at all. So we must ask whether these people have run into imitators and been fooled — whether, looking at the poets' works, they fail to notice that these stand at the third remove from what is, and are easy to make without any knowledge of the truth, since they are appearances and not realities. Or is there something in what they say, and do good poets really know the things the crowd thinks they describe so well?

We certainly have to examine it, he said.

Do you think, then, that if a man could make both — the thing imitated and the image — he would seriously give himself over to crafting images, and set that up as the best thing in his life, as if he had nothing better in him?

I do not.

If he truly understood the things he imitates, I imagine he would put his effort into deeds rather than imitations of them. He would try to leave behind many fine works as memorials of himself, and would rather be the man praised than the man writing the praise.

I should think so, he said. The honor and the profit are of a different order.

Then let's not demand an account from Homer or the other poets on most subjects. Let's not ask whether any of them was really a doctor, rather than an imitator of the way doctors talk — whether any poet, old or new, is said to have healed the sick as Asclepius did, or to have left behind students of medicine as he left his sons. Let's not press them about the other crafts either; let those go. But about the greatest and finest things Homer undertakes to speak of — wars, generalship, the founding of cities, the education of a man — about these it is surely fair to question him. 'Dear Homer,' we'll say, 'if you are not third from the truth about virtue, a maker of images — what we defined as an imitator — but even second, and able to tell which pursuits make men better or worse in private and public life, then name one city that was governed better because of you. Sparta has Lycurgus; many cities, great and small, have their own men. Italy and Sicily name Charondas, and we name Solon. Who names you?' Will he have any city to name?

I think not, said Glaucon. Even the Homeridae themselves claim nothing of the sort.

Well, is any war in Homer's day remembered as being won under his command, or by his counsel?

None.

Then is he credited with many ingenious inventions for the crafts or for practical life, the way a man wise in his works would be — like Thales of Miletus or Anacharsis the Scythian?

Nothing of the kind at all.

Then if there is no public service, is Homer said to have been a guide in private to any men who loved his company in his lifetime and handed down a Homeric way of life to those who came after — the way Pythagoras was loved for exactly that, and his followers even now call their way of life Pythagorean and seem set apart by it?

No, nothing like that is told of him, Socrates. Homer's own companion Creophylus would look even more ridiculous for his education than for his name, if the stories about Homer are true. They say Creophylus barely took notice of Homer while the man was alive.

That is the tradition, I said. But Glaucon, if Homer had really been able to educate men and make them better — if he had genuine knowledge of these things and not just a power of imitating them — do you think he wouldn't have made many companions, who honored and loved him? Look at Protagoras of Abdera and Prodicus of Ceos and the rest: each of them can convince the men of his day, in private conversation, that they will never manage their house or their city unless he supervises their education — and for this wisdom they are loved so fiercely that their students all but carry them around on their shoulders. If Homer or Hesiod had really been able to help men toward virtue, would their contemporaries have let them wander from town to town reciting? Wouldn't they have held onto them harder than onto gold, and forced them to stay home? And if the masters wouldn't stay, wouldn't the disciples have followed them anywhere, until they had gotten education enough?

Socrates, he said, I think that is exactly true.

Then shall we settle it: all the poets, beginning with Homer, are imitators of images — of virtue and of everything else they write about — and they never touch the truth. As we were just saying, the painter will make what passes for a cobbler, though he knows nothing of cobbling, for viewers who know nothing of it either and judge by colors and shapes.

Quite so.

In the same way, I suppose, the poet lays down colors of a sort — his words and phrases — over each of the crafts, understanding nothing of them except how to imitate; so that to others like him, who see things only through language, his talk of cobbling or generalship or anything else seems very fine, as long as it comes with meter and rhythm and melody. That is how strong an enchantment these things carry by nature. Strip the poets' works of their musical coloring and say them plain, and you know how they look — you have surely seen it.

I have, he said.

Like the faces of the young who were never beautiful, only fresh — once the bloom has left them.

Exactly.

Then come, consider this. The maker of the image — the imitator, we say — understands nothing of what is, only of what appears. True?

Yes.

Then let's not leave the point half-made; let's see it whole.

Go on.

A painter, we say, will paint reins and a bit?

Yes.

But it is the leatherworker and the smith who will make them.

Certainly.

Then does the painter understand what the reins and the bit ought to be like? Even the makers hardly do — the smith and the leatherworker. Only the man who knows how to use them: the horseman.

Very true.

And won't we say it is the same with everything?

How?

That for each thing there are three arts: one that will use it, one that will make it, one that will imitate it.

Yes.

And the excellence, the beauty, the rightness of every implement and creature and action — don't they all come back to the use for which each was made, or for which nature made it?

That is so.

So it is an absolute necessity that the user of each thing has the most experience of it, and reports to the maker how his product performs, well or badly, in actual use. The flute player, for instance, tells the flute maker which flutes serve him in the playing; he prescribes how they should be made, and the maker obeys him.

Of course.

So the one has knowledge, and pronounces on good and bad flutes; the other trusts him, and makes them accordingly.

Yes.

So for one and the same instrument, the maker will have correct belief about its excellence and its faults — from keeping company with the one who knows, and having to listen to him — while the user has knowledge.

Quite.

And the imitator? Will he have knowledge, from use, of whether the things he paints are fine and right? Or correct opinion, from having to keep company with the one who knows and being told what he should paint?

Neither.

So the imitator will neither know nor rightly judge what he imitates — neither its excellence nor its faults.

Apparently not.

A lovely position for the poetic imitator to be in — so wise about the things he makes.

Not exactly, he said.

And still he will imitate anyway, without knowing what makes each thing good or bad. What he will imitate, it seems, is whatever looks fine to the crowd — to people who know nothing.

What else?

So we are fairly agreed on this much: the imitator knows nothing worth mentioning about what he imitates; imitation is a kind of game, not serious work; and the tragic poets, whether they write in iambics or in epic lines, are imitators to the last degree.

Very true.

Then tell me, by Zeus: this business of imitating is concerned with something at the third remove from the truth. Isn't it?

Yes.

And the power in a human being that it works on — what kind of thing is that?

What sort of power do you mean?

This kind. The same object doesn't look the same size to the eye from close up as it does from far away.

No, it doesn't.

And the same things look bent in water and straight out of it, concave or convex as the eye is misled by colors — clearly there is every kind of confusion of this sort in our soul. It is this weakness in our nature that trick painting preys on, and conjuring, and every sleight of that family; nothing short of sorcery.

True.

And measuring, counting, and weighing have proved to be our most gracious helpers here — so that what rules in us is not the apparent bigger or smaller, the apparent more or heavier, but the part of us that has calculated, measured, weighed.

Certainly.

And that is the work of the reasoning part of the soul.

It is.

But often, when this part has measured, and reports that one thing is bigger or smaller than another, or equal to it, the same soul sees the opposite at the same moment about the same things.

Yes.

And didn't we say it is impossible for the same part to hold opposite judgments about the same things at the same time?

We did, and rightly.

Then the part of the soul that judges against the measurements cannot be the same as the part that judges with them.

No.

And the part that trusts measure and calculation would be the best part of the soul.

Of course.

Then what opposes it is one of the weak things in us.

Necessarily.

This is what I wanted agreed when I said that painting — and imitation as a whole — does its own work far from the truth, and keeps company with the part of us that is far from wisdom: a companionship and friendship with nothing healthy or true about it.

Exactly.

So imitation is a poor thing that mates with a poor thing and breeds poor offspring.

So it seems.

Only the imitation we look at, I said? Or also the kind we listen to — the kind we call poetry?

Probably that too, he said.

Well, let's not lean on 'probably' just because it matches painting. Let's go straight to the very part of the mind that poetic imitation keeps company with, and see whether it is trivial or serious.

Yes, we should.

Put it this way. Imitation, we say, imitates human beings in action — acting under compulsion or by choice, judging that they have done well or badly by how it turns out, and through it all feeling grief or joy. Was there anything beyond that?

Nothing.

And in all of this, is a person of one mind with himself? Or, just as with sight — where he was divided, holding opposite judgments about the same things at the same moment — is he divided in his actions too, at war with himself? But actually I remember: we don't need to settle this now, because in the earlier arguments we settled it well enough — the soul is crowded with ten thousand oppositions of this kind, arising all at once.

Rightly settled, he said.

Rightly, I said. But what we left out then, I think we now have to work through.

What did we leave out?

A decent man, we said, who meets some misfortune — losing a son, or something else he holds most dear — will bear it more easily than other men.

Certainly.

But now consider: will he feel no grief at all? Or, since that is impossible, will he keep some measure in his pain?

The second, he said, is nearer the truth.

Then tell me this about him. Do you think he will fight the grief and resist it more when his equals can see him — or when he is alone with himself, in some empty place?

Far more when he is watched, he said.

Alone, I imagine, he will let himself say much that he would be ashamed to have heard, and do much that he would never let anyone see.

That is so.

Now, what commands him to resist is reason and law; what drags him toward the grief is the suffering itself.

True.

And when opposite pulls arise in a man about the same thing at the same time, we say there must be two things in him.

Of course.

One of them ready to obey the law wherever it leads?

How so?

The law says, roughly, that it is finest to stay as calm as we can in misfortune and not cry out — because the good and evil in such things is not clear; because taking it hard gains nothing for the future; because nothing human deserves great seriousness; and because grief blocks the very thing we need to arrive quickest of all.

What thing? he asked.

Deliberation, I said. We should think about what has happened, and — as at a throw of the dice — arrange our affairs to suit the throw, in whatever way reason judges best. Not like children who have taken a fall, clutching the hurt place and spending our time howling; we should train the soul to turn as fast as it can to healing and setting upright what fell and what sickened — letting medicine drive out lamentation.

That would certainly be the rightest way to face fortune, he said.

And the best part of us is willing to follow this reasoning.

Clearly.

While the part that pulls us back to the memory of the suffering, back to the wailing — the part that can never get enough of it — won't we call that unreasoning, idle, a friend of cowardice?

We will.

Now, the fretful part of us offers rich and varied material for imitation. But the wise and steady character, always nearly the same as itself, is not easy to imitate — nor, when imitated, easy to understand, least of all for a festival crowd, all sorts of people packed into a theater. What is being imitated is an experience foreign to them.

Absolutely.

Then plainly the imitative poet is not made for this reasoning part of the soul, and his skill is not set on pleasing it — not if he wants fame with the many. He is made for the fretful, changeable character, because it is easy to imitate.

Plainly.

So at this point we can fairly take the poet and stand him beside the painter. He resembles him in making things that are worthless by the measure of truth, and he resembles him in this too: he consorts with a part of the soul that is not the best. So we are now justified in refusing to admit him into any city that is to be well governed — because he wakes this part of the soul, feeds it, makes it strong, and destroys the reasoning part. Just as in a city, when someone strengthens the vicious and hands the state to them, betraying the better sort — the imitative poet, we will say, does the same thing: he plants a bad constitution in the private soul of each man, gratifying the part that has no judgment, the part that cannot tell larger from smaller but calls the same things now big, now little — an image-maker whose images stand a very long way from the truth.

Exactly.

But we haven't yet brought the heaviest charge against poetry. Its power to damage even good men — with very few exceptions — is surely the frightening thing.

Frightening indeed, if it really does that.

Listen, and judge. When the best of us hear Homer, or one of the tragedians, imitating some hero in grief, stretching out a long speech of lamentation, or singing and beating his breast — you know how we enjoy it. We give ourselves over; we follow, suffering along with him; and in all seriousness we praise as a fine poet whoever does this to us most.

I know it. Of course.

But when private grief comes to one of us, you notice we pride ourselves on the opposite — on staying calm and bearing it — as if that were a man's part, and the thing we praised in the theater a woman's.

I do notice it, he said.

Then is that praise really in order? Looking at a man behave as we would refuse — would be ashamed — to behave ourselves, and instead of being disgusted, delighting in it and praising it?

No, by Zeus, he said — it doesn't look reasonable.

Yes it does, I said — if you look at it this way.

Which way?

Consider it like this. The part of us that in our own misfortunes is held down by force — the part that has hungered for tears, for a good long cry, for its fill of grief, because that is what it naturally desires — this is exactly the part the poets fill up and delight. And our best part, insufficiently trained by reason and habit, slackens its watch over this mournful part, on the grounds that the sufferings it is watching belong to someone else; there is no shame, it thinks, in praising and pitying another man who calls himself good while he grieves without measure — and the pleasure feels like pure gain, not to be thrown away by despising the whole poem. Only a few, I think, ever reckon that what we take in from the feelings of others comes home to our own — that after feeding the pity there until it is strong, it is not easy to hold it down in our own sufferings.

Very true, he said.

And doesn't the same argument hold for laughter? There are jokes you would be ashamed to make yourself, yet in a comic imitation — or in private — they delight you enormously, and you don't hate them as vulgar. Isn't this the same thing as with pity? The part of you that wants to play the clown, which you used to hold back by reason for fear of being thought a buffoon, you let loose there; and having made it frisky in the theater, you are carried off unawares, and end up the comedian at home.

Exactly so, he said.

And with sex, and anger, and every appetite and pain and pleasure in the soul — the ones we say follow every action of ours — poetic imitation works the same effects on us. It waters them when they should be left to dry; it sets them to rule over us when they should be ruled, so that we might become better and happier instead of worse and more wretched.

I can't say otherwise, he said.

Then, Glaucon, when you run into men who praise Homer — who say this poet educated Greece, that for the management and refinement of human life he is worth taking up and studying, your whole life arranged according to him — you should love them and greet them as the best people they know how to be, and grant them that Homer is the most poetic of poets and first of the tragedians. But hold fast to this: the only poetry to be admitted into our city is hymns to the gods and praises of good men. Once you let in the honeyed Muse, in lyric or in epic, pleasure and pain will be kings in your city, in place of law and of the reasoning that the community in each case judges best.

Very true, he said.

So let this be our defense, now that we have come back to the subject of poetry: given her nature, we were reasonable to banish her from the city then. The argument compelled us. And let us say to her — so she can't accuse us of harshness or bad manners — that there is an ancient quarrel between philosophy and poetry. There are the old taunts: 'the yelping bitch shrieking at her master,' and 'great in the empty talk of fools,' and 'the mob of over-wise heads that rules,' and 'the subtle thinkers who are beggars after all' — countless signs of the old feud. Even so, let it be said that if poetry made for pleasure, imitative poetry, can give any argument that she belongs in a well-governed city, we would be delighted to take her back. We know her spell on us first-hand. But it would be impious to betray what we hold true. Come, my friend — doesn't she charm you too, especially when you watch her through Homer?

Very much, he said.

Then it is just to let her come home from exile on these terms: that she plead her own defense, in lyric or whatever meter she likes.

By all means.

And we would allow her champions too — those who aren't poets themselves but love poetry — to speak for her in prose: to argue that she is not only sweet but useful, to cities and to human life. And we will listen kindly. We stand to gain, surely, if she turns out to be not only pleasant but good for us.

Of course we stand to gain, he said.

But if not, my dear friend — then like men who have fallen in love, and who judge the love is doing them no good, and force themselves away from it — we will do the same. Reared as we are in the love of this kind of poetry by the upbringing our fine cities give, we will be glad for her to prove as good and true as can be; but as long as she cannot make her defense, we will listen to her while chanting this argument of ours to ourselves as a counter-charm, taking care not to fall back into the boyish passion for her that most men never leave. One thing we know: such poetry is not to be taken seriously, as if it were a serious thing laying hold of truth. The listener must be on his guard against her, fearing for the city inside himself, and must hold to what we have said about poetry.

I fully agree, he said.

Yes, dear Glaucon — because the stakes are great, greater than they look: whether a man becomes good or bad. So neither honor nor money nor any office — no, and not poetry either — is worth neglecting justice for, and the rest of virtue with it.

The argument has convinced me, he said. I think it would convince anyone.

And yet, I said, we haven't even mentioned the greatest wages of virtue, or the prizes waiting for it.

Then they must be unimaginably great, he said, if they are greater than the ones we've named.

What could ever grow great in a little time? I said. And this whole span from childhood to old age is little, next to all of time.

It is nothing at all, he said.

Well then: should an immortal thing take this little interval so seriously — instead of the whole?

The whole, of course, he said. But why do you ask?

Haven't you realized, I said, that our soul is immortal, and is never destroyed?

He looked at me, astonished. By Zeus, no, he said. Are you actually prepared to claim that?

I am — or I'd be in the wrong. And so could you. It isn't hard.

It is for me, he said. But I would gladly hear from you this thing that isn't hard.

Then listen.

Just speak.

You call something good, and something evil?

I do.

And do you think of them the way I do — evil is everything that destroys and corrupts, good is what preserves and benefits?

Yes.

And each thing has its own evil and its own good? Eye disease for the eyes, sickness for the whole body, blight for grain, rot for timber, rust for bronze and iron — for almost everything, as I say, its own natural evil and its own sickness.

Yes.

And when one of these evils gets into a thing, it makes what it touches bad — and in the end dissolves the whole of it and destroys it.

Of course.

So each thing is destroyed by its own natural evil and its own badness; and if that doesn't destroy it, nothing else will. The good will never destroy anything, and neither will what is neither good nor evil.

How could they?

Then if we find something that has an evil which makes it worse, but which cannot dissolve it or destroy it — won't we know at once that a thing of such a nature cannot be destroyed at all?

That seems likely.

Well then — does the soul have something that makes it evil?

Very much so, he said — everything we were just going through: injustice, self-indulgence, cowardice, ignorance.

Then does any of these dissolve and destroy it? Think carefully here, so we aren't tricked into supposing that when the unjust and foolish man is caught, he has been destroyed by his injustice — by badness of soul. Do it this way instead. Disease, the badness of the body, wastes a body, dissolves it, brings it to the point of not being a body at all; and everything we listed just now is brought to nonexistence by its own particular evil settling into it and inhabiting it. Isn't that so?

Yes.

Then look at the soul the same way. When injustice and the rest of vice inhabit it, do they — by settling in and dwelling there — waste the soul and wither it, until they bring it to death and split it from the body?

That they never do, he said.

But surely it is unreasonable that a thing could be destroyed by the badness of something else, when its own badness cannot destroy it.

Unreasonable, he said.

Think, Glaucon: we don't suppose the body has to be destroyed by the badness of food itself — staleness, rottenness, whatever it might be. If the food's own badness breeds bodily evil in the body, we will say the body was destroyed through the food, but by its own evil — by disease. But by the badness of food, which is one thing, the body, which is another, will never be destroyed — not unless that alien evil implants the body's own natural evil in it.

That is exactly right, he said.

By the same argument, then: unless the body's badness breeds the soul's own badness in the soul, let's never suppose the soul is destroyed by an alien evil apart from its own — one thing by another thing's evil.

That makes sense, he said.

Then either let us refute this argument — or, as long as it stands unrefuted, let us never say that fever, or any other disease, or the knife at the throat, or chopping the whole body into the smallest possible pieces, brings a soul one step nearer to being destroyed — not until someone proves that through these sufferings of the body the soul itself becomes more unjust and more unholy. As long as it is an alien evil arising in a thing, and its own evil is absent, let no one be allowed to say that a soul, or anything else, is destroyed.

Well, he said, no one will ever prove that — that the souls of the dying become more unjust because of death.

But suppose someone dares to close with the argument, and says that the dying man does become worse and more unjust — just so he won't be forced to admit that souls are immortal. We will hold, I suppose, that if he is telling the truth, injustice must be deadly to its possessor, like a disease: the thing itself kills those who catch it, the worst cases fastest, the milder more slowly — instead of what happens now, where the unjust die of injustice only because other people execute them for it.

By Zeus, he said, then injustice would turn out not so terrible after all, if it were fatal to whoever caught it — that would be a release from troubles. But I think it will prove the exact opposite: a thing that kills other people if it can, while making its owner positively lively — lively, and wide awake besides. So far from being deadly, it has pitched its camp a long way from death.

Well said, I replied. For when the soul's own badness, its own evil, cannot kill or destroy it, then an evil assigned to destroy something else will hardly destroy a soul — or anything except the thing it was assigned to.

Hardly — so it seems, at least.

Then since it is not destroyed by any evil at all, its own or another's, clearly it must be something that always exists. And if it always exists, it is immortal.

Necessarily.

Let that stand, then. And if it does, you see that the souls will always be the same souls. They can't grow fewer, since none is destroyed — and they can't grow more numerous either. For if anything immortal increased, you know its increase would have to come out of what is mortal, and in the end everything would be immortal.

True.

But we won't believe that — the argument won't permit it — and we won't believe either that the soul in its truest nature is a thing packed with variety, unlikeness, and difference from itself.

How do you mean? he said.

It isn't easy, I said, for a thing to be everlasting when it is composite — put together out of many parts — and not put together in the finest way; yet that is how the soul appeared to us just now.

No, probably not.

That the soul is immortal, then, this argument and the others would force us to admit. But to know what it is like in truth, you must see it not as we see it now, maimed by its partnership with the body and by other evils, but as it is when it has become pure — that you must gaze at, and thoroughly, with reasoning. You will find it a far more beautiful thing, and you will see through to justice and injustice and everything we have gone through. What we have said so far is true of the soul as it appears at present. But we have been looking at it the way men look at the sea-god Glaucus: they can hardly make out his original nature, because the old parts of his body have been broken off or ground down and altogether disfigured by the waves, and other things have grown onto him — shells and seaweed and stones — so that he looks more like any beast at all than what he naturally was. That is the state we see the soul in, disfigured by ten thousand evils. But we must look elsewhere, Glaucon.

Where?

At its love of wisdom. Notice what the soul reaches for, what company it craves — kin as it is to the divine and immortal and everlasting — and what it would become if it followed that kinship whole and entire, lifted by the impulse out of the sea it now lives in, the stones and shells knocked away: all that wild, earthy overgrowth crusted onto it from the so-called happy feasting here, because it feeds on earth. Then you would see its true nature — whether it has many forms or one, and how it stands. But for now, I think, we have described well enough what happens to it in human life, and the shapes it wears.

We certainly have, he said.

Well then, I said. We have cleared the rest of the case, and we haven't invoked the wages or reputations of justice, as you said Hesiod and Homer do. We found justice itself best for the soul itself — found that the soul should do what is just whether or not it owns the ring of Gyges, and the cap of Hades on top of the ring.

Very true.

Then, Glaucon, can there be any objection now to our giving back to justice and the rest of virtue their wages too — all the wages, in their number and kind, that they win for the soul from men and from gods, while a man still lives and after he has died?

None whatever, he said.

Then will you give me back what you borrowed in the argument?

What, exactly?

I granted you that the just man could seem unjust, and the unjust man just. You asked for it: even if no one could actually hide the truth from gods and men, still it should be conceded for argument's sake, so that justice itself could be judged against injustice itself. Don't you remember?

It would be unjust of me not to, he said.

Well, they have now been judged — so on behalf of justice I demand back her reputation. She must be given the esteem she actually enjoys with gods and with men, so that we agree she also carries off the prizes of appearing — the prizes she wins by seeming, and gives to those who hold her — now that she has clearly given the goods of being, and does not cheat those who truly take hold of her.

A just demand, he said.

Then first give this back: neither of them is hidden from the gods — what each of the two really is.

We give it back.

And if neither is hidden, then one is loved by the gods and one is hated — as we agreed at the very start.

That is so.

And won't we agree that everything that comes from the gods to the man they love comes as the best it can be — unless some evil was already owed him from an earlier fault?

Certainly.

Then this is how we must think about the just man: whether poverty comes to him, or sickness, or any other seeming evil, it will end in some good for him, living or dead. For the gods never neglect a man whose heart is set on becoming just — on becoming, through the practice of virtue, as much like a god as a human being can.

It stands to reason, he said, that such a man is not neglected by what he resembles.

And of the unjust man we should think the opposite?

Emphatically.

From the gods, then, these are the victor's prizes for the just man.

That is certainly my view, he said.

And from men? What does he get there? Isn't the truth of it this, if we are to say what actually happens: your clever unjust men are like runners who run the first leg well but not the return — off the mark at a sprint, but by the finish they look like fools, ears drooping on their shoulders as they slink off uncrowned. True runners come in at the end, take the prizes, and wear the crown. And isn't that how it usually goes with the just? Toward the end of every action, every partnership, and of life itself, they carry the honors and take the prizes men have to give.

Very much so.

Then will you bear it if I say about the just what you yourself said about the unjust? This is what I'll say: the just, when they are older, hold office in their own city if they want office; they marry where they choose, and give their children in marriage where they please — everything you said of the one lot, I now say of the other. And the unjust — most of them, even if they go unnoticed while they are young, get caught at the end of the race and are laughed at; by the time they are old they are miserable, insulted by foreigners and citizens alike, whipped — and then everything you rightly called too savage for polite ears: they will be racked, their eyes will be burned out. Consider the whole catalogue recited; I have repeated it. See whether you will bear with me.

Easily, he said. What you say is just.

So these are the prizes, the wages, the gifts the just man receives while he lives, from gods and from men — over and above those goods that justice provided of herself.

And they are beautiful and lasting things, he said.

Yet all of that, I said, is nothing in number or size next to what awaits each of the two after death. And it ought to be heard, so that each of them can take away, in full, what the argument owes him.

Then speak, he said. There are few things I would rather hear.

Well, I said, it isn't the long tale told to Alcinous that I'm going to tell you, but the tale of a brave man — Er the son of Armenius, a Pamphylian by birth. He was killed in battle. On the tenth day, when the dead were taken up already decaying, he was taken up whole; he was carried home for burial, and on the twelfth day, laid out on the pyre, he came back to life — and once alive again, he told what he had seen there. He said that when his soul went out of him, it traveled with a great company until they came to an uncanny place, where two openings stood in the earth, side by side, and opposite them, above, two others in the sky. Judges sat between. When they had passed judgment, they ordered the just to take the right-hand road, upward through the sky, hanging signs of the judgment on their chests; and the unjust the left-hand road, downward, wearing on their backs the signs of everything they had done. When Er himself came forward, they told him he was to be a messenger to mankind of the things in that place, and they charged him to hear and watch everything there. So he watched: through one opening of the sky and one of the earth, the souls departing after judgment; and out of the other two, souls returning — from the earth, dusty and caked with the road; from the sky, clean and bright. The souls kept arriving, looking glad to be done with a long journey, and they turned aside into the meadow as if to a festival and camped there. Those who knew one another embraced, and they traded questions — the ones from the earth asking about the things above, the ones from heaven about the things below. The souls from under the earth wept and wailed as they told what they had suffered and seen on their journey beneath the ground — a journey of a thousand years — while the souls from heaven told of joys, and of sights beautiful past telling. The whole account, Glaucon, would take a long time; but the sum of it, he said, was this. For every wrong a soul had ever done, and every person it had wronged, it paid the penalty in turn, ten times over for each — that is, once every hundred years, a hundred years being the length of a human life — so that the price of each wrong was paid tenfold across the thousand years. If, say, men had caused many deaths, betraying cities or armies, or throwing others into slavery, or had a hand in any other crime, they suffered ten pains for every one of them; and if they had done good deeds and been just and holy, they carried off rewards in the same proportion. About those who died at birth or lived only a short while, he said other things not worth recording. And for piety and impiety toward gods and parents, and for murder by one's own hand, he described wages greater still. He said he was standing there when one soul asked another, 'Where is Ardiaeus the Great?' This Ardiaeus had been tyrant in a city of Pamphylia a thousand years before: he had killed his old father and his elder brother, and done many other unholy things, so the story went. And the soul who was asked said: 'He has not come. He will not come. For this was one of the terrible sights we saw. When we were near the mouth, about to come up with all the rest of it behind us, suddenly we saw him — Ardiaeus, and others with him, most of them tyrants, though there were private men among them too, great sinners. They thought they were finally about to go up, and the mouth would not take them: it bellowed whenever one of the incurables, or anyone who had not paid in full, tried to ascend. Wild men stood by there, fiery to look at, who understood the sound; and they seized some and carried them off — but Ardiaeus and the others they bound hand and foot and head, threw them down, flayed them, and dragged them along the roadside, carding them on thorn-bushes like wool, telling everyone who passed why this was done, and that they were being taken to be hurled into Tartarus.' And of all the many fears they had felt there, he said, this one rose above them all: that the mouth would bellow when each of them went up. When it stayed silent, each climbed out with utter joy. Such were the penalties and punishments, he said — and the blessings were their equal and opposite.

Now when the souls in the meadow had each spent seven days there, on the eighth they had to rise and march; and on the fourth day out, he said, they reached a place from which they could see a straight light stretched from above through the whole of heaven and earth, like a pillar — most like the rainbow, but brighter and purer. Another day's march brought them into it, and there, at the middle of the light, they saw the ends of its bindings stretched down from heaven. For this light is what ties heaven together: as the undergirding cables hold a trireme, so it holds the whole turning frame of the universe in one. And from the ends hangs the spindle of Necessity, by which all the revolutions are turned. Its shaft and hook are of adamant, and its whorl of adamant mixed with other stuffs. The whorl's nature is this. In shape it is like an ordinary whorl here on earth; but from his account you must picture one great hollow whorl, scooped clean through, and a second, smaller one fitted snugly inside it — like those jars that nest into one another — then a third inside that, and a fourth, and four more. Eight whorls in all, set one within another, their rims showing from above as circles, and forming together the continuous back of one single whorl around the shaft, which is driven straight through the center of the eighth. The first, outermost whorl has the broadest rim; the sixth is second in breadth, the fourth third, the eighth fourth, the seventh fifth, the fifth sixth, the third seventh, and the second last. The rim of the largest is spangled; the seventh is brightest; the eighth takes its color from the seventh's light falling on it; the second and fifth are like each other, and yellower than the others; the third carries the whitest light; the fourth is reddish; the sixth is second in whiteness. Now the whole spindle revolves as one; but within the whole as it turns, the seven inner circles wheel slowly the opposite way. Of these the eighth moves fastest; next the seventh, sixth, and fifth, keeping pace together; third in speed, it appeared to them, moved the fourth, in this counter-wheeling course; fourth came the third, and fifth the second. And the spindle turns on the knees of Necessity. On top of each circle rides a Siren, carried around with it, sounding one single note, one pitch; and all eight notes together make a single harmony. Around them, at equal distances, three others are seated, each on a throne: the Fates, daughters of Necessity, robed in white with garlands on their heads — Lachesis, Clotho, and Atropos — singing to the Sirens' harmony: Lachesis of what has been, Clotho of what is, Atropos of what is to come. Clotho, from time to time, lays her right hand on the outer rim of the spindle and helps turn it; Atropos with her left hand does the same for the inner circles; and Lachesis takes hold of each in turn, first with one hand, then with the other.

When they arrived, they had to go straight to Lachesis. And first a kind of spokesman arranged them in order; then he took from the knees of Lachesis lots and patterns of lives, climbed onto a high platform, and spoke: 'The word of Lachesis, maiden daughter of Necessity. Souls of a day, here begins another round of death-bearing life for mortal kind. No guardian spirit will draw you by lot; you will choose your guardian spirit. Let the one who draws the first lot be first to choose a life — a life that will then be his by necessity. Virtue has no master: as each honors her or dishonors her, he will have more of her or less. The blame belongs to the chooser. God is blameless.' With that he flung the lots out over them all, and each soul picked up the one that fell beside it — all except Er, who was not allowed. And as each picked up its lot, it saw what number it had drawn. Next the spokesman set out on the ground before them the patterns of lives — far more than the souls present, and of every kind: lives of every animal, and every human life. There were tyrannies among them, some running the full term, others cut down midway, ending in poverty and exile and beggary. There were lives of famous men — famous for beauty of form, for strength and victory in the games, or for their birth and the virtues of their ancestors — and lives of men obscure for the opposite reasons; and lives of women, the same way. But no rank of soul was fixed in them, since a soul necessarily becomes different according to the life it chooses. Everything else was there, mixed together — wealth with poverty, sickness with health — and the middle conditions too. And here, dear Glaucon, it seems, is the whole danger for a human being. This is why each of us must set aside every other study and care most for this one: to seek and find whether there is anyone who can give him the power and the knowledge to tell a good life from a bad one — anyone to learn it from — and to choose, always and everywhere, the best life that is possible. He must reckon what everything we have said tonight, taken together and taken separately, contributes to virtue in a life: what beauty does when mixed with poverty or wealth, and joined to which condition of soul; what noble and humble birth do, private station and public office, strength and weakness, quickness and slowness of mind, and everything of that sort the soul has by nature or takes on — what they work when combined with one another. Out of all this he will be able to reason, keeping his eye on the nature of the soul, and to choose between the worse life and the better — calling worse whatever leads the soul toward becoming more unjust, and better whatever leads it toward becoming more just. Everything else he will let go. For we have seen that this is the sovereign choice, for a man living and for a man dead. And he must go down to the house of the dead holding this conviction hard as adamant, so that there too he will not be dazzled by wealth and evils of that kind, will not stumble into tyrannies and doings of that sort — working many incurable wrongs on others, and suffering worse himself — but will know how to choose, always, the life that keeps the middle way, fleeing the extremes on either side, in this world as far as he can and in all the life to come. For this is how a human being becomes happiest.

And then, according to the messenger from that world, the spokesman spoke as follows: 'Even for the last to come forward, if he chooses with sense and lives with effort, a life worth loving is laid out here — not a bad one. Let the first chooser not be careless, and let the last not lose heart.' The moment he had spoken, Er said, the man who drew the first lot strode up and chose the greatest tyranny. Out of folly and greed he took it without examining it all, and missed the fate folded inside it — that he was destined, among other horrors, to eat his own children. When he studied it at leisure, he beat his breast and wailed over his choice, forgetting everything the spokesman had proclaimed; for instead of blaming himself for his troubles, he blamed fortune, and the spirits, and everything rather than himself. He was one of those who had come down from heaven — a man who had lived his former life in an orderly city, and had taken his share of virtue by habit, without philosophy. Indeed, broadly speaking, more than half of those caught out this way were of the company come down from heaven, because they had never been trained by suffering; while the ones up from the earth, who had suffered themselves and watched others suffer, were in no rush to make their choice. Because of this — and because of the luck of the lot — most of the souls traded a good destiny for an evil one, or an evil for a good. Yet if a man, every time he entered this life, practiced philosophy soundly, and the lot for choosing did not fall to him among the very last, then, from what Er reported, the odds are fair not only that he would be happy here, but that his journey from here to there and back again would run not underground and rough, but smooth, and through heaven. The sight itself, he said, was worth seeing — how the several souls chose their lives — pitiable to watch, and laughable, and astonishing. For the most part they chose by the habits of their former life. He saw the soul that had once been Orpheus choosing the life of a swan: hatred of womankind, because women had been his death, made him unwilling to be conceived and born of a woman. He saw the soul of Thamyras choosing a nightingale's life; and a swan changing over to choose a human life, and other musical animals doing the same. The soul that drew the twentieth lot chose the life of a lion: it was the soul of Ajax the son of Telamon, who shunned becoming a man because he remembered the judgment of the arms. After him came Agamemnon: he too hated the human race because of what he had suffered, and exchanged it for the life of an eagle. The soul of Atalanta had drawn a lot toward the middle: she caught sight of the great honors of a male athlete's life, and could not pass them by, and took it. After her he saw the soul of Epeus, son of Panopeus, entering the nature of a craftswoman; and far off among the last, the soul of the clown Thersites putting on an ape. And as chance had it, the soul of Odysseus had drawn the last lot of all, and came forward to choose. The memory of his old labors had cured him of ambition, and he went around a long time looking for the life of a private man who minds his own business — and found it at last, lying somewhere, passed over by everyone else. When he saw it, he said he would have done the same if his lot had come first, and he chose it gladly. And it went the same way with the animals: they passed into men and into one another — the unjust changing into the wild kinds, the just into the tame — mixed in every combination.

When all the souls had chosen their lives, they went before Lachesis in the order of their lots; and she sent with each the guardian spirit it had chosen, to watch over the life and fulfill the choice. The spirit led the soul first to Clotho — under her hand as it turned the whirling spindle — ratifying there the destiny it had drawn and chosen; and after touching her, the spirit led it on to the spinning of Atropos, who made the threads unalterable. From there, without turning back, each soul passed beneath the throne of Necessity; and when all had come through, they marched together to the plain of Forgetting, through terrible, stifling heat — for the plain is bare of trees, bare of everything the earth grows. Evening was coming on, and they camped beside the river of Heedlessness, whose water no vessel can hold. All of them were required to drink a measure of that water; and those not saved by good sense drank past the measure; and as each one drank, he forgot everything. When they had gone to sleep, at midnight there came thunder and an earthquake, and suddenly they were swept up and away, this way and that, to their births, like shooting stars. Er himself had been prevented from drinking the water. But how and by what way he came back to his body, he could not say: he simply opened his eyes at dawn and found himself lying on the pyre.

And that, Glaucon, is how the tale was saved and not lost. And it can save us, if we believe it: then we will cross the river of Forgetting safely, and our soul will not be stained. So here is my counsel: let us hold forever to the upward road, and practice justice with wisdom in every way — believing the soul immortal, and able to endure every evil and every good — so that we may be friends to ourselves and to the gods, both while we remain here and when we carry off the prizes justice wins, like victors in the games making their round to gather the gifts. And both here, and on the thousand-year journey we have described, may we fare well.
