The Republic, Book VI

The Sun and the Divided Line

Book VI6 of 10

So there, Glaucon: it took a long stretch of argument, and some struggle, but the true philosophers and the false have finally shown themselves for what they are.

I doubt the road could have been any shorter, he said.

Probably not, I said. Though I think we would have seen them both more clearly if this had been the only subject before us — if there weren't so many questions still waiting, which anyone who wants to see how the just life differs from the unjust has to work through.

So what comes next? he asked.

Whatever follows in order, I said. Since philosophers are the ones able to grasp what always holds the same, unchanging, while those who cannot — who wander lost among the many things that shift and vary — are not philosophers, the question is this: which of the two should lead our city?

And how do we answer that sensibly?

Whichever of them proves able to guard the laws and the ways of the city — make those the guardians.

Good.

And here's a question that answers itself, I said: should a watchman set to guard anything have sharp eyes, or be blind?

It answers itself.

Then what about people who genuinely lack knowledge of what each thing is — who carry no clear pattern of it in their soul, and cannot do what a painter does: fix their eyes on what is most true, refer back to it constantly, study it as exactly as possible, and only then lay down the standards of the beautiful, the just, and the good here below — laying down new ones where needed, and standing guard to preserve the ones already in place? Aren't such people, frankly, blind?

Honestly, he said, that's more or less their condition.

Then shall we make them our guardians — when there are others available who know what each thing truly is, who match them in experience, and who fall short of them in no part of virtue?

It would be absurd to pass over men who have that — the greatest advantage of all. They should hold first place, unless they fail somewhere else.

Then let's spell out, I said, how the same men can have both — the knowledge and the other qualities.

By all means.

First, as we said at the start of all this, we have to grasp the philosopher's nature. Once we agree on that, I think we'll also agree that the combination is possible — and that no one else should lead a city but these.

How so?

Let's agree on this much about philosophic natures: they are always in love with any learning that shows them the being that always is — the reality that doesn't drift about between birth and decay.

Agreed.

And further, that they love the whole of it, and won't willingly give up any part, small or large, more honored or less — just as we said before about lovers and about men who chase honors.

True.

Now consider: if they're going to be what we described, mustn't there be something else in their nature too?

What?

Truthfulness — a refusal ever to accept a lie into themselves willingly. They hate the false and love the true.

That seems a safe thing to say of them.

Not just safe, my friend — necessary. A man in love with anything by nature has to love everything that belongs to it, everything akin to what he loves.

Right, he said.

And could you find anything more akin to wisdom than truth?

How could there be?

Then can the same nature love wisdom and love the false?

Never.

So the genuine lover of learning must, from earliest youth, reach for truth of every kind, with everything in him?

Absolutely.

But we also know from experience that when a man's desires run strongly in one channel, they run weaker everywhere else — like a stream diverted into a single bed.

Of course.

So when a man's desires flow toward learning and everything like it, he'll be absorbed in the pleasure of the soul itself, by itself, and let the body's pleasures fall away — if he's a true philosopher and not a counterfeit one.

That follows necessarily.

A man like that will have self-command, and be the last man alive to be greedy. The reasons other people chase money and spend it grandly have no purchase on him.

Very true.

And there's something else to check when you're sorting the philosophic nature from the unphilosophic.

What's that?

Watch for any hidden streak of smallness. Pettiness is the exact opposite of a soul that means to keep reaching for the whole of things — everything divine, everything human.

Very true, he said.

And a mind of that scale, which looks out over all time and all being — do you think human life can seem a great thing to it?

It can't.

Then such a man won't count death as something terrible either?

Not at all.

So a cowardly, small nature has no share in true philosophy?

None.

And a well-balanced man — not money-loving, not petty, not a boaster, not a coward — could he ever drive a hard bargain, or deal unjustly?

Impossible.

So when you're sorting the philosophic soul from the other kind, you'll look for this too, and early: whether a man is just and gentle, or savage and hard to deal with.

Certainly.

And you won't leave out one more thing, I think.

What?

Whether he learns with ease or with pain. You can't expect anyone to love a task that hurts him, where enormous effort buys almost no progress.

No.

And if he can hold nothing of what he learns — if he's all forgetting — can he help ending up empty of knowledge?

He can't.

Then, laboring for nothing, won't he be forced in the end to hate himself and the whole business? — Yes.

So a forgetful soul never makes the list of genuinely philosophic natures. We have to insist on a good memory.

Certainly.

And a nature with no music in it, no grace — where does it get dragged but toward what's out of all measure?

Nowhere else.

And is truth kin to measure, or to the lack of it?

To measure.

Then along with everything else, we're looking for a mind that is measured and graceful by nature — one whose native bent carries it easily toward the way each thing really is.

Certainly.

Well then — don't all these qualities hang together? And aren't they, every one of them, necessary to a soul that is going to take hold of being fully and completely?

Absolutely necessary, he said.

Then can you find any fault with a pursuit that no one can practice well unless he is by nature retentive and quick to learn, large-minded and graceful — a friend and kinsman of truth, justice, courage, and self-command?

Not even Momus himself, he said, could find fault with that.

Then when age and education have brought men like that to completion, I said — wouldn't you hand the city to them, and to them alone?

Here Adeimantus broke in. Socrates, he said, nobody can argue with you about any of this. But here is what happens to your hearers every time you talk this way. They feel that because they're inexperienced at this game of question and answer, the argument bends them a little off course at each step — nothing much, each time — but when all the littles are added up at the end of the discussion, the fall is a big one, and their first position has been turned upside down. Like weak checkers players cornered by expert ones until at last they have no move left, your hearers are cornered in this other game, played with words instead of pieces, until at last they have nothing left to say — and yet the truth isn't a bit more on your side for that. I say this looking at the present case. Because right now any one of us would admit he can't oppose you at any single question, and yet look at the facts: of the people who take up philosophy — not the ones who touch it young as part of their education and then drop it, but the ones who stay with it far too long — most turn out to be complete cranks, not to say utterly rotten, while even the ones who seem best are still left useless to their cities by this very pursuit you're praising.

And do you think the people who say that are wrong?

I don't know, he said. I'd like to hear what you think.

Then hear it: I think they're telling the truth.

Then how can it be right to say that cities will have no rest from their troubles until philosophers rule in them — when we've just agreed philosophers are useless to cities?

That question, I said, needs an answer given in a picture.

And you, of course, said he, never talk in pictures.

Very funny, I said — mock the man you've just pushed into an argument this hard to win. Hear the picture, then, and you'll see all the better how greedy for pictures I am. What the most decent men suffer at the hands of their cities is so hard to describe that no single thing is like it; to plead their case you have to assemble the likeness from many parts, the way painters paint goat-stags and other composite creatures. Imagine it happening on a fleet of ships, or on one. The shipowner is bigger and stronger than everyone else on board, but a little deaf, a little short-sighted, and his knowledge of seafaring runs about as deep. The sailors are quarreling over the helm: every one of them is convinced he should steer, though not one has ever learned the art — none can name his teacher or point to the time he studied it — and beyond that they insist it can't be taught at all, and stand ready to carve up anyone who says it can. They crowd around the shipowner himself, begging, doing anything to make him hand them the tiller; and whenever they fail and other men get his ear instead, they kill those others or pitch them overboard, and then — after knocking out the worthy owner with mandrake, or drink, or something else — they take command of the ship and help themselves to her stores, drinking and feasting as they sail the way you'd expect such men to sail. And there's more: the man clever at helping them seize control — at working the owner by persuasion or force — they praise as a seaman, a real helmsman, a man who knows ships; the one who won't, they damn as useless. As for the true helmsman, it never so much as enters their heads that he must study the seasons of the year, the sky, the stars, the winds — everything that belongs to his art — if he's genuinely to be fit to command a ship; and as for how he's to steer, whether the crew likes it or not, they don't believe there's any art or discipline of that to be mastered along with the helmsman's craft. With all this going on aboard, don't you think the sailors on ships run that way would call the true helmsman a stargazer, a babbler, of no use to them at all?

They certainly would, said Adeimantus.

I don't think you need the picture taken apart, I said, to see that it's the image of the cities and how they treat true philosophers. You take my meaning.

I do.

Then offer the picture first to that man who keeps marveling that philosophers get no honor in their cities, and try to persuade him it would be far more of a marvel if they did.

I will.

And tell him he's right that the best people in philosophy are useless to the many — but tell him to blame the uselessness on those who make no use of them, not on the decent men themselves. It isn't in the nature of things for the helmsman to beg the sailors to be governed by him, or for the wise to wait at rich men's doors — whoever coined that clever saying lied. The natural truth is that a sick man, rich or poor, must go to the doctor's door, and everyone who needs governing to the door of the man who knows how to govern; a ruler worth anything doesn't go begging his subjects to accept his rule. But today's political leaders — go ahead and liken them to the sailors we just described, and the men they call useless stargazers to the true helmsmen.

Exactly right, he said.

So for these reasons, and in these conditions, philosophy — the noblest pursuit there is — can hardly expect honor from men whose whole practice runs the other way. But the far greater and heavier slander falls on her from those who claim to practice her — the ones your critic has in mind when he says that most of philosophy's followers are utterly rotten and the best of them useless. And I agreed that you were right.

Yes.

And we've now seen why the decent ones are useless?

We have.

Shall we take up next why most of them inevitably go bad, and try to show that philosophy is no more to blame for that than for the other?

By all means.

Let's start our questions and answers by going back to the nature we described — the man born fine and noble. Truth led him, you remember; he had to follow her wholly and everywhere, or be a fraud with no share at all in genuine philosophy.

Yes, that was said.

Well, that one point alone runs violently against current opinion of him, doesn't it?

It does, he said.

Then won't a fair defense of him run like this? — that a genuine lover of learning is born to strive toward what is; he doesn't settle among the many particular things that only seem to be, but presses on, the edge of his desire never dulled, his longing never spent, until he touches the nature of each thing that really is — touches it with the part of the soul fit to touch it, the part that is its kin — and drawing near through that kinship, coupling with true being, begetting intelligence and truth, he knows; and then he truly lives, is truly nourished, and then, and only then, is he released from his labor pains.

No defense could be fairer, he said.

Then will a taste for lies have any part in him? Just the opposite — won't he hate the lie?

He will.

And with truth leading the way, we'd never expect a chorus of vices trailing along behind her?

Never.

Rather a sound and just character, with self-command following in its train?

Right, he said.

Then there's no need to muster the whole choir of the philosopher's nature again from the top. You remember: courage, largeness of mind, quickness at learning, memory — all these belonged to him. And you objected that while no one could contradict any of it, still, if a man dropped the arguments and looked at the actual people they point to, he'd say that some are plainly useless and most of them bad through and through. Hunting the cause of that slander, we've arrived at the present question — why are most of them bad? — and that is why we took up the true philosopher's nature again and were forced to define it.

That's it.

Now we have to look at how that nature gets ruined — how it's destroyed in most men, while a small number escape: the ones people call not wicked, only useless. And after that, the natures that imitate the philosophic nature and set themselves up in its occupation — what sort of souls they are, that reach for a pursuit too high for them, beyond their strength, and by striking false notes everywhere fasten onto philosophy, in all men's mouths everywhere, the reputation you describe.

What sorts of ruin do you mean? he said.

I'll try to walk you through it, I said. This much, I think, everyone would grant us: a nature with everything in it that we just demanded of the man who's to be a philosopher is a rare growth, and appears seldom among human beings.

Extremely rare.

Then consider how many great forces are working to destroy those few.

What forces?

Strangest of all, every one of the very qualities we praised in that nature — courage, self-command, all the things we went through — ruins the soul that has it and tears it loose from philosophy.

That does sound strange, he replied.

And besides these, all the so-called goods corrupt it and pull it away: beauty, wealth, bodily strength, powerful family connections in the city — everything of that kind. You have the general shape of it.

I do, he said; but I'd gladly learn more exactly what you mean.

Grab hold of the thing as a whole, I said, in the right way, and it will come clear — nothing strange about what was said before it.

How, then? he asked.

Take any seed or growing thing, plant or animal: we know that if it doesn't get the food, the weather, or the soil that suits it, then the more vigorous it is, the more it suffers from the lack — for the bad is more the enemy of the good than of what is not good.

Of course.

So it stands to reason that the finest nature, under conditions foreign to it, comes off worse than a second-rate one.

It does.

Then, Adeimantus, shall we say the same of souls: the best-endowed, when they get a bad upbringing, turn out exceptionally bad? Or do you think great crimes and unmixed wickedness spring from a feeble nature, and not from a vigorous one wrecked by its rearing — while a weak nature never manages anything great, for good or for evil?

No, he said, you have it right.

So the nature we gave the philosopher: with the right teaching, I think it has to grow into every virtue. But if it's sown, planted, and raised in the wrong ground, it grows into the exact opposite — unless some god happens to rescue it. Or do you believe, with the crowd, that any young men worth mentioning are corrupted by sophists — that private teachers corrupt them to any degree worth the name? Isn't it the very people who say this who are the greatest sophists of all — schooling young and old, men and women, most thoroughly, and molding them into exactly what they want them to be?

When do they do that? he said.

Whenever the many pack themselves into assembly seats, or courtrooms, or theaters, or army camps, or any other mass gathering, and with a huge racket blame some of what's said and done and praise the rest — both beyond all measure — shouting and banging, while the rocks and the whole place echo them back and double the roar of the blame and the praise. In a scene like that, what state do you suppose a young man's heart is in, as the saying goes? What private education will hold firm and not be washed out by that flood of blame and praise, swept downstream wherever the current runs — until he calls the same things beautiful and ugly the crowd calls, practices what they practice, and becomes one of them?

The pressure leaves him no choice, Socrates.

And we still haven't named the greatest pressure of all, I said.

Which is?

What these educators and sophists add in deed when their words fail to persuade. You know, surely, that they discipline the disobedient with loss of civic rights, with fines, with death?

They certainly do — in dead earnest.

So what other sophist, what private man's arguments, do you think can pull against all that and win?

None, I think, he said.

None, I said — even to try is deep folly. A character trained to virtue against the education the public itself provides doesn't exist, hasn't existed, and won't exist — a human character, I mean, my friend; the divine, as the proverb goes, we leave out of the account. Because you should understand this clearly: whatever is saved and turns out well under the present condition of cities, you won't be wrong to say a god's dispensation saved it.

That's how it looks to me too, he said.

Then let one more thing look that way to you as well.

What?

That every one of those private wage-earners — the ones the public calls sophists and treats as rivals — teaches nothing but the opinions of the many, the very things the crowd believes when it sits down together. That is what they call wisdom. It's exactly as if a keeper were raising a great, powerful beast, and made a study of its moods and appetites — how to approach it, how to handle it, when it turns most dangerous and when most mild, and what makes it so; the meaning of its various cries, and which tones of voice soothe it or set it raging. Then imagine that, having learned all this by living alongside the animal, he calls it wisdom, packages it as an art, and sets up to teach — knowing nothing, in truth, about which of these opinions and appetites is honorable or shameful, good or bad, just or unjust, but fitting all these names to the great beast's reactions: what pleases the beast he calls good, what irritates it he calls bad. He has no other account of them; whatever is merely necessary he names just and noble, because he has never seen — and couldn't show anyone else — how vast the difference really is between the nature of the necessary and the nature of the good. Wouldn't a man like that, by Zeus, make a strange sort of educator?

He would.

Well, is he any different from the man who thinks wisdom is having sized up the moods and pleasures of the assembled crowd — in painting, in music, or, for that matter, in politics? Whoever mixes with the many to show them a poem or some other piece of work, or a service to the city, making the crowd his master beyond anything forced on him, is under what people call the necessity of Diomede to produce whatever they praise. But that what the crowd praises is really good and beautiful — have you ever heard one of them give an argument for that which wasn't laughable?

No, he said, and I don't expect to.

With all of that in mind, remember our earlier point: can the many possibly tolerate or believe that there is a beautiful itself, as against the many beautiful things — that each thing itself exists, as against the many particulars?

Not in the least.

Then the many can't be philosophers?

They can't.

So the men who do philosophize are bound to be sneered at by them?

Bound to be.

And by the private men who hang on the crowd and ache to please it?

Obviously.

Given all that, what rescue do you see for a philosophic nature — any way it stays in its calling and reaches the end? Think it through from what came before. We agreed that quickness at learning, memory, courage, and largeness of mind belong to this nature.

Yes.

Then from boyhood on, won't a boy like that be first in everything — especially if his body is born to match his soul?

Why wouldn't he be? he said.

And as he gets older, his relatives and his fellow citizens will want to use him for their own affairs?

Of course.

So they'll grovel at his feet with requests and honors, staking their claim early, flattering in advance the power that's on its way to him.

That's usually how it goes, he said.

And what do you expect a young man to do in that position — especially if he's from a great city, rich and well-born in it, and good-looking and tall besides? Won't he fill up with impossible hopes, sure he'll be competent to run the affairs of Greeks and barbarians alike, and lift himself sky-high on the strength of it, stuffed with posturing and empty pride, and no sense in him at all?

He certainly will.

Now suppose that while he's in that state, someone quietly comes up and tells him the truth — that there's no understanding in him, that he needs it, and that it can't be had except by slaving for it. Do you think it will be easy for him to hear, through all that noise around him?

Anything but.

But suppose, I said, that thanks to a good nature and a native kinship with reason, one such young man does take the point — bends, and lets himself be drawn toward philosophy. What do we imagine the others will do, when they see they're losing his use and his friendship? Is there a deed they won't do, a word they won't say — about him, so he won't be persuaded, and about the man persuading him, so he won't be able to persuade — plotting in private, hauling them into court in public?

It's inevitable.

Then is there any way that young man becomes a philosopher?

Not really.

So you see we weren't wrong to say that even the very components of the philosophic nature, when the rearing is bad, are in their own way a cause of a man's falling out of the pursuit — they, and the so-called goods, wealth and all its apparatus?

No, he said — it was said rightly.

There, my remarkable friend, is the ruin — that, and that large — of the best nature aimed at the best of all pursuits; a nature rare enough at any time, as we keep saying. And from these same men come the ones who do the greatest harm to cities and to individuals — and the greatest good, if the current happens to carry them that way. A small nature never does anything great, to a man or to a city.

Very true, he said.

So the men who belong to her most fall away and leave philosophy abandoned, her marriage unmade, while they live a life that is false and none of theirs; and she, orphaned of her kin, is set upon by other men — unworthy ones — who disgrace her, and hang on her the reproaches you say her detractors use: that of those who keep her company, some are worth nothing, and most deserve every bad thing they get.

Yes, that's what gets said.

And no wonder, I said. Other little men look over and see the ground open — a field thick with fine names and showy pretensions — and like convicts bolting out of prison into a sanctuary, the ones who happen to be cleverest at their own petty trades leap gladly out of those trades and into philosophy. Because even faring as she does, philosophy still keeps a standing prouder than any other calling — and that's what draws the crowd of stunted natures: bodies mangled by their crafts and trades, souls cramped and crushed by the same drudgery. Isn't that unavoidable?

It is.

And do they look any different, I said, from a little bald tinker who's come into money — just out of his chains, fresh from the bathhouse, wearing a brand-new coat, done up like a bridegroom — about to marry his master's daughter, because she's poor and there's no one to protect her?

No different at all, he said.

And what will a marriage like that breed? Won't the children be bastards — poor, mean things?

They can't be anything else.

So when men unworthy of education come courting philosophy and pair with her above their station, what kind of thoughts and opinions shall we say they father? Won't they be exactly what deserve the name sophisms — nothing legitimate in the litter, nothing that belongs to real intelligence?

Exactly that, he said.

That leaves a very small remnant, Adeimantus, of those who keep philosophy's company worthily. Maybe a fine, well-raised character caught by exile, who for want of anyone to corrupt him stays with her according to his nature. Or a great soul born in a small city, who looks at the city's politics and looks straight past them. And a gifted few may come over to her from some other craft they rightly despise. Some might be held back by the bridle of our friend Theages — Theages had everything else set to pitch him out of philosophy and into public life, but his sickly body reins him in and keeps him out. My own case — the divine sign — is hardly worth mentioning; it has come to almost no one before me, maybe no one at all. Now, the men of this little band have tasted how sweet and blessed a possession philosophy is, and they've watched the madness of the many long enough. They've seen that practically no one in politics does anything sound, and that there's no ally left with whom a man could march to the defense of justice and survive. He'd be like a man dropped among wild animals — refusing to join the pack in its crimes, unable single-handed to hold off the whole snarling circle, certain to be torn apart before he could do his city or his friends any good, useless to himself and everyone else. Once he's reckoned all that, he keeps quiet and minds his own work, like a traveler in a storm who steps behind a wall while the wind drives the dust and the sleet past; and watching everyone else fill up with lawlessness, he is content if he can somehow live this life clean of injustice and unholy acts, and make his exit graciously, in good temper, with fair hope.

Well, he said, that's no small thing to have accomplished before leaving.

But not the greatest either, I said — not without a city that fits him. In one that fits, he'll grow to his full height himself, and save the commonwealth along with his own soul.

Well then: why philosophy has a bad name, and how unjustly — that seems to me fairly laid out by now. Unless you have something to add?

Nothing more on that, he said. But which of today's regimes do you say is the one that fits her?

None whatsoever, I said. That is exactly my complaint: no city as constituted now is worthy of the philosophic nature. That's why the nature twists and changes — like a foreign seed sown in strange ground, which fades, is overpowered, and passes into the local strain, this stock too fails under present regimes to keep its own power and degenerates into an alien type. But if it ever meets the best regime, being itself the best, then it will show that it was truly divine all along, and everything else — every other nature and pursuit — merely human. And now, obviously, you're going to ask what that regime is.

Wrong, he said. I wasn't going to ask that — I was going to ask whether it's the city we've been founding in our talk, or some other one.

Ours, I said, in every other respect. But you may remember my saying even then that there would always have to be some authority alive in the city, holding to the same account of the constitution that you, the lawgiver, followed when you laid down the laws.

You did say it, he said.

But not clearly enough, I said — you all scared me off with your objections, which showed the proof would be long and hard. And what's left is hardly the easiest part to go through.

What's left?

How a city can take philosophy in hand without being destroyed by it. Everything great is precarious, and the fine things really are hard, as the saying goes.

Even so, he said, settle this too, and the demonstration is complete.

If anything stops me, I said, it won't be lack of will — lack of ability, maybe. You'll see my eagerness firsthand. In fact, look how eagerly, how recklessly, I'm about to declare that a city ought to take up this pursuit in exactly the opposite way from now.

How?

Today, I said, the ones who touch philosophy at all are boys barely out of childhood. In the interval before household and moneymaking close in, they get a taste of its hardest part and then quit — and these pass for the most philosophical of men. By the hardest part I mean argument itself, dialectic. Later in life, if they're invited when others are doing it, they condescend to sit in the audience of a lecture and count it a great favor, since they think philosophy should be a side pursuit. And toward old age, all but a very few of them go out more completely than Heraclitus' sun — they are never lit again.

And what should they do instead?

Exactly the reverse. As boys and adolescents they should get a boy's education and a boy's philosophy, and take particular care of their bodies through the years when the body is growing and knitting, securing it as a servant for philosophy later. Then, as the age comes on in which the soul begins to reach its full growth, they should stiffen its exercises. And when strength fails and politics and soldiering are behind them, then let them range loose at pasture at last and do nothing else in earnest, except as a sideline — these men who are to live happily here, and crown the life they've lived with a matching portion there.

I really do believe you're in earnest, Socrates, he said. But I think most of your hearers will oppose you even more earnestly, and won't be persuaded at all — starting with Thrasymachus.

Don't set Thrasymachus and me quarreling, I said — we've just become friends, and we weren't enemies even before. We will spare no effort until we either persuade him and the others, or accomplish something they can use against the day when they live again and meet arguments like these in another life.

Just a little while off, then, he said.

Nothing at all, I said, measured against the whole of time. Still, it's no wonder the many don't believe what we're saying — they have never seen the thing we're describing. What they've seen is phrases like these deliberately rhymed with one another, not falling together on their own the way ours just did. But a man balanced and matched with virtue itself, to the limit of the possible, in word and in deed, holding power in a city of the same quality — that they have never seen. Not one such man, let alone many. Do you think they have?

No.

Nor, my dear friend, have they listened often enough to talk that is fine and free — the kind that strains after truth flat-out, by every means, for the sake of knowing, and keeps a polite distance from the clever quibbles whose only aim is reputation and quarrel, in the courts and in private company alike.

No, he said, they haven't heard that either.

It was because of all this — we saw it coming — that, frightened as we were, the truth compelled us to say: no city, no regime, and likewise no man will ever reach completion until some chance necessity forces this small band of philosophers — the not-wicked ones, the ones now called useless — to take charge of a city whether they want to or not, and forces the city to obey; or until a true passion for true philosophy falls, by some divine breath, on the sons of the men now holding power and kingship, or on those men themselves. That either or both of these is impossible — I say there's no reason to think it. If there were, we'd deserve the laughing at, as men mouthing wishes and daydreams. Isn't that so?

It is.

So if, somewhere in the boundless reach of time gone by, men at the peak of philosophy have been forced to take charge of a city — or are now, in some barbarian land far beyond our horizon — or ever will be, we are ready to insist on this to the last: the constitution we've described has existed, does exist, and will exist whenever this Muse of philosophy gains control of a city. It isn't impossible; we're not describing impossibilities. That it's hard, we admit ourselves.

I think so too, he said.

But the many, you're about to say, don't agree?

They probably don't, he said.

My friend, I said, don't be so hard on the many. They'll change their opinion if, instead of picking a fight, you soothe them and clear away the slander on the love of learning — by pointing out whom you mean by philosophers, and defining, as we just did, their nature and their pursuit, so the many see you don't mean the people they think you mean. Once they view him that way, you'll grant they'll take on a different opinion and answer in a different key. Or do you think anyone answers gentleness with harshness, or envies where no envy is shown — someone himself unenvious and mild? I'll answer for you: a temper that harsh turns up in a few people somewhere, not in the majority.

Count me convinced, he said.

Then agree with this too: what has set the many against philosophy is that crowd from outside who came crashing in where they don't belong — trading insults, quarreling, forever talking about personalities — behavior nothing could suit philosophy less.

Nothing less, he said.

Because, Adeimantus, a man whose thought is truly set on the things that are has no leisure to look down on the affairs of men, and fight with them, and fill himself with envy and spite. He looks at things ranged in order, always the same — nothing wronging or wronged there, everything in pattern and proportion, arranged according to reason — and these he imitates, and grows as like them as a man can. Or do you think there's any way to keep company with what you admire and not imitate it?

There isn't.

So the philosopher, keeping company with the divine and the orderly, becomes as orderly and divine as a human being can become. Though there's plenty of slander to go around, for everyone.

Completely true.

Now suppose some necessity forces him, I said, to practice putting what he sees up there into human characters, private and public — to shape more than just himself. Do you think he'll prove a poor craftsman of self-command and justice and the whole of civic virtue?

The least poor there is.

And if the many ever realize we're telling them the truth about him, will they still be savage toward philosophers? Will they still doubt us when we say a city can never be happy unless it's drawn by the painters who work from the divine pattern?

They won't stay savage, he said, once they realize it. But what kind of drawing do you mean?

They'd take the city and the characters of its people like a painter's panel, I said, and first they'd wipe it clean — not easy at all, but you see right away that this alone separates them from every other lawgiver: they refuse to touch an individual or a city, refuse to draft a single law, until they've received a clean surface or made one themselves.

And rightly so, he said.

After that, wouldn't they sketch the outline of the constitution?

Surely.

Then, as they fill in the work, I imagine they'd keep glancing both ways — toward what is by nature just and beautiful and self-commanded and all the rest, and toward what they're producing in mankind — blending and mixing, out of men's ways of life, the true color of a man, judging it by that very thing which Homer too, when it appears in human beings, calls the form and likeness of a god.

Right, he said.

And they'd rub one feature out, I suppose, and paint another in, until they had made human characters as dear to God as human characters can be.

No painting could be more beautiful, he said.

Well then, I said — the people you described as charging us at full stretch: are we starting to persuade them that this is the constitution-painter we were praising to their faces? They bristled because we were handing cities over to him — are they any calmer, hearing it now?

Much calmer, he said, if they have any sense.

What could they still dispute? That philosophers are lovers of what is, and of truth?

That would be absurd, he said.

Or that the philosopher's nature, as we went through it, is close kin to the best?

Not that either.

Well then — will they say that such a nature, given the practices that suit it, won't grow perfectly good and philosophic, if any nature ever will? Or that the ones we ruled out will do it better?

Surely not.

Then will they still snarl when we say that until the philosophic kind takes control of a city, neither city nor citizens will have rest from evils, and the constitution we've been telling like a story will never be completed in fact?

Less, maybe, he said.

Not just less, if you don't mind, I said — let's say they've gone completely tame and been persuaded, so that shame, if nothing else, makes them agree.

Certainly, he said.

Take them as persuaded of that, then, I said. Next: will anyone contend that kings and rulers could never have sons born with philosophic natures?

No one, he said.

And once born, can anyone claim they must inevitably be corrupted? That saving them is hard, we concede ourselves. But that in the whole of time not one out of all of them could ever be saved — would anyone press that?

How could they?

But surely one is enough, I said. One man, with a city that obeys him, and everything now disbelieved comes to pass.

One is enough.

Because if a ruler establishes the laws and the practices we've described, it's surely not impossible that the citizens would be willing to live by them?

Not at all impossible.

And is it any wonder, any impossibility, that what seems right to us should seem right to others too?

I don't think so.

And that these arrangements are best, if they're possible — I think we showed that well enough already.

We did.

So the upshot of our lawgiving is this: what we propose is best if it can be done; and doing it is hard, but not impossible.

That's the upshot.

So — that struggle behind us, we take up what remains: in what way, through what studies and practices, the preservers of the constitution will be formed in our city, and at what ages they should take each subject up.

Yes — we have to say.

My earlier cleverness gained me nothing, I said — putting off the awkward business of acquiring wives, and begetting children, and appointing the rulers, because I knew how unpopular the full truth would be, and how hard to bring about. It all had to be gone through anyway. The women and children are settled; the rulers we must now pursue from the very beginning. We were saying, if you recall, that they had to prove themselves lovers of their city, tested in pleasures and in pains — shown to keep that conviction through hardships, through fears, through every reversal of fortune — and whoever broke was to be rejected, while whoever came out clean everywhere, like gold tried in fire, was to be installed as ruler and given honors and prizes, in life and in death. Something like that is what was being said, while the argument slipped past with her face veiled, afraid to stir up exactly what now lies in front of us.

True, he said. I remember.

I balked, my friend, at saying what we've now dared, I said. So let the daring stand: the guardians in the strictest sense must be philosophers.

Let it stand, he replied.

Then notice how few of them you can expect. The parts of the nature we specified rarely consent to grow together in one person; mostly the nature comes up scattered — one piece here, another there.

Meaning what? he said.

You know how it goes: quick learners with good memories, shrewd, fast, and everything that follows from that — youthful fire, large ambitions — don't usually also come with a willingness to live an orderly, quiet, steady life. Men like that are carried by their quickness wherever chance takes them, and all the steadiness drains out of them.

That's the truth, he said.

And the settled characters, the steady dependable ones — the men you'd trust anywhere, who in war stand unmoved in the face of fear — are just as unmoved in the face of a lesson. They're numb where learning is concerned, as if the part had frozen, and they yawn and doze the moment there's mental work to do.

So it is.

But we insist that a man have a good, generous share of both, or he gets no part of the highest education, no honor, no office.

Rightly, he said.

So don't you think that combination will be rare?

Very rare.

Then the candidate must be tested in the labors, fears, and pleasures we named before — and also in something we passed over then and add now: he must be exercised in many studies, while we watch whether his nature can bear the greatest of them, or will flinch and fail, the way men fail in athletic trials.

It's certainly right to look, he said. But which studies do you call the greatest?

You remember, I said, that we distinguished three forms in the soul, and worked out from them what justice, self-command, courage, and wisdom each are?

If I'd forgotten that, he said, I'd deserve to hear none of what's left.

And do you remember what was said just before it?

What?

We said, roughly, that to see these matters at their finest, a longer road had to be traveled — walk it, and they become plain — though it was possible to attach demonstrations on the level of what had already been said. You all said that would do, and so the account went forward with, by my standards, less than exactness. Whether it satisfied you is yours to say.

I thought you gave us fair measure, he said; apparently the others did too.

My friend, I said, in things like this, no measure that falls short of what is — in any respect — is fair measure at all. Nothing incomplete is the measure of anything. Though people sometimes decide the incomplete is already plenty, and that further search is wasted effort.

Plenty of people feel that way, he said — out of laziness.

And it's the last feeling permitted, I said, in a guardian of a city and its laws.

Fair enough.

Then a guardian, my friend, must go round by the longer road, and must labor as hard at learning as at the gymnasium — or, as we were just saying, he will never carry the greatest and most fitting study through to the end.

So these aren't the greatest? he said. There's something greater still than justice and the virtues we described?

Something greater, I said. And even the virtues themselves — we must not go on looking at a sketch of them, as we do now, and cheat ourselves of the most finished rendering. Isn't it ridiculous to strain every nerve for maximum precision and clarity about small things, and then not demand the highest precision for the greatest?

A worthy thought, he said. But do you imagine anyone will let you go without asking what this greatest study is — and what it's about?

Not at all, I said — ask away. Though you've heard the answer plenty of times; right now either it's slipped your mind, or you intend to make trouble for me by objecting. I suspect the latter. You have heard, many times over, that the greatest study is the idea of the good — the thing that just things and everything else must borrow before they become useful and beneficial. You knew more or less that this is what I was going to say — and, on top of it, that we don't know it adequately. And if we don't know it, you realize that even knowing everything else as fully as possible profits us nothing without it — exactly as owning everything profits us nothing without owning the good. Or do you think there's any gain in possessing everything except what's good? Or in understanding everything else minus the good — grasping nothing beautiful and good at all?

By Zeus, I don't.

And you certainly know this too: to the many, the good is pleasure; to the more refined, it's knowledge.

Of course.

And, my friend, that the ones who say this can't point to what kind of knowledge they mean — in the end they're forced to say: knowledge of the good.

Which is ridiculous.

Ridiculous is right, I said. They scold us for not knowing the good, then turn around and talk to us as if we did know it — the good, they say, is knowledge of the good, as though we understand the phrase the moment they pronounce the word.

Perfectly true, he said.

And the ones who define the good as pleasure — are they any less lost? Aren't they forced to admit, in their turn, that some pleasures are bad?

They certainly are.

Which lands them, I think, in conceding that the same things are good and bad. True?

It does.

So the disputes about it are many and large — that much is plain?

It is.

And here's another plain thing. With the just and the beautiful, many people would choose the appearance — doing it, having it, seeming it, without the reality. But with the good, nobody settles for owning the appearance; here everyone hunts the real thing, and the seeming is despised by all.

Completely, he said.

Here, then, is what every soul pursues — the thing for whose sake it does everything it does. The soul divines that the good is something, but it is baffled: it can't grasp adequately what it is, or trust it with the steady confidence it has about other things, and because of that it loses whatever benefit those other things might have carried. On a matter of that kind and that magnitude — are we to say that the best men in the city, the ones we'll put everything into the hands of, must stay in the dark?

Anything but, he said.

At any rate, I said, just things and beautiful things won't have found much of a guardian in a man who doesn't even know in what way they are good. And I have a hunch that no one will know them adequately before he knows that.

A sound hunch, he said.

So our constitution will be in perfect order only if the guardian watching over it is a man who knows these things?

Necessarily, he said. But you, Socrates — do you say the good is knowledge, or pleasure, or some third thing?

That's you all over, I said. You've made it perfectly clear for a while now that other people's opinions on this weren't going to satisfy you.

Well, Socrates, he said, it doesn't seem right for a man who has spent this long on these questions to be able to recite everyone else's convictions and never his own.

And does it seem right to you, I said, for a man to talk about what he doesn't know as though he knew it?

As though he knew it, no, he said — but he ought to be willing to say what he thinks, as his thinking.

Really? I said. Haven't you noticed that opinions without knowledge are all ugly things? The best of them are blind. When men hold a true opinion without understanding, are they any different from blind men who happen to take the right road?

No different.

Then do you want to look at ugly things — blind and crooked — when you could be hearing bright and beautiful ones from other people?

By Zeus, Socrates, said Glaucon, don't pull up now, with the end in sight. It will satisfy us if you go through the good the way you went through justice and self-command and the rest.

It would more than satisfy me too, my friend, I said. But I'm afraid I won't be up to it, and that in my eagerness I'll cut a ridiculous figure. No, gentlemen — let's leave aside, for now, what the good itself is; to reach what I currently think about it looks like more than this push of ours can manage. But what appears to be a child of the good, its closest likeness — that I'm willing to describe, if you'd like. If not, we drop it.

Tell us, he said. You'll pay off the account of the father another time.

I wish I could hand over the full payment, I said, and you could collect it, instead of just the interest. Collect this interest, then — this child of the good itself. Only be careful I don't somehow deceive you against my will, and pass you a counterfeit account of the offspring.

We'll be as careful as we can, he said. Just go on.

First let me get your agreement, I said, and remind you of what was said earlier in this discussion — and often enough before now.

What is it?

That there are many beautiful things, I said, and many good things, and so on for each kind — that's how we speak of them, how we mark them out in words.

Yes, we do.

And there is the beautiful itself, and the good itself — and likewise for everything we were just now calling many: turning around, we posit a single idea for each, as though each were one, and we call it what each thing is.

That's so.

And the many, we say, are seen but not thought, while the ideas are thought but not seen.

Exactly.

Now, with what part of ourselves do we see the things that are seen?

With sight, he said.

And with hearing what is heard, I said, and with the other senses everything else that's perceived?

Of course.

Then have you noticed, I said, how extravagantly the craftsman of the senses built the power of seeing and being seen — how much more it cost him than the rest?

Not really, he said.

Then look at it this way. Do hearing and sound need some third kind of thing before the one can hear and the other be heard — something whose absence means hearing won't hear and sound won't be heard?

They need nothing, he said.

Nor do most of the other senses, I said — maybe none of them. Can you name one that does?

I can't.

But the power of sight, and of being seen — haven't you noticed that it does demand one?

How so?

Sight may be present in the eyes, and its owner may set out to use it, and color may be right there in things — but unless a third kind of thing is present, made by nature for exactly this purpose, you know that sight will see nothing and the colors will stay unseen.

What thing do you mean?

The thing you call light, I said.

True, he said.

Then no small nature joins them: the yoke between sight and visibility is a more precious yoking than any other pairing — by a wide margin, unless light is a thing without honor.

And it's very far from that, he said.

Then which of the gods in heaven can you name as master of all this — the one whose light makes our sight see, as beautifully as sight can see, and makes the seen things seen?

The same one you would, he said, and everyone else: obviously you're asking about the sun.

Now, sight stands to this god in the following relation, doesn't it?

Which?

Sight is not the sun — neither sight itself nor the place where it lives, which we call the eye.

No.

But of all the organs of sense, I think, the eye is the most sun-like.

By far.

And the very power it has, it holds as a kind of allowance dispensed from the sun — an overflow streaming in.

Just so.

So the sun is not sight; but it is the cause of sight, and sight itself sees it.

That's right, he said.

Then here is what I meant by the child of the good, I said. The good begot the sun as its own analogue: what the good is in the intelligible region, to intelligence and the things that are thought, the sun is in the visible region, to sight and the things that are seen.

How? he said. Take me through it.

You know what eyes do, I said, when you turn them on things whose colors the light of day no longer holds — things lit only by the gleams of the night: they go dim, they seem nearly blind, as if there were no clear sight in them.

Very much so.

But turn them on what the sun is shining on, and the same eyes see distinctly — the sight is plainly in them after all.

Certainly.

Think of the soul the same way. When it settles on what truth and being shine upon, it understands, it knows, and it shows that it has intelligence. But when it settles on what is mixed with darkness — on what comes to be and passes away — it opines and goes dim, changing opinions up and down, and looks, this time, like a thing with no intelligence in it.

It does.

Then this is what you should say the idea of the good is: the thing that gives truth to what is known and the power of knowing to the knower. It is the cause of knowledge and of truth insofar as truth is known; and beautiful as both of these are — knowledge and truth — you'll be right to think the good is something else again, more beautiful still than either. As in the other realm it was correct to consider light and sight sunlike, but wrong to think them the sun, so here it is correct to consider knowledge and truth both good-like, but wrong to think either of them is the good. The standing of the good must be honored higher yet.

That's a beauty past reckoning you're describing, he said — it provides knowledge and truth, and outdoes them both in beauty. You certainly can't mean pleasure.

Watch your tongue, I said. Better to examine its image a little further, like this.

Like what?

The sun, I think you'd agree, gives visible things more than the power of being seen: also their generation, their growth, their nourishment — though the sun itself is not generation.

Of course not.

Then say the same of the things that are known: the good not only gives them their being-known; it also confers on them their existence and their being — though the good is not being. It stands beyond being, surpassing it in rank and in power.

And Glaucon said, quite comically: Apollo! What an unearthly exaggeration!

Blame yourself, I said. You're the one who forced me to say what I think about it.

And don't stop, he said — at the very least, go through the sun comparison again, if you're leaving anything out.

Oh, I'm leaving out plenty, I said.

Then don't skip a bit of it.

I suspect a great deal will get skipped, I said. Still, as far as it's possible at the moment, I won't leave anything out on purpose.

Don't, he said.

Think of it, then, as we've said: there are two of them — two kings, one reigning over the intelligible kind and region, the other over the visible. I don't say 'over the heavens,' or you'll think I'm playing on the word. Either way, you have the two kinds before you, visible and intelligible?

I have them.

Then take a line cut into two unequal segments — one for the visible kind, one for the intelligible — and cut each segment again in the same ratio. Now rank the subsections by their relative clearness and obscurity. In the visible half, the first subsection is images. By images I mean, first, shadows; then reflections in water and in every surface that is dense, smooth, and polished; and everything of that sort. Do you see?

I see.

Now set down the second subsection: what the first is a likeness of — the animals around us, everything that grows, and the whole class of things that are made.

Done.

And would you also say, I asked, that the visible half divides by truth and its lack — that as the likeness stands to what it's a likeness of, so the realm of opinion stands to the realm of knowledge?

I would, he said — emphatically.

Then look next at how the intelligible segment is to be cut.

How?

Like this. In its first subsection, the soul is forced to conduct its search from hypotheses, using as images the things that were themselves originals a moment ago — and it travels not toward a beginning but toward an end, a conclusion. In the second, the soul moves from hypothesis toward a beginning that is above hypothesis, without the images the first subsection used, making its way through forms themselves, by means of them alone.

I didn't quite follow that, he said.

Then again, I said — it will be easier after this preface. You know how people who work at geometry and calculation and their kin proceed. They lay down the odd and the even, the figures, the three kinds of angle, and whatever else belongs to each inquiry, as hypotheses — as things they know. They give no account of them, to themselves or to anyone, as if they were plain to everybody; they simply start from them, and go through the remaining steps until they arrive, all consistently, at the thing they set out to examine.

Yes, he said — that much I know.

Then you also know that they use visible figures and build their arguments about them, while thinking not about these but about what these are like: their arguments are for the sake of the square itself, the diagonal itself — not the diagonal they've drawn. And so on throughout. The very things they mold and draw — which have shadows and reflections in water of their own — those they use in turn as mere images, seeking to see the things themselves, which can be seen by nothing but thought.

True.

This, then, is the kind I called intelligible — yes; but the soul is forced to use hypotheses in going after it, and does not travel back to a beginning, because it can't climb out above its hypotheses; and it uses as images the very objects that the things below copy — objects which, next to their own shadows, are reputed brilliant and prized for their clarity.

I understand, he said. You mean what happens in geometry and its sister arts.

Then by the second subsection of the intelligible, understand me to mean what reason itself grasps by the power of dialectic: treating its hypotheses not as first principles but as hypotheses in the true sense — footholds and springboards — so that it can rise all the way to what needs no hypothesis, the first principle of everything. And having touched that principle, it turns and holds to what follows from it, and comes down in that way to a conclusion — using nothing sensible at all, only forms: moving through forms, into forms, and ending in them.

I follow, he said — not adequately, since this sounds to me like an enormous undertaking — but this much I take you to be laying down: the slice of being and the intelligible that dialectic's knowledge studies is clearer than what the so-called arts study, whose starting points are hypotheses. Students of the arts are compelled, true, to study their objects with thought rather than the senses; but because they examine them without climbing back to a first principle — from hypotheses only — you judge that they don't exercise intelligence upon them, even though the objects themselves are intelligible when taken together with a first principle. And the state of mind of geometers and their kind you call, I think, thought and not intelligence — thought sitting somewhere between opinion and intelligence.

You've taken it in perfectly adequately, I said. Now, matching the four segments, accept these four conditions that arise in the soul: intelligence for the highest; thought for the second; assign conviction to the third; and to the last, imaging. And set them in proportion: as the objects they're set over share in truth, so the conditions share in clearness.

I understand, he replied. I agree, and I arrange them as you say.
