# The Republic, Book VIII

The Decay of Regimes

Good, Glaucon. So we're agreed: in the city that's going to be governed at the highest level, wives are common, children and their whole upbringing are common, the pursuits of war and peace are common — and its kings must be the men who have proved best in philosophy and in war.

We're agreed, Glaucon replied.

Yes — and we also agreed that once the rulers are installed, they'll lead the soldiers into the kind of housing we described: nothing private in it, nothing anyone's own, common to all. And beyond housing, you remember what we agreed about property?

I remember. None of them was to own anything that other people own. They were to be athletes of war and guardians, drawing their maintenance from the other citizens as the wage of their guarding — a year's keep, nothing more — and their business was to look after themselves and the whole city.

That's right. But now that we've finished with all that, let's fix the point where we turned off the road, so we can go back the way we came.

That's not hard. You were talking then, much as you are now, as if you had finished describing the city. You were saying that the city you'd described was good, and so was the man who matched it — though as it turns out, you had a still finer city and a still finer man to tell us about. At any rate, you said that if this one was right, the others were mistaken; and of the mistaken regimes, as I remember, you said four kinds were worth discussing — worth seeing where they go wrong — along with the men who match them, so that once we'd seen them all and agreed on the best man and the worst, we could ask whether the best is also the happiest and the worst the most miserable, or whether it's otherwise. I was asking which four regimes you meant when Polemarchus and Adeimantus broke in — and that's when you took up the argument that has brought us here.

Your memory is exact, I said.

Then do what a wrestler does, he said: give me the same hold again. I'll ask the same question, and you try to give me the answer you were about to give then.

I will if I can, I said.

Then in particular, I want to hear which four regimes you meant.

That's easily answered, I said. The four I mean are the ones that have names. First, the Cretan and Spartan type, which most people praise. Second — and second in most people's esteem — the one called oligarchy, a regime riddled with evils. Next its adversary, democracy, which follows out of it. And then the noble tyranny itself, outstripping all of them — the fourth and final sickness of a city. Can you think of another kind that forms a distinct type? There are hereditary lordships and purchased kingships and other regimes of that sort in between, but they're hybrids — you find them among Greeks and barbarians alike.

Yes, he said — one hears of plenty of strange ones.

And do you realize, I said, that there must be as many types of human character as there are types of regime? Or do you suppose regimes grow out of 'oak and rock' somewhere, and not out of the characters of the people in the cities — which tip the scale, so to speak, and drag everything else after them?

No, he said — they come from nowhere else. The cities are as the people are; the regimes grow out of their characters.

Then if there are five forms of city, there will be five conditions of soul in individuals as well?

Certainly.

The man who matches aristocracy — rule of the best — we've already described, and we rightly call him good and just.

We have.

Then next we should walk through the worse men: the lover of victory and honor, who corresponds to the Spartan type of regime; then the oligarchic man; then the democratic; then the tyrannical. That way we can set the most unjust beside the most just, and finish our inquiry — how pure justice stands against pure injustice, in the happiness or misery of the man who has it — and either follow Thrasymachus and pursue injustice, or follow the argument as it's coming to light, and pursue justice.

That's exactly what we must do, he said.

Shall we do what we did before, then — start with the regime rather than the individual, since it shows up more clearly there? On that plan the honor-loving regime comes first. I know of no name in common use for it; call it timocracy, or timarchy. We'll examine the matching man beside it; then oligarchy and the oligarchic man; then democracy, and a look at the democratic man; and fourth, having come to the city under tyranny and seen it, we'll look in turn into a tyrannical soul — and try to become competent judges of the question we set ourselves.

That would be a reasonable way to see it and judge it, he said.

Come, then, I said — let's try to say how timocracy arises out of aristocracy. Or is it simply this: every regime changes from within, from the part of it that holds office, when that part splits against itself; and so long as that part is of one mind, however small it is, the regime cannot be shaken?

Yes, that's so.

Then how, Glaucon, will our city be shaken? How will the auxiliaries and the rulers fall into faction with each other, or among themselves? Shall we do what Homer does, and pray the Muses to tell us 'how faction first fell upon them'? Shall we say they answer us in high tragic style — playing with us, teasing us as if we were children — while pretending to be in dead earnest?

Saying what?

Something like this. 'A city so composed is hard to move. But everything that comes into being must decay, and even a constitution like this will not hold forever; it will be dissolved. And this is its dissolution. Not only for plants in the earth but for animals on it, there come seasons of fruitfulness and barrenness, of soul and body alike, whenever the revolutions of each complete their circuits — short orbits for the short-lived, the opposite for their opposites. And for your own race: the men you have educated to lead the city, wise as they are, will not hit the seasons of fruitfulness and barrenness by calculation joined with perception; the seasons will slip past them, and they will beget children at the wrong time. For a divine creature there is a period embraced by a perfect number. For a human creature it is the first number in which increases of root and square, taking three distances and four limits of the like and the unlike, the waxing and the waning, render all things commensurable and rational with one another. Of these, the base of four-thirds, wedded to five and three times increased, yields two harmonies: one a square, so many times a hundred; the other of equal length one way but oblong — a hundred squares of the rational diameters of five, each diminished by one, or of the irrational, diminished by two, and a hundred cubes of three. This whole geometric number is lord of better and worse begettings; and when your guardians miss it, and join brides to bridegrooms out of season, the children will not be favored by nature or by fortune. Their elders will install the best of them in office; but being unworthy all the same, and come into their fathers' powers, they will begin to neglect us Muses, guardians though they are — undervaluing music and poetry first, and physical training after it — and so your young will grow up less cultivated. From them will come rulers with little of the guardian left in them, rulers who fail at testing Hesiod's races and yours — gold, silver, bronze, and iron. Iron will be alloyed with silver, and bronze with gold, and from the mixture will come unlikeness and jarring inequality; and wherever those arise, they always breed war and hatred. This is the ancestry of faction, wherever it breaks out — so the Muses declare.'

And we can take it their answer is true, he said.

It has to be, I said — they're Muses. How could Muses speak falsely?

And what do the Muses say next?

Once faction had broken out, the two strains pulled against each other: the iron and bronze strain toward moneymaking and the owning of land and houses, of gold and silver; the gold and silver strain — not poor, but rich by nature in their souls — back toward virtue and the old order. They wrenched and strained against one another until they settled on a middle course: they distributed the land and the houses as private property, and the people they had guarded till then as free men — their friends, the ones who fed them — they now enslaved, holding them as dependents and servants, while they occupied themselves with war, and with standing guard over them.

I think that's exactly where the change comes from.

And this regime will be something midway between aristocracy and oligarchy?

Very much so.

That's how the change will happen, then. And once it's happened, how will they live? Isn't it obvious that, lying midway between the earlier regime and oligarchy, the city will imitate partly the one and partly the other, and have some features all its own?

It will, he said.

In honoring its rulers; in its fighting class keeping clear of farming, of crafts, of moneymaking in general; in common meals and the devotion to athletics and the training for war — in all this it will imitate the earlier city?

Yes.

But it will be afraid to bring the wise into office, because the wise men it has now are no longer simple and single-minded but mixed; it will lean instead toward spirited, simpler men, made more for war than for peace; it will hold the tricks and stratagems of war in honor, and it will spend its whole life making war. Most of that will be its own.

Yes.

Men of this stamp will be greedy for money, I said, like the men in oligarchies — nursing a fierce, secret reverence for gold and silver, now that they own storehouses and private treasuries where they can hide what they keep, and walled compounds around their houses, real private nests, where they can spend lavishly on women and on whomever else they please.

Very true, he said.

They'll be stingy with money too, since they prize it and can't acquire it openly — yet love spending other people's, when appetite calls. They'll pluck their pleasures in secret, running from the law the way boys run from a father, because they were educated not by persuasion but by force: they neglected the true Muse, the companion of argument and philosophy, and honored physical training above music.

The regime you're describing is a thorough mixture of bad and good, he said.

It is mixed, I said. But because the spirited part is in charge, one thing stands out in it above everything else: the love of victory and the love of honor.

Strongly, he said.

That, then, is how this regime comes to be, and that's what it's like — an outline, no fine detail, because even a sketch is enough to show us the most just man and the most unjust; and to go through every regime and every character, leaving nothing out, would be an unmanageably long road.

Fair enough, he replied.

Then who is the man who matches this regime? How does he come about, and what sort of man is he?

I think, said Adeimantus, that as far as love of victory goes, he's not far from Glaucon here.

In that one point, maybe, I said. But in others the resemblance fails.

Which others?

He'd have to be more stubborn, I said, and somewhat less cultivated — though a lover of culture, and fond of listening, while no kind of speaker himself. A man like that is harsh with slaves, instead of merely looking down on them the way a properly educated man does; gentle with the free-born; sharply obedient to authority. He loves office and loves honor, and stakes his claim to rule not on his speaking or anything of that kind, but on his deeds in war and everything to do with war — a lover of his exercise, and of the hunt.

Yes, that's the character that matches the regime.

As for money: a man like this despises it while he's young, but the older he grows the more he welcomes it, because he has a share of the money-lover's nature — and because he isn't pure toward virtue. He's lost the best of guardians.

Which guardian? said Adeimantus.

Reason, I said, blended with music. It's the only thing that, once it moves in, stays with a man his whole life as the preserver of his virtue.

Well said.

That, I said, is the timocratic youth — the image of the timocratic city.

Exactly.

And he comes about something like this. Sometimes he's the young son of a good father who lives in a badly governed city — a father who avoids its honors and offices and lawsuits and all the usual busyness, and is willing to be shortchanged rather than have the trouble.

Then how does the son turn out this way?

It starts when he listens to his mother. She's aggrieved: her husband isn't one of the rulers, and the other women slight her for it. Then she sees he isn't much concerned about money, doesn't battle and brawl in the courts and the assembly but takes everything of that kind quietly, and keeps his attention always on his own thoughts — treating her, meanwhile, with neither much reverence nor much disrespect. All of it makes her resentful, and she tells the boy his father is unmanly and far too slack — along with the whole litany women love to chant on that theme.

Plenty of refrains, said Adeimantus, and just like the singers.

And you know, I said, that the house servants — the ones who seem loyal to the family — sometimes say the same kinds of things to the sons in private. If they see the father failing to prosecute a man who owes him money or has wronged him some other way, they urge the boy: when you're grown, punish all people of that sort, and be more of a man than your father. And when he goes out, he hears and sees more of the same — the men who mind their own business in the city are called simpletons and counted for little, while the meddlers are honored and praised. The young man hears and sees all this; and he also hears his father's words, and watches his way of life at close range beside the ways of the others; and the two pull him opposite directions — his father watering the reason in his soul and making it grow, everyone else feeding appetite and spirit. And because he isn't a bad man by nature, only fallen into bad company, he's dragged by the two of them to a middle place, and hands over the rule of himself to the middle part — the contentious, spirited part — and becomes a proud and honor-loving man.

You've described exactly how he comes about, I think.

Then we have our second regime, I said, and our second man.

We have.

Next, then, shall we say with Aeschylus,

'another man, stationed against another city,'

— or rather follow our plan, and take the city first?

By all means.

Next in order, I believe, comes oligarchy.

And what arrangement do you mean by oligarchy?

The regime founded on a property assessment, I said: the rich rule, and the poor man has no share in office.

I see, he replied.

Shouldn't I start with how timocracy first changes into oligarchy?

Yes.

Well, I said, even a blind man could see how it changes.

How?

That treasury each man keeps filling with gold, I said — that is what destroys the regime. First they invent expenses to indulge themselves, and bend the laws to allow it; they and their wives simply stop obeying.

Likely enough.

Then, I suppose, one man watches another grow rich and wants to match him, until they've made the great mass of citizens just like themselves.

Likely.

From there they sink deeper into moneymaking; and the more honor they give money, the less they give virtue. Isn't virtue set against wealth like the two pans of a scale, each forever tipping opposite the other?

Very much so.

So when wealth and the wealthy are honored in a city, virtue and the good are honored less.

Clearly.

And what is honored gets practiced, and what is dishonored gets neglected.

So it is.

So in the end, instead of lovers of victory and honor, they become lovers of moneymaking and money; they praise the rich man and admire him and bring him into office, and the poor man they despise.

Exactly.

Then they pass a law that draws the boundary line of the oligarchic regime: a fixed amount of property — larger where the oligarchy is tighter, smaller where it's looser — and they announce that no one whose wealth falls short of the assessment may hold office. They push this through by force of arms, or else they've already installed the regime by fear. Isn't that how it goes?

It is.

That, broadly speaking, is how it's established.

Yes, he said. But what's the character of the regime? What are the defects we said it had?

First, I said, the very line that defines it. Think what would happen if ships' pilots were appointed by property assessment, and the poor man never given the helm even if he were the better pilot —

A sorry voyage they'd make of it, he said.

And isn't the same true of ruling anything else?

I'd think so.

Except a city? Or does it hold for a city too?

For a city most of all, he said — the greater and harder the rule, the more it holds.

So that's one defect of oligarchy, and a large one.

So it appears.

And here's another, hardly smaller.

What?

That a city like this is necessarily not one city but two: a city of the poor and a city of the rich, living on the same ground and forever plotting against each other.

Every bit as large, he said.

Nor is this a handsome trait: they're probably incapable of fighting a war. Arm the multitude, and they must fear it more than the enemy; leave it home, and they arrive on the battlefield oligarchs in earnest — few to fight, as they are few to rule. And all the while, being money-lovers, they're unwilling to pay for the war.

Not handsome at all.

And what about the thing we condemned long ago — the same men in such a regime farming, making money, and making war, all at once? Does that look right?

Not in any way.

Now see whether this regime is the first that admits the greatest of all these evils.

Which?

Letting a man sell everything he has, and letting another man acquire it — and letting the seller go on living in the city while belonging to no part of it: not a businessman, not a craftsman, not a cavalryman or a hoplite, just what they call a pauper, a man without means.

Yes — it's the first.

At any rate nothing prevents it in oligarchies. Otherwise you wouldn't get the over-rich at one end and the destitute at the other.

True.

But consider this. Back when he was rich and spending, was this man of any more use to the city in the ways we just mentioned? Or did he only seem to belong to the ruling class, while in truth he was neither ruler nor servant of the city — merely a spender of whatever was at hand?

That's it, he said. He seemed; he was nothing but a spender.

Shall we say, then, that as a drone grows up in its cell, a disease to the hive, this man grows up in his house, a drone and a disease to the city?

Just so, Socrates.

And hasn't the god made all the winged drones stingless, Adeimantus, while of these walking drones some are stingless and some carry terrible stings? From the stingless kind come the men who end their days as beggars; from the stinging kind, everyone we call a criminal.

Most true, he said.

Then wherever you see beggars in a city, somewhere close by are hidden thieves, and cutpurses, and temple-robbers, and the craftsmen of every evil of that kind.

Clearly.

Well then — in oligarchic cities, don't you see beggars?

Nearly everyone but the rulers, he said.

Then shouldn't we suppose there are plenty of the stinging sort there too — criminals the authorities take care to hold down by force?

We should.

And shall we say such men spring up there from lack of education, from bad rearing, and from a bad ordering of the regime?

We shall.

That, then, is the oligarchic city, and those are its evils — those, and perhaps a few more.

Perhaps so.

Then let's call this regime finished as well — the one they name oligarchy, which takes its rulers by property. Next, the man who matches it: how he comes about, and what he's like once he has.

By all means.

Doesn't the change from that timocratic man to the oligarchic go mostly like this?

Like what?

He has a son. At first the son admires his father and follows in his footsteps. Then he watches him strike the city like a ship striking a hidden reef, and spill everything overboard — himself included. The father had held a generalship or some other great office; then he was dragged into court by informers, and put to death, or exiled, or stripped of his rights with all his property confiscated.

Likely enough.

The son sees it all, my friend — suffers it, loses the estate — and takes fright, I imagine, and throws love of honor and spirit headfirst off the throne in his soul. Humbled by poverty, he turns to moneymaking; and greedily, little by little, by scrimping and hard work, he piles up a fortune. Don't you think a man like that then seats appetite — the money-loving part — on the empty throne, and makes it the Great King within him, girded with tiaras and collars and scimitars?

I do, he replied.

And reason and spirit, I imagine, he sets on the ground beneath it, one on either side, and teaches them their place as slaves. The first he allows to calculate and study nothing but how money will breed more money; the second to admire and honor nothing but wealth and the wealthy, and to take pride in nothing whatever but the owning of money and whatever brings it in.

There's no other change, he said, so quick and so sure as the honor-loving young man's into the money-loving one.

And this, I said, is the oligarchic man?

Well — the man he changed out of was like the regime that oligarchy itself came out of.

Then let's see whether he's like oligarchy.

Let's see.

First, then, he's like it in prizing money above everything?

Of course.

And in being thrifty and hardworking — satisfying only his necessary desires, allowing himself no other spending, and holding the rest of his desires down as so much waste.

Certainly.

A squalid sort of man, I said, wringing a profit out of everything, laying up treasure — the very type the crowd applauds. Isn't this the man who matches that regime?

So it seems to me. Money, at any rate, is what's honored — by the city and by him alike.

Because culture, I take it, was never part of his upbringing.

I'd say not, he said — or he wouldn't have set a blind god at the head of his chorus and given him first honors.

Good, I said. But consider this: won't we say that from that same lack of culture, drone desires live in him too — some beggarly, some criminal — held down forcibly by his general carefulness?

We will.

And do you know where to look, I said, if you want to catch those criminal desires at work?

Where?

Watch him where he's guardian of an orphan's estate — anywhere he gets a free hand to do wrong.

True.

Doesn't that make it plain that in his other dealings — the ones that earn him his reputation for seeming just — he's holding his bad desires down by a kind of decent compulsion? Not persuading them they're wrong, not taming them with argument, but with necessity and fear, because he trembles for the rest of his estate.

It's quite plain.

And believe me, my friend: when it's someone else's money to be spent, you'll find the drone's desires are in him after all.

And strong in him, too.

A man like this, then, isn't free of faction inside himself. He isn't one man but somehow two — though for the most part his better desires master his worse ones.

True.

And for that reason he'll be more presentable than many. But the true virtue of a soul in harmony with itself, one and undivided, escapes him by a long way.

I'd expect so.

And the thrifty man makes a feeble competitor, in his own person, for any prize of victory in the city or any other honorable distinction. He won't spend money for the sake of glory — he's terrified of waking his spending desires and calling them in as allies for the contest — so he fights like a true oligarch, with a small part of his forces, and mostly loses. And stays rich.

He does.

Then can we still doubt, I said, that the thrifty moneymaker takes his place beside the oligarchic city as its likeness?

Not at all.

Next, it seems, we should look at democracy — how it comes about, and what it's like once it has — so that when we know the character of the matching man in his turn, we can bring him up for judgment.

That would keep to our method, he said.

Well then, I said, doesn't the city change from oligarchy to democracy in some such way as this — through the insatiable pursuit of the good it set before itself, the demand to become as rich as possible?

How so?

Since the men who rule it rule because they own so much, I suppose they're unwilling to restrain by law those of the young who turn profligate — unwilling to stop them spending and wasting what they have — because it's by buying up such men's property and lending against it at interest that they become richer and more honored still.

Nothing plainer.

And isn't it plain by now that honoring wealth and having any adequate self-command can't coexist in the citizens of a city? One or the other has to be let go.

Fairly plain, he said.

So in oligarchies, by this indulgence — this license — men of good family are sometimes driven all the way down to poverty.

Often.

And there they sit in the city, I suppose — stung, and armed: some in debt, some stripped of their rights, some both — hating the men who now hold what was theirs, plotting against them and against everyone else, hungry for revolution.

That's so.

Meanwhile the money-men, hunched over their business, seeming not even to see the men they've already ruined, keep injecting their silver into anyone else in the crowd who yields — wounding him with it — and collecting interest, that many-times-multiplied child of the parent sum. So they make the drone and the beggar swarm in the city.

Swarm they do, he said.

And the evil blazes on like a fire, I said, and they won't put it out — not by the one way, barring a man from doing whatever he likes with his own property, and not by the other, the law that would dissolve abuses of this kind.

What law?

The one that's second-best after that — an ordinance that compels the citizens to care about their character. Make the rule that voluntary contracts are entered, in general, at the lender's own risk, and men would lend with less shamelessness in the city, and fewer of the evils we've been describing would grow there.

Far fewer.

But as it is, I said, for all the reasons I've named, the rulers reduce the ruled to this condition. And as for themselves and their own — don't their young grow luxurious, idle in body and mind, too soft to hold out against pleasure or pain, and lazy besides?

What else?

And the men themselves neglect everything but making money, and give no more real attention to virtue than the paupers do.

No more.

So they're formed. And when rulers and ruled in this condition run into one another — on the road, or in some shared undertaking, a festival embassy or a campaign, shipmates or fellow soldiers — and watch each other in the moment of danger itself, where the poor man is in no way despised by the rich; when a lean, sunburnt poor man, posted in battle beside a rich man raised in the shade and carrying plenty of borrowed flesh, watches him gasping, helpless — don't you think he concludes that men like this are rich because the poor are cowards? And when the poor meet in private, isn't the word that passes among them: 'These men are ours for the taking. They are nothing'?

I know very well that's what they do, he said.

Then just as a sickly body needs only a small push from outside to fall ill — and sometimes falls into disorder with no push at all — doesn't a city in this condition fall sick on the slightest pretext and go to war with itself, one side importing allies from an oligarchic city, the other from a democratic one? And sometimes doesn't faction seize it with no outsiders at all?

Violently so.

Then democracy is born, I take it, when the poor win: when they kill some of the other side and exile others, and share the regime and its offices equally with the rest — and for the most part, the offices in it are assigned by lot.

Yes, he said, that's how democracy is established — whether it's done by arms, or the other side takes fright and slips away.

And how do these people live? What is this regime like in its turn? Clearly the man who matches it will turn out to be the democratic man.

Clearly, he said.

First of all, aren't they free? Doesn't the city fill up with freedom and free speech — with license to do whatever you want in it?

So it's said, at any rate.

And where that license exists, plainly each man arranges his own life inside the city however it pleases him.

Plainly.

Then this regime, I take it, would grow more varieties of human being than any other.

Of course.

It may be the most beautiful of the regimes, I said. Like a many-colored cloak embroidered with every kind of flower, this city, embroidered with every kind of character, might well look the most beautiful of all. And no doubt, I said, many people would judge it so — the way women and children judge, when they see bright things in every color.

Many would.

And it's the convenient place, my friend, to go looking for a constitution.

How so?

Because its license means it has every kind of constitution in it. Anyone wanting to found a city, as we've just been doing, should probably go to a democratic city first, as to a general store of constitutions, and pick out whichever pattern suits him — and then do his founding.

He wouldn't be short of models, at least.

And there's no compulsion to rule in this city, even if you're capable of ruling, nor to be ruled if you don't feel like it; no compulsion to be at war when the city's at war, or to keep the peace when everyone else keeps it, if you don't want peace; and if some law bars you from office or from a jury — no compulsion not to hold office and sit on juries anyway, if the mood takes you. Isn't that, for the moment, a divine and delicious way to live?

For the moment, he said, perhaps.

And isn't the gentleness of the place toward convicted men exquisite? Or haven't you seen it in a regime like this — men sentenced to death or exile staying on regardless, hanging about in the middle of everything, and the man strolling the streets like a hero, as if no one saw and no one cared?

I've seen plenty, he replied.

And its forgivingness — the total absence of pettiness — its contempt for everything we said so solemnly when we were founding our city: that unless a man's nature were exceptional, he could never become good unless from earliest childhood his play was among beautiful things and his practices all of that kind. How magnificently it tramples all this underfoot, without a thought for what practices a man was raised in before he entered public life — ready to honor him if only he says he's the people's friend.

Very noble of it, he said.

These traits, then, and their cousins, belong to democracy. A pleasant regime, you'd say — no one in charge, every color in the cloth, dealing out a kind of equality to equals and unequals alike.

Nothing you're saying is unfamiliar, he said.

Then look at the man himself, the private version. Or should we first ask, as we did with the regime, how he comes about?

Yes, he said.

Isn't it like this? He'd be the son, I take it, of that thrifty oligarchic man — raised by his father in his father's habits.

Naturally.

So he too rules his pleasures by force — the spending kind, the ones that don't earn, the ones called unnecessary.

Obviously.

Then so we're not arguing in the dark, do you want us first to mark off which desires are necessary and which are not?

I do.

Wouldn't 'necessary' rightly mean the ones we can't turn aside, and all the ones whose satisfaction does us good? Our nature is compelled to reach for both kinds — isn't it?

Certainly.

So we'll be right to call these necessary.

We will.

And the ones a man could be rid of if he trained against them from youth — the ones whose presence does no good, and in some cases does real harm — if we said all of these are unnecessary, we'd be saying it well?

Well indeed.

Shall we pick an example of each, so we have a general picture of them?

We should.

Wouldn't the desire to eat — plain food, and something to eat it with, as far as health and fitness require — be a necessary one?

I'd think so.

The desire for food itself is necessary on both counts: it does us good, and if it goes unmet, life stops.

Yes.

But the desire for relish is necessary only insofar as it serves fitness.

Certainly.

And the desire that goes past that — for delicacies beyond plain fare — a desire most people can be rid of if it's checked and educated young, and that is harmful to the body, and harmful to the soul in its pursuit of wisdom and self-command: wouldn't that rightly be called unnecessary?

Entirely rightly.

And shouldn't we say the unnecessary desires are spenders, while the necessary ones earn — since they're useful for the work itself?

Surely.

And the same holds for the desires of sex, and for the rest?

The same.

And the man we just now named a drone — didn't we mean the man crammed with pleasures and desires of this kind, ruled by the unnecessary ones, while the man ruled by the necessary was the thrifty oligarch?

We did.

Now back to how the democratic man grows out of the oligarchic. It seems to me it usually happens like this.

Like what?

When a young man raised the way we just described — without education, and on a tight rein — first tastes the drones' honey, and falls in with fierce and dangerous beasts who can supply him with pleasures of every color and kind and complication: there, you can be sure, is the beginning of his change, from an oligarchy inside him to a democracy.

It can't help but be.

And just as the city changed when one of its parties got help from like-minded allies outside, doesn't the young man change when one class of desires in him gets help from a kindred class outside — like reinforcing like?

Exactly.

And if a counter-alliance comes to the aid of the oligarchic part of him — from his father, say, or the rest of his family, warning and scolding — then there's faction in him, and counter-faction, and a battle with himself.

There is.

And sometimes, I suppose, the democratic party gives ground to the oligarchic: some of the desires are put down, others driven out; a kind of shame rises in the young man's soul, and order is restored.

That does happen sometimes, he said.

But then, as the old desires are driven out, new ones — kin to them — are quietly reared in their place, and because the father doesn't know how to bring anyone up, they grow many and strong.

That's usually the way, he said.

They drag him back to the old crowd, and breeding in secret, they multiply.

True.

Until at last, I suppose, they capture the acropolis of the young man's soul — having found it empty of learning, of beautiful practices, of true accounts: the best watchmen and guardians there are in the minds of men the gods love.

By far the best.

And into the emptiness march false and swaggering words and opinions, and take the citadel in their place.

They do indeed.

So back he goes to live among the Lotus-eaters — openly now. And if reinforcements arrive from his family for the thrifty part of his soul, those swaggering words shut the gates of the royal wall against them: they won't let the allies in, and they won't so much as receive the wise old counsel of private men sent as envoys. They win the battle themselves. Shame — which they rename silliness — is thrown out, a dishonored fugitive; self-command — which they call cowardice — is dragged through the mud and pitched over the border; and measure and orderly spending they persuade him are rustic and beneath a free man, and with the help of a mob of useless desires they drive them out of the country.

That they do.

And once they've emptied and purged the soul they hold — the soul they are initiating, now, into their great mysteries — the next step is to lead insolence and anarchy and waste and shamelessness home from exile: in torchlight, garlanded, with a great chorus behind them, singing their praises and calling them by sweet names. Insolence is now good breeding; anarchy is freedom; waste is magnificence; shamelessness is courage. Isn't that roughly how a young man raised among the necessary desires changes into one who frees and releases the useless, unnecessary pleasures?

Yes, he said — you've made the change vivid enough.

After that, I take it, he lives spending money and effort and time on the unnecessary pleasures no less than the necessary ones. If he's lucky, and the frenzy doesn't run through him unchecked — if, as he gets older and the great commotion has passed, he readmits some of the exiled party and doesn't surrender himself entirely to the invaders — then he settles his pleasures into a kind of equality, and lives handing the rule of himself to whichever one comes along, as though it had drawn the lot for office, until it's satisfied; then to another, despising none of them, feeding them all alike.

Just so.

And a true account, I said, he keeps outside the walls — he won't receive it, won't let it into the guardhouse. If someone tells him that some pleasures belong to fine and good desires and others to bad ones, and that the first should be practiced and honored and the second checked and enslaved, he shakes his head at all of it and says one pleasure is as good as another, and every one deserves equal honor.

That's exactly what a man in that condition does.

And so he lives out his days, I said, indulging whatever appetite comes along. One day he's drunk, with pipes playing at the party; the next it's plain water and a diet. Now it's exercise; then a stretch of idleness in which nothing matters; then a spell of what passes for philosophy. Every so often it's politics — up he jumps, and says whatever occurs to him, and does it too. If he catches admiration for military men, he swings that way; if for businessmen, then their way instead. There's no order in his life and no necessity, but he calls this life sweet and free and blessed, and he keeps it to the end.

You've described the life, he replied, of a man for whom everything has equal rights.

And I suppose he's a man of every color and kind, I said — stuffed with more characters than any other, beautiful and various like his city. Many a man and many a woman would envy his life, with its endless samples of constitutions and characters inside it.

That's the man.

Then let him take his place opposite democracy — the one who'd rightly be called the democratic man.

Let him, he said.

Now for the most beautiful regime, I said, and the most beautiful man, still left to describe: tyranny, and the tyrant.

So they are, he said.

Come then, dear friend — how does tyranny arrive? That it changes out of democracy is fairly obvious.

It is.

And doesn't tyranny come out of democracy somewhat the way democracy came out of oligarchy?

How do you mean?

The good that oligarchy set before itself — the thing the regime was built on — was wealth. Yes?

Yes.

And it was the insatiable appetite for wealth, and the neglect of everything else for the sake of moneymaking, that destroyed it.

True.

Then does democracy too define a good for itself — and does the insatiable appetite for that dissolve democracy in its turn?

What good do you mean?

Freedom, I said. In a democratic city you'll hear that this is its finest possession, and that this is why it's the only city worth living in for a man who is free by nature.

Yes — you hear that line everywhere.

Well then, as I started to say: isn't it the insatiable appetite for freedom, and the neglect of everything else, that transforms this regime too — and sets it up to need a tyrant?

How?

When a democratic city, thirsty for freedom, gets bad cupbearers pouring for it, and drinks past the point of drunkenness on freedom taken neat, then — unless its rulers are utterly mild and hand out liberty in quantity — it punishes them, and accuses them of being filthy oligarchs.

Yes, he said — that they do.

And the men who obey the rulers it drags through the mud as willing slaves and nobodies; the rulers it praises and honors, in public and in private, are the ones who act like subjects — and the subjects who act like rulers. In a city like that, doesn't freedom have to run to its limit?

How could it not?

And it seeps down, my friend, into the private houses — until in the end the anarchy takes root even in the animals.

What do you mean by that?

I mean that a father gets used to being like his child, and to fearing his sons; and a son gets used to being like his father, with no respect and no fear of his parents — that's how he proves he's free. Resident alien is the equal of citizen, citizen of resident alien, and the foreigner is as good as either.

Yes, that's how it goes.

That, and other small evils like it. In such a place the teacher fears his students and flatters them, and the students hold their teachers cheap — their tutors too. The young in general ape their elders and compete with them in word and deed; and the old lower themselves to the level of the young, brimming with wit and charm, imitating them so as not to seem disagreeable or despotic.

Quite true, he said.

And the far edge of the crowd's freedom is reached, my friend, when bought slaves, men and women, are as free as their buyers. And the equality of women with men and men with women — the full liberty on both sides — I almost forgot to mention.

Then, as Aeschylus says, shouldn't we 'speak what was on our lips just now'?

By all means, I said — that's what I'm doing. No one who hadn't seen it would believe how much freer even the animals under human ownership are here than anywhere else. The dogs come to be just like their mistresses — the proverb has it exactly — and there are horses and donkeys in the habit of marching down the road at full liberty and full dignity, barging into whoever meets them and doesn't step aside. And everything else is just as bloated with freedom.

You're telling me my own dream, he said. It happens to me every time I walk out to the country.

And the sum of it all, when you add it up, is this: it makes the souls of the citizens so tender that at the least whiff of servitude they're furious and won't stand it. In the end, as you know, they stop caring even about the laws, written or unwritten — so that no one, in any way, will be master over them.

I know it very well, he said.

This, then, my friend, I said — so beautiful, so full of youth — is the beginning out of which tyranny grows, as it seems to me.

Full of youth indeed, he said. But what comes next?

The same disease, I said, that broke out in oligarchy and destroyed it breaks out here too — wider and stronger, because of the license — and enslaves democracy. And in fact, doing anything to excess likes to repay us with a great swing to the opposite side: in the seasons, in plants, in bodies, and in regimes not least.

So one would expect.

Too much freedom, it seems, can collapse into nothing else but too much slavery — for a man and for a city alike.

Yes — that follows.

Then tyranny, it's fair to guess, is established out of no other regime than democracy: out of the summit of freedom, the largest and most savage slavery.

That stands to reason, he said.

But that wasn't your question, I think, I said. You asked what the disease is that grows in oligarchy and in democracy alike, and enslaves the second.

True, he replied.

I meant, then, that breed of idle and extravagant men — the boldest of them leading, the more timid following. We compared them to drones: the leaders to drones with stings, the followers to drones without.

Rightly, too.

These two kinds, I said, make trouble in any regime they appear in, the way phlegm and bile make trouble in a body. And the good physician and lawgiver of a city must take his precautions far in advance, like a shrewd beekeeper — best of all so they never appear; but if they do appear, to have them cut out as fast as possible, cells and all.

Yes, by Zeus — completely.

Then let's take it this way, I said, so we can see what we're after more distinctly: divide the democratic city in speech into the three parts it actually has. One part is this breed of drones, which grows in it no less than in the oligarchic city, because of the general license.

So it does.

But it's far fiercer here than it was there.

How so?

There, because it's shut out of office and kept from exercise, it stays unpracticed and weak. In a democracy, with few exceptions, this breed is the presiding class of the city: its fiercest part talks and acts, while the rest settle around the speaker's platform, buzzing, and won't tolerate a word from the other side. So in a regime like this, everything, apart from a few matters, is managed by the drones.

Quite so, he said.

Then there's a second group that keeps separating itself out of the mass.

Which?

When everyone's busy making money, the most orderly natures generally end up the richest.

Probably.

And there, I take it, is the deepest honey for the drones — and the easiest to squeeze out.

Well, he said, who could squeeze it out of people who have next to nothing?

So 'the rich' is what this class is called — the drones' pasture.

More or less, he said.

And the third class is the people — everyone who works with his own hands, stays out of politics, and owns very little. This is the largest class in a democracy, and once assembled, the most powerful thing in it.

It is, he said — but it won't assemble often unless it gets some share of the honey.

And it does get a share, I said — as much as its leaders can manage, stripping the men of property of what they own and distributing it to the people, while keeping the largest cut for themselves.

Yes, he said — that's the kind of share it gets.

And the men being stripped are forced to defend themselves, I suppose — speaking before the people, acting however they can.

What else can they do?

For which the other side accuses them — even if they have no appetite for revolution at all — of plotting against the people, of being oligarchs.

Of course.

Until in the end, when they see that the people — not out of malice but out of ignorance, deceived by the men who slander them — are truly set on wronging them, then, like it or not, they become oligarchs in earnest. Not willingly: that evil too is bred by the drone's sting.

Precisely.

Then come impeachments and prosecutions and trials, each side against the other.

Yes.

And isn't it always the people's way to put one man at its head as its special champion — to feed him, and grow him great?

It is.

Then this much is plain, I said: whenever a tyrant sprouts, it's from this root, the people's champion, and from nowhere else.

Very plain.

So how does the change begin — champion into tyrant? Isn't it obviously when the champion starts doing what the story tells of at the shrine of Lycaean Zeus in Arcadia?

What story?

That whoever tastes the one piece of human innards minced in among the innards of the other sacrifices must turn into a wolf. Haven't you heard it?

I have.

It's the same with the people's champion. Once the mob obeys him in everything, he doesn't hold back from a kinsman's blood. He hauls a man into court on the usual false charges and murders him — blotting out a human life, tasting kindred blood with unholy tongue and lips. He exiles, he kills; and all the while he drops hints about the cancellation of debts and the redistribution of land. After that, isn't such a man bound by necessity — isn't it his fate — either to be destroyed by his enemies, or to rule as tyrant: to turn from a man into a wolf?

It's inevitable.

He, then, is the one who raises the faction against the men of property.

He is.

And if he's driven into exile and comes back in spite of his enemies, he comes back a finished tyrant.

Clearly.

And if they can't manage to expel him, or to have him put to death by turning the city against him, they plot to kill him quietly, by violence.

That's the usual course, he said.

And then comes the demand that everyone who has reached this stage discovers — the famous tyrant's request: a bodyguard, so that the people's helper may be kept safe for the people.

Exactly.

And they give it to him, I suppose — afraid for him, and serene about themselves.

Very much so.

And when a man with money — money, and with it the reputation of hating the people — sees this, then, my friend, in the words of the oracle to Croesus, he

'flees along the pebbled Hermus, does not stay, and is not ashamed to be a coward.'

Quite right, he said — he'd never get a second chance to be ashamed.

True, I said — anyone caught is put to death.

He has to be.

As for the champion himself — obviously he doesn't lie 'mighty in his mightiness' in the dust. He has toppled many a rival, and he stands upright in the chariot of the city, champion no longer: the finished, absolute tyrant.

What else, he said.

Then shall we describe the happiness of this man, I said — and of the city in which such a mortal arises?

Yes, he said, let's describe it.

In his first days — his early season — doesn't he have a smile and a greeting for everyone he meets? He says he's no tyrant; he promises everything, in private and in public; he forgives debts and parcels out land, to the people and to the men around him; he plays at being gracious and gentle with all.

He has to, he said.

But once he's settled with his enemies abroad — come to terms with some, destroyed the rest — and that front is quiet, his first move is to keep stirring up one war after another, so that the people will keep needing a leader.

Naturally.

And for another reason too, isn't it — so that the taxes will impoverish them, force them down to the day's needs, and leave them less leisure to plot against him?

Clearly.

And if he suspects certain men of free thoughts — of not intending to leave the ruling to him — hasn't he a ready pretext for destroying them: hand them to the enemy? For all these reasons a tyrant is compelled to be forever stirring up war.

He is.

And doing all this, he becomes, day by day, more hateful to the citizens.

Of course.

And then some of the men who helped raise him up — men who hold power of their own — start speaking their minds, to him and to one another, and the boldest of them criticize what's happening to his face.

Likely enough.

So the tyrant, if he means to keep ruling, must quietly remove them all, until he has no one left, friend or enemy, who is worth anything.

Obviously.

He must keep a sharp eye out, then, for who is brave, who is high-minded, who is intelligent, who is rich. And such is his happiness that he must be the enemy of all of them, whether he wants it or not, and plot against them until he has purged the city.

A fine purge, he said.

Yes, I said — the opposite of the one physicians give a body. They draw off the worst and leave the best; he does the reverse.

It seems he has no choice, he said — if he's going to rule.

A blessed necessity binds him, then, I said: live with the mob of the worthless, and be hated by them even so — or not live at all.

That's his necessity.

And the more the citizens hate him for all this, the more bodyguards he'll need, and the more loyal ones?

Certainly.

And who are the loyal? Where will he send for them?

They'll come flying on their own, he said — plenty of them — if he pays the wage.

By the dog, I said — drones again, I think you're telling me. Foreign ones, of every stripe.

You're right; that's what I'm telling you.

But locally — wouldn't he be willing to recruit on the spot?

Meaning what?

To take their slaves away from the citizens, free them, and enroll them in the bodyguard around him.

Absolutely, he said — since they're actually the most loyal men he has.

What a blessed thing you make the tyrant out to be, I said, if these are the friends and the faithful he keeps once he's destroyed the ones he had before.

Well, he said, these are what he keeps.

And these companions admire him, I said, and the new citizens keep his company — while the decent hate him, and keep out of his way.

What else would they do?

No wonder, I said, that tragedy has the general name of wisdom — and Euripides the name of its master.

Why?

Because among the rest, he produced this piece of concentrated thinking:

'tyrants grow wise by keeping company with the wise.'

— and he clearly meant that the wise are the company we've just seen the tyrant keeping.

Yes, he said — and he praises tyranny as 'godlike' too, along with much else; and the other poets do the same.

Then surely, I said, being wise, the tragic poets will forgive us — us and everyone whose regime resembles ours — for refusing them entry into the city, seeing that they sing tyranny's praises.

I think the subtle ones among them will forgive us, he said.

Meanwhile, I imagine, they'll make the rounds of the other cities, drawing the crowds, hiring voices — beautiful, big, persuasive voices — and pulling the regimes toward tyrannies and democracies.

So they will.

And they're paid for it, and honored for it — most of all by tyrants, as you'd expect, and second by democracy. But the higher they climb up the slope of the regimes, the more their honor gives out, as if it were short of breath and could go no further.

Quite so.

But we've wandered, I said. Back to the tyrant's camp — that beautiful, numerous, many-colored, ever-changing army of his. How is it fed?

Obviously, he said, he'll spend whatever sacred treasure is in the city, as long as it holds out, together with the property of the men he has destroyed — so that the levies he forces on the people can stay smaller.

And when that runs out?

Then clearly, he said, he'll feed off his father's estate — he, and his drinking companions, his men and his mistresses.

I understand, I said. The people, which fathered the tyrant, will feed him and his companions.

It will have no choice, he said.

And what do you say to this? I said. Suppose the people get angry, and say a grown son has no business being fed by his father — the feeding should run the other way; that a father doesn't beget a son and set him up so that, once the son has grown great, the father must serve his own slaves and feed his son along with the slaves and the whole rabble around him — but so that with this champion at its head it might be freed from the rich and the so-called gentlemen of the city. Suppose the people now order him out, him and his friends, the way a father drives a son and his rowdy drinking crowd out of the house.

Then, by Zeus, he said, the people will find out what it is — what a creature it fathered and welcomed and grew great — and that it is the weaker trying to drive out the stronger.

What do you mean? I said. Will the tyrant dare to lay hands on his father — to beat him if he doesn't comply?

Yes, he said — once he's taken away his weapons.

A father-beater, then, I said — a tyrant makes a harsh nurse of old age. And this, it seems, is tyranny now in the open, acknowledged for what it is; and the people, as the saying goes, fleeing the smoke of enslavement to free men, has fallen into the fire of a despotism of slaves. In place of that abundant, unseasonable freedom, it has pulled on the harshest and bitterest of slaveries — slavery to slaves.

That's exactly what happens, he said.

Well then, I said — will anyone quarrel with us if we say we've adequately described how tyranny evolves out of democracy, and what it is once it has arrived?

We've described it quite adequately, he said.
