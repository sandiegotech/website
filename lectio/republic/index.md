# The Republic

Plato

- 375 BCE
- Western tradition
- Philosophy
- Lectio Edition 2026

Plato's dialogue on justice. Socrates asks what justice is and whether it is worth having, and to answer he builds a city in words. The Cave, the divided line and the philosopher-king all come from this book.

## It is a conversation, and it should read like one.

Benjamin Jowett's translation of 1871 is lucid and long-lived. It is also Victorian. His speakers swear "By heaven", his Cave is a "den", and his key words have moved since he chose them. "Temperance" now means not drinking. "Idea" now means a thought in someone's head.

The Lectio Edition renders all ten books as talk between people, in current English, and fixes one English word for each term the argument turns on. Jowett stays in the app beside it.

## How it begins.

> **Book I · What Is Justice?**

> I went down to the Piraeus yesterday with Glaucon, the son of Ariston, to offer my prayers to the goddess, and to see how they would put on the festival, this being the first time it was held. I thought the procession our own people made was beautiful, though the one the Thracians marched in looked every bit as good. We said our prayers, watched the spectacle, and set off back toward the city. And just then Polemarchus, the son of Cephalus, caught sight of us from a distance, starting on the road home, and sent his slave running ahead to tell us to wait for him. The boy caught my cloak from behind and said: Polemarchus asks you to wait.

> I turned around and asked where his master was.

> There he is, said the boy — coming up behind you. Just wait.

> Of course we'll wait, said Glaucon; and in a few minutes Polemarchus came up, with Adeimantus, Glaucon's brother, Niceratus the son of Nicias, and several others, all on their way from the procession.

The first 176 words of the Lectio Edition, as they stand in the bundle the app reads.

## What this edition decided.

The review found that this edition's terms drifted from book to book. These are the rules that now hold.

- Explanations are never printed as Plato's text. A gloss on a name, a pun or an allusion comes out of the sentence and goes to the margin notes. The review found a translator's note inside the Muses' speech in Book VIII.
- The four virtues are wisdom, courage, self-command and justice. *Sophrosyne* had been "self-discipline", "self-restraint" and "temperance" in different books.
- *Gymnastike* is "physical training". "Gymnastics" now means a sport.
- When the Greek swears by Zeus, the English says "by Zeus". "God" and "Good lord" are not used as exclamations.
- A line of Homer quoted twice has one wording.
- Words that date the text came out: "girlfriend", "the haves", "consumer", "payroll", "the gym".

### Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 30 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 7. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

### τὸ τοῦ κρείττονος συμφέρον

Book I, 338c

**Older:** "justice is nothing else than the interest of the stronger" (Jowett)

**Lectio:** justice is nothing other than the advantage of the stronger

"Interest" has drifted toward finance and polite concern. "Advantage" keeps Thrasymachus as blunt as Plato makes him.

### γενναῖόν τι ἓν ψευδομένους

Book III, 414b

**Older:** "just one royal lie" (Jowett)

**Lectio:** One noble lie

*Gennaios* means well-born. Plato's phrase is a contradiction on purpose, a lie of good family. "Royal" names who hears the lie. "Noble" keeps the discomfort.

### σωφροσύνη (*sophrosyne*)

Book IV and throughout

**Older:** "temperance" (Jowett)

**Lectio:** self-command

Plato explains the word by the phrase "stronger than himself": the better part of a man holding the worse. "Self-command" keeps that in view each time.

### ἐὰν μὴ οἱ φιλόσοφοι βασιλεύσωσιν

Book V, 473c

**Older:** "Until philosophers are kings" (Jowett)

**Lectio:** Unless philosophers become kings in our cities

The Greek is a hard condition and promises nothing. "Until" tells the reader the day is coming. Plato does not say so.

### καταγείῳ οἰκήσει σπηλαιώδει

Book VII, 514a

**Older:** "an underground den" (Jowett)

**Lectio:** a kind of cave

A den is where an animal lives. The image depends on its being a place where people live and which they take for the world.

### ἡ τοῦ ἀγαθοῦ ἰδέα

Book VII

**Older:** "the idea of good" (Jowett)

**Lectio:** the form of the good

To a modern ear an idea is a thought. Plato means the opposite: the thing most real, seen last and with effort.

### δαίμων (*daimon*)

Book X, 617e

**Older:** "genius" (Jowett)

**Lectio:** guardian spirit

"Genius" now means talent. The *daimon* is the spirit that goes with a life. In the myth of Er the soul chooses its own, which is why the blame is the chooser's.

### Still open

What the first review left for the editor to decide or restore.

- The editor's own read of all ten books.
- Book VII: short replies now use contractions, as in the books around it. The long speeches do not. The rest needs to be settled by ear.
- Three places where "Honestly" may stand for an oath in the Greek. The Greek has not been checked.
- One gloss is still in the text at 392d, and a note defends it. Decide whether it stays.
- The explanations taken out of the text (Bendis, the nuptial number, the 729 calculation and others) have not yet been written into the margin notes.
- One note quotes "the turning around of the soul" where the text has "of a soul".
