# The Republic, Book VII

The Cave

Next, I said, compare our nature — how far it is educated, how far not — to a condition like this. Picture people in a home under the earth, a kind of cave, with a long entrance rising toward the daylight, as wide as the cave itself. They have been there since childhood, legs and necks in chains, so that they stay put and look only at what is in front of them — the chains keep them from turning their heads. Their light comes from a fire burning far above and behind them, and between the fire and the prisoners, higher up, there runs a road, with a low wall built along it, like the screen a puppeteer sets between himself and his audience, over which he shows the puppets.

I see.

Then see also men carrying things along that wall — all kinds of objects that stick up over it: vessels, and statues of people and animals in stone and wood and every sort of material. Some of the carriers, as you would expect, are talking; others are silent.

A strange image, he said, and strange prisoners.

Like us, I said. Because first of all, do you think such men would have seen anything of themselves and one another except the shadows the fire throws on the cave wall in front of them?

How could they, he said, if they were forced to keep their heads still their whole lives?

And the things being carried past — isn't it the same? They would see only the shadows?

Of course.

So if they could talk with one another, don't you think they would take the shadows they saw for the real things they were naming?

They'd have to.

And suppose the prison had an echo from the wall facing them. Whenever one of the men passing behind spoke, do you think they would take the voice to be anything but the passing shadow's?

Nothing else, he said.

Then to men like that, I said, the truth would be nothing else than the shadows of the carried objects.

It would have to be.

Now watch, I said, what their release from the chains and their cure from this folly would be like, if something of the sort naturally happened to them. Suppose one of them were set free and suddenly made to stand up, turn his neck, walk, and lift his eyes toward the light. Every movement would hurt, and the glare would leave him unable to make out the things whose shadows he used to see. What do you think he would say if someone told him that all this time he had been looking at rubbish — that now he is closer to what is, turned toward things that are more real, and seeing more truly? And if his instructor pointed to each of the things passing by and pressed him to answer what it was — don't you think he would be at a loss, and believe that what he saw before was truer than what is being shown to him now?

Much truer.

And if he were forced to look at the light itself, wouldn't it hurt his eyes? Wouldn't he turn and run back to the things he can see, convinced that those really are clearer than what is being shown to him?

He would.

And suppose, I said, someone dragged him out of there by force, up the rough, steep climb, and did not let go until he had hauled him into the light of the sun. Wouldn't he suffer, and resent being dragged? And when he came into the light, his eyes flooded with the glare, he would not be able to see a single one of the things now called real.

Not at first, anyway, he said.

He would need time to adjust, I think, before he could see the things above. At first he would make out shadows most easily; then the reflections of people and other things in water; then the things themselves. After that he would find it easier to look at what is in the sky, and at the sky itself, by night — the light of the stars and the moon — than at the sun and the sun's light by day.

Of course.

And last of all, I suppose, he would be able to look at the sun — not its reflection in water or in some borrowed place, but the sun itself, by itself, in its own region — and see it as it is.

Necessarily.

And by then he would be reasoning about it: that this is what gives the seasons and the years, and governs everything in the visible world, and is in a way the cause of all the things he and his companions used to see.

Clearly, he said, that's where he would come next.

Well then. When he remembered his first home, and the wisdom of that place, and his fellow prisoners, don't you think he would count himself happy in the change, and pity them?

Very much so.

And if they had held honors and prizes among themselves back then — awards for whoever saw the passing shadows most sharply, and best remembered which of them usually came first, which later, and which together, and so was best at guessing what would come next — do you think he would crave those prizes, or envy the men the prisoners honored and put in power? Wouldn't he rather feel what Homer says — that it is

'better to work the earth as a hired hand for some other man, a man with nothing of his own,'

and endure anything at all, rather than believe what they believe and live the way they live?

Yes, he said. I think he would suffer anything rather than live like that.

Then think about this too, I said. If a man like that went back down and sat in his old seat, wouldn't his eyes be filled with darkness, coming suddenly out of the sun?

They certainly would.

And suppose he had to compete again at judging those shadows, against men who had never left their chains, while his sight was still dim and his eyes had not yet settled — and the resettling might take no short time. Wouldn't he be laughed at? Wouldn't they say he had gone up and come back down with his eyes ruined, and that it is not worth even trying the climb? And the man who tried to free them and lead them up — if they could somehow get their hands on him and kill him, wouldn't they kill him?

They would, he said.

Now this whole image, dear Glaucon, I said, must be fitted to everything we said before. The world our eyes show us is the prison home, and the firelight inside it is the power of the sun. If you set the climb upward and the sight of the things above as the soul's ascent into the intelligible world, you will have my hope of it right — since that is what you wanted to hear. Whether it is true, a god knows. But this is how it appears to me: in the world of what can be known, the form of the good is seen last, and seen only with effort; and once it is seen, the conclusion must be that it is the cause of everything right and beautiful in anything — in the visible world it fathers light and the lord of light, and in the intelligible world it is itself sovereign, the source of truth and understanding — and that anyone who means to act sensibly, in private or in public, must have seen it.

I agree, he said, as far as I can follow you.

Then agree with me on this too, I said, and don't be surprised that those who have climbed that high are unwilling to busy themselves with human affairs — their souls keep pressing to spend their time above. That is natural, if our image holds.

It is, he said.

And is it any wonder, I said, if a man who comes from divine contemplations to the miseries of human life cuts a poor figure and looks completely ridiculous — while his eyes are still blinking, before he has grown used to the darkness around him — when he is forced to fight, in law courts or anywhere else, about the shadows of justice, or the statues that cast those shadows, and to argue over how these things are understood by people who have never seen justice itself?

No wonder at all, he said.

But anyone with sense, I said, would remember that the eyes are confused in two ways, from two causes: moving from light into darkness, and moving from darkness into light. He would realize that the same holds for the soul, and when he saw one confused and unable to make something out, he would not laugh mindlessly. He would ask first whether it had come from a brighter life and was dark from being out of its element, or had come out of deeper ignorance into a brighter place and was dazzled by the flood of light. The first he would count happy in what it had lived and seen; the second he would pity — and if he did want to laugh, the laugh would fall less absurdly on the soul coming up from below into the light than on the one coming down from above.

That's a fair way to put it, he said.

Then if this is true, I said, we have to conclude that education is not what some of the people who profess to teach it say it is. They claim, more or less, that there is no knowledge in the soul and that they put it in — like putting sight into blind eyes.

So they claim, he said.

But our argument shows, I said, that the power to learn is already in every soul, along with the instrument each of us learns with. Just as an eye cannot be turned from darkness to light except together with the whole body, so this instrument must be wheeled around, together with the whole soul, away from the world of becoming, until it can bear to look at what is — and at the brightest of what is, which we say is the good. Isn't that so?

Yes.

Then wouldn't there be an art of just this, I said — of the turning around: how the soul can be turned most easily and effectively? Not an art of putting sight into it — it has sight already — but it faces the wrong way, looking where it should not, and that is what this art would work on.

So it seems.

Now the other so-called virtues of the soul do seem close to the virtues of the body — where they are absent at first, they really can be built in later by habit and practice. But the virtue of understanding, it seems, belongs to something more divine, which never loses its power, and which becomes useful and beneficial, or useless and harmful, depending on which way it is turned. Haven't you noticed, in men called clever but vicious, how sharply their little soul sees, how keenly it makes out the things it is turned toward? Its sight is not weak. It has been forced into the service of vice, so the sharper it sees, the more harm it does.

I certainly have, he said.

And yet, I said, if a nature like that had been pruned back in earliest childhood — cut free of the leaden weights of becoming, the weights that feasting and gluttony and pleasures of that kind bolt onto the soul and that drag its vision downward — if it had been freed of all that and turned toward what is true, the very same faculty in the very same men would have seen the truth as sharply as it now sees the things it is turned to.

Very likely.

And here is another thing that is likely, I said — in fact it follows necessarily from what we have said: a city will never be well governed by the uneducated, who have no experience of truth, nor by men who are allowed to spend their whole lives in education. Not the first, because they have no single target in life at which everything they do, in private and in public, must aim. Not the second, because they will not act if they can help it — they think they have already moved out to the Isles of the Blest.

True, he replied.

Then it is our job, I said — the job of the founders — to compel the best natures to reach the study we said before was the greatest: to see the good, to make that climb; and once they have climbed and seen enough, not to allow them what is allowed now.

What is that?

Staying up there, I said — refusing to go back down among the prisoners, or to share their labors and their honors, whether those honors are worth much or little.

But won't we be wronging them? he said. Won't we make them live a worse life, when a better one is open to them?

You have forgotten again, my friend, I said, that the law is not concerned with making any one class in the city especially happy. It works to bring happiness about in the whole city, holding the citizens together by persuasion and by necessity, making them share with one another the benefit each can bring to the community. The law itself makes men like this in the city — not so that each can turn wherever he pleases, but so that the law can use them to bind the city into one.

True, he said. I had forgotten.

Then notice, Glaucon, I said, that we won't be wronging the philosophers who grow up among us — we will be saying only what is just when we compel them to care for the others and stand guard over them. We will tell them: in other cities, men of your kind can reasonably refuse a share in the city's labors, because there they spring up on their own, against the will of the regime; and what grows by itself, owing its food to no one, is right to feel no debt for its upbringing. But you we bred deliberately — for yourselves and for the rest of the city, like leaders and kings in a hive — educated better and more completely than the philosophers elsewhere, and more able to take part in both lives. So each of you in turn must go down to the common dwelling of the others and get used to seeing in the dark. Once you are used to it, you will see ten thousand times better than the people down there; you will know each of the images for what it is and what it is an image of, because you have seen the truth about the beautiful and the just and the good. And so the city — ours and yours — will be governed awake, not in a dream, the way most cities are governed now, by men fighting one another over shadows and quarreling over rule, as if rule were some great good. The truth is surely this: the city where the men who are to rule are least eager to rule is of necessity the best and most quietly governed, and the city that gets the opposite kind of rulers, the worst.

Exactly so, he replied.

Then do you think our pupils will disobey us when they hear this, and refuse to share the city's labors, each group in its turn, while living most of their time with one another in the clear air above?

Impossible, he said. We will be laying a just command on just men. But there is no doubt each of them will go to office as an unavoidable thing — the very opposite of the men who rule in every city now.

That is how it is, my friend, I said. If you can find a life better than ruling for the men who are going to rule, a well-governed city becomes possible for you. Only in that city will the rulers be truly rich — not in gold, but in what a happy man must be rich in: a good and sensible life. But if beggars come to public office, men starved for goods of their own, expecting to snatch their good from there, it cannot work. Rule becomes a thing to fight over, and a war like that — domestic, inside the walls — destroys the fighters and the rest of the city with them.

Most true, he replied.

And do you know of any life that looks down on political office, I said, other than the life of true philosophy?

No, by Zeus, I don't, he said.

But surely the ones who go to rule must not be lovers of ruling. Otherwise the rival lovers will fight.

Of course.

Then whom else will you compel to go and guard the city but the men who understand best how a city is best governed, and who have honors of another kind, and a better life than the political one?

No one else, he replied. Those are the men I'll choose.

Then do you want us to consider next how such men will come to exist, and how we will lead them up to the light — the way some are said to have gone up from Hades to the gods?

Of course I do.

This, it seems, is not the flip of a shell; it is the turning around of a soul, from a day as dark as night toward the true day — the climb up into being, which we say is true philosophy.

Quite so.

Then shouldn't we ask which studies have the power to do this?

Certainly.

So what study, Glaucon, would draw the soul from becoming toward being? And here is something else that occurs to me as I speak: didn't we say these men had to be athletes of war in their youth?

We did.

Then the study we're looking for needs one more quality.

What quality?

It must be useful in war.

Yes, if possible.

Earlier, we educated them in two arts, didn't we?

Just so.

And physical training is busy with what comes to be and passes away — it presides over the growth and decay of the body.

Apparently.

So that can't be the study we're looking for.

No.

Then is it music, as we described it before?

But music, he said — if you remember — was the counterpart of physical training. It educated the guardians through habit: its harmonies gave them a kind of harmoniousness, not knowledge; its rhythms, a rhythmic grace; and its stories, whether fable or nearer truth, carried kindred qualities of the same sort. There was nothing in it leading to the kind of good you are looking for now.

Your memory is exact, I said. There really was nothing of that kind in it. But then, my dear Glaucon, what study would do it? The crafts, we agreed, were all beneath free men.

Obviously. And yet if music and physical training are out, and the crafts are out, what's left?

Well, I said, if we can't take anything outside those, let's take something that runs through all of them.

What sort of thing?

This common thing, for instance — what every art and every form of thought and every science uses, and one of the first things everyone must learn.

What is it?

The trivial business, I said, of telling one from two from three. I mean, in short, number and calculation. Isn't it true of them that every art and every science must have a share?

It is.

Then the art of war too?

It has no choice.

Then Palamedes, I said, makes Agamemnon look like an utterly absurd general — every time the tragedians put him on stage. Haven't you noticed? He claims that by inventing number he laid out the positions of the army at Troy and counted the ships and everything else — as if before him nothing had been counted, and Agamemnon apparently did not even know how many feet he had, since he could not count. What kind of general do you make of that?

A strange one, he said, if that's true.

Then can we deny, I said, that a man of war must learn to calculate and count?

He must, more than anything — if he is to understand anything at all about ordering troops; or rather, if he is even to be a human being.

Now do you notice, I said, what I notice about this study?

What?

It looks like one of the studies we are after — one that naturally leads toward thought — but no one uses it rightly, though it genuinely draws the soul toward being.

What do you mean? he said.

I will try to explain how it seems to me, I said. As I sort out for myself which things lead toward thought and which do not, look at them with me — agree or disagree — so we can see more clearly whether this is what I suspect it is.

Show me, he said.

Here is what I mean, I said. Some things the senses report do not summon the understanding to examine them, because the sense judges them adequately by itself. Others absolutely demand that examination, because the sense yields nothing sound.

You obviously mean things seen at a distance, he said, and paintings done in light and shadow.

No, I said — you haven't quite caught my meaning.

Then what do you mean?

The ones that do not summon thought, I said, are those that do not spill over into the opposite perception at the same time. The ones that do spill over, I count as summoners — whenever the sense reports a thing no more than its opposite, whether it strikes from near or far. You will see my meaning better this way: here, we say, are three fingers — the little one, the second, and the middle.

Right.

And assume I'm talking about them seen up close. Now consider this about them.

What?

Each of them appears equally a finger, and in this respect it makes no difference whether it sits in the middle or on the end, whether it is pale or dark, thick or thin, and so on. In all this, the ordinary soul is not forced to ask the understanding what a finger is — sight nowhere signals to it that the finger is at the same time the opposite of a finger.

No, it doesn't.

So a perception like that, I said, naturally doesn't summon or wake the understanding.

Naturally not.

But what about the bigness and smallness of the fingers? Does sight see those adequately, and does it make no difference to it whether the finger sits in the middle or at the end? And the same for touch with thick and thin, soft and hard? And the other senses — don't they report such things defectively? Doesn't each of them work like this: the sense set over hardness is forced to be set over softness too, and it passes the word to the soul that it feels the same thing as both hard and soft?

That is right, he said.

Then in such cases, I said, isn't the soul bound to be puzzled about what this sense means by hard, if it calls the same thing soft as well — and what the sense of light and heavy means by light and heavy, if it reports the heavy as light and the light as heavy?

Yes, he said — those are strange reports for the soul to receive, and they call for examination.

So it is likely, I said, that in cases like these the soul first calls in calculation and understanding, and tries to make out whether each of the things being reported is one or two.

Of course.

And if they appear to be two, each of the two appears as one and distinct?

Yes.

Then if each is one and the pair of them two, the soul will think the two as separated — because if they were inseparable, it would not be thinking two, but one.

Right.

Now sight, we say, saw big and small too — but not separated: run together. Yes?

Yes.

And to get this clear, the understanding was forced to see big and small the other way around — separated, not run together — the opposite of what sight did.

True.

And isn't it from here that the question first comes to us — what's the big, then, and what's the small?

Absolutely.

And that's how we came to call one thing the intelligible and the other the visible.

Exactly right, he said.

This is what I was trying to say just now — that some things summon the intellect and others do not. The ones that strike the sense together with their opposites, I count as summoners; the ones that do not, do not wake the understanding.

Now I understand, he said, and I agree.

Well then — number and the one: which side do they fall on?

I can't tell, he replied.

Work it out from what we have said, I said. If the one, by itself, is adequately seen — or grasped adequately by any other sense — it would not pull the soul toward being, any more than the finger did. But if some contradiction is always seen along with it, so that it appears no more one than the opposite of one, then something would be needed to judge; the soul would be forced into puzzlement, forced to search, stirring the thinking inside itself, and to ask what the one itself actually is. And so the study of the one would be among the studies that lead and turn the soul toward the contemplation of what is.

Well, he said, the sight of the one certainly has that feature — as much as anything does. We see the same thing as one and as unlimited in multitude at the same time.

And if that's true of the one, I said, isn't it true of all number?

Of course.

And calculation and arithmetic are entirely about number?

Certainly.

And they plainly lead the soul toward truth?

Extraordinarily well.

Then this, it seems, is one of the studies we are looking for. A soldier must learn it to marshal his troops; a philosopher, because he must rise up out of the sea of becoming and take hold of being, or never become a reasoner at all.

That's so.

And our guardian happens to be both soldier and philosopher.

Of course.

Then it would be fitting, Glaucon, to make this study law, and to persuade the men who are going to share in the city's greatest affairs to take up calculation — and not as amateurs. They must stay with it until they arrive at the contemplation of the nature of numbers by thought alone, practicing it not for buying and selling, like merchants and shopkeepers, but for war, and for the ease of the soul's own turning, away from becoming and toward truth and being.

Beautifully said, he replied.

And now that the study of calculation has been mentioned, I said, I realize how fine a thing it is, and how many ways it serves our purpose — as long as a man pursues it for the sake of knowing, and not for shopkeeping.

How so?

In just the way we were saying. It pulls the soul powerfully upward somewhere, and forces it to talk about the numbers themselves, never tolerating anyone who holds up numbers attached to visible or touchable bodies and argues from those. You know how the masters of the subject are: if you try to cut up the one itself in argument, they laugh at you and will not allow it. If you divide it, they multiply — standing guard so that the one never shows up as many pieces instead of one.

Very true.

Then what do you think, Glaucon? Suppose someone asked them: 'My remarkable friends, what numbers are these you are talking about — numbers in which the one is what you demand it to be, each unit equal to every other, without the smallest difference, and containing no parts?' What do you think they would answer?

This, I think: that they're talking about numbers which can only be thought — numbers there's no other way to handle.

Then you see, my friend, I said, that this study looks genuinely indispensable to us, since it plainly forces the soul to use thought itself to reach truth itself.

It certainly does do that.

And here is another thing — have you noticed how those born to calculate are naturally quick at practically every study, while the slow, if educated and trained in it, still gain this much even if they gain nothing else: they all become quicker than they were?

That's so, he said.

And I think you won't easily find many studies that cost the learner and the practicer more labor.

No.

Then for all these reasons the study must not be dropped, and the best natures must be educated in it.

I agree, he said.

Let that stand, then, as our first subject, I said. Second, let's consider whether the study that borders on it suits us.

Which? You mean geometry?

That very thing, I said.

As much of it as bears on war obviously suits us, he said. For pitching camp, taking up ground, pulling an army together and stretching it out, and all the other formations armies take in battle and on the march, it makes all the difference whether a man is a geometer or not.

But for that much, I said, a small share of geometry and calculation would be enough. What we must examine is the greater and more advanced part of it — whether it tends toward our goal, toward making it easier to see the form of the good. And everything tends that way, we say, that forces the soul to turn itself toward the region where the most blessed part of what is resides — the part the soul must see at any cost.

You are right, he said.

So if geometry forces the soul to look at being, it suits us; if at becoming, it doesn't.

So we say, at least.

Well, I said, no one with even a little geometry will dispute this much: the science itself is the direct opposite of the way the men who work at it talk about it.

How so?

They talk about it, I said, in a way that is laughable and beggarly. Everything they say sounds like business — 'squaring,' 'applying,' 'adding,' and so on — as if they were men of affairs and every proof were for use. But the whole study, surely, is pursued for the sake of knowing.

Absolutely, he said.

And must we agree on one thing more?

What?

That it is knowledge of what always is — not of something that comes to be at one moment and perishes at another.

That's easy to agree to, he said. Geometry is knowledge of what always is.

Then, my noble friend, it would pull the soul toward truth, and produce a philosopher's cast of mind — directing upward what we now wrongly direct down.

As surely as anything can, he replied.

Then as surely as anything can be ordered, I said, the men of your beautiful city must be ordered not to neglect geometry in any way. Even its side effects are not small.

What side effects?

The ones you named — its uses in war, I said. And beyond that, for taking in any study better, we know it makes all the difference everywhere whether a man has grasped geometry or not.

All the difference in the world, by Zeus, he said.

Then shall we set this down as the second study for the young?

Let us, he replied.

And what about astronomy as the third? Or do you think not?

Fine with me, he said. Keeping close track of the seasons and the months and the years suits not only the farmer and the sailor — it suits the general just as much.

You amuse me, I said. You seem afraid of the crowd — afraid of looking like a man who prescribes useless studies. Yet it is no small thing, and it is hard to believe, that in these studies a certain instrument in every man's soul is scoured clean and rekindled, after other pursuits have ruined and blinded it — an instrument more worth saving than ten thousand eyes, since only by it is truth ever seen. People who share that conviction will think you are speaking wonderfully well. People who have never noticed any such thing will naturally think you are talking nonsense, since they can see no other profit in these studies worth naming. So decide, here and now, which of the two you are arguing with. Or is it neither — are you making your arguments mostly for your own sake, though you would not begrudge anyone else whatever he can take from them?

That's what I choose, he said: to speak, ask, and answer mostly for my own sake.

Then step back, I said — just now we set the wrong study after geometry.

How did we go wrong? he said.

After plane surfaces, I said, we took up solids already in revolution, before taking them by themselves. The right way is to take the third dimension straight after the second — the dimension of cubes, of everything that has depth.

That's true, Socrates, he said. But those questions don't seem to have been worked out yet.

For two reasons, I said. No city holds these studies in honor, so they are pursued feebly — and they are hard. And the searchers need a director, without whom they will not make their discoveries; but such a man is hard to come by in the first place, and even if he appeared, as things now stand the men who search in this field are too full of themselves to obey him. If a whole city stood behind the work and honored it, though, they would obey, and steady, earnest inquiry would bring the answers to light. Even now — despised by the many, cut back by the very men who pursue it and cannot say what it is good for — the subject keeps growing all the same, forced up by its own charm; and it would be no surprise if it came fully to light.

The charm is there, certainly — exceptional charm, he said. But explain the order to me more clearly. First you set down the geometry of plane surfaces.

Yes, I said.

Then astronomy after it — and then you stepped back?

Yes, I said. In my hurry to get through everything quickly, I have made slower going. The study of depth came next in order; but because its investigation is in such a laughable state, I skipped past it and named astronomy — solid bodies in motion — right after geometry.

That is right, he said.

Then let's put astronomy fourth, I said, assuming the study we're passing over will exist the moment a city takes it up.

That is reasonable, he replied. And since you scolded me just now, Socrates, for praising astronomy in a vulgar way, let me praise it your way instead. It is obvious to anyone, I think, that astronomy compels the soul to look upward, and leads it from things here to things there.

Obvious to everyone but me, perhaps, I said — because that isn't how I see it.

Then how? he said.

The way it's handled now, by the men who would raise it into philosophy, it makes us look straight down.

What do you mean? he asked.

You have a generous notion, I said, of what the study of the things above really is. Apparently if a man tipped his head back and studied the decorations on a ceiling, you would say he was contemplating with his understanding and not with his eyes. Maybe you are right and I am the simpleton. But I cannot count any study as making the soul look upward except the one that concerns what is and what cannot be seen. Whether a man gapes at the sky or squints at the ground, if what he is trying to learn is something the senses can reach, I say he will never learn it — no such thing admits of knowledge — and his soul is looking down, not up, even if he does his learning floating on his back at sea or lying flat on the land.

A fair hit, he said — the rebuke is deserved. But then how did you mean astronomy should be learned, differently from now, if learning it is to serve our purposes?

Like this, I said. These embroideries in the sky — since they are stitched onto something visible, let them be counted the most beautiful and the most exact of visible things; but they fall far short of the true ones: the true motions, in which real speed and real slowness, in true number and in all the true figures, are carried in relation to one another and carry what is inside them. Those are grasped by reason and thought, not by sight. Or do you think otherwise?

Not at all, he replied.

Then, I said, we should use the embroidered sky as a set of model diagrams for the study that aims at those true things — just as if we had come across diagrams drawn and worked out with exceptional care by Daedalus or some other craftsman or draftsman. A man who knew geometry would look at them and admire the beauty of the workmanship; but he would think it laughable to study them in earnest, expecting to find in them the truth about equals or doubles or any other proportion.

Of course it would be laughable, he replied.

Then don't you think a real astronomer will feel the same when he looks at the movements of the stars? He will hold that the sky and everything in it have been framed by their maker as beautifully as such works can be framed. But the ratio of night to day, of these to the month, of the month to the year, and of the other stars to all of these and to one another — don't you think he will find it strange to believe that these always hold the same and never deviate anywhere, when they are bodies and visible — and stranger still to strain every nerve hunting the truth in them?

I think so, he said — now that I hear you say it.

Then we will pursue astronomy the way we pursue geometry, I said — by means of problems — and leave the things in the sky alone, if we mean to take real hold of astronomy, and to turn the naturally intelligent part of the soul from useless to useful.

The task you're laying down, he said, is many times heavier than anything astronomy does now.

And I think our prescriptions will run the same way elsewhere too, I said, if we are to be any good as lawgivers. But can you suggest any other suitable study?

Not right now, he said — not offhand.

Well, motion offers not one form but several, I said. A wise man could probably name them all; but two are plain even to us.

What are they?

Besides astronomy, I said, its counterpart.

What is that?

It seems that as the eyes are fixed on astronomy, I said, so the ears are fixed on harmonic motion — and that these are sister sciences, as the Pythagoreans say, and as we, Glaucon, agree. Don't we?

We do, he replied.

Then since the work is large, I said, we will ask them what they say about it, and whether there is anything else besides. But through all of it we will guard our own principle.

Which one?

That the men we are raising never try to learn any of these studies in an incomplete form — a form that fails to arrive where everything must arrive, as we were just saying about astronomy. Don't you know they do the same thing with harmony? Measuring the heard concords and notes against one another, they labor — like the astronomers — to no purpose.

By the gods, yes, he said — and absurdly too. They talk about their 'close-packed intervals,' and lay their ears alongside the strings as if hunting a voice from the neighbor's house. Some say they can still make out a note in between — the smallest interval, they claim, the proper unit of measure. Others insist the two sounds are already the same. Both parties set their ears ahead of their understanding.

You mean those good souls, I said, who harass the strings and put them to the question, racking them on the pegs. I could keep the image going — the blows of the plectrum, the strings charged with denial and bluster and refusing to speak — but I will drop it and say that these are not the men I mean. I mean the ones we just said we would question about harmony: the Pythagoreans. They do the same as the astronomers. They hunt for the numbers in the concords that are heard; they never climb up to the problems — to examining which numbers sound together and which do not, and why each is so.

A superhuman undertaking, he said.

A useful one, I said, when it is sought for the sake of the beautiful and the good — though pursued in any other spirit, useless.

That's likely, he said.

And I would think, I said, that if the pursuit of all these studies we have described reaches the point where their community and kinship with one another come into view, and it is worked out how they are akin, then the labor spent on them contributes to what we want and is not wasted — and otherwise it is.

So I'd prophesy too, he said. But it's an enormous work you're describing, Socrates.

What do you mean, I said — the prelude, or what? Surely we know that all of this is only the prelude to the song itself, the song that has to be learned. Because you do not think that men who are clever at these studies are dialecticians?

No, by Zeus, he said — except a very few of the ones I've met.

But men who cannot give an account and take one — did you ever think they would know any of the things we say must be known?

No again.

Then isn't this at last, Glaucon, I said, the song itself, the one dialectic performs? It belongs to the intelligible; but the power of sight imitates it. Sight, we said, at last tries to look at the animals themselves, and the stars themselves, and finally at the sun itself. In the same way, when a man tries by argument — without any of the senses — to press through to what each thing itself is, and does not give up until he grasps by thought itself what the good itself is, he arrives at the very end of the intelligible, as the prisoner, in our image, arrived at the end of the visible.

Absolutely, he said.

Well — don't you call that journey dialectic?

What else?

And the release from the chains, I said, and the turning from the shadows to the images and the firelight; the climb out of the cave into the sun; and up there, the inability at first to look at animals and plants and the sun's light, and looking instead at divine reflections in water, and at shadows of the things that are — no longer shadows of images, thrown by another light that is itself a kind of shadow beside the sun — all of this is what the practice of the arts we have described can do: it leads the best part of the soul up toward the sight of the best among the things that are, just as, in our image, the clearest organ of the body was led up to the sight of the brightest thing in the bodily, visible world.

I accept it, he said — though it seems very hard to accept, and then again, from another side, hard to deny. Still, since this is not something to be heard once today and never again — we will have to come back to it many times — let us assume it stands as stated, and go on to the song itself, and work through it the way we worked through the prelude. Tell us, then: what is the character of the power of dialectic, into what kinds does it divide, and what are its roads? For these, it seems, are the roads that lead at last to the place of rest at the end of the journey.

You will not be able to follow me there, my dear Glaucon, I said — though there would be no lack of willingness on my part. What you would see would no longer be an image of what we are describing, but the truth itself, as it appears to me. Whether it is exactly so or not is not worth insisting on yet; but that there is something of the sort to be seen — that we must insist on. Yes?

Of course.

And this too: that only the power of dialectic can reveal it, and only to someone experienced in the studies we have described — no other way is possible?

That too is worth insisting on, he said.

At any rate, I said, no one will dispute us when we say this: there is no other method that undertakes, systematically and in every case, to grasp what each thing itself is. The other arts are all turned toward human opinions and desires, or toward generation and assembly, or toward the care of things grown and assembled. And the rest — the ones we said lay hold of something of what is, geometry and the studies that follow it — we can see that they only dream about what is. They cannot see it awake as long as they use hypotheses and leave them untouched, unable to give any account of them. When the starting point is something a man does not know, and the conclusion and everything in between are woven out of what he does not know, what device could ever turn agreement like that into knowledge?

None, he said.

Then only the dialectical method, I said, travels this road: doing away with the hypotheses, it goes straight to the starting point itself, to make it secure. It finds the eye of the soul truly buried in a barbarian bog, and gently pulls it out and leads it upward, using the arts we have listed as helpers in the turning. We have called those arts sciences more than once, out of habit; but they need some other name — brighter than opinion, dimmer than science. Somewhere earlier we settled on 'thought.' But men with an inquiry as large as ours in front of them do not quarrel about a name.

No indeed, he said — any name will do, so long as it says clearly what's meant.

Then it will satisfy us, I said, to call them what we called them before: the first section science, the second thought, the third conviction, and the fourth imaging — the last two together opinion, the first two together intelligence; opinion concerned with becoming, intelligence with being. And to set it out as a proportion:

As being is to becoming, so intelligence is to opinion; and as intelligence is to opinion, so science is to conviction, and thought is to imaging.

But the ratio between their objects, and the division of each realm into two — the opinable and the intelligible — let us leave alone, Glaucon, so it does not bury us in arguments many times longer than the ones already behind us.

Well, he said, as far as I can follow, I agree about the rest.

And do you call a man a dialectician when he grasps the account of the being of each thing? And the man who has no account — insofar as he cannot render one, to himself or to anyone else — to that extent you deny he has understanding of the thing?

How could I say he had? he said.

And the same goes for the good. A man who cannot mark off the form of the good in his account, cutting it out from everything else; who cannot fight his way through every test of it, as in battle, pressing to test it by being rather than by opinion; who cannot come through all of that with his account still on its feet — a man like that, you will say, knows neither the good itself nor any other good. If he lays hold of some image of it, he holds it by opinion, not knowledge. He dreams and dozes through his present life, and before he ever wakes up here, he arrives in Hades and falls asleep for good.

Yes, by Zeus, he said — I'll say all of that, and emphatically.

Well then, your own children, the ones you are raising and educating in our argument — if you ever raised them in fact, I do not suppose you would let them rule the city and decide its greatest affairs while they were as irrational as incommensurable lines.

Certainly not, he said.

Then you will lay it down as law that they get, above all, the education that will make them able to ask and answer questions with the greatest possible knowledge?

I'll lay it down, he said — with you.

Then doesn't it seem to you, I said, that dialectic sits above all the studies like a coping-stone — that no other study could rightly be set higher, and that the account of the studies has now reached its end?

It does, he said.

Then what remains for you, I said, is the distribution: to whom we'll give these studies, and how.

Clearly.

Do you remember the earlier selection of the rulers — what kind of men we chose?

Of course, he said.

In most respects, then, the same natures must be chosen, I said. Preference goes to the steadiest and the bravest and, as far as possible, to the best-looking. But beyond that, we must look not only for noble and hardy characters — they must also have the natural gifts this education needs.

Which gifts, exactly?

Sharpness for study, my friend, I said, and no trouble learning. Souls flinch far sooner in hard study than in physical training: the labor is closer to home — it belongs to the soul alone and is not shared with the body.

True, he replied.

And the man we are looking for must have a good memory, be unbreakable, and love labor of every kind. How else would anyone consent to work through the body's toils and complete so much study and practice on top of them?

No one would, he said, unless his nature had every gift.

The mistake nowadays, I said — and the disgrace that has fallen on philosophy through it, which I mentioned before — is that she is taken up by men who are not worthy of her. Bastard sons should not have taken her up; trueborn ones should have.

How do you mean? he said.

First, I said, the man who takes her up should not limp in his love of work — half of him work-loving, half work-shy. That is what you get when a man loves physical training and hunting and every labor of the body, but does not love learning or listening or inquiring, and hates the labor in all of that. And the man whose love of labor runs all the other way is lame too.

Very true, he said.

And with respect to truth, I said, won't we likewise count a soul maimed if it hates the willing lie — cannot stand it in itself and boils with anger when others lie — and yet calmly accepts the unwilling lie, and is not distressed when it is caught knowing nothing, but wallows in its ignorance as contentedly as a pig in mud?

Absolutely, he said.

And with respect to self-command, I said, and courage and largeness of spirit and every part of virtue, we must watch just as carefully for the bastard and the trueborn. When a city or a private man does not know how to look into all this, they end up leaning on the lame and the bastard without noticing — as friends in the one case, as rulers in the other.

That's exactly how it goes, he said.

So we must take every care in these things, I said. If the men we bring to so great a study and so great a training are sound of limb and sound of mind when we educate them, justice herself will find no fault with us, and we will keep the city and its constitution safe. Bring men of another stamp, and we will accomplish the exact opposite — and pour an even greater flood of ridicule over philosophy.

That would be shameful, he said.

It would, I said. But something laughable seems to have just happened to me.

What?

I forgot we were playing, I said, and I spoke rather too intensely. As I talked, I was looking at Philosophy, and seeing her dragged through the mud — undeservedly — I think I lost my temper at the men responsible, and said what I said too much in anger.

No, by Zeus, he said — not to this listener.

But too much for this speaker, I said. And here is something we must not forget: in the earlier selection we chose old men, but in this one that will not do. We must not believe Solon that a man growing old can learn many things — he could sooner learn to run. All great and repeated labors belong to the young.

Necessarily, he said.

Then calculation and geometry and all the preparatory studies that must be taught before dialectic should be set in front of them while they are children — and not with the shape of forced instruction.

Why not?

Because a free man, I said, should learn nothing like a slave. Bodily labors done under force do the body no harm; but no forced study stays in the soul.

True, he said.

Then don't raise the children in their studies by force, my excellent friend, I said, but through play — and you will also see better what each of them is naturally fit for.

That makes sense, he said.

And do you remember, I said, that we said the children were to be taken to war too, as spectators on horseback — and where it was safe, brought up close and given a taste of blood, like young hounds?

I remember, he said.

Then in all of this, I said — labors, studies, dangers — whoever proves nimblest is to be enrolled on a select list.

At what age? he said.

When they are released from the required physical training, I said. That stretch — two years or three — is useless for anything else: exhaustion and sleep are the enemies of study. And at the same time, how each of them shows up in physical training is itself one of the tests, and not the least.

Clearly, he replied.

After that, I said, from the age of twenty, those who are chosen out will receive greater honors than the rest; and the studies that came to them scattered through their childhood education must now be gathered together, into one view of how the studies are akin to one another and to the nature of what is.

At least, he said, that's the only learning that holds firm in the ones who take it in.

And it is also the greatest test, I said, of who has a dialectical nature and who does not. The man who can see things together is a dialectician; the man who cannot, is not.

I agree, he said.

These, then, are the things you will have to watch, I said. Those who show most of this capacity, and who stay steady in study and steady in war and in the other duties the law lays down — these, once they have passed thirty, you will choose out of the chosen and raise to greater honors, and you will test them by the power of dialectic, to see which of them can let go of the eyes and the other senses and travel, with truth, toward what is itself. And here, my friend, is work that calls for great caution.

Why caution, particularly? he said.

Haven't you noticed, I said, the evil that infects dialectic today — how great it has grown?

What evil? he said.

Its students, I said, are filled up with lawlessness.

Very much so, he said.

Well, do you find anything astonishing in what happens to them? I said. Don't you find it forgivable?

How forgivable, exactly?

Take a parallel, I said. Imagine a changeling son, raised in a great and wealthy house, in a large family, surrounded by flatterers, who discovers when he has grown to manhood that he is not the child of the couple who call themselves his parents — and cannot find the ones who actually are. Can you divine how he would feel toward the flatterers, and toward his supposed parents, first in the years when he did not know about the substitution, and then in the years when he did? Or would you rather hear my guess?

I'd rather hear it, he said.

Then my guess is this, I said. As long as he does not know the truth, he will honor his father and his mother and the rest of his supposed family more than the flatterers; he will be less willing to see them go without; less willing to say or do anything improper to them; and less willing to disobey them in the things that matter.

Likely enough, he said.

But once he has learned the truth, I would guess that his honor and attention slacken toward the family and quicken toward the flatterers. He listens to them now as he never did before; he begins to live by their standards and keeps their company openly; and unless his nature is exceptionally decent, he cares nothing anymore for that father of his, or the rest of the old supposed family.

It would all happen just as you say, he said. But how does the image bear on the men who take up arguments?

This way. We have, from childhood, convictions about what is just and what is honorable; we are raised in them as by parents, obeying them and honoring them.

We are.

And there are other practices, opposite to those and carrying pleasure with them, which flatter the soul and pull at it — though they do not persuade men of any decency, who go on honoring their fathers' convictions and obeying them.

So it is.

Well then, I said. A question comes to a man in that condition and asks: what is the honorable? He answers what he heard from the lawgiver, and the argument refutes him. Refuted again and again, in many ways, he is beaten down into the opinion that the honorable is no more honorable than shameful — and the same with the just, and the good, and everything he held most in reverence. After that, what do you think he will do about honoring and obeying them?

He can't possibly, he said, go on honoring and obeying them as before.

Then when he no longer holds those convictions to be honorable and his own, I said, and cannot find the true ones, is there any life he can plausibly turn to except the life that flatters him?

There isn't, he said.

And so, I suppose, he'll appear to have become an outlaw, where he was law-abiding before.

He must.

Now all of this, I said, is what naturally happens to men who take up arguments in that way — and, as I said just now, it deserves a great deal of forgiveness.

Yes, he said — and pity too.

Then so that you won't have to pity your thirty-year-olds, mustn't every kind of care be taken in how they take up arguments?

Very much so, he said.

And isn't one great precaution this: that they not taste arguments while they are young? I suppose you have noticed how boys, when they get their first taste of arguments, treat them as a game and use them for nothing but contradiction. Imitating the men who refute them, they refute others in turn, delighting like puppies in dragging and tearing with the argument at anyone who comes near.

Delighted beyond measure, he said.

Then when they have refuted many and been refuted by many, they fall, fast and hard, into believing nothing they believed before. And with that, they and the whole business of philosophy get a bad name with everyone else.

Very true, he said.

An older man, though, I said, will not be willing to share that madness. He will imitate the man who wants to converse and examine what is true, rather than the one who contradicts for sport and play; and being more measured himself, he will make the practice more honored instead of more despised.

Right, he said.

And wasn't everything said before this said as a precaution too — that the natures we let near arguments must be orderly and steady, and not, as now, whoever wanders in, however unfit?

Certainly, he said.

Is it enough, then, to stay at arguments continuously and intensely, doing nothing else — training in them as the counterpart of the bodily training — for twice as many years?

Do you mean six, he said, or four?

No matter, I said — call it five. After that, you will send them back down into the cave, and they must be compelled to hold command in war and every other office that suits the young, so that they will not fall short of the others in experience either. And in those offices too they must be tested, to see whether they hold their ground when they are dragged in every direction — or flinch.

And how much time do you give that? he said.

Fifteen years, I answered. And when they reach fifty, those who have come through it all and proved best in everything — in every labor and every branch of knowledge — must be led at last to the goal. They must be compelled to lift the light of the soul upward and look toward what gives light to everything; and when they have seen the good itself, they must use it as their pattern for setting in order the city, the citizens, and themselves, each in his turn, for the rest of their lives — spending most of their time in philosophy, but when the turn comes, laboring at politics and ruling for the city's sake, treating it not as something splendid but as something necessary. And so, having educated others of the same kind in each generation and left them in their place as guardians of the city, they will depart to the Isles of the Blest and make their home there; and the city will set up memorials to them and offer sacrifices at public expense — as to divinities, if the Pythian oracle consents; and if not, then as to men blessed by fortune, and divine.

Like a sculptor, Socrates, he said, you've finished your rulers — and they're beautiful.

And the women who rule as well, Glaucon, I said. Do not suppose that anything I have said applies to men any more than to women — those among them whose natures are equal to it.

Rightly so, he said, if they're to share everything equally with the men, as we described.

Well then, I said, do you agree that what we have said about the city and its constitution is not mere wishing? Hard, yes — but possible in a certain way, and in no other way than the one described: when true philosophers, one or more of them, come to power in a city; when they despise the honors of today as slavish and worthless, and prize above everything what is right and the honors that come from it; when they treat the just as the greatest and most necessary thing of all, serving it and building it up as they set their own city in order?

How will they do it? he said.

Everyone in the city over ten years old, I said, they will send out into the countryside. They will take the children in hand, out of reach of the habits their parents live by now, and raise them in their own ways and their own laws — the laws we have described. That is the quickest and easiest way for the city and the constitution we have discussed to be established, to prosper, and to bring the greatest good to the people among whom it comes to be.

Much the quickest, he said. And I think you've described well how it would come into being, Socrates — if it ever does.

Then haven't we said enough at last, I said, about this city, and about the man who matches it? For it is clear, surely, what kind of man we will say he must be.

It's clear, he replied. And as for your question — I think we've reached the end.
