# The Republic, Book IV

Justice Found

Here Adeimantus broke in with a question. Socrates, he said, how would you defend yourself if someone charged that you aren't making these men very happy — and that it's their own fault? The city really belongs to them, and they get nothing good out of it. Other men acquire land, build big beautiful houses, furnish them to match, offer their own sacrifices to the gods, entertain their guests, and own the things you just mentioned — gold and silver and everything that's supposed to come with a blessed life. Your guardians, he'd say, sit in the city like hired garrison troops, doing nothing but standing watch.

Yes, I said — and add this to the charge: they serve for their food alone. On top of their keep they draw no pay, the way other men do, so they can't take a trip abroad at their own expense even if they want one, can't keep a mistress, can't spend money on any of the other things that men who are counted happy spend it on. These accusations, and plenty more of the same kind, you've left out of the indictment.

Well, he said, take all of that as part of the charge.

And you're asking how we'll answer it, I said?

Yes.

I think we'll find the answer, I said, by staying on the road we've been traveling. Our answer is this: it would be no surprise if even these men, living as they do, were the happiest men anywhere. But in any case, we did not found this city to make one class in it outstandingly happy — we founded it to make the whole city as happy as it can be. We thought we'd find justice most surely in a city like that, and injustice most surely in the worst-run one, and that by looking at both we could settle the question we've been chasing all along. Right now we're modeling the happy city — not carving out a few people and setting them up happy inside it, but shaping the whole. We'll examine the opposite kind in due course. Suppose we were painting a statue and a man walked up and objected: why aren't you putting the most beautiful colors on the most beautiful parts? The eyes are the finest feature of the body, and you've painted them black instead of purple. We'd think it a fair reply to tell him: don't ask us, sir, to paint eyes so beautiful that they no longer look like eyes — the same for the other features. Look instead at whether, by giving each part what suits it, we're making the whole thing beautiful. So don't force us now to fasten onto the guardians the kind of happiness that would make them anything but guardians. We could do it, you know. We could dress our farmers in embroidered robes, hang gold on them, and invite them to work the land whenever the mood took them. We could stretch our potters out on couches by the fire, feasting and passing the cup, with the wheel set beside them for whenever they felt like making a few pots. We could hand out that kind of blessedness to everyone, so that the whole city would be happy. Don't ask it of us — because if we listen to you, the farmer will be no farmer, the potter no potter, and no one will keep any of the shapes out of which a city is made. For most of the city it matters less. If cobblers go bad, and rot, and pretend to be cobblers when they're not, a city survives it. But when the guardians of the laws and of the city are guardians in appearance only, you can see that they destroy the whole city from the ground up — and they alone hold the key to governing it well and making it happy. We are making true guardians, then, the men least likely to harm the city. Our objector, it seems, is thinking of farmers at a fair — happy feasters at a festival, not citizens in a city. If so, he and we mean different things, and he's talking about something other than a city. So the question we have to face is this: are we planting our guardians with an eye to giving them the greatest happiness we can, or is our eye on the whole city, watching to see that happiness grows there? Because if it's the whole, then the guardians and the auxiliaries — and everyone else with them — must be pressed, and persuaded, to become the best possible workers at their own work. And with the whole city growing that way and in good order, each class can be left to take the share of happiness its nature allows.

Well said, I think, he replied.

Then I wonder, I said, whether you'll also think me right about that remark's twin brother.

What is it?

Consider whether there are two things that corrupt the rest of the workmen, until they turn bad at their work.

What two things?

Wealth, I said, and poverty.

How do they do it?

Like this. When a potter gets rich, do you think he'll still care about his craft?

Not at all, he said.

He'll grow lazier and more careless by the day?

Much more.

And so he becomes a worse potter?

Yes — far worse.

And from the other side: if poverty keeps a man from buying tools or anything else his craft needs, his work will come out worse, and his sons and apprentices will learn the work worse from him.

Of course.

So both of them — poverty and wealth — make the craftsman's work degenerate, and the craftsman with it?

Evidently.

Then we've found a second set of enemies, I said, that the guardians must watch for in every way, so they never slip into the city unseen.

What enemies?

Wealth, I said, and poverty. One breeds luxury, idleness, and revolution; the other breeds servility and shoddy work — and revolution all over again.

True enough, he replied. But tell me this, Socrates: how will our city be able to fight a war if it has no money laid up — especially against an enemy that is big and rich?

Obviously, I said, fighting one such enemy is hard. Fighting two is easier.

How do you mean?

First of all, I said, if fight they must, won't they be trained men of war going up against rich men?

Yes, that much is true.

Well then, Adeimantus — don't you think one boxer, trained to the peak of the art, could easily handle two rich, fat men who don't box?

Maybe not both at once, he said.

Not even if he could give ground, then wheel and hit whichever one came at him first — and do it again and again, under a scorching sun? Couldn't a man like that take down more than a pair of them?

No wonder in that, he said — none at all.

And don't you think the rich know more of boxing, in skill and in practice, than they know of making war?

I do.

Then in all likelihood our athletes will fight double and triple their own number with ease.

I'll grant you that, he said; I think you're right.

Now suppose that before the fighting starts they send an embassy to one of the two enemy cities and tell them the plain truth: Gold and silver are no use to us — we aren't permitted to have them. But you are. Fight beside us, and take the other city's wealth for yourselves. Do you think anyone who heard that offer would choose to fight against lean, hard dogs rather than stand with the dogs against fat and tender sheep?

No, I don't. But if the wealth of the other cities piles up in one of them — watch that it doesn't become a danger to the city that has none.

What an innocent you are, I said, to think anything deserves the name city except the kind we've been founding.

What should I call the rest?

Something bigger, I said. Each of them is not one city but many cities — like the joke in the board game. Whatever else it contains, every one of them holds two cities at war with each other: the city of the poor and the city of the rich. And inside each of those, many more. Approach them as one city and you'll miss everything. Approach them as many — offer one side the other side's money, power, even its people — and you will always have plenty of allies and few enemies. As long as your city keeps the sober order we've just set down, it will be the greatest of cities. I don't mean the greatest in reputation — I mean genuinely greatest, even if it can field only a thousand fighting men. One city as great as that you will not easily find, among Greeks or barbarians, though you'll find plenty that look many times its size.

Very true, he said.

Then wouldn't this, I said, be the best boundary-stone for our rulers — fixing how big the city should grow, and how much land to mark off for that size before they let the rest alone?

What boundary?

This one, I said: let it grow as long as growing leaves it one city — that far, and no farther.

Excellent, he said.

So here is one more order we'll lay on the guardians: guard in every way against the city seeming small or seeming grand; let it be one city, and enough.

A light enough burden, he said, to put on them.

Lighter still, I said, is the one we mentioned earlier — that if a child of the guardians is born inferior, he must be sent down to the other classes, and if a child of the others is born superior, he must be raised up among the guardians. The point of that was this: every citizen in the whole city is to be set to the one work nature fitted him for — one man, one work — so that each man stays one man and not many, and the whole city grows to be one city and not many.

Yes, he said — that one is even easier.

These orders we're giving them, my good Adeimantus, are not the long list of heavy burdens someone might imagine. They are all small things, so long as the guardians guard the one great thing, as the saying goes — though I'd rather call it not great but sufficient.

And what is that?

Education, I said, and upbringing. If our citizens are well educated and grow into reasonable men, they will see their own way through all of this easily — and through the things we're passing over for now: the marrying of wives, and marriage itself, and the getting of children — seeing that all of it should follow, as far as possible, the proverb that friends hold everything in common.

Yes, he said, that would be the straightest way.

And once a city gets a good start, I said, it rolls on and grows like a circle. Sound upbringing and education, faithfully kept, breed good natures; and good natures, taking hold of that same education, come out better than the generation before them — better in every way, including for breeding, as with the other animals.

Likely enough, he said.

To put it briefly, then: the overseers of the city must hold fast to this one thing, so that it is never corrupted behind their backs — they must guard it beyond everything else. No innovation in physical training or in music against the established order. Let them guard that order with all they have, and be afraid when they hear someone say that people praise most warmly

'the newest song that hovers about the singers' lips,'

For they should be afraid that the poet will be taken to mean not new songs but a new way of singing, and that this is what gets the praise. It should not be praised, and that is not what the line means. Changing over to a new form of music is a thing to beware of as a danger to the whole — for the modes of music are never shaken anywhere without the city's greatest laws shaking with them. So Damon says, and I believe him.

Count me among the convinced as well, said Adeimantus — add my vote to Damon's and yours.

Then it's here in music, I said, so it seems, that the guardians must build their guard-post.

Yes, he said — lawlessness of that kind creeps in easily, without being noticed.

It does, I said — dressed as play, as though it did no harm at all.

And it does none, he said — except that little by little it settles in, and seeps quietly into a man's character and habits. From there it swells and spills over into his dealings with other men; and from those dealings it moves against the laws and the constitution, Socrates, with real insolence, until it ends by turning everything upside down, private life and public alike.

Really, I said — it goes that far?

I think so, he replied.

Then, as we were saying at the start, our children's play must be lawful play from the very beginning — because if play turns lawless, and the children with it, there is no growing law-abiding, serious men out of them.

Certainly, he said.

But when children begin their play right, and take good order in through their music, then order — the very opposite of what happens to those others — follows them into everything, and grows as they grow, and sets upright whatever in the city had fallen before.

True, he said.

And so those men rediscover the rules that look small, the ones the generation before them had let die out entirely.

Such as?

Things like this: when the young should keep silent in front of their elders; giving up your seat to them, and rising when they enter; the care owed to parents; how to wear your hair; what clothes to put on, what shoes; the whole carriage of the body — all of that. Don't you agree?

I do.

But to legislate about them would be naive, I think. It isn't done anywhere, and no rule about such things, spoken or written down, ever holds.

How could it?

At any rate, Adeimantus, I said, the direction a man's education launches him in is likely to decide everything that comes after. Doesn't like forever call to like?

Of course.

Until at last, I suppose, it arrives at some single, finished, vigorous result — good, or the opposite of good.

No denying it.

That, I said, is why I wouldn't try to legislate any further about such things.

Fair enough, he replied.

Then what about business in the marketplace — the deals people cut with one another there, and contracts with tradesmen if you like, and slander and assault, the filing of lawsuits and the seating of juries, whatever dues need collecting or paying at the markets and the harbors, and in general the whole regulation of market, city, and port? By the gods, are we going to bring ourselves to legislate any of that?

No, he said — there's no need to dictate to good men. Most of what wants regulating they'll find for themselves easily enough.

Yes, my friend, I said — provided a god grants them the safekeeping of the laws we've already laid down.

And if not, said he, they'll spend their whole lives passing rules of that kind and then amending them, expecting to lay hold of what's best.

You mean, I said, they'll live like the sick men who lack the self-command to give up a rotten way of living?

Exactly.

And what a charming life those men have! Forever at the doctor's and getting nowhere — except that their illnesses grow bigger and more elaborate — and forever hoping that the next drug someone recommends will be the one that makes them well.

Yes, he said — that's exactly how it goes with invalids of that sort.

And here's another charm of theirs, I said: they treat as their worst enemy the man who tells them the truth — that until they quit the drinking and the stuffing and the whoring and the idleness, no drug will do them a bit of good, no cautery and no surgery, no charm chanted over them, no amulet, nothing of the kind.

Charming indeed! he said. There's nothing charming about raging at a man for telling you what's right.

You are no admirer, I said, it seems, of men like that.

By Zeus, no.

Then neither will you praise it when a whole city behaves the same way — as we just described. Isn't that exactly what the badly governed cities do? Their citizens are warned not to touch the constitution as a whole — to tamper with that is death. And meanwhile, whoever serves the city just as it is, and most sweetly — who flatters it, runs ahead of its wishes, and is clever at filling them — he is counted a good man, wise in great affairs, and the city heaps its honors on him.

Yes, he said, they act just like those invalids — and I have no praise for them whatever.

But the men who are willing, even eager, to serve such cities — don't you admire their courage, and how lightly they take it?

I do, he said — except for the ones the crowd's applause has fooled into believing they're statesmen in earnest.

What do you mean? Have you no sympathy for them? I said. When a man can't measure, and a crowd of others who can't measure either keep telling him he's six feet tall, do you think he can help believing it of himself?

No, he said — not that.

Then don't be hard on them — they're the most charming of the whole breed. They pass laws about the things we just listed, and amend them, always convinced that this time they'll put a stop to cheating on contracts and the rest of what I was describing — never seeing that all the while they're only hacking heads off a Hydra.

It's true, he said — that's all they're doing.

So I, for one, I said, wouldn't have thought the true lawgiver needs to bother with laws and government of that class at all, in a badly run city or a well-run one — in the one because it's useless and changes nothing, in the other because anyone could find some of those rules for himself, and the rest follow of their own accord from the practices already in place.

Then what's left for us, he asked, in the way of lawgiving?

For us, I answered, nothing. But for Apollo, the god at Delphi, there remain the greatest of the laws, the finest and the first.

Which are those?

The founding of temples, and sacrifices, and the whole service of gods, spirits, and heroes; and the burial of the dead, and the rites owed to those who dwell in the world below, if we want them gracious to us. These are things we don't understand ourselves; and in founding our city we will trust no other interpreter of them, if we have any sense, than the god of our fathers. For this god is the interpreter of such matters, by inheritance, to all mankind, and he interprets them seated at the center, on the very navel of the earth.

Well said, he replied — that's what we must do.

So there it stands, son of Ariston, I said: your city is founded. Now look inside it. Get yourself a light from somewhere and search — call in your brother and Polemarchus and the rest of them — and let's see whether we can spot where the justice in it is and where the injustice, how the two differ from each other, and which of them a man must possess if he means to be happy, whether every god and every man sees him possess it or not.

Nonsense, said Glaucon. You promised to do the searching yourself — you said it would be impious of you not to come to justice's rescue with everything you had, every way you could.

So I did, I said — you're right to remind me. I'll be as good as my word. But all of you have to help.

We will, he said.

Then here's how I hope to hunt it down, I said. I take it that our city — if it has been founded rightly — is completely good.

It has to be.

Then clearly it is wise, it is brave, it has self-command, and it is just.

Clearly.

So whichever of these we find in it first, won't the remainder be what's not yet found?

Very good.

Take any four things, I said, and suppose we were hunting for one of them in something. If we spotted it first, that would be enough. But if we identified the other three first, by that alone we'd know the thing we were hunting — clearly it could only be whatever was left.

That's right, he said.

So with these virtues, since there are four of them, shouldn't we hunt the same way?

Obviously.

Well, the first of them I think I see plainly is wisdom. And there's something odd about it.

What?

The city we've described really is wise, I think — for it's sound in judgment, isn't it?

True.

And sound judgment is clearly some kind of knowledge — it's by knowing, not by ignorance, that people judge well.

Clearly.

Now the city holds knowledge of many different kinds.

Of course.

So is it because of its carpenters' knowledge that a city should be called wise and sound in judgment?

Hardly, he said — that makes it good at carpentry.

Then it's not the knowledge that deliberates about how wooden implements can be made best, I said, that earns a city the name of wise.

No, certainly not.

Nor knowledge about vessels of bronze, nor anything else of that sort?

Nothing of that sort, he said.

Nor the knowledge of raising crops from the soil — for that, a city gets called good at farming.

So I'd say.

Then is there, I said, in this city we've just founded, a knowledge held by some of its citizens that deliberates not about any one thing in the city, but about the city as a whole — what course is best for it, within itself and toward other cities?

There is.

What is it, I said, and where does it live?

It's the guardians' knowledge, he replied, and it lives in those rulers we were just now calling the perfect guardians.

And what do you call the city on account of that knowledge?

Sound in judgment, he said, and truly wise.

Now which do you think our city will hold more of, I said — smiths, or these true guardians?

Smiths, he said — far more.

Of all the groups who take a name from the knowledge they hold, wouldn't the guardians be the smallest?

By a wide margin.

Then it's from its smallest class and part — from the knowledge living in the part that presides and rules — that a whole city founded according to nature is wise. And this class, it seems, whose portion is the one knowledge that of all knowledges deserves the name wisdom, is by nature the smallest class there is.

Very true, he said.

So here — though how, I can't say — we've found one of our four, both the thing itself and the seat it occupies in the city.

Found well enough for me, he replied.

And courage next — the thing itself, and the part of the city it lives in, the part that gets the whole city called brave — isn't very hard to see.

Why not?

Who would call a city cowardly or brave, I said, by looking at any part of it except the part that defends it and takes the field on its behalf?

No one, he said, would look anywhere else.

Because the rest of the people in it, I take it, could be cowards or brave men without the city being the one thing or the other on their account.

They could.

So a city is brave, too, by a part of itself — because in that part it has a power that will preserve, through everything, its judgment about what is to be feared: that the fearful things are exactly those things, and of exactly that kind, which the lawgiver declared them to be in the education. Isn't that what you call courage?

I didn't quite follow, he said. Say it once more.

I mean, I said, that courage is a kind of preserving.

A preserving of what?

Of the judgment, bred by law through education, about what is to be feared — what things they are, and of what kind. And by preserving it 'through everything' I meant holding onto it in pain and in pleasure, in desire and in fear, and never throwing it away. I'll give you a picture of it, if you like.

Please.

You know, I said, that when dyers want to dye wool that true sea-purple, they begin by choosing, out of all the colors of wool, the one natural ground — the white. Then they prepare it, working it over with no small preparation, so that it will take the color at its fullest bloom. Only then does the dyeing begin. Wool dyed that way holds fast; no washing, with soap or without it, can strip the bloom out of it. Done the other way — well, you know what it looks like, whether it's purple or anything else laid onto an unprepared ground.

I do, he said — washed-out, and ridiculous.

Then understand, I said, that we were doing the very same thing, as best we could, when we chose our soldiers and educated them in music and physical training. Don't imagine we designed all that for anything else but this: that, persuaded, they should take the laws into themselves the way wool takes dye, so that their judgment about what's to be feared — and about everything else — would be fixed fast in them, nature and rearing having both been right, and so that no solvent could scour the dye out: not pleasure, which works at that harder than any potash or lye, and not pain, fear, or craving, which outscour every soap there is. This power, then — the preserving, through everything, of right and lawful judgment about what is and is not to be feared — is what I call courage, and I'll stand by it unless you say otherwise.

I say nothing against it, he replied. For I take it that when right opinion about these same things arises without education — in an animal, say, or a slave — you don't count it truly lawful at all, and you'd call it something other than courage.

Exactly right, I said.

Then I accept this as your account of courage.

Accept it as an account of civic courage, I said, and you'll accept rightly. We'll go through it more fully another time, if you like. Right now the hunt is for justice, not courage, and for that purpose I think we've said enough.

Well said, he replied.

That leaves two things, I said, still to be seen in the city: self-command, and then the one for whose sake we've been searching through all the rest — justice.

Yes, exactly.

Is there any way we could find justice and spare ourselves the trouble of self-command?

I don't know one, he said — and I wouldn't want justice to show itself first if it means losing sight of self-command. If you want to please me, look into self-command before the other.

Of course I'm willing, I said. I'd be wrong not to be.

Then look into it, he said.

I will, I said. And seen from where we stand now, it's more like a concord, a harmony, than the two that came before it.

How so?

Self-command is a kind of good order, I said — a mastery over certain pleasures and appetites. That's what people are pointing at when they say a man is 'stronger than himself,' an odd expression, I admit; and other sayings of the kind are tracks of the same thing. Aren't they?

Certainly.

But 'stronger than himself' is absurd on its face. A man who's stronger than himself is also, presumably, weaker than himself, and the weaker one is the stronger — it's the same man being talked about the whole time.

Of course it is.

What the phrase means, I think, is this: in the man himself — in his soul — there is a better part and a worse part; and when the naturally better part holds the worse in hand, that is what 'stronger than himself' points to. It's praise. But when bad rearing, or bad company, leaves the better part small and overrun by the sheer mass of the worse — that draws blame and reproach. A man in that state is called weaker than himself, a slave of himself, a man without restraint.

That sounds right.

Now look at our young city, and you'll find one of those two conditions in it. You'll admit the city deserves to be called master of itself — if a thing in which the better rules the worse deserves to be called self-commanded, and master of itself.

I'm looking, he said, and what you say is true.

Notice, too, that desires of every shape and number — and the pleasures and pains that ride with them — turn up mostly in children, in women, in household servants, and, among the so-called free, in the many of the common sort.

Certainly.

While the simple and measured desires — the ones that go where reasoning leads, guided by intelligence and right opinion — you'll meet in few people: the ones born best and educated best.

True.

And you can see both of these in our city — where the desires of the common many are mastered by the desires, and the wisdom, of the decent few.

I can.

So if any city deserves to be called master of its pleasures and appetites — master of itself — this one does.

Absolutely.

And self-commanded too, on all these counts?

Yes, very much.

Then again: if there's any city where the rulers and the ruled hold the same conviction about who ought to rule, it's this one. Don't you think?

Without question.

And when the citizens agree like that, where would you say the self-command lives — in the rulers, or in the ruled?

In both, I suppose, he said.

You see? I said. Our hunch just now was a good one — that self-command is like a kind of harmony.

How so?

Because it doesn't work the way courage and wisdom do. Each of those lives in one part, and yet the one makes the whole city wise and the other makes it brave. Self-command isn't like that. It's strung through the whole city, straight through the octave, and it sets the weakest and the strongest and those in the middle all singing the same song — weakest and strongest in judgment, if you like, or in muscle, or in numbers or money or whatever you please. So we'd be exactly right to say that self-command is this unison: a concord between the naturally worse and the naturally better about which of the two should rule — in a city, and in each single person.

I entirely agree.

Good, I said. Three of them are now sighted in our city — as far as this kind of judgment goes. So what would the last form be, the one still needed to make the city good? Justice, clearly.

Clearly.

Now, Glaucon — now we do like hunters ringing a thicket. Close in and keep your eyes open, so that justice doesn't slip out somewhere and vanish and get clean away. She's here somewhere; that much is plain. Look hard — and if you catch sight of her before I do, sing out.

I wish I could, he said. What I'm really good for is following — a man with eyes just sharp enough to see what he's shown. Take me for that, and you have me right.

Then follow, I said, and pray with me as we go.

I will, he said — just lead.

The ground here looks bad, I said — thick shade, hard going. Dark, and not easy to beat through. But on we go all the same.

On we go.

And then I saw something. Aha, Glaucon! I called out. I think we have a track — I don't believe she's going to get away from us after all.

Good news, he said.

Honestly, I said, what a pair of fools we've been.

How so?

All this time, my friend — from the very beginning — the thing has been rolling around in front of our feet, and we never saw it. We've been completely ridiculous. You know how people sometimes hunt for the very thing they're holding? That was us. We never looked at it. We kept peering off into the distance — which is probably exactly how we missed it.

What do you mean?

I mean, I said, that I think we've been saying it, and hearing it said, all along — without ever grasping that in a way we were the ones saying it.

A long prelude, he said, for a man who's eager to hear the song.

Then listen, I said, and see whether there's anything in it. The thing we laid down at the very start, when we were founding the city — the rule everything else had to follow — that, I think, or some form of it, is justice. And what we laid down, and repeated again and again if you remember, was that each one man should practice one occupation in the city: the one his own nature is best suited for.

Yes, we said that.

And that doing your own work, and not meddling in another's, is justice — that we've heard from plenty of other people, and said ourselves, over and over.

We have.

Then this, my friend, I said — done in a certain way — is likely to be what justice is: doing your own work. Do you know where I get my evidence?

No — tell me.

This, I think, is what's left over in the city after the ones we've already examined — self-command, courage, and wisdom: the thing that gave all three of them the power to arise in it, and that keeps them safe, once arisen, for as long as it remains. And we said that whatever was left, once we'd found the other three, would be justice.

It would have to be.

And suppose we had to judge, I said, which of these does our city the most good by being in it. It would be a hard call. Is it the agreement of rulers and ruled? Or the soldiers' keeping, unbroken, of lawful judgment about what is and is not to be feared? Or the wisdom and watchfulness of the rulers? Or is the city's greatest good this — found in child and woman, in slave and free man and craftsman, in ruler and ruled alike — that each of them, being one person, did his own work and didn't meddle in anyone else's?

A hard call, he agreed — no question.

So as a power making a city good, it seems, each person doing his own work goes head to head with the city's wisdom, its self-command, and its courage.

It certainly does.

And the rival of those three in making a city good — wouldn't you name that justice?

Absolutely.

Now look at it from this side too, and see if you agree. It's the rulers you'll assign to try the city's lawsuits, isn't it?

Certainly.

And when they judge, will they aim at anything more than this — that no man have what belongs to another, and no man be stripped of what is his own?

No — that's exactly what they'll aim at.

Because it's just?

Yes.

So from this angle too, justice would be agreed to be the having and the doing of what is one's own — of what properly belongs to you.

That's so.

Now see whether you agree with me on this. Suppose a carpenter tries his hand at a cobbler's work, or a cobbler at a carpenter's — they trade tools, they trade standing, or one man takes on both trades, and swap whatever else you please. Do you think that does the city much harm?

Not much.

But when a man nature meant for a craftsman or some other kind of money-maker gets puffed up — on wealth, or a following, or muscle, or anything else of the sort — and pushes his way into the warriors' class; or a warrior pushes into the class of counselors and guardians without being worthy of it; when these men trade tools and standing between them, or when one man grabs at all of it at once — then I think you'll agree with me that this swapping and this meddling is the destruction of the city.

It is — absolutely.

So with three classes in the city, meddling and switching from one into another is the greatest injury to it, and could most rightly be called the worst kind of wrongdoing.

Precisely.

And the worst wrongdoing against your own city — won't you call that injustice?

Of course.

That, then, is injustice. Now say it the other way around: when the money-making class, the auxiliaries, and the guardians each keep to their own work — each doing its own in the city — that is justice, and that is what makes the city just.

I see it exactly that way, he said.

Let's not set it in stone quite yet, I said. If this same form, carried into each single human being, is agreed to be justice there too, then we'll concede the point — what more could we say? If not, we'll look elsewhere. For now let's finish the inquiry we began with this thought: that if we first tried to watch justice in something larger that contains it, it would be easier to see what it is in one man. The larger thing, we agreed, was a city, so we founded the best city we could, knowing well that justice would be in a good one. Now let's carry what showed itself there back down to the individual. If it agrees, all is well. If something different turns up in the individual, we'll go back to the city and test again — and maybe, examining the two side by side and rubbing them against each other like fire-sticks, we can make justice flash out; and once it has shown itself, we'll fix it firm in ourselves.

That's the right road, he said. Let's take it.

Well then, I said: when a bigger thing and a smaller thing are called by the same name, are they unlike in the respect the name fits them, or alike?

Alike, he said.

So a just man, in respect of the very form of justice, will be no different from a just city — he'll be like it.

He will.

Now, the city seemed to us just when each of the three kinds of natures in it did its own work; and self-commanded, and brave, and wise, through certain other conditions and habits of those same three kinds.

True.

Then, my friend, we'll expect the same of the individual: that he has these same three forms in his own soul, and that the same conditions in them rightly earn him the same names we gave the city.

It has to follow.

Well, here's another trivial little question we've tumbled into, I said: does the soul have these three forms in it, or not?

It doesn't look so trivial to me, he said. But maybe, Socrates, the old saying is true: fine things are hard.

So it seems, I said. And I'll tell you plainly, Glaucon: in my judgment we will never grasp this precisely by the kinds of methods we're using in the argument now. There is another road to it, longer and larger. Still, we can perhaps reach an answer worthy of what we've said and examined so far.

Isn't that enough, then? he said. For me, for the present, it would do.

And it will more than do for me, I said.

Then don't flag, he said — go on and look.

Well, isn't it strictly necessary, I said, to admit that the same forms and habits that are in the city live in each of us? They certainly didn't arrive there from anywhere else. It would be laughable if someone imagined that the spirited element in cities didn't come from the individual citizens who bear that reputation — the peoples of Thrace and Scythia, say, and generally of the north; or the love of learning, which you'd credit chiefly to our own part of the world; or the love of money, which you'd say is found not least among the Phoenicians and the people of Egypt.

Quite so, he said.

That's how it stands, then, I said, and there's nothing hard in knowing it.

No, nothing.

But here's what is hard: do we do each of these things with the same one thing in us, or are there three, and we do a different thing with each — learn with one, grow angry with another part of ourselves, and with some third crave the pleasures of food and sex and everything of their family? Or is it the whole soul acting, all of it, in every one of them, once we've set ourselves going? That is what will be hard to mark off in any way worthy of the argument.

I think so too, he said.

Then let's try to draw the line this way — to see whether these things are the same as each other or different.

How?

Clearly the same thing will not consent to do or undergo opposites in the same respect, in relation to the same thing, at the same time. So if we ever find that happening in these elements of ours, we'll know we were dealing not with one thing but with several.

All right.

Then consider what I ask: can the same thing, in the same respect, be standing still and moving at the same time?

Not at all.

Let's agree on it more precisely still, so we don't fall to quarreling later. Suppose someone said of a man standing still, but moving his hands and his head, that the same man is at rest and in motion at once. We wouldn't accept that way of putting it, I think. We'd say that one part of him is at rest and another part moving. Isn't that right?

It is.

And suppose our objector turned wittier still and refined the point: tops, he says, stand still and move as wholes, whenever they spin with the peg planted in one spot — and so does anything else that circles in place. We wouldn't take it — because in those cases it is not in respect of the same parts of themselves that the things stand and turn. We'd say each has an axis in it and a circumference: in respect of the axis it stands still, since it leans to no side, and in respect of the circumference it goes round and round. But if the upright tilts right or left, forward or back, while the thing spins, then it isn't standing still in any way at all.

That's correct, he said.

Then no objection of that kind will scare us, or come any nearer to persuading us that anything, while remaining the same thing, could ever undergo, or be, or do opposites at the same time, in the same respect, toward the same thing.

It won't persuade me, at least, he said.

All the same, I said, to spare ourselves the tedium of walking through every such quibble and proving each one false, let's assume this is so and push on — with the understanding that if it ever looks otherwise to us, everything we've concluded from it comes undone.

Yes, he said, that's what to do.

Now then, I said: nodding yes and shaking no; reaching for a thing and refusing it; drawing it toward you and pushing it away — wouldn't you set all these down as opposites to one another, whether they count as doings or as undergoings? For that makes no difference here.

Yes, he said — opposites.

And thirst, I said, and hunger, and the appetites generally — and again willing and wishing — wouldn't you place all of these somewhere in the classes we just named? Won't you say, for instance, that the soul of a man in desire either reaches for what it desires or draws toward itself what it wants to have; or again that, insofar as it wills that something be handed to it, it nods assent to itself, as though someone had asked it a question, straining toward the getting of the thing?

I would.

And what of not-wanting, not-wishing, not-desiring? Won't we put those with the soul's pushing-away and driving-off — with all the opposites of the first class?

Of course.

That being so, shall we say there is a class of the appetites, and that the sharpest examples in it are the two we call thirst and hunger?

Let's say it, he said.

One of them is desire for food, the other for drink?

Yes.

Then here's the point. Insofar as it is thirst, is it a desire in the soul for anything more than that? Is thirst, as thirst, a thirst for hot drink or cold, for much drink or little — in a word, for drink of any particular kind? Isn't it rather that if heat is present along with the thirst, it adds on the desire for something cold, and if cold is present, for something hot; and if the thirst is great, the drink desired will be much, and if slight, little — but thirst by itself will never be a desire for anything but its natural object, drink by itself, as hunger is for food?

Just so, he said: each desire, taken by itself, is only of its natural object taken by itself; desire for an object of this or that kind is something added on.

Now let's not be caught off guard, I said, by someone springing up with the objection that no one desires drink, but good drink, and not food, but good food — because everyone, after all, desires good things; so if thirst is a desire, it must be a desire for good drink, or good whatever-it-is, and the same for the other desires.

Well, he said, the man who says that might seem to have a point.

But surely, I said, with all things that are relative to something, the ones that are qualified are relative to a qualified object, while each thing taken just by itself is relative to its object taken just by itself.

I don't follow, he said.

Don't you know that the greater is such as to be greater than something — than a less?

Certainly.

And the much greater than the much less?

Yes.

And the once-greater than the once-less, and the greater-that-will-be than the less-that-will-be?

Of course, he said.

And the same again for the more and the fewer, the double and the half and everything of that stamp; and the heavier and the lighter, the faster and the slower; and again the hot and the cold, and every other pair like them — doesn't each go with its own correlate?

Yes.

And what about the branches of knowledge — doesn't the same principle hold? Knowledge, taken just as itself, is knowledge of the knowable taken just as itself, or of whatever we should say knowledge is of; but a particular knowledge, of a particular kind, is of a particular object of a particular kind. I mean this sort of thing: when a knowledge of making houses arose, didn't it differ from the other knowledges, and so get called house-building?

Certainly.

And wasn't that because it's a particular kind of knowledge, different from all the others?

Yes.

And it became a particular kind because its object was of a particular kind? And the same holds for the rest of the arts and knowledges?

It does.

Well then, I said, say that this is what I meant before — if you've now got hold of it: with everything that is relative to something, the thing taken alone goes with its object taken alone, and the thing qualified goes with its object qualified. I'm not saying the relative shares the quality of its object — that the knowledge of health and sickness is itself healthy or sick, or the knowledge of good and evil is itself good or evil. I mean that when a knowledge arose whose object was not the bare thing knowledge is of, but an object of a particular kind — namely health and sickness — the knowledge itself came out qualified in turn; and that is why it's no longer called simply knowledge but, with the kind added on, medicine.

I understand, he said, and I think you're right.

Then take thirst again, I said. Won't you set it among the things that are what they are in relation to something? Thirst, presumably, is thirst —

Yes, he said — for drink.

So a particular kind of thirst is for a particular kind of drink; but thirst by itself is not for much drink or little, not for good drink or bad — in a word, not for drink of any kind at all. Thirst by itself is, by nature, only for drink by itself.

Absolutely.

Then the soul of a thirsty man, insofar as he thirsts, wants nothing else but to drink — that is what it strains after, that is what it starts toward.

Plainly.

So if something ever hauls it back while it's thirsting, that would be a different thing in the soul from the thing that thirsts and drives it, like an animal, toward drinking. For we've agreed that the same thing cannot act in opposite ways, with the same part of itself, toward the same object, at the same time.

It can't.

Just as it's no good saying of an archer that his hands push the bow away and pull it in at the same time. The truth is that one hand pushes and the other pulls.

Exactly right, he said.

Now, can we say that people are sometimes thirsty and yet unwilling to drink?

Certainly, he said — many people, and often.

Then what should we say about them? I said. Isn't there something in their soul commanding them to drink, and something else forbidding it — a different thing, which overrules the thing that commands?

So I think, he said.

And doesn't the forbidder, when it appears, appear out of reasoning — while the things that drive and drag arrive through passions and diseases?

It seems so.

Then it's no unreasonable claim, I said, that these are two things, different from each other. The part of the soul with which it reasons we may call the reasoning part; and the part with which it lusts and hungers and thirsts and flutters around the other appetites — that is the unreasoning and appetitive part, companion of certain feedings and pleasures.

No, he said — it's the natural thing to think.

Then let these stand, I said, as two forms marked off in the soul. Now what of spirit — the thing in us we get angry with? Is it a third, or does it share its nature with one of the two?

Maybe with the second, he said — the appetitive.

But I once heard a story, I said, and I trust it. Leontius, the son of Aglaion, was coming up from the Piraeus along the outside of the north wall when he noticed corpses lying on the ground beside the public executioner. Part of him craved to look at them; part of him recoiled and turned away. For a while he fought himself and kept his face covered — but at last, overpowered by the appetite, he wrenched his eyes wide open, ran up to the corpses, and cried out at his own eyes: There, you wretches — drink your fill of the beautiful sight!

I've heard the story too, he said.

Now what it points to, I said, is that anger sometimes makes war on the appetites — one thing against a different thing.

It does point to that, he said.

And don't we notice the same elsewhere, all the time? When appetites force a man against his reasoning, he curses himself and rages at the thing inside him that's doing the forcing; and in this faction-fight, as between two parties, the man's spirit takes up arms for his reason. But for spirit to make common cause with the appetites, when reason has ruled they must be resisted — that, I think you'd say, you have never seen happen in yourself, nor in anyone else either.

No, by Zeus, he said.

And what about when a man thinks he himself is in the wrong? I said. Isn't it true that the nobler he is, the less he can manage anger at going hungry, or cold, or suffering anything else of the kind at the hands of the man he believes has a right to inflict it? His spirit simply refuses to rise against that man — that's my claim.

True, he said.

But when a man believes he is the one wronged? Then his spirit boils and turns fierce, and enlists on the side of what seems to him just; and through hunger, through cold, through every suffering of the kind, it holds out and it conquers, and it will not give up the noble fight until it wins, or dies — or until the reason within calls it back and gentles it, the way a shepherd calls back his dog.

That's the very image of it, he said. And in fact, in our city we made the auxiliaries exactly that — dogs, obedient to the rulers, who are the shepherds of the city.

You've caught my meaning beautifully, I said. But there's one thing more — have you noticed it?

What?

That the spirited part now shows us the opposite face from the one it showed a moment ago. Then we thought it something appetitive; now we say it's anything but — that in the civil war of the soul it sets its arms far rather on the side of the reasoning part.

Absolutely.

Then is it different from reason too? Or is it some form of reason — so that the soul holds not three forms but two, the reasoning and the appetitive? Or is the soul like the city, which was held together by three classes — the money-makers, the auxiliaries, the deliberators — so that in the soul as well the spirited is a third thing, the natural auxiliary of reason, unless bad rearing has ruined it?

It must be a third, he said.

Yes, I replied — if it shows itself different from the reasoning part, the way it showed itself different from the appetitive.

That's not hard to see, he said. Look at small children: they're full of spirit from the moment they're born; but as for reason — some of them, to my eye, never come into any at all, and most only late in the day.

Beautifully put, by Zeus, I said. And in animals, too, you can see what you mean. And besides all that, there's the witness of Homer — the line we quoted earlier:

'He struck his chest and rebuked his own heart,'

for there Homer has plainly made one thing rebuke another — the part that has reasoned out better and worse scolding the part that rages without reason.

Exactly right, he said.

Well, Glaucon, I said — we've swum through all that with some hard strokes, and made it to shore. We're decently agreed that the same kinds that are in the city live also in the soul of each single man, and that they're equal in number.

So we are.

Then doesn't this follow at once: the individual man is wise in the same way, and by the same thing in him, that made the city wise?

Of course.

And what makes a man brave, and the way it makes him brave, is what makes the city brave, and in the same way — and everything else that bears on virtue holds alike in both?

It has to.

And a just man, Glaucon — we'll say he is just, too, in the very way the city was just.

That follows, no escaping it.

And surely we haven't forgotten that the city was just by each of the three classes in it doing its own work.

I don't think we've forgotten that, he said.

Then we must remember this too: each of us will be just — a man who does his own work — when each of the parts within him is doing its own work.

Yes, he said — we'd better remember it.

So: to the reasoning part it belongs to rule, because it is wise and carries the forethought for the whole soul; and to the spirited part it belongs to obey it and fight beside it.

Certainly.

And won't it be, as we said, the blend of music and physical training that draws these two into tune — tightening the one and feeding it on fine words and fine learning, while it slackens the other, calming it with gentle words, taming it by harmony and rhythm?

Perfectly so, he said.

And these two, reared that way — truly taught their own work, truly educated — will stand watch over the appetitive part, which in each of us is the largest part of the soul and by nature can never have enough of possessions. They'll keep guard over it, so that it doesn't gorge itself full of the so-called pleasures of the body, grow big and strong, stop doing its own work, and set about enslaving and ruling the parts it has no birthright to rule — overturning the whole of everyone's life.

Very true, he said.

And wouldn't the same two mount the finest guard against enemies outside as well, on behalf of the whole soul and the whole body — the one deliberating, the other doing the fighting, following its ruler and carrying out its decisions with courage?

They would.

Brave, then, I think, is what we call each man by this part — when his spirit carries safe through pains and pleasures alike what reason has declared to be fearful, and not.

Rightly so.

And wise by that small part, the one that ruled in him and issued those declarations — holding in itself, in turn, the knowledge of what benefits each of the three, and the whole community of them together.

Yes, exactly.

And self-commanded — isn't a man that by the friendship and concord of these same parts: when the ruler and its two subjects agree together that the reasoning part must rule, and raise no faction against it?

Self-command is that and nothing else, he said — in a city or in a single man.

And just, at last — a man will be just by what we've now said so many times, and in exactly that way.

It must be so.

Well then, I said. Has justice gone blurry on us at all? Does it look like anything different from what it showed itself to be in the city?

Not to me, he said.

If any part of our soul still wants to argue, I said, we can settle it for good with a few everyday tests.

Which are?

Take our city, for instance, and the man whose nature and training match it — and suppose a deposit of gold or silver had been placed in his hands. Do you think anyone would expect him, sooner than men of a different sort, to make off with it?

No one would, he said.

And temple-robbery, theft, betrayal — of friends in his private life, or of the city in public — wouldn't he be beyond them all?

Beyond them all.

Nor would he ever be faithless, in any way, to an oath or to any other agreement.

Never.

Adultery, neglect of parents, gods left unserved — all of that belongs to any man sooner than to him.

Any man sooner, he said.

And isn't the cause of all of it this — that each of the parts within him does its own work, whether its work is ruling or being ruled?

That's the cause, and nothing else.

Then are you still hunting for justice to be anything more than this power — the power that produces men like that, and cities like that?

No, by Zeus, he said — not I.

Then our dream has come completely true — the suspicion we spoke aloud the moment we began founding the city, that by some god's grace we had likely stumbled, right at the start, onto the origin and pattern of justice.

It has, absolutely.

So it really was an image of justice, Glaucon — and that's why it was so useful — the rule that the man born a cobbler had better make shoes and do nothing else, and the born carpenter build, and so on down the line.

So it appears.

But in truth, it seems, justice was something of this kind — only not about a man's outward doing of his own work, but about the inward doing of it: about himself, in the truest sense, and what is truly his own. The just man does not allow any part of himself to do another part's work, or the kinds within his soul to meddle in one another's business. He sets his own house in order — his own in the truest sense. He rules himself. He puts himself in order, becomes his own friend, and tunes the three parts of him together exactly like the three fixed notes of a scale — low, high, and middle, and whatever happens to lie between — binding them all until, out of many, he has become entirely one: a man of self-command, in harmony with himself. Then, and only then, he acts — if he acts at all — whether he's making money or tending his body, in politics or in private business. And through all of it, the action he holds and names as just and beautiful is the one that preserves this condition and helps complete it, and the knowledge that presides over such action he calls wisdom; while the action that breaks this condition down, at any point, he names unjust, and the opinion that presides over it, ignorance.

That's the whole truth, Socrates, he said.

Well then, I said. If we claimed to have found the just man, and the just city, and what justice actually is in them — I don't think we'd be counted liars.

By Zeus, no, he said.

Shall we claim it, then?

Claim it.

So be it, I said. Next in line, I suppose, is injustice.

Obviously.

Then injustice must be a kind of civil war among these same three — a meddling, an interfering in one another's work, an uprising of one part of the soul against the whole: the part rebelling so that it can rule inside the soul when rule is not its place, because its nature made it fit to serve what is of ruling stock. Something like this is what we'll say, I think — that this confusion and wandering of the parts is injustice, and lack of restraint, and cowardice, and ignorance: in one word, the whole of vice.

That's what it is, he said — exactly.

Then acting unjustly and being unjust — and acting justly, for that matter — all of that now stands in plain view too, doesn't it, if injustice and justice do?

How so?

Because, I said, they're no different from the healthy and the diseased: what those are in the body, these are in the soul.

In what way?

What's healthy breeds health, and what's diseased breeds disease.

Yes.

And doesn't doing just things breed justice, and doing unjust things injustice?

It must.

Now, to produce health is to set the elements of the body so that they master, and are mastered by, one another according to nature — and to produce disease is to set them ruling and being ruled contrary to nature.

It is.

Then producing justice, in turn, is setting the parts of the soul to master, and be mastered by, one another according to nature — and producing injustice is setting them to rule and be ruled contrary to it?

Exactly.

So virtue, it seems, would be a kind of health and beauty and good condition of the soul — and vice its disease, its ugliness, its weakness.

That's so.

And don't fine practices lead a man toward virtue, and shameful ones toward vice?

They must.

Then what's left for us to examine, it seems, is this: whether it pays to act justly, to practice what is fine, and to be just — whether or not anyone knows you are — or to do injustice and be unjust, provided you never pay the penalty and are never made better by punishment.

But Socrates, he said, that question has turned ridiculous now. When the body's nature is wrecked, we think life isn't worth living — not with every food and every drink, not with all the money and all the power there is. And when the nature of the very thing we live by is deranged and wrecked, life is going to be worth living, is it — as long as a man can do whatever he pleases except the one thing that would free him from vice and injustice and win him justice and virtue? Now that both have shown themselves to be what we've described?

Ridiculous, yes, I said. But still — now that we've climbed high enough to see as sharply as we ever will that this is how things stand, we mustn't get tired.

The last thing in the world to do, he said, is tire now.

Then come up here, I said, and see how many forms vice has — the ones worth looking at, anyway, as I see it.

I'm right behind you, he said. Just tell me.

Well, I said, from up here — as from a watchtower, now that the argument has carried us this high — there appears to be one single form of virtue, and unlimited forms of vice; and among them, some four that are worth mentioning.

What do you mean?

It's likely, I said, that there are as many types of soul as there are types of constitution with distinct forms of their own.

How many is that?

Five of constitutions, I said, and five of the soul.

Tell me which, he said.

One of them, I say, is the constitution we've just described — though it could carry two names. If one outstanding man arises among the rulers, it will be called a kingship; if more than one, an aristocracy.

True, he said.

This, then, I count as a single form, I said. Whether it's several who arise or only one, they won't shake any law of the city worth mentioning — not if they've had the rearing and the education we described.

No, he said — it isn't likely.
