The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XI

Inferno: Canto XI11 of 100

Along the edge of a high bank we came,
built up of great rocks broken in a ring,
and reached a still more cruel pen of souls.

And there, because of the appalling stench
the deep pit threw up at us, we drew aside
and took shelter behind a great tomb,

on which I saw an inscription that read:
"I hold Pope Anastasius, the one
Photinus drew out of the straight way."

"We have to go down slowly," said my guide,
"so the senses get used to the foul blast first,
and then we'll pay it no more mind."

So said the Master, and I answered him:
"Then find us something, so the time's not wasted."
And he: "You see, I'm already thinking of it.

My son, inside these rocks," he began,
"there are three smaller circles, one below
the next, like the ones you are leaving now.

All of them are packed with cursed spirits.
But so the sight alone will tell you later,
learn how they are penned, and why.

Every malice that earns Heaven's hatred
has injury as its aim, and every such aim
harms someone — by force, or else by fraud.

But because fraud is the vice unique to man,
it offends God more. So the fraudulent
lie lowest, and the heavier pain is theirs.

The whole first circle is for the violent.
But since force can be turned against three persons,
it is built and divided in three rounds.

Against God, against ourselves, against
our neighbor — against them and what they own
we can use force, as I'll make plainly clear.

Death by violence, and grievous wounds,
are dealt to a neighbor; and against his goods,
ruin, and arson, and extortion.

So the first round torments, in separate bands,
murderers, and any who strikes to wound,
plunderers, and those who rob the roads.

A man can lay violent hands upon himself
and on his own goods; so in the second round
he must repent, and all to no avail,

whoever robs your world of his own self,
or gambles away and squanders all he has,
and weeps there, where he should have been glad.

Violence can be done against the Godhead too —
denying him in the heart, and cursing him,
and scorning Nature and what she gives.

And so the smallest round stamps with its mark
Sodom and Cahors, and everyone
who scorns God and speaks against him from the heart.

Fraud, which gnaws at every conscience,
a man can work against one who trusts him,
or against one who lends him no such trust.

This second kind seems only to cut through
the bond of love that Nature herself makes;
and so, down in the next circle, nest

hypocrisy, flattery, those who deal in spells,
forgery, theft, and the selling of holy things,
pimps, grafters, and filth of that kind.

The first kind forgets not only the love
that Nature makes, but the trust that's added on,
the special bond it builds beyond the rest.

So in the smallest circle — the still point
of all the universe, where Dis sits enthroned —
whoever betrays is eaten forever."

And I: "My Master, your reasoning runs clear,
and it lays out this pit for me precisely,
and the people who fill it, group by group.

But tell me — those in the thick of the swamp,
the ones the wind drives, and the rain beats down,
and those who clash with such bitter tongues —

why aren't they punished inside the red city,
if God is truly angry with them? And if
he isn't, why are they suffering at all?"

And he to me: "Why does your mind
wander so far from its usual track?
Or where else are your thoughts looking now?

Have you forgotten the words your Ethics uses,
the ones that lay out, point by point, the three
dispositions Heaven will not abide —

incontinence, malice, and the mad
brutality? And how incontinence
offends God less, and earns less blame?

If you take that conclusion to heart,
and call to mind exactly who they are
doing their penance up there outside the walls,

you'll see clearly why they are set apart
from these worse felons, and why divine justice
comes down on them with a lighter hammer."

"O Sun, that heals every clouded sight,
you content me so much when you untie a knot
that doubting pleases me as much as knowing.

Go back a little, once more," I said,
"to where you said that usury offends
God's goodness — and undo that knot for me."

"Philosophy," he said, "to one who heeds it,
points out, and not in one place only,
how Nature takes the course she takes

from the divine Intellect and its art.
And if you read your Physics carefully,
you'll find, not many pages in,

that your human art follows hers as far
as it can — the student following the master —
so that your art is, you might say, God's grandchild.

From these two, art and Nature — if you call
the opening of Genesis to mind —
mankind is meant to make its living and rise.

But the usurer takes another road:
he scorns Nature herself, and her follower, art,
since he puts his hope somewhere else.

Now follow me — I'd like to be moving on.
The Fish are quivering up at the horizon,
the Wain lies wholly over Caurus now,
and the cliff goes down, farther on, over there."
