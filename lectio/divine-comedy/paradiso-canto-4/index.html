The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto IV

Paradiso: Canto IV71 of 100

Set a free man between two foods, both equally
far off and equally tempting, and he would starve
before his teeth could close on either one.

A lamb would stand stock-still between the jaws
of two fierce wolves, in equal dread of both;
a hound would freeze between two waiting deer.

So if I held my tongue, I cannot blame myself,
driven as I was in equal measure by twin doubts —
it had to be; I neither praise nor fault it.

I said nothing. But my longing was painted plain
upon my face, and the question in it asked
far more, and hotter, than any spoken word.

Beatrice did then what Daniel once had done
when he lifted from Nebuchadnezzar the rage
that had made the king unjustly merciless;

she said: "I see clearly how the one wish and the other
pull at you, so that your own care binds itself
so tight it cannot find the breath to speak.

You reason: 'If my good will holds firm and lasts,
by what right does another's violence
diminish the measure of what I have earned?'

And here is the second thing that breeds your doubt —
that souls appear to journey back to their stars,
as Plato seems to teach the matter went.

These are the questions pressing on your will
with equal weight; and so I will treat first
the one that carries the most poison in it.

Not the Seraph most deeply drawn into God,
not Moses, Samuel, nor whichever John
you care to name — not even Mary herself —

has his seat in any other heaven than these
spirits who just now showed themselves to you,
nor more nor fewer years of being blessed;

all of them make the first circle beautiful,
and share its sweet life in differing degrees,
feeling the eternal breath more or less.

They showed themselves here not because this sphere
has been assigned to them, but to give a sign
of the rank in heaven that rises least of all.

Speaking this way is what suits your minds,
since only through the senses do you grasp
what you then make fit for the intellect.

For this same reason Scripture stoops to meet
your faculties, giving God a face and hands
and feet — and meaning something other by them;

and Holy Church will paint Gabriel for you
with a human face, and Michael, and the one
who gave Tobias back his healthy sight.

What Timaeus argues about the soul
is not the truth you see displayed up here,
for he appears to mean just what he says.

He says the soul goes home to its own star,
believing it was severed from that place
the moment nature gave it as a form.

But perhaps his meaning runs another way
than the words sound, and may well carry
a sense that is not to be mocked.

If what he means is that the honor and the blame
of their influence return to these turning wheels,
then perhaps his arrow strikes a kind of truth.

This principle, ill understood, once warped
nearly the whole world off its course, until
it ran astray to Jove and Mercury and Mars.

The other doubt that troubles you has less
venom in it, for its malice could not
ever draw you off to anywhere but me.

That our justice should look unjust to mortal eyes
is an argument for faith, and not
for heresy and wickedness.

But since your own grasp has the power to reach
clear to the bottom of this truth, I'll give you
the rest you ask for, and set the matter right.

If it counts as violence only when the one
who suffers gives no help to the force applied,
then these souls have no such excuse to plead;

for will, unless it wills, is never quenched,
but does as fire does, springing back to its place
though violence wrench it aside a thousand times.

So when it bends, by much or little, it abets
the force — and these did just that, having the power
to turn back toward the holy place they'd left.

Had their will held whole and unbroken, the will
that kept Lawrence steady on the burning grill,
that made Mucius hard against his own scorched hand,

it would have driven them straight back up the road
they had been dragged from, the instant they were free —
but a will so solid as that is far too rare.

And by these words, if you have gathered them
the way you should, the argument is undone
that would have plagued you many times again.

But now a fresh strait crosses your path, and one
you could not thread alone — you would grow weary
long before you worked your way to the far side.

I have set firmly in your mind, for certain,
that a soul made blessed could never tell a lie,
for it dwells close beside the primal Truth;

and yet from Piccarda you might have heard
that Constance still kept her love for the veil —
so that she seems, in this, to contradict me.

Many a time, brother, it has come to pass
that, to escape some peril, men do against their will
the very thing it was not right to do —

as Alcmaeon, begged on by his own father,
killed his own mother, and so, rather than
lose pity, made himself a pitiless man.

Here is the point I want you to hold onto:
force is mixed in with will, and together they
make the offense one that cannot be excused.

Will, taken absolute, gives no consent to wrong;
but it consents just so far as it fears
that holding back will pitch it into worse.

So when Piccarda speaks the way she does,
she means the absolute will, and I the other —
and both of us, in saying this, speak true."

Such was the flowing of that holy river
that poured from the spring where all truth has its source;
it laid to rest, at once, both of my doubts.

"O love of the First Lover, O divine one,"
I said straightway, "whose speech comes flooding in
and warms me so, it quickens me the more —

my own love is not deep enough to pay you
grace for the grace you give; let Him who sees
and can reward it answer that for me.

Well do I see that our intellect is never
satisfied unless the Truth shines into it,
beyond which no further truth has room to stretch.

In that Truth it rests, like a wild beast in its den,
the moment it arrives — and it can arrive;
if it could not, then every desire would fail.

And so, at the foot of truth, doubt springs up
like a green shoot; and this is nature's doing,
urging us upward, peak to peak, to the height.

This invites me, this gives me the assurance
to ask you, Lady — and with all reverence —
about another truth still dark to me.

I want to know whether a man can make it good
with you for broken vows by other deeds,
so your scales will not find them weighing light."

Beatrice looked at me, and her eyes were full
of such sparks of love, and so divine, that mine,
overcome, gave way — I turned my face aside

and almost lost myself, with my eyes cast down.
