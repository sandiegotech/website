The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XVI

Purgatorio: Canto XVI50 of 100

The dark of hell, and of a night stripped bare
of every planet, under a starved sky
clouded as deep as cloud can make it black —

none of it ever drew so thick a veil
across my eyes as did that smoke which wrapped us,
nor a veil so harsh and coarse against the skin.

It would not let a single eye stay open;
so my escort, knowing and faithful both,
drew close to me and offered me his shoulder.

The way a blind man walks behind his guide
so as not to stray, and not to knock against
something that could hurt him, or could kill,

I made my way through that foul, bitter air,
listening to my Leader, who said only,
over and over, "Keep from being cut off from me."

I heard voices, and every one of them
seemed to be begging peace and mercy from
the Lamb of God who takes away our sins.

"Agnus Dei" was how each one began;
one word ran through them all, and one same measure,
so that among them there was perfect concord.

"Master," I said, "are these the spirits I hear?"
And he to me: "You hear it rightly — these
are loosing, strand by strand, the knot of wrath."

"Now who are you, splitting our smoke apart
and speaking of us as though you still divided
your months and days by the calendar of the living?"

So spoke a voice out of the dark to us;
at which my Master said: "Give him your answer,
and ask if this is the way that climbs up."

And I: "O soul who cleanses yourself clean
to go back beautiful to the One who made you,
follow me and you'll hear a thing of wonder."

"I'll follow you as far as I'm allowed,"
he answered; "and if the smoke won't let us see,
our hearing will hold us together in its place."

Then I began: "I'm still wrapped in the flesh
that death unwinds, and I am climbing upward;
I came here through the anguish of the inferno.

And since God in his grace has gathered me in,
so far as to will that I should see his court
in a way completely unknown to the present age,

don't hide from me who you were before you died,
but tell me — and tell me whether I'm headed right
for the pass; let your words be our guide through this."

"I was a Lombard, and my name was Marco;
I knew the world, and I loved that worth and honor
at which now every man has unstrung his bow.

To climb on up, you're going the right way."
So he replied, and added: "I beg of you,
pray for me once you've reached the world above."

And I to him: "On my faith I bind myself
to do the thing you ask; but I am bursting
inside with a doubt, unless I work it loose.

It was a single doubt, and now it's doubled
by what you've said, which makes me sure of something
here and elsewhere — and I set yours beside it.

The world is truly stripped of every virtue,
empty of it, exactly as you tell me,
and swollen and overgrown with wickedness;

but show me, I beg you, where the cause of it lies,
so I can see it and point it out to others;
for some put it in heaven, some down here."

A deep sigh, which grief wrung down into a groan,
he sent out first; and then he began: "Brother,
the world is blind, and clearly you come from it.

You who are still alive trace every cause
back up to the heavens, as though they swept
all things along with them, by sheer necessity.

If that were so, then free will would be destroyed
in you, and there would be no justice left
in joy for doing good, or grief for evil.

The heavens do set your impulses in motion —
not all, I say; but even granting they did,
you've been given a light to know good from evil,

and free will too; and if that will should tire
in its first battles against the heavens' pull,
well nourished, it goes on to conquer all.

Though free, you are subject to a greater power
and a better nature — and that is what creates
the mind in you the heavens do not govern.

So if the present world has gone astray,
the cause is in yourselves; look there for it,
and I will be your faithful scout in this.

Out of the hands of One who loves it dearly
before it even exists — like a little girl
who cries and laughs in her small childish play —

comes the simple soul, that knows nothing at all,
except that, set in motion by a glad Maker,
it turns with delight to whatever gives it pleasure.

First it tastes the savor of some small good;
there it's deceived, and it goes chasing after it,
unless a guide or a rein bends back its love.

And so it was right to set down law as a rein,
right to have a king who could at least make out
the tower of the true city from far off.

The laws exist — but whose hand is on them now?
No one's; because the shepherd who walks in front
can chew the cud, but his hoof is not divided;

and so the people, seeing their own guide
lunge only at the good that he is greedy for,
feed on that too, and ask for nothing higher.

You can see plainly: it is bad leadership
that is the cause the world has turned corrupt —
not any nature gone rotten inside you.

Rome, which once made the world good, used to have
two suns, and they lit up the one road and the other —
the road of the world, and the road of God.

Now one has put the other out; the sword
is welded to the crook, and the two of them
forced into one hand can only go badly,

because, once joined, the one no longer fears
the other. If you doubt me, look to the grain:
every plant is known by the seed it grows from.

In the country the Po and the Adige water,
worth and courtesy used to be easy to find,
before Frederick ran into his quarrel;

now anyone can travel through there safely
who, out of shame, would rather keep his distance
from good men — would rather not be seen with them.

True, three old men are left there still, in whom
the old age shames the new, and they think God
is slow to call them back to the better life:

Currado da Palazzo, and good Gherardo,
and Guido da Castello — better named,
the way the French would say it, the plain Lombard.

Say from now on that the Church of Rome,
by fusing the two governments in itself,
falls in the mire and fouls itself and its load."

"O my Marco," I said, "you argue it well;
and now I see why the sons of Levi were
shut out from any share in the inheritance.

But which Gherardo is this, the one you name
as a remnant of a vanished breed, a reproach
still standing against this barbarous age?"

"Either your speech deceives me, or it's testing me,"
he answered; "for, speaking Tuscan to me here,
you seem to know nothing of good Gherardo.

I know him by no other name than that,
unless I take one from his daughter, Gaia.
God be with you; I can come no farther now.

Look — there's the dawn, beginning to whiten
through the smoke; I have to leave you now
(the angel is there) before I come in his sight."

So he turned back, and would not hear me more.
