The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto XV

Paradiso: Canto XV82 of 100

A will that wishes only good — the will
in which the love that breathes straight and true
always shows itself, as greed shows in the crooked —

imposed a silence on that sweet lyre
and stilled the holy strings that Heaven's hand
draws taut or lets go slack as it sees fit.

How could they be deaf to a just request,
those souls who, to give me the will to ask them,
fell silent, all of them, with one accord?

It is right that he should grieve without end
who, for the love of what cannot last,
strips himself forever of that love.

As through the clear and quiet evening air
a sudden fire shoots across the sky,
turning eyes that had been fixed before,

and seems a star that has changed its place —
except that where it kindled, nothing's missing,
and the streak itself lasts hardly any time:

so from the right arm of the cross there ran
a star, down to the foot, out of the host
of lights that blaze along that constellation.

And the gem never left its ribbon of light
but slid the whole length of that radiant band,
like a flame seen burning behind alabaster.

With just such love Anchises' shade reached out —
if we can trust our greatest Muse at all —
when in Elysium he saw his son.

"O sanguis meus, O superinfusa
gratia Dei, sicut tibi, cui
bis unquam Coeli janua reclusa?"

So that brightness spoke, and I gave it my full
attention; then I turned my eyes back to my Lady,
and on this side and that I stood amazed —

for in her eyes there burned so deep a smile
that I thought my own had touched the very floor
of my grace and of my Paradise.

Then the spirit, a joy to hear and see,
added to what it had begun things
so deep I could not follow what it said.

It did not hide itself from me by choice
but by necessity — for what it conceived
set itself above the reach of mortal minds.

And when the bow of its burning sympathy
had eased enough that its speech came down
to where our understanding could receive it,

the first thing I was able to make out
was "Blessed be You, Three and One,
who have been so gracious to my seed."

And it went on: "A long and grateful hunger,
drawn from reading the great volume
whose white and black are never changed,

you have satisfied in me, my son,
within this light from which I speak to you —
thanks to her who gave you wings for this high flight.

You believe your thought flows through to me
from the First Mind, the way five and six
ray out from one, once one is understood;

and so you do not ask me who I am,
or why I seem more full of joy to you
than anyone else in this glad company.

You believe the truth: for in this life
the small and great alike gaze into the mirror
where, before you think it, your thought is shown.

But so the sacred love in which I keep
unbroken watch, and which makes me thirst
with sweet desire, may be the better filled,

let your voice, sure and frank and glad, ring out
your wish, ring out the longing — to which
my answer is already decreed."

I turned to Beatrice, and she heard me
before I spoke, and smiled me such a sign
that the wings of my desire grew wider;

and then I began: "Love and understanding,
the moment the first Equality dawned on you,
became of one same weight in each of you —

for in the Sun that lit you and that warmed you
with its heat and light, the two are so equal
that every comparison falls short.

But in us mortals, will and the words to say it,
for a reason plain to you, are feathered
unevenly, the one outflying the other.

So I, who am mortal, feel that unevenness
in myself; and so I give no thanks
but in my heart for this fatherly welcome.

Truly I beg you, living topaz, you
who are set as a jewel in this precious gem:
let me hear your name, and be satisfied."

"O leaf of mine, in whom I took delight
even while I waited — I was your own root."
So he began his answer to me.

Then he said: "The man your family is named for,
who for a hundred years and more has circled
the first ledge of the mountain, going round,

was a son of mine, and your great-grandfather:
it is right that you should shorten his long labor
for him with works of your own.

Florence, inside the ring of her old walls —
from which she still takes the bells of terce and nones —
lived in peace, sober and chaste.

She wore no chain of gold, no crown,
no embroidered sandals, no sash designed
to catch the eye more than the woman did.

A daughter's birth did not yet frighten her father:
the age of marriage and the size of the dowry
had not run past all measure on either side.

There were no houses emptied of their families,
nor had Sardanapalus arrived yet
to show what could be done behind closed doors.

Your Uccellatoio had not yet outdone
the hill of Montemalo — which, outdone
in its rising, will be outdone in its fall.

I saw Bellincion Berti go out belted
in plain leather and bone, and his wife
leave the mirror with her face unpainted;

I saw the men of the Nerli and the Vecchio
content in unlined buckskin, and their wives
at the spindle and the flax.

Lucky women! Each was sure of where
her grave would be, and none was yet abandoned
in her bed for the sake of France.

One kept her watchful vigil at the cradle
and, soothing the child, used the baby-talk
that fathers and mothers first delight in;

another, drawing the wool out from her distaff,
would tell her household the old stories
of the Trojans, of Fiesole, of Rome.

A Cianghella then, or a Lapo Salterello,
would have been as great a wonder to that city
as a Cincinnatus or a Cornelia is to ours.

Into a life so peaceful and so fair,
into a citizenry so trustworthy,
into so sweet a place to dwell,

Mary gave me — called on with loud cries —
and in your ancient Baptistery I became,
in one stroke, a Christian and Cacciaguida.

Moronto was my brother, and Eliseo;
my wife came to me out of the Po valley,
and from that place your surname was drawn.

Afterward I followed the Emperor Conrad,
and he girded me into his knighthood —
so well I pleased him by my good deeds.

I rode in his train against the wickedness
of that creed whose people seize what is yours
by right — and the fault is your shepherd's.

There, by that loathsome race, I was set free
from the bonds of the deceiving world,
the love of which corrupts so many souls,

and I came from martyrdom into this peace."
