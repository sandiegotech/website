The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto III

Paradiso: Canto III70 of 100

That Sun which first had warmed my heart with love
had now unveiled for me the lovely face
of truth, by proof and counter-proof made plain;

and so, to own myself convinced and sure
as far as it was fitting that I should,
I raised my head a little, ready to speak.

But a vision appeared and drew me to it,
pressing so close to be seen
that I forgot the confession I had meant to make.

The way a face comes faintly back to us
through clear and polished glass, or through still water
so untroubled and not so deep the bed is lost —

the outline returning as faint
as a pearl on a white brow
comes to the eye —

so I saw many faces eager to speak,
and fell into the error opposite
to the one that lit love between the boy and the spring.

The moment I became aware of them,
taking them for reflections in a mirror,
I turned my eyes to find whose they might be,

and saw nothing; and turned them forward again,
straight into the light of my sweet Guide,
whose holy eyes had kindled as she smiled.

"Don't wonder," she said, "that I smile
at this childish notion of yours, since it will not yet
set its foot on the truth,

but turns you, as it always does, on emptiness.
These are true substances you see before you,
set here for having left some vow undone.

So speak with them, and listen, and believe;
for the true light that gives them peace
will not let them turn their feet away from it."

And I turned toward the shade that seemed most eager
to speak, and I began,
like a man bewildered by too much eagerness:

"O spirit well created, you who taste,
in the rays of life eternal, that sweetness
which, untasted, can never be understood,

it would be a kindness if you'd content me
with your name and with your destiny."
At which, with smiling eyes, she answered at once:

"Our love never shuts its door
against a rightful wish — no more than the Love
that wills its whole court should be like itself.

In the world I was a virgin, a vowed sister;
and if your memory searches itself with care,
my being more beautiful now will not hide me from you,

but you will know again that I am Piccarda,
who, stationed here among these other blessed,
am blessed myself in the slowest of the spheres.

All our affections, which take fire
only in the pleasure of the Holy Spirit,
rejoice to be shaped to his order;

and this place, which seems so low,
is given to us because our vows
were left undone, in some part void."

Then I to her: "In your wondrous faces
something divine shines through I cannot name,
transforming you from what I first remembered.

That is why I was slow to know you again;
but what you tell me now so helps me
that the refiguring comes easier to me.

But tell me — you who are happy in this place —
do you long for a higher station,
to see more, or to be more dear to God?"

She smiled a little, first, with the other shades;
then answered me so full of gladness
she seemed to burn in love's first fire:

"Brother, the power of charity quiets our will
and makes us wish only for what we have,
and gives us no thirst for anything more.

If we longed to be set higher,
our longing would fall out of tune
with the will of Him who assigns us here;

and you will see there is no room for that
within these circles, if living in love
is necessary here — and if you look well into what love is.

No, it belongs to the heart of this blessed life
to hold itself within the will divine,
so that our wills are gathered into one;

so that, however we are ranked from sphere to sphere
across this realm, it pleases all the realm,
as it pleases the King who draws our will into his own.

And in his will is our peace. It is the sea
toward which all things are moving —
all that his will creates, and all that nature makes."

Then it was clear to me how everywhere
in heaven is Paradise, even though the grace
of the highest Good does not rain down in one measure.

But as it happens that when one food fills us
and the craving for another still remains,
we ask for that, and decline the first with thanks —

so I did, with gesture and with word,
to learn from her the weaving in which
she had not drawn the shuttle to the end.

"A perfect life and a high merit lift
a lady higher up," she said, "and by her rule
down in your world they take the veil and gown,

so that till death they may both wake and sleep
beside the Bridegroom who accepts every vow
that charity has shaped to his own pleasure.

To follow her I fled the world while still a girl,
and shut myself inside her habit,
and pledged myself to the way of her order.

Then men more used to evil than to good
tore me out of the sweet cloister;
God knows what my life became after that.

And this other brightness, who shows herself
to you here at my right hand, and burns
with all the light our sphere can give —

what I have said of myself applies to her:
a nun she was, and from her head, as well,
the shadow of the sacred veil was torn.

But when she too was returned to the world,
against her will and against all right usage,
from the veil of her heart she was never stripped.

This is the radiance of the great Constance,
who from the second wind of Swabia
brought forth the third and final power."

So she spoke to me, and then began
to sing "Ave Maria" — and singing, faded,
like something heavy sinking through deep water.

My sight, which followed her as long
as it was able, when it had lost her,
turned back toward the mark of its greater longing,

and gave itself wholly again to Beatrice;
but she flashed such lightning into my eyes
that at first my sight could not endure it —

and that made me slower to ask what came next.
