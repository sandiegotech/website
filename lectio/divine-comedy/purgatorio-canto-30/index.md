# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XXX

When the Sevenfold Stars of the highest heaven  
— that never knew a setting or a rising,  
nor any veil but the veil of sin,

the stars that taught each soul there what to do,  
the way the lower Wain steers the helmsman  
home to his harbor — came to a halt,

the truthful people, who had walked till now  
between those lights and the Griffin, turned  
toward the chariot as toward their peace.

And one of them, as if sent down from Heaven,  
sang out three times, "Veni, sponsa, de Libano,"  
and all the rest took up the cry behind him.

As the Blessed will rise at the last trumpet,  
each one quickened out of his own grave,  
lifting the clothed-again flesh, light now and singing,

so over that celestial chariot rose  
a hundred, ad vocem tanti senis —  
ministers and messengers of life eternal.

They were all saying, "Benedictus qui venis,"  
and, flinging flowers up and all around,  
"Manibus o date lilia plenis."

I have seen, at the break of day, before now,  
the whole eastern sky go rose, and the rest  
of heaven settle into a clear calm;

and the sun's face come up so screened and softened  
by the tempering kindness of the mists  
that the eye could hold it a long while —

and so, inside a cloud of flowers, thrown up  
from the angels' hands and falling down again,  
back into the car and over its sides,

above a white veil bound with olive leaves  
a lady appeared to me, in a green mantle,  
robed in the color of living flame.

And my own spirit, which for so long now  
had not been broken down with awe,  
trembling in her presence —

with no further knowledge come through my eyes,  
but by a hidden power going out from her,  
felt the old love in all its ancient force.

The instant that high power struck my sight  
— the one that had already run me through  
before I was even out of boyhood —

I turned to the left with the same trust  
with which the small child runs to his mother  
when he is frightened, or when he is hurt,

to say to Virgil: "There is not a drop  
of blood left in me that is not shaking.  
I know the signs of the ancient flame."

But Virgil had left us empty of himself,  
Virgil, the gentlest of all fathers,  
Virgil, to whom I gave myself to be saved.

And all that our first mother lost in Eden  
could not keep my cheeks, washed clean of dew,  
from turning dark again with weeping.

"Dante, though Virgil is leaving you,  
do not weep yet, do not weep yet —  
there is another sword you must weep for."

Like an admiral who walks from stern to bow  
to watch the crews at work in the other ships  
and spur them on to do their best,

there on the left-hand rail of the chariot  
— when I turned at the sound of my own name,  
which I set down here only because I must —

I saw the Lady, who a moment before  
had stood veiled in the angels' festival,  
turn her eyes on me across the stream.

Though the veil that fell down from her head,  
circled with Minerva's olive leaves,  
still would not let me see her clearly,

in bearing royal and unbending yet,  
she went on like one who, as she speaks,  
holds her hottest words back in reserve:

"Look hard at me. I am — I truly am — Beatrice.  
How did you dare, at last, to climb the mountain?  
Did you not know that here a man is happy?"

My eyes dropped down to the clear stream below,  
but, seeing myself in it, I sought the grass,  
so heavy was the shame that bent my brow.

As a mother can seem stern to her own son,  
so she seemed to me; for there is something bitter  
in the taste of love that comes as judgment.

She fell silent; and at once the Angels sang,  
"In te, Domine, speravi," but they  
went no further than "pedes meos."

As the snow, among the living rafters  
along the spine of Italy, will freeze,  
packed and driven hard by Slavonian winds,

and then, dissolving, trickles down through itself  
the moment the shadowless land breathes warm,  
like fire melting a candle from within —

so I stood, without a tear, without a sigh,  
before the singing of those who sing forever  
in time with the music of the eternal spheres.

But when I heard, inside their gentle voices,  
their pity for me — more than if they had said,  
"Lady, why do you shame him so?" —

the ice that had packed itself around my heart  
turned to breath and water, and in my anguish  
came pouring out through my mouth and eyes.

And she, still standing firm on the right-hand rail  
of the chariot, turned and aimed her words  
at those compassionate beings, and said:

"You keep your watch in the unending day,  
so that no night, no sleep, can steal from you  
one step the ages take along their road;

so I will answer with the greater care,  
that he may hear me, the one weeping there,  
and let the sorrow match the sin in measure.

Not only by the working of the great wheels,  
that drive each seed toward some appointed end  
according to the stars it rises under,

but by the open hand of grace from Heaven,  
whose rains come down from clouds so high above us  
our sight cannot come near them — this man,

in his new life, had so much given him  
in promise, that every good and rightful habit  
would have borne wonders in him, had it grown.

But the more vigor the soil holds in itself,  
the wilder and more poisonous it runs  
when it is left untilled and sown with weeds.

For a while I held him up with my own face;  
turning my young eyes full upon him,  
I led him with me, walking the right way.

But the moment I had crossed the threshold  
into my second age, and changed my life,  
he took himself from me and gave himself to others.

When I had risen from the flesh to spirit,  
and beauty and virtue had grown greater in me,  
I was to him less dear, less his delight;

and he turned his steps onto the untrue ways,  
chasing the false images of the good  
that never make good on a single promise.

Nor did it help that I begged for grace to reach him,  
calling him back to me in dreams and waking —  
so little did he care to lift his head.

He sank so low that every means of saving him  
had fallen short by then, except the one:  
to show him the people who are lost.

And so I went down to the gates of the dead,  
and to the one who has led him up this far  
my prayers were carried, and carried with my weeping.

The high decree of God would be undone  
if Lethe could be crossed, and its waters tasted,  
without some payment first —

the toll of penitence, that pours out in tears."
