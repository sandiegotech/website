The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XXII

Purgatorio: Canto XXII56 of 100

The Angel was already behind us now,
the one who had turned us toward the sixth ring,
having wiped one more mark away from my brow,

and those who set their hunger upon justice
had called out to us, in their singing voices,
"Beati," and the word "sitiunt" — no more.

And I, lighter now than at the earlier passes,
climbed on so freely that with no effort at all
I kept up the swift-footed spirits' pace,

when Virgil began: "Love kindled by virtue
will always light another love in turn,
so long as its flame shows plainly on the outside.

So from the hour that Juvenal came down
among us, into the Limbo of Hell,
and made your fondness for me known to me,

my own goodwill toward you grew as strong
as any a man has felt for one unseen —
so strong these stairs will seem too short to me.

But tell me, and as a friend forgive me
if too much trust has let my reins run slack,
and from here on speak to me as a friend:

how could it ever find a place inside you —
such greed for money — in a breast so filled
with all the wisdom your hard work had earned?"

These words at first moved Statius a little
toward laughter; then he gave his answer:
"Every word of yours is a dear sign of your love.

Truly, how often things will wear a look
that feeds our doubts with false and empty reasons,
because the real causes stay out of sight.

Your question shows me you believe I was
a miser in the other life — perhaps
because of the very circle where I stood.

Then know this: greed was set so far from me
it was no fault of mine. The opposite vice —
throwing money away — is what I'm paying for,

and thousands of turning moons have punished it.
And had I not corrected my own bent
when I came to the place where you cry out,

as if in outrage at the human heart,
'Why, O holy hunger for gold,
do you not rule the appetites of men?' —

I would be rolling weights in the grim jousts below.
Then I saw how wide the hands can spread
their wings in spending, and I repented —

that fault, and all my other sins, alike.
How many will rise again with shaven heads
because they never knew this for a sin,

so that no repentance reached them, in life or death!
And know that any vice which runs dead against
another, by sheer opposition, dries

its green growth here together with that other.
So if I have been among the people who weep
their avarice, to be made clean again,

it is for the contrary fault this came to me."

"Now when you sang the cruel clash of arms
in the twofold grief of Jocasta's sons,"
said the singer of the shepherd songs,

"from what your Muse Clio strikes up there with you,
it doesn't seem that you had yet been brought
to the faith without which good works aren't enough.

If that's so, then what sun, or what candles,
so scattered your darkness that from then on
you set your sails to follow the Fisherman?"

And he replied: "You were the first to send me
toward Parnassus, to drink in its caves,
and you the first to light my way toward God.

You did as one who walks the road at night
and carries the lamp behind him — no help to himself,
but he makes the ones who follow wise —

when you said: 'The age begins again;
justice returns, and the first human time,
and a new race comes down from the heavens.'

Through you I became a poet; through you, a Christian.
But so you can see more clearly what I mean,
I'll reach out my hand and add the color in.

By then the world in every quarter
was pregnant with the true belief, sown wide
by messengers of the everlasting kingdom,

and the words of yours I quoted just now
rang in tune with what the new preachers said.
So I took up the habit of visiting them.

They came to seem so holy in my eyes
that when Domitian set his hounds upon them,
their weeping was never without tears of mine,

and all the years I still remained on earth
I stood by them; their good and upright ways
made me look down on every other sect.

And before I led the Greeks, in my poem,
to the rivers of Thebes, I was baptized —
but out of fear I kept my faith a secret,

wearing the mask of a pagan a long while;
and for that lukewarmness I circled the fourth ring
more than four hundred years before I climbed.

You, then — you who lifted the cloth
that hid from me the good I've been describing —
while we still have time to spare on the way up,

tell me where our old friend Terence is,
and Caecilius, Plautus, Varro, if you know:
tell me if they're damned, and in what quarter."

"They, and Persius, and I, and many more,"
my Leader said, "are with that Greek the Muses
suckled more deeply than they did the rest,

down in the first ring of the sightless prison.
Often we talk together of the mountain
where our nurses, the Muses, live forever.

Euripides is there with us, Antiphon,
Simonides, Agathon, and many other
Greeks who once crowned their brows with laurel.

There, too, you may see some of your own people:
Antigone, Deiphyle, and Argia,
and Ismene, mournful as she ever was.

There is the one who showed them the Langia spring;
there is Tiresias' daughter, and Thetis,
and there Deidamia among her sisters."

By now both poets had fallen silent,
intent once more on looking all around,
free of the climb and free of the walls,

and four of the day's handmaids were already
left behind, and the fifth stood at the chariot pole,
still aiming its burning horn straight up,

when my Guide said: "I think we'd best turn
our right shoulders toward the outer edge
and circle the mountain the way we always have."

So custom was our compass in that place,
and we took up our way with less misgiving
because that worthy soul had given his nod.

They walked ahead, and I came on alone
behind them, listening to their talk,
which taught me, as I went, the craft of song.

But soon a tree broke off their sweet exchange —
a tree we came upon mid-road, its apples
sweet and inviting to the smell.

And as a fir tapers narrower upward
from branch to branch, this one tapered down,
so that, I think, no one could climb it.

On the side where our path was hemmed by rock,
clear water fell from the cliff above
and spread itself across the leaves.

The two poets drew near to the tree,
and from within the leaves a voice cried out:
"This food will be denied to you."

Then it said: "Mary's thought was more for making
the wedding feast complete and honorable
than for her own mouth, which now pleads for you.

And the women of old Rome were content
to drink plain water; and Daniel scorned
fine food, and gained his understanding.

The first age was as beautiful as gold:
it made the acorns savory with hunger,
and turned every stream to nectar with thirst.

Honey and locusts were the food that fed
the Baptist in the wilderness — and so
he is glorious, and great, as the Gospel shows you."
