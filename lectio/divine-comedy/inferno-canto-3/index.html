The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto III

Inferno: Canto III3 of 100

"Through me you go to the city of grief.
Through me you go to everlasting pain.
Through me you go among the people who are lost.

Justice moved my maker on high.
Divine power made me,
the highest wisdom, and the first love.

Before me nothing was created
but things eternal, and I last forever.
Abandon every hope, you who enter here."

These words, in a dark color, I saw
carved over the top of a gate.
I said: "Master, their meaning is hard for me."

And he, like one who already knew, replied:
"Here you must let go of every doubt.
Every cowardice must die here.

We have come to the place I told you of,
where you will see the suffering people
who have lost the good of the mind."

And then he laid his hand on mine,
with a calm, glad look that steadied me,
and led me in among the hidden things.

There sighs and weeping and loud wailing
rang through the starless air,
so that at first I wept to hear it.

Strange tongues, monstrous accents,
words of pain, voices of fury,
high cries and hoarse ones, and the smack of hands —

all of it made a tumult that wheels on
forever through that air forever black,
the way sand swirls up when the whirlwind blows.

And I, my head ringed round with horror,
said: "Master, what is this I'm hearing?
Who are these people so broken by their pain?"

And he to me: "This wretched way of being
belongs to the dreary souls of those
who lived with no disgrace and no praise.

They are mixed in with that worthless choir
of angels who were neither rebels
nor faithful to God, but only for themselves.

Heaven cast them out, to keep its beauty pure,
and the depths of Hell won't take them either,
since the damned would find some glory next to them."

And I: "Master, what weighs on them so hard
that it makes them cry out like this?"
He answered: "I'll tell you, and keep it short.

These have no hope of ever dying,
and their blind life is so debased
they envy every other fate there is.

The world allows no memory of them.
Mercy and justice both look down on them.
Let's not talk about them — look, and move on."

And I, looking again, saw a banner
that ran in circles, spinning so fast
it seemed to scorn the thought of ever stopping.

And behind it came so long a line
of people, I would never have believed
that death had undone so many.

When I had recognized a few of them,
I looked, and I saw the shade of the one
who out of cowardice made the great refusal.

At once I understood, and I was sure,
that this was the crowd of worthless souls
hateful to God and to his enemies alike.

These wretches, who were never truly alive,
were naked, and were stung again and again
by the wasps and hornets swarming there.

The stings streaked their faces with blood
that ran down, mixed with their tears,
and was gathered at their feet by loathsome worms.

And when I turned to look still farther on,
I saw people along the bank of a great river,
and I said: "Master, tell me now,

so I may know who these are, and what law
makes them seem so eager to cross over,
as far as I can tell in this dim light."

And he to me: "All this will be made clear
to you, the moment we stop and stand
on the dismal shore of Acheron."

Then, with my eyes lowered in shame,
afraid my words had begun to annoy him,
I held my tongue until we reached the river.

And look — coming toward us in a boat,
an old man, white with the hair of age,
shouting: "Misery on you, you wicked souls!

Give up all hope of ever seeing the sky.
I come to carry you to the other shore,
into the eternal dark, the heat and frost.

And you over there — you, the living soul —
get away from these, for they are dead."
But when he saw that I would not go,

he said: "By another way, another port
you'll reach the shore to cross, not here.
A lighter craft will have to carry you."

And my guide: "Charon, don't fight it.
This is willed where what is willed is done,
so ask no more."

At that, the shaggy cheeks went still
on the ferryman of the livid swamp,
who had wheels of flame around his eyes.

But those souls, worn out and naked,
went pale and ground their teeth together
the moment they heard the cruel words.

They cursed God and cursed their parents,
the whole human race, the place, the time,
the seed they sprang from, the day they were born.

Then all of them drew back together,
weeping bitterly, toward the cursed shore
that waits for every man who does not fear God.

Charon the demon, with eyes like burning coals,
signals to them and herds them all aboard,
beating with his oar any soul that lags.

The way the leaves let go in autumn,
one after another, until the branch
sees all its riches lying on the ground —

just so the evil seed of Adam
casts itself down from that bank one by one,
at his signal, like a bird answering its call.

So they go off across the dark water,
and before they have landed on the far side,
a fresh crowd is already gathering on this one.

"My son," the gentle master said to me,
"all those who die under the wrath of God
come together here from every land.

And they are eager to cross the river,
because the justice of Heaven drives them on
until their fear is turned into desire.

No good soul ever passes this way.
So if Charon complains about you,
you can understand now what his words mean."

When he had finished, the whole dark plain
shook so violently that even now
the memory of that terror bathes me in sweat.

The land of tears let out a blast of wind
that flared with a flash of crimson light,
and it overcame every sense in me,

and I fell, like a man taken by sleep.
