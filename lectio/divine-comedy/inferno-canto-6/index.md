# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto VI

When my mind came back to itself —  
it had closed before the pity of those two  
relatives, who left me lost in sorrow —

new torments and new tormented souls  
rose up around me, wherever I moved,  
whichever way I turned, whatever I looked at.

I am in the third circle now, the circle  
of rain — eternal, cursed, cold, and heavy.  
Its rule and its kind never change.

Huge hail, and filthy water, and snow  
pour down through the dark air without end;  
the ground that takes it stinks.

Cerberus, a cruel and monstrous beast,  
barks like a dog out of three throats  
over the people sunk in the muck.

His eyes are red, his beard greasy and black,  
his belly huge, his hands armed with claws;  
he tears the spirits, skins them, rips them apart.

The rain makes them howl like dogs.  
They use one flank to shield the other;  
over and over the wretches twist and turn.

When Cerberus, the great worm, caught sight of us,  
he opened his mouths and bared his fangs;  
not a single limb of him held still.

And my guide reached out with both hands,  
scooped up the earth, and with his fists full  
threw it down into those ravenous throats.

Like a dog that barks and barks with hunger  
but goes quiet the moment it has food to gnaw,  
straining and fighting only to swallow it down —

that was how those filth-smeared muzzles went,  
those jaws of the demon Cerberus, whose roar  
leaves the souls wishing they were deaf.

We walked on across the shades the heavy rain  
beats down, and we set our feet  
on their emptiness, which looks like a real body.

Every one of them lay flat on the ground,  
except for one, who sat straight up  
the instant he saw us passing in front of him.

"You there, being led through this Hell,"  
he said to me, "recognize me, if you can.  
You were made before I was unmade."

And I to him: "The agony you're in  
may be what blots you out of my memory,  
so that it seems I've never seen you before.

But tell me who you are, to be set  
in a place so wretched, in such a punishment  
that if some are worse, none is more revolting."

And he to me: "Your city — so full  
of envy now that the sack runs over —  
held me inside it in the bright life above.

You citizens used to call me Ciacco.  
For the ruinous sin of gluttony  
I lie here, as you see, beaten down by the rain.

And I'm not the only sad soul here;  
all of these are under the same penalty  
for the same sin." And he said no more.

I answered him: "Ciacco, your suffering  
weighs on me so it brings me to tears.  
But tell me, if you know, what will become

of the citizens of that divided city?  
Is there a single just man left? And tell me  
why so much strife has torn it apart."

And he to me: "After long quarreling  
they'll come to blood, and the rustic party  
will drive the other out with brutal force.

Then within three years that side must fall  
in turn, and the first will rise again  
by the power of one who now plays both shores.

For a long while it will hold its head high,  
keeping the other crushed under heavy loads,  
however much it weeps and rages at it.

The just are two, and no one there will hear them.  
Pride, envy, and greed are the three sparks  
that have set every heart on fire."

Here he brought his tearful words to an end.  
And I to him: "I want you to teach me more —  
give me the gift of a little more speech.

Farinata and Tegghiaio, who were so worthy,  
Jacopo Rusticucci, Arrigo, Mosca,  
and the others who set their minds on doing good —

tell me where they are, let me know them.  
A great longing drives me to learn  
whether Heaven sweetens them or Hell poisons them."

And he: "They are among the blackest souls.  
A different sin drags them to the bottom.  
If you go down far enough, you'll see them.

But when you are back in the sweet world,  
I beg you, bring me to the minds of others.  
No more will I tell you, no more will I answer."

Then he twisted his straight gaze to the side,  
looked at me a moment, and bowed his head —  
and fell with it, face down, among the other blind.

And my guide said to me: "He won't wake again  
until the angel's trumpet sounds,  
when the hostile Power comes at last.

Each one will find his miserable grave again,  
will take back his flesh and his own shape,  
will hear the verdict that thunders through eternity."

So we made our slow way across that foul  
mixture of shades and rain, our steps dragging,  
touching a little on the life to come.

And so I asked: "Master, this torment here —  
after the great sentence is passed,  
will it grow worse, or ease, or stay this fierce?"

And he to me: "Go back to your science,  
which holds that the more perfect a thing is,  
the more it feels of pleasure and of pain.

Even though these cursed people  
can never come to true perfection,  
they look to be more whole then than now."

We circled around that road, talking  
of much more than I'll repeat here,  
until we reached the point where the path drops down.

There we found Plutus, the great enemy.
