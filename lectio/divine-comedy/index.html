Lectio Edition · 2026

The Divine Comedy

Dante Alighieri

  • 1308–1320
  • Western tradition
  • Epic poem
  • Lectio Edition 2026

Dante's journey through Hell, Purgatory and Paradise, led first by Virgil and then by Beatrice. One hundred cantos in three-line stanzas, written in Italian between about 1308 and 1320.

Why this editionGoal and purpose

Longfellow kept Dante's lines and lost his speed.

The public-domain Dante in the bundle is Longfellow's of 1867. It is faithful, line for line. It is also full of the inverted word order of its century: "a forest dark", "All hope abandon, ye who enter in".

Dante's Italian is concrete and quick. It was the spoken language of his city, chosen over Latin so that people could read it.

The Lectio Edition keeps Dante's form, one tercet at a time, on his lines, and puts the words in the order English uses now. Longfellow stays in the app beside it, and because both follow Dante's lines the two can be compared stanza by stanza.

The openingFrom the Lectio Edition

How it begins.

Inferno: Canto I

Midway along the journey of our life
I came to myself in a dark wood,
for the straight road had been lost.

How hard it is to say what it was like —
that wood, so wild and rough and unrelenting
that the fear comes back the moment I think of it.

It was so bitter that death is hardly worse.
But to tell of the good I found there too,
I'll speak of the other things I saw.

How I came to enter it I can't say clearly,
so heavy was I with sleep at the moment
I wandered off from the true way.

But once I had reached the foot of a hill,
there where the valley came to an end
that had pierced my heart with dread,

I looked up and saw its shoulders
already wrapped in that planet's rays
that lead us straight down every road.

The first 150 words of the Lectio Edition, as they stand in the bundle the app reads.

The choicesPolicies and divergences

What this edition decided.

Three reviewers took one canticle each and wrote their rules separately. They were then applied as one rule across all hundred cantos.

  • One tercet to a segment, on Dante's lines. Where the English had slipped a line, the lines were moved back.
  • Each canto ends on a single line, and that line stands alone.
  • Latin stays Latin. Where Dante quotes a hymn, a psalm or scripture in Latin, the edition prints the Latin and puts no English beside it in the verse. The draft had translated or glossed many of these.
  • Dante's "Ahi" is "Ah". It is never "God".
  • Leftover archaism came out: "Behold", "yonder", "the while".
  • A word that would mislead a modern reader is replaced by the thing Dante means. The draft had "specialist" for a physician.

Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 309 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 8. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

selva oscura

Inferno I, line 2

Older
"a forest dark" (Longfellow)
Lectio
a dark wood

The Italian is plainly noun then adjective, and English wants it the other way round. It is the canto's governing image and should be bare.

Lasciate ogne speranza, voi ch'intrate

Inferno III, line 9

Older
"All hope abandon, ye who enter in!" (Longfellow)
Lectio
Abandon every hope, you who enter here.

A plain and terrible command: verb, object, address. "Every" because Dante counts each hope given up.

il gran rifiuto

Inferno III, line 60

Older
"Who made through cowardice the great refusal" (Longfellow)
Lectio
who out of cowardice made the great refusal

Dante does not name this shade. The edition does not name him either, and does not explain the phrase. The withholding is the point.

Amor, ch'al cor gentil ratto s'apprende

Inferno V, lines 100 to 106

Older
"Love, that on gentle heart doth swiftly seize" (Longfellow)
Lectio
Love, which is quick to seize the gentle heart

Francesca begins three tercets in a row with "Love". All three openings are kept so the repetition is heard.

fatti non foste a viver come bruti

Inferno XXVI, lines 118 to 120

Older
"Ye were not made to live like unto brutes" (Longfellow)
Lectio
Consider where you came from.

Ulysses to his crew before the last voyage. The tercet goes on: you were not made to live like beasts, but to follow after virtue and knowledge.

Vexilla regis prodeunt inferni

Inferno XXXIV, line 1

Older
left in Latin (Longfellow)
Lectio
Vexilla regis prodeunt inferni

The only line of Latin in the Inferno, a Good Friday hymn with one word added to turn it toward Satan. The draft had put an English version in the verse. The review took it out, as Dante and Longfellow have it.

a riveder le stelle

Inferno XXXIV, line 139

Older
"Thence we came forth to rebehold the stars" (Longfellow)
Lectio
to see again the stars

Each of the three canticles ends on the word "stars". After Hell the line has to open and lift, and a simple verb does it.

l'amor che move il sole e l'altre stelle

Paradiso XXXIII, line 145

Older
"The Love which moves the sun and the other stars" (Longfellow)
Lectio
by the Love that moves the sun and the other stars.

The last line of the poem. Here there is almost nothing to change, and the edition changes almost nothing.

Still open

What the first review left for the editor to decide or restore.

  • The editor's own read of all one hundred cantos.
  • In seven cantos the closing line shares the last segment with the tercet before it, because segment numbers are frozen. One ruling is needed for all seven.
  • Purgatorio XXII is one line off from its twelfth tercet to the end. Realigning it would move every later stanza number in that canto. Paradiso XXX had the same fault and was realigned. Decide whether Purgatorio XXII gets the same.
  • Arnaut Daniel's Occitan in Purgatorio XXVI: three segments that held an English version in brackets are set aside. Decide whether to retire them.
  • Three Latin phrases that Dante sets inside an Italian sentence are still in English. The canticles' rules differ on this.
  • "Behold" survives seven times in the Purgatorio and Paradiso. The rule against it was applied only to the Inferno.
  • Fourteen of the edition's notes paraphrase the text instead of quoting it.
