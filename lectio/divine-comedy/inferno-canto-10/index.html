The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto X

Inferno: Canto X10 of 100

Now my master goes along a hidden path
between the city wall and the tormented,
and I follow at his back.

"O highest power, who wheel me as you please
through these unholy circles," I began,
"speak to me, and satisfy my longing.

The people lying here in these tombs —
could they be seen? All the lids
are already lifted, and no one stands guard."

And he to me: "They will all be sealed
when they come back here from Jehoshaphat
with the bodies they left up above.

On this side they have their burial ground,
Epicurus and all his followers,
who make the soul die along with the body.

But the question you put to me
will be answered soon enough in here,
and so will the wish you keep to yourself."

And I: "Good guide, I don't hide my heart
from you, except to say less than I might —
and not only now have you inclined me to that."

"O Tuscan, you who go alive
through the city of fire, speaking so courteously,
be pleased to stop a moment in this place.

Your way of speaking makes it clear
you were born in that noble homeland
to which I was, perhaps, too much a scourge."

All at once this sound came out
from one of the tombs; and so, afraid,
I drew a little closer to my guide.

And he said to me: "Turn around. What are you doing?
Look at Farinata, who has risen there —
from the waist up you'll see the whole of him."

I had already fixed my eyes on his,
and he was rising, with his chest and brow erect,
as if he held all Hell in great contempt.

And my guide's hands, bold and quick,
pushed me toward him between the tombs,
saying: "Let your words be clear and exact."

As soon as I stood at the foot of his tomb,
he looked me over, and then, almost scornful,
asked me: "Who were your ancestors?"

And I, who wanted only to obey,
hid nothing, but laid it all open to him;
at which he raised his brows a little.

Then he said: "They were fierce enemies
to me, to my forefathers, and to my party,
so that twice over I scattered them."

"If they were driven out, they came back —
from every side, both times," I answered him.
"But your people never quite learned that art."

Then, rising into view beside him,
uncovered to the chin, a shade appeared;
I think he had risen onto his knees.

He looked around me, as if he longed
to see whether someone else was with me;
and when his hope was wholly spent,

weeping, he said: "If you go through this blind
prison by the height of your own genius,
where is my son? Why isn't he with you?"

And I to him: "I don't come on my own.
The one waiting over there leads me here —
perhaps to one your Guido held in disdain."

His words, and the kind of punishment he bore,
had already spelled his name out to me;
that's why my answer was so full.

Suddenly standing bolt upright, he cried:
"What? You said held — held? Is he not still alive?
Does the sweet light no longer strike his eyes?"

When he saw that I had paused,
that I held back a moment before answering,
he fell flat again, and showed himself no more.

But that other, the great-souled one, at whose word
I had stayed, did not change his look,
nor move his neck, nor bend his side.

Picking up his first thread, he went on:
"If they have learned that art so badly,
it torments me more than this bed does.

But the face of the lady who reigns down here
will not be rekindled fifty times
before you'll know the weight of that art yourself.

And as you hope to return to the sweet world,
tell me: why is that people so merciless
against my line in every one of its laws?"

And I to him: "The slaughter and the carnage
that stained the Arbia red
makes such prayers be said in our temple."

Then, having shaken his head with a sigh,
he said: "There I was not alone,
nor surely without cause did I move with the rest.

But I was alone, there where every one of them
agreed to wipe Florence off the earth —
I was the one who defended her openly."

"Ah, so may your seed find rest one day,"
I begged him, "untie this knot for me
that has tangled up my thinking here.

It seems, if I hear you rightly, that you see
beforehand what time is bringing on,
but have some other way with the present."

"We see, like men with failing sight,
the things," he said, "that are far off from us;
that much the highest Ruler still grants us.

When they draw near, or arrive, our minds
are blank; and unless someone brings us word,
we know nothing of your human state.

So you can understand that all our knowledge
will be dead from the very moment
the door of the future is shut."

Then, stung as if at fault, I said:
"Now will you tell that fallen one
that his son is still joined to the living;

and if I stood mute just now before answering,
tell him I did it because I was already
thinking of the error you have solved for me."

And now my master was calling me back;
so I asked the spirit, more urgently,
to tell me who else lay there with him.

He said: "More than a thousand lie here with me.
Inside is the second Frederick,
and the Cardinal; of the rest I say nothing."

Then he hid himself; and I turned my steps
back to the ancient poet, turning over
that saying that had sounded like a threat.

He moved on, and as we went, he said:
"Why are you so lost in yourself?"
And I answered the question he had asked.

"Keep in your mind what you have heard
spoken against you," the sage commanded me.
"And now listen here," and he raised his finger:

"When you stand before the sweet light
of her whose lovely eyes see everything,
from her you'll learn the journey of your life."

Then he turned his feet to the left.
We left the wall and went toward the center,
along a path that cuts down into a valley

whose stench, even up there, turned the stomach.
