# The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto V

"If I blaze upon you in the heat of love  
beyond the measure ever seen on earth,  
so that I overpower the light of your eyes,

do not be amazed; for this comes  
from perfect sight, which, as it takes the good in,  
so moves its steps toward the good it has grasped.

I see well how already the eternal light  
is shining into your mind — the light  
which, once seen, kindles love and nothing else;

and if some other thing seduces your love,  
it is only a trace of that same light,  
poorly understood, glinting through it.

You wish to know whether some other service  
can be paid for a broken vow — enough  
to keep the soul safe from any further claim."

So Beatrice began this canto; and,  
like one who does not break the thread of speech,  
she carried her holy argument on:

"The greatest gift that God, in His largeness,  
made in creating — the gift most like  
His own goodness, and the one He prizes most —

is the freedom of the will, with which  
every creature that has intelligence,  
all of them and only them, was and is endowed.

Now you will see, if you reason it through,  
the high worth of a vow — if it is made  
so that, when you consent, God consents too;

for in sealing the compact between God and man,  
a sacrifice is made of this treasure,  
the one I speak of, and made by its own act.

What, then, can be paid back in its place?  
If you think to make good use of what you offered,  
you would do a good deed with stolen goods.

Now you are sure of the larger point.  
But because Holy Church grants dispensations here,  
which seem against the truth I have just shown you,

you still must sit a while longer at the table,  
because the heavy food you have taken in  
needs more help yet before you can digest it.

Open your mind to what I now reveal,  
and fix it there inside; for hearing a thing  
and not holding it is not the same as knowing.

Two things go into the substance of this sacrifice:  
the one is the matter of which it is made,  
the other is the compact, the agreement itself.

This last can never be cancelled out  
unless it is carried through; and on this point  
I spoke with precision a little while ago.

And so it was laid on the Hebrews to keep offering,  
though now and then the thing they offered  
might be exchanged, as you ought to know.

The other part — the one you know as matter —  
may well be such that you do no wrong  
if you trade it for some other matter.

But let no one shift the burden on his shoulder  
at his own say-so, without the turning  
of both the white key and the gold.

And count every exchange as foolish  
unless the thing laid down is held inside  
the thing put in its place, as four is in six.

So whatever weighs so much in worth  
that it tips down every scale  
cannot be made good by any other payment.

Let no one take a vow as a joke.  
Be faithful, but not blind in keeping it,  
the way Jephthah was in his first offering —

who would have done better to say, 'I did wrong,'  
than to do worse by keeping his word; and as foolish  
you will find that great leader of the Greeks,

for whom Iphigenia wept her lovely face,  
and made both the wise and the simple weep with her  
when they heard of worship carried out like that.

Christians, be slower and graver in how you move.  
Do not be like a feather in every wind,  
and do not think any water can wash you clean.

You have the New Testament and the Old,  
and the shepherd of the Church to guide you:  
let that be enough for your salvation.

If evil appetite cries anything else to you,  
be men, and not like silly sheep,  
so the Jew among you cannot mock you.

Do not be like the lamb that leaves  
its mother's milk and, frisking and foolish,  
picks fights with its own self for sport."

So Beatrice spoke to me, just as I write it;  
then, all longing, she turned again  
toward the part where the world is most alive.

Her falling silent and her changed face  
laid silence on my own eager mind,  
which already had new questions waiting;

and like an arrow that strikes the mark  
before the bowstring has gone quiet,  
so we sped up into the second realm.

There I saw my Lady so full of joy,  
as she passed into the brightness of that heaven,  
that the planet itself grew more luminous.

And if the very star changed and smiled,  
what became of me, who by my nature  
shift and change in every way?

As in a fish-pond, clear and still,  
the fish draw toward whatever falls in from outside,  
taking it for something they can feed on,

so I saw more than a thousand splendors  
drawing toward us, and in each one was heard:  
"Here is one who will increase our loves."

And as each came toward us,  
the shade was seen brimming with happiness,  
by the clear radiance that streamed out of it.

Think, Reader: if what is only beginning here  
went no further, how sharp and aching  
your own need to know more would be —

and from yourself you will see how I  
longed to hear of these souls' conditions  
the moment they came clear before my eyes.

"Well-born soul, to whom Grace grants  
the sight of the thrones of the eternal triumph  
before you have laid your warfare down —

we are kindled by the light that spreads  
through all of heaven; so if you wish  
to know us, take your fill at your own pleasure."

So one among those holy spirits spoke  
to me, and Beatrice said: "Speak, speak  
with confidence, and trust them as you would gods."

"I see well how you nest yourself  
within your own light, and draw it from your eyes,  
for they sparkle when you smile;

but I do not know who you are, nor why,  
august spirit, you hold your station in this sphere  
that veils itself from mortals in another's rays."

This I said, turned toward the light  
that had first spoken to me; at which it grew  
far more shining than it was before.

Just as the sun, which hides itself  
in too much light once the heat has burned away  
the tempering screen of the thick vapors,

so, in a greater rapture, the holy figure  
hid itself from me within its own radiance;  
and so, wrapped close, close in its light, it answered me

in the manner the following canto sings.
