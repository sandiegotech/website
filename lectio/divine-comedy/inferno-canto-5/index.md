# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto V

So I went down from the first circle  
into the second, which rings a smaller space  
but so much greater pain that it goads them to howling.

There stands Minos, horrible, and snarls.  
At the entrance he weighs the sins of each;  
he judges, and sends them down by the coiling of his tail.

I mean that when a soul born to evil  
comes before him, it confesses everything,  
and that connoisseur of sins

sees what place in Hell is fit for it,  
and coils his tail around himself as many times  
as the number of circles it must be flung down.

Always a crowd of them stands before him.  
They come, each in turn, to be judged;  
they speak, they hear, and then they are hurled below.

"You there, who come to this house of grief,"  
said Minos when he saw me,  
breaking off from the work of his great office,

"watch how you enter, and whom you trust.  
Don't let the wide gate fool you."  
And my guide to him: "Why do you too cry out?

Don't block his journey — it is fated.  
This is willed where what is willed is done,  
so ask no more."

And now the notes of grief begin  
to reach my ears; now I have come  
where a great wailing beats against me.

I came into a place where all light is silent,  
that bellows like the sea in a storm  
when it is battered by warring winds.

The hurricane of Hell, which never rests,  
drives the spirits on before its force,  
and wheeling and battering them, gives them no peace.

When they come up against the ruined edge,  
there are the screams, the sobbing, the laments;  
there they curse the power of God.

I understood that to a torment like this  
the sinners of the flesh were condemned,  
who let their reason give way to their hunger.

And as the wings of starlings carry them  
in the cold season, in a wide, packed flock,  
so that blast carries the wicked spirits:

this way, that way, down and up, it drives them;  
no hope can ever comfort them —  
not of rest, not even of lighter pain.

And as the cranes go chanting their cries,  
drawing themselves out in a long line through the air,  
so I saw them come, wailing,

shades borne along on that same wind.  
So I said: "Master, who are these people  
the black air punishes so hard?"

"The first of those you want to know about,"  
he said to me then,  
"was empress over many tongues.

She was so broken by the vice of lust  
that she made desire lawful in her code,  
to wipe out the blame she had fallen into.

She is Semiramis, of whom we read  
that she succeeded Ninus and was his wife;  
she held the land the Sultan rules today.

The next is the one who killed herself for love  
and broke faith with the ashes of Sychaeus.  
After her, Cleopatra, who lived for pleasure."

I saw Helen, for whom so many cruel  
years wheeled round, and I saw great Achilles,  
who fought with Love at the very end.

I saw Paris, Tristan — more than a thousand  
shades he named and pointed out with his finger,  
all the ones Love had cut away from our life.

After I had listened to my teacher  
name the ladies and the knights of long ago,  
pity overcame me, and I was almost lost.

I began: "Poet, I would gladly  
speak to those two who go together  
and seem so light upon the wind."

And he to me: "Watch for them when they come  
closer to us; then call to them  
by the love that drives them, and they will come."

As soon as the wind bent them toward us,  
I raised my voice: "O weary souls,  
come speak with us, if no one forbids it."

As doves, called onward by desire,  
with wings held open and still glide  
to the sweet nest, carried by their own will,

so these two left the flock where Dido is  
and came to us through the malignant air,  
so strong was the love in my call.

"O living creature, gracious and kind,  
who come visiting through this dark air  
to us who stained the world with blood,

if the King of all things were our friend  
we would pray to him to give you peace,  
since you take pity on our twisted grief.

Whatever you would like to hear and say,  
that we will hear, and we will say to you,  
while the wind, as now, is still.

The city where I was born sits  
on the shore where the Po comes down  
to rest in peace with all the streams that follow it.

Love, which is quick to seize the gentle heart,  
seized this man for the beautiful body  
taken from me — and the way of it still wounds me.

Love, which absolves no loved one from loving,  
seized me so strongly with delight in him  
that, as you see, it has not left me yet.

Love led the two of us to a single death.  
Caina waits for the one who put out our lives."  
These were the words they carried over to us.

When I had heard those wounded souls,  
I bowed my face and held it down so long  
that at last the poet said: "What are you thinking?"

When I answered, I began: "Oh —  
how many sweet thoughts, how much longing  
brought these two to the road of grief."

Then I turned back to them and spoke,  
and began: "Francesca, what you suffer  
makes me weep for sorrow and pity.

But tell me: in the time of the sweet sighs,  
by what signs, and how, did Love let you  
know the desires you only half-guessed?"

And she to me: "There is no greater pain  
than to remember the happy time  
in misery — and that your teacher knows.

But if you long so much to know  
the first root of our love,  
then I will be like one who weeps as she speaks.

One day, for pleasure, we were reading  
of Lancelot, how love had taken hold of him.  
We were alone, and suspected nothing.

Again and again that reading drew our eyes  
together and drained the color from our faces;  
but it was one moment alone that overcame us.

When we read how the longed-for smile  
was kissed by such a lover,  
this man, who never will be parted from me,

kissed me on the mouth, trembling all over.  
A Galeotto was that book, and the one who wrote it.  
That day we read in it no further."

While the one spirit said this,  
the other wept so hard that out of pity  
I fainted, as if I were dying,

and I fell as a dead body falls.
