# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XX

A will fights badly against a better will;  
so, against my own wish, to do as he wished,  
I drew the still-thirsty sponge back from the water.

I moved on, and my Leader moved on too,  
through the open spaces, hugging the rock face,  
the way you walk a wall close to its battlements;

for the people who pour out, drop by drop,  
through their eyes the sickness that grips the whole world,  
lie too near the edge on the outer side.

A curse on you, you ancient she-wolf,  
who seize more prey than any other beast,  
driven by a hunger that has no bottom!

O heaven, in whose turning men seem to trust  
that the conditions down here can be changed —  
when will he come, the one who drives her off?

We went on with slow and careful steps,  
and I was listening hard to the shades, who wept  
and made their grief heard, piteous to hear;

and by chance I heard, ahead of us, a voice  
cry out "Sweet Mary!" through the weeping,  
the way a woman cries in giving birth;

and it went on: "How poor you were is plain  
from that mean shelter, that stable inn,  
where you laid down your holy burden."

After that I heard: "O good Fabricius,  
you chose virtue with poverty over holding  
great wealth at the price of a crooked life."

These words gave me such pleasure that I drew  
farther on, wanting to learn the truth  
about the spirit they seemed to come from.

He was still speaking, now of the gift  
Nicholas made to the three young maidens,  
to bring their youth to an honorable life.

"O soul who speak so well of so much good,  
tell me who you were," I said, "and why  
you alone renew these well-earned praises.

Your word will not go unrepaid, if I  
return to finish the short road  
of that life now flying to its end."

And he: "I'll tell you — not for any comfort  
I expect from the world below, but because  
so much grace shines in you before you die.

I was the root of that evil plant  
that casts its shadow over all Christendom,  
so that good fruit is rarely picked from it.

But if Douai and Ghent, Lille and Bruges  
had the power, vengeance would soon be taken;  
and this I beg of Him who judges all.

Over there I was called Hugh Capet;  
from me were born the Philips and the Louis  
by whom France is governed in these latter days.

I was the son of a Paris butcher.  
When the old line of kings had all died out,  
except for one turned monk in coarse gray cloth,

I found the reins of the realm's government  
gripped tight in my own hands, and such fresh power  
from new lands won, and so many friends,

that to the widowed crown my own son's head  
was raised up — and from him  
began the line of these anointed bones.

As long as the great dowry of Provence  
had not yet stripped my blood of its shame,  
it was worth little, but at least did no harm.

There it began, with force and with lies,  
its plunder; and then, by way of "amends,"  
it seized Ponthieu, Normandy, Gascony.

Charles came to Italy and, by way of amends,  
made a victim of Conradin; and then, by way  
of amends, thrust Thomas back to heaven.

I see a time, not far off now,  
that draws another Charles out of France  
to make both him and his own kind better known.

He goes unarmed, carrying only the lance  
Judas jousted with — and he drives it home  
so hard he bursts the belly of Florence.

From it he'll win no land, but sin and shame,  
the more crushing to himself the lighter  
he holds the wreckage he leaves behind.

The other, who once stepped off his ship a prisoner,  
I see selling his own daughter, haggling  
the way pirates do over slave women.

O Avarice, what more can you do to us,  
now that you've drawn my blood so close to you  
that it no longer cares for its own flesh?

So the evil to come and the evil already done  
may seem less, I see the fleur-de-lis enter Anagni,  
and Christ made captive in his own Vicar.

I see him mocked a second time; I see  
the vinegar and the gall brought back again,  
and I see him killed between living thieves.

I see the new Pilate, so pitiless  
that even this won't fill him: with no decree  
he runs his greedy sails into the Temple.

O my Lord, when will I be made glad  
to see the vengeance that, still hidden,  
makes your anger sweet within your secret will?

What I was saying of that one bride  
of the Holy Spirit, the words that made you  
turn toward me for some explanation —

that is the answer set to all our prayers  
as long as the day lasts; but when night falls  
we take up the opposite sound instead.

Then we tell again of Pygmalion,  
whom his greedy lust for gold made  
a traitor, a thief, and a murderer of kin;

and of the misery of grasping Midas,  
which followed his greedy demand —  
a thing one can only ever laugh at.

Then each of us recalls the foolish Achan,  
and how he stole the spoils, so that the wrath  
of Joshua seems to bite him even here.

We accuse Sapphira and her husband next;  
we praise the hooves that trampled Heliodorus;  
and the whole mountain rings in infamy

with Polymnestor, who killed Polydorus.  
Last of all the cry goes up: "Crassus,  
tell us — you know — what does gold taste like?"

Sometimes one speaks loud and another low,  
as the feeling that spurs us to speak  
drives us now to a quicker, now a slower pace.

So in the good we speak of here by day,  
I was not alone just now; but nearby  
no other voice was lifted up so high."

We had already left him behind us,  
and were working to make our way along the road  
as far as our strength would let us go,

when I felt the mountain shake, like something  
about to fall — and a chill took hold of me,  
the kind that grips a man going to his death.

Surely Delos did not shudder so hard  
before Latona made her nest in it  
to give birth to the two eyes of heaven.

Then on every side a cry went up  
so loud that my Master drew close to me,  
saying, "Don't be afraid, while I am your guide."

"Gloria in excelsis Deo" — that was what  
they were all saying, as far as I could tell  
from those near enough for the cry to be heard.

We stood still, motionless and in suspense,  
like the shepherds who first heard that song,  
until the trembling stopped and the song was done.

Then we took up again our holy path,  
watching the shades lying along the ground,  
already returned to their accustomed weeping.

No ignorance ever, with so fierce a struggle,  
made me ache to know — if my memory  
does not go wrong about it here —

as I seemed to feel then, turning it over;  
and for haste I did not dare to ask,  
nor could I make anything out for myself:

so I went on, fearful and deep in thought.
