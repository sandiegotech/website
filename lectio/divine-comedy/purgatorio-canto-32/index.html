The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XXXII

Purgatorio: Canto XXXII66 of 100

My eyes were fixed so hard, so wholly bent
on slaking a thirst ten years deep,
that every other sense in me was dead.

On either side they had a wall of indifference
built around them — so the holy smile
drew them to itself with the same old net —

when my face was wrenched, against my will,
to the left, by those three goddesses,
because I heard them say, "Too fixed a stare!"

And the way the eyes go, just after
the sun has struck them full — that blindness
left me without sight for a little while.

But once my vision shaped itself again
to the lesser thing — I say "lesser" only
beside the great light I was forced to leave —

I saw the glorious army had wheeled
to the right and was turning back, with the sun
and the seven flames carried before their faces.

The way a troop will swing in behind its shields
to save itself, and pivot with the banner
before the whole line can come around,

so that soldiery of the kingdom of Heaven,
all of it that marched in the van, had passed us
before the chariot had swung its pole.

Then the women turned back to the wheels,
and the Griffin moved his blessed load
so smoothly that not one feather of him stirred.

The lovely lady who had drawn me through the ford,
and Statius, and I, followed the wheel
that traced its circle on the shorter arc.

So we walked the high forest, empty now
by the fault of her who trusted the serpent,
and an angel's singing kept our steps in time.

An arrow loosed from the string might cover,
in three flights, about the distance we had moved
when Beatrice stepped down to the ground.

I heard them all murmuring, "Adam," together;
then they ringed a tree stripped bare,
not a leaf, not a flower on any branch.

Its crown, that spreads the wider the higher
it climbs, would have been marveled at for height
by the people of India in their forests.

"Blessed are you, Griffin, that your beak
tears nothing from this wood, sweet to the taste —
for by it the belly was twisted to evil."

So the others cried, ringed around the sturdy tree;
and the twin-natured creature answered:
"This is how the seed of all the just is kept."

And turning to the pole he had been dragging,
he hauled it close to the widowed trunk
and left it bound there, wood to its own wood.

The way our trees, when the great light comes down
mingled with the light that shines behind
the heavenly Carp, swell at the buds,

and then renew themselves, each in its color,
before the sun yokes his horses
under another star —

so, opening a color less than rose
and more than violet, the tree made itself new
that had stood with its boughs so bare before.

I never heard it, and down here it isn't sung —
the hymn that people sang just then —
nor could I bear the melody all the way through.

If I could draw how those pitiless eyes,
hearing of Syrinx, fell asleep —
the eyes whose long watch had cost so dear —

then, like a painter working from a model,
I'd sketch how I drifted off; but let it be
someone who can paint sleep falling well.

So I skip to the moment I woke,
and say: a brightness tore the veil of sleep
off me, and a voice — "Rise. What are you doing?"

The way Peter and John and James were brought
to see the blossom of that apple tree
whose fruit makes the angels hungry

and keeps the endless wedding feast in Heaven,
and were overcome, and came to themselves
at the word that has broken deeper sleeps,

and saw their company thinned — gone
both Moses and Elijah — and saw
their Master's robe changed before them:

so I came back, and saw that merciful one
standing over me — she who before
had been the guide of my steps along the river.

Lost in doubt, I said, "Where is Beatrice?"
And she: "See her, seated under the new leaves,
there, on the root of the tree itself.

See the company that rings her round.
The rest are climbing after the Griffin,
with a song sweeter and more deep."

Whether she said more, I don't know —
because by then she was already in my eyes,
the one who had shut out all else from me.

Alone she sat on the bare ground,
as if left there to guard the chariot
I had watched the twiform creature bind.

Around her the seven nymphs had made
a cloister of themselves, holding those lights
that are safe from the north wind and the south.

"Here you will be a forester a short while;
then, with me, forever, a citizen
of that Rome where Christ himself is Roman.

So, for the good of the world that lives so badly,
keep your eyes on the chariot now, and what you see —
once you are back on earth — see that you write it."

So Beatrice; and I, who was wholly devoted,
down to her feet, to whatever she commanded,
bent my mind and eyes where she willed.

Never did fire fall with so swift a motion
from a thick cloud — when the rain is coming
out of the region farthest off —

as I saw the bird of Jove come plunging down
through the tree, ripping away the bark,
and the flowers too, and the new leaves;

and it struck the chariot with all its force,
so that it lurched like a ship in a storm,
beaten by the waves, now starboard, now port.

Then I saw a fox spring into the body
of that triumphal car — a fox that looked
starved of any decent food.

But, charging her with her foul offenses,
my Lady put her to flight as fast
as those fleshless bones could carry her.

Then, back the way it first had come,
I saw the Eagle drop down into the hull
of the chariot, and leave it feathered with its plumes;

and the way a voice breaks from a grieving heart,
such a voice came out of Heaven, and said:
"My little ship — how badly you are loaded!"

Then it seemed to me the ground split open
between the two wheels, and out of it rose a dragon
that drove its tail up through the chariot;

and the way a wasp draws back its sting,
pulling its wicked tail in toward itself,
it tore out part of the floor and went off gloating.

What was left — the way live, rank ground
is overrun with weeds — covered itself again
with the feathers, offered, it may be,

with a clean and kindly intent; and with them
the pole and both the wheels were covered over
in less time than a sigh holds the lips apart.

Transformed like this, the holy structure
thrust out heads from its parts — three on the pole
and one at each of the four corners.

The first three were horned like oxen,
but the four had a single horn on the brow:
no one ever saw a monster like it.

Secure as a fortress on a high peak,
there appeared to me, seated on the thing,
a brazen harlot, her eyes darting everywhere;

and, as if to keep her from being taken,
I saw a giant standing at her side,
and now and then the two of them kissed.

But because she turned her greedy, roving eye
on me, that savage lover
scourged her from head to foot;

then, full of suspicion and raw with rage,
he loosed the monster and dragged it off
through the forest, so far that the trees alone

made a shield between me and the harlot and the strange new beast.
