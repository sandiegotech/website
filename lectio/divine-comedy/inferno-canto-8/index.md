# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto VIII

I should say, going on, that long before  
we had reached the foot of the high tower,  
our eyes had gone up to its top,

because of two small flames we saw set there,  
and another, far off, signaling back,  
so far that the eye could barely catch it.

And I turned to that sea of all understanding  
and said: "What does this one say? And what reply  
does that other fire give? Who lit them?"

And he to me: "Out across the murky waves  
you can already make out what is coming,  
if the marsh-fog doesn't hide it from you."

No bowstring ever drove an arrow from itself  
that cut through the air so fast  
as the little boat I saw in that moment,

coming toward us over the water,  
under the steering of a single pilot,  
who was shouting, "Now you're caught, vicious soul!"

"Phlegyas, Phlegyas, you cry out for nothing  
this time," said my lord. "You'll have us  
only long enough to cross the swamp."

Like a man who hears of some great trick  
that has been played on him, and then seethes,  
so Phlegyas seethed in his gathered rage.

My guide stepped down into the boat,  
and then he had me get in after him,  
and only when I entered did it sit low in the water.

As soon as my guide and I were aboard,  
the ancient prow moves off, cutting  
more of the water than it does for others.

While we were running across the dead channel,  
one rose up in front of me, caked in mud,  
and said, "Who are you, coming before your hour?"

And I to him: "If I come, I do not stay.  
But who are you, gone so foul?"  
He answered: "You see I am one who weeps."

And I to him: "With your weeping and your wailing,  
you cursed spirit, stay right where you are,  
for I know you, filthy as you are."

Then he reached both his hands toward the boat,  
but my wary master shoved him back,  
saying, "Get away there, with the other dogs!"

Then he put his arms around my neck,  
kissed my face, and said: "Disdainful soul,  
blessed is the woman who carried you.

In the world that one was an arrogant man.  
No good deed adorns his memory,  
so here his shade is wild with fury.

How many up there are taken for great kings  
who will lie here like pigs in the muck,  
leaving nothing behind but contempt!"

And I: "Master, I would dearly love  
to see him ducked down into this sludge  
before we make it out of the lake."

And he to me: "Before the far shore  
comes into view, you'll be satisfied.  
It's only right you should enjoy that wish."

A little after, I watched the people of the mud  
tear into him with such fury  
that to this day I praise and thank God for it.

They were all screaming, "Get Filippo Argenti!"  
and that raging Florentine spirit  
turned his teeth against his own flesh.

We left him there — I'll tell no more of him.  
But a sound of grief struck against my ears,  
and I strained my eyes wide ahead.

And the good master said: "Now, my son,  
the city is coming close whose name is Dis,  
with its grim citizens, its great throng."

And I: "Master, I can already see  
its mosques there, plainly, down in the valley,  
glowing red as if they'd come out of fire."

And he to me: "The eternal fire  
that burns inside them turns them red,  
as you can see, here in lower Hell."

Then we came inside the deep moats  
that wall that desolate city all around.  
The ramparts looked to me to be of iron.

Not without making a wide circuit first,  
we came to a place where the pilot  
bellowed at us, "Out — here is the entrance."

Over the gates I saw more than a thousand  
rained down from Heaven, who in their fury  
were saying, "Who is this, with no death in him,

who walks through the kingdom of the dead?"  
And my knowing master made a sign  
that he wished to speak with them in private.

Then they held back a little of their great scorn  
and said: "You come alone, and let him go away —  
the one who's pushed so boldly into this realm.

Let him go back alone on his crazed road.  
Let him try, if he can. You will stay here,  
you who have led him through such dark country."

Imagine, reader, how I sank  
at the sound of those accursed words,  
for I believed I would never come back.

"O my dear guide, who more than seven times  
have handed me back my safety, and drawn me  
out of the dangers standing in my path,

don't leave me wrecked like this," I said.  
"And if going on is forbidden us,  
let's turn back together, and quickly."

And that lord, who had brought me there,  
said to me: "Don't be afraid. No one  
can take our crossing — it is granted by One above.

But wait for me here, and feed your worn spirit,  
comfort it with a better hope,  
for I will not abandon you in this low world."

So he goes off and leaves me there,  
my sweet father, and I am left in doubt,  
with Yes and No at war inside my head.

I couldn't hear what he proposed to them,  
but he had not stood with them long  
before each one scrambled back inside, racing.

They slammed the gates, those enemies of ours,  
in my lord's face — and he was left outside,  
and turned to me with slow, dragging steps.

His eyes were on the ground, his brow stripped  
of all its boldness, and he said with sighs:  
"Who has shut the houses of grief against me?"

And to me: "Don't you be afraid  
because I'm angry — I will win this trial,  
whatever they scheme up for defense inside.

This arrogance of theirs is nothing new.  
They tried it once at a less hidden gate,  
which stands to this day without a lock.

Over it you saw the inscription of death.  
And already this side of it, coming down the slope,  
crossing the circles with no escort,

is one by whose power the city will be opened."
