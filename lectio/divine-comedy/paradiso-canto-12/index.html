The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto XII

Paradiso: Canto XII79 of 100

The moment that blessed flame had spoken
its final word and let it go,
the holy millstone began to turn —

and before it had wheeled fully around,
another ring closed in and circled it,
motion matched to motion, song to song:

song that outstrips our Muses and our Sirens
in those sweet trumpets as far
as first light outshines what only reflects it.

And as two rainbows, parallel and matched
in color, arch across a thin cloud
when Juno gives her handmaid the command —

the outer one born of the inner, the way
that wandering nymph's voice answered, she
whom love burned off as the sun burns mist —

two bows that let the people here below
read the pledge God made with Noah:
that no flood will ever drown the world again —

just so the two garlands of those eternal roses
wheeled around us, twin wreaths,
the outer answering the inner perfectly.

And once the dancing and the rest of the great joy —
the singing, and the flaring out
of light to light, tender and glad —

all stopped together in a single instant,
the way two eyes, when the will moves them,
must close and open in one accord,

out of the heart of one of the new lights
a voice came, and it turned me toward it
as the needle turns to the pole star.

It began: "The love that makes me beautiful
draws me to speak now of the other leader,
the one on whose account mine was so praised.

Where you name one, it's right to bring the other,
so that, as they fought side by side,
their glory may shine side by side as well.

The army of Christ, that had cost so much
to arm again, was moving behind the standard
slow, and doubtful, and few in number,

when the Emperor who reigns forever
moved to save His host in its peril —
by grace alone, and not because it deserved it —

and, as was said, brought rescue to His Bride
with two champions, whose deeds and whose words
drew the scattered people back together.

In that region where the sweet west wind
rises to open the new leaves with which
Europe is seen to clothe herself again,

not far from the beating of the waves
behind which, at the end of his long course,
the sun sometimes hides himself from everyone,

sits fortunate Calaruega, sheltered
under the great shield where the lion
is subject below and sovereign above.

There was born the devoted lover
of the Christian Faith, the holy athlete,
kind to his own and merciless to the enemy.

And the instant his mind was made, it brimmed
with such living power that, while she carried him,
it turned his mother into a prophet.

Once the marriage was complete
at the holy font between him and the Faith,
where each endowed the other with rescue,

the woman who had given assent for him
saw in a dream the wondrous fruit
that would come from him and from his heirs;

and so that his name would say what he was,
a spirit went forth from this place to name him
with the possessive of the One he wholly belonged to.

Dominic he was called; and I speak of him
as of the farmhand Christ chose
to help Him work His garden.

He looked, in truth, like Christ's own envoy and servant,
for the first love that showed in him
was the first counsel Christ ever gave.

Many a time his nurse found him
awake and silent on the ground,
as if to say, 'This is why I came.'

O his father, truly Felix!
O his mother, truly Joanna,
if the names mean what they're said to mean!

Not for the world that men now toil for,
following the man of Ostia and Taddeo,
but out of hunger for the true manna,

he became, in a short time, so great a teacher
that he set out to walk the vineyard,
which withers fast if its keeper fails it.

And from the Holy See — once kinder
to the honest poor (no fault of its own,
but of the one who sits there and goes rotten) —

he asked no leave to pay out two or three
for six, no first wealthy benefice that fell vacant,
non decimas, quae sunt pauperum Dei;

he asked only for leave to fight
the straying world, on behalf of the seed
from which these twenty-four plants ring you round.

Then, with doctrine and will joined as one,
he moved out with the office of an apostle,
like a torrent driven from a high spring;

and into the thickets of heresy his force
struck with the fiercest fury
wherever the resistance ran hardest.

From him, in time, the streams branched off
that keep the Catholic garden watered,
so its plantings stand more alive.

If this was one wheel of the chariot
by which the Holy Church defended herself
and won her civil war in open field,

then the worth of the other should be plain to you —
the one Thomas was so generous about
before I came among you.

But the track cut by the topmost part
of that wheel's rim is abandoned now:
where the wine-crust was, there is mold.

His own family, that once walked straight
in his footprints, has turned itself around
and now plants the toe where the heel should fall.

Soon they'll see what the harvest of this
bad farming yields, when the weeds protest
that the barn has been shut against them.

And yet I'll grant: search our book
leaf by leaf, and you'd still turn up a page
that reads, 'I am what I always was.'

But it won't come from Casale, nor Acquasparta —
where the ones who come to our written Rule
either dodge it or pull it too tight.

I am the soul of Bonaventure of Bagnoregio,
who, in the great offices I held,
always put worldly concerns last.

Here are Illuminato and Augustine,
among the first barefoot poor
who put on the cord and became God's friends.

Hugh of Saint Victor is here with them,
and Peter Comestor, and Peter of Spain,
who shines below in his twelve books;

Nathan the prophet; Chrysostom the bishop;
Anselm; and Donatus, who stooped
to lay his hand to the first of the arts;

Rabanus is here, and beside me shines
the Calabrian abbot Joachim,
gifted with the spirit of prophecy.

What moved me to honor so great a champion
was the burning courtesy and the measured words
of Friar Thomas —

and they have moved this whole company with me."
