The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto VII

Inferno: Canto VII7 of 100

"Pape Satàn, pape Satàn aleppe!"
Plutus began, in his strangled, clucking voice;
and that gentle sage, who understood all things,

said, to steady me: "Don't let your fear
harm you. Whatever power he may have,
he will not keep us from going down this rock."

Then he turned to that swollen, bloated face
and said: "Be quiet, you cursed wolf.
Feed on yourself, eat away at your own rage.

This descent into the dark is not without cause.
It is willed on high, there where Michael
took his vengeance on the proud rebellion."

The way sails, swollen with the wind,
collapse in a tangle when the mast snaps,
so the cruel beast fell to the ground.

And so we went down into the fourth ditch,
pushing on farther along the dismal shore
that bags up all the evil of the universe.

Justice of God! Who packs together so many
strange new toils and torments as I saw?
And why does our own guilt wreck us like this?

The way the wave there over Charybdis
breaks itself against the wave it runs into,
so here the people have to dance their round.

Here I saw more of them than anywhere else,
on one side and the other, with huge howls,
rolling great weights forward with their chests.

They crashed into each other, and right there
each one wheeled around, rolling back again,
shouting, "Why hoard?" and "Why throw it away?"

So they came back along the gloomy ring,
on either side, to the opposite point,
shouting again their shameful refrain;

then each, when he reached his end of it,
turned through his half-circle to the next clash.
And I, with my heart all but pierced through,

said: "Master, explain it to me now —
who are these people? And were they all clerics,
these shaven crowns here on our left?"

And he to me: "Every one of them squinted
so badly in the mind, in the first life,
that they never spent with any measure.

Their voices bark it out clearly enough
when they come to the two points of the circle
where the opposite faults split them apart.

These were clerics, with no hairy covering
on the head — and popes, and cardinals,
in whom greed works its worst excess."

And I: "Master, among ones like these
I really ought to recognize a few
who were fouled with these same diseases."

And he to me: "You're chasing an empty thought.
The undiscerning life that made them filthy
now makes them too dim for anyone to know.

Forever they will come to these two collisions.
These will rise from the grave with fists clenched shut,
and these with the hair shorn off their heads.

Giving wrong and keeping wrong has cost them
the lovely world and set them in this brawl.
What that brawl is, I won't dress up in words.

Now you can see, my son, the brief charade
of the goods that are handed over to Fortune,
the ones the human race keeps fighting for.

All the gold that is under the moon,
or ever was, could not buy a moment's rest
for a single one of these worn-out souls."

"Master," I said to him, "tell me this too:
this Fortune you bring up — what is she,
who holds the world's goods so tight in her claws?"

And he to me: "O foolish creatures,
how deep the ignorance is that wounds you!
Now I want you to take in my judgment of her.

He whose knowledge surpasses everything
made the heavens and gave them guides,
so that every part shines on every part,

spreading the light out in equal measure.
In the same way, for the splendors of the world,
he set a general minister and guide

to shift the empty treasures, time and again,
from people to people, from one blood to another,
beyond all the defenses of human wits.

So one nation triumphs and another
withers, all by the verdict she hands down,
hidden the way a snake is hidden in grass.

Your knowledge has no defense against her.
She foresees, she judges, she carries out
her reign, the way the other gods rule theirs.

Her shiftings never come to any truce.
Necessity keeps driving her at speed,
so fast does one man's turn keep coming round.

And this is the one so often nailed to the cross
by the very people who ought to praise her,
who blame her wrongly and give her a bad name.

But she is in bliss, and does not hear them.
With the other first creatures, glad, she turns
her sphere, and rejoices in her own happiness.

Now let's go down to a greater sorrow.
Already every star is sinking that was rising
when I set out, and we are not allowed to linger."

We crossed the circle to the other bank,
beside a spring that boils and pours itself
down a channel that drains away from it.

The water was far darker than deep purple,
and we, keeping company with the murky waves,
made our way down by a rough, broken path.

When it has gone down to the foot
of the gray malignant slopes, this dreary stream
makes a marsh, and the marsh is named the Styx.

And I, who stood there straining to see,
made out people in that bog, smeared with mud,
all of them naked, all with furious looks.

They struck at each other, not with hands alone,
but with their heads, their chests, their feet,
and tore each other to pieces with their teeth.

The good master said: "Son, now you see
the souls of those whom anger overcame.
And I want you to know this for certain too:

down under the water there are people sighing,
and they make it bubble up at the surface,
as your eye tells you wherever it turns.

Stuck in the slime they say, 'We were sullen
in the sweet air that the sun makes glad,
carrying a lazy smoke inside ourselves.

Now we are sullen here in the black mire.'
This hymn they gurgle down inside their throats,
because they cannot get the words out whole."

So we went on around the filthy swamp,
in a wide arc between the dry bank and the bog,
our eyes on those who were gulping down the mud —

and we came at last to the foot of a tower.
