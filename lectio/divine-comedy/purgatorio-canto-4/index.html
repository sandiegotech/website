The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto IV

Purgatorio: Canto IV38 of 100

Whenever some pleasure, or else some pain,
takes hold of one power in us, the soul
gathers itself entirely into that one,

and seems to attend to nothing else —
which answers the error that supposes
soul kindled above soul within us.

And so, when something heard or seen
holds the soul bent close upon it,
time slides by and we never feel it go,

because the power that listens is one thing,
and the power that holds the whole soul is another:
the second is bound, while the first runs free.

Of this I had plain proof in myself,
listening to that spirit and gazing at him —
for the sun had climbed a full fifty degrees

and I had never noticed, when we came
to where those souls cried out to us
with one voice: "Here is what you're asking for."

A wider gap is often hedged shut
by the farmer with one little forkful of thorns,
when the grape is darkening on the vine,

than the cleft we climbed through then —
only my Leader, and I close behind him,
once that company had left us alone.

A man can walk up San Leo, or down into Noli,
or scale the top of Bismantova
on his feet — but here he has to fly:

fly, I mean, on the quick wings and feathers
of great longing, drawn up after the one
who gave me hope and made a light for me.

We climbed on through the split in the rock,
the walls of it pressing in on either side,
the ground beneath us demanding hands and feet.

When we had come up onto the upper lip
of the high bank, out on the open slope,
"My Master," I said, "which way do we go?"

And he to me: "Don't lose a single step.
Keep winning your way up the mountain behind me
until some guide who knows it shows himself."

The summit stood so high it beat my sight,
and the slope was steeper, far, than the line
drawn from the quadrant's midpoint to its center.

I was worn out when I began to say:
"O my sweet Father, turn and look —
I'll be left here alone unless you stop!"

"My son," he said, "drag yourself up to there" —
and he pointed me to a ledge a little higher
that runs around that side of the whole hill.

His words so spurred me on that I forced
every nerve, scrambling up behind him
until the rim of it was under my feet.

There the two of us sat down together,
facing east, back toward the way we'd come —
for it lifts every heart to look back.

First I sent my eyes down to the low shores,
then lifted them to the sun, and was amazed
that it struck us here from the left.

The Poet saw well enough that I stood
bewildered by the chariot of the light,
entering between us and the north wind.

So he said to me: "If Castor and Pollux
were keeping company with that mirror
that carries its light both up and down,

you'd see the zodiac, in its red glowing wheel,
turning still closer in toward the Bears —
unless it strayed clean off its ancient track.

If you want the power to grasp how that can be,
gather yourself, and picture Zion
standing on earth together with this mount,

so that the two share one single horizon
but lie in different hemispheres: then the road
that Phaethon, poor wretch, could not drive straight —

you'll see how it must of necessity pass
this mountain on one side, and Zion on the other,
if your mind attends to it clearly."

"Truly, my Master," I said, "I never yet
saw anything as plainly as I see this,
right where my wits had seemed to fail me:

that the mid-circle of the highest motion,
which in one art they call the Equator,
and which stays always between the sun and winter,

is — for the reason you give — set off
toward the north from here, while the Hebrews
used to see it off toward the warm lands.

But, if it please you, I would gladly learn
how far we still must go — for the hill rises
higher than my eyes have the power to climb."

And he to me: "This mountain is so made
that the start of it, down low, is always hard,
and the higher a man climbs, the less it hurts.

So, when it comes to seem so easy to you
that going up is no more work than drifting
downstream in a boat with the current,

then you'll have reached the end of this path.
There you can look to rest your tired breath.
I say no more — and this much I know is true."

And just as he finished saying these words,
a voice nearby us spoke up: "Maybe
you'll need to sit down before you get there."

At the sound of it we both turned around
and saw, off to the left, a great boulder
that neither he nor I had noticed before.

We made our way over. There were people there,
standing in the shade behind the rock
the way men stand when laziness owns them.

And one of them, who looked spent to me,
was sitting down with his arms around his knees,
his face dropped low between them.

"O my sweet Lord," I said, "look there
at that one — he carries himself more idly
than if Sloth herself had been his sister."

Then he turned to us and gave us his attention,
just lifting his eyes up past his thigh,
and said: "Go on up, then, since you're so strong."

Then I knew who he was — and the strain
that still had my breath coming a little quick
didn't keep me from going to him; and once

I'd reached him, he barely raised his head
to say: "So you've seen clearly how the sun
drives his chariot over your left shoulder?"

His sluggish slouch and his clipped little words
tugged my lips toward a smile, and I began:
"Belacqua, from now on I won't grieve for you.

But tell me — why are you sitting right here?
Are you waiting for a guide? Or has the old
habit just taken hold of you again?"

And he: "Brother, what's the use of climbing?
The angel of God who sits up at the gate
wouldn't let me go on to the torments.

First the heavens have to wheel me around
outside, as long as they did in my life —
because I put off my good sighs to the end —

unless, before that, a prayer can come to help me,
one that rises out of a heart living in grace.
What good are the rest, that heaven doesn't hear?"

And by now the Poet was climbing ahead,
saying: "Come on now — see, the sun has touched
the meridian, and on the shore the night

is already setting her foot on Morocco."
