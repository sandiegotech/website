The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XVIII

Inferno: Canto XVIII18 of 100

There is a place in Hell called Malebolge,
all of stone the color of iron,
like the cliff that wheels around it.

Right in the middle of that wicked ground
there gapes a well, enormously wide and deep,
whose shape I'll describe when I come to its place.

What's left between the well and the foot
of the high, hard bank is a circular strip,
and its floor is divided into ten valleys.

Picture how moats, ring inside ring,
are dug to defend a castle's walls,
the whole pattern they cut around the stronghold —

that was the image those ditches made.
And just as such fortresses run little bridges
from their gates out to the far bank,

so from the base of the cliff ran ridges of rock,
cutting across the dikes and the ditches
out to the well that chops them off and gathers them.

This is the place where we found ourselves,
shaken down off Geryon's back. The poet
bore to the left, and I came on behind him.

On my right hand I saw new misery,
new torments, and new hands swinging the whip —
the first ditch was packed with them.

Down at the bottom the sinners were naked.
On the near side of the middle they came toward us;
on the far side, our own way, but faster.

It was just like the Romans, the year of Jubilee,
when the crowd was so huge they worked out
a way to move the people across the bridge:

on one side everyone faces the Castle
and walks toward Saint Peter's;
on the other they walk toward the Mount.

Here and there, along the dead-gray stone,
I saw horned demons with enormous lashes
beating the sinners cruelly from behind.

Ah, how they made them lift their heels
at the first stroke! Not one of them
waited around for the second or the third.

As I walked on, my eyes fell on one of them,
and right away I said, "I've seen this man before —
I haven't gone hungry for the sight of him."

So I stopped where I was to make him out,
and my gentle guide stopped with me
and let me step a little way back.

And the scourged one tried to hide his face,
lowering it — but it did him no good,
because I said, "You, with your eyes on the ground,

if the features you're wearing aren't a lie,
you're Venedico Caccianemico.
What brings you to such bitter sauces?"

And he: "I tell it against my will,
but your plain speaking forces me to —
it drags the old world back to mind.

I was the one who got the lovely Ghisolabella
to give in to the wishes of the Marquis,
however the filthy story gets told.

And I'm not the only Bolognese weeping here.
No — this place is so crammed with us
that fewer tongues are taught today

to say sipa between the Reno and the Savena.
And if you want a pledge or proof of it,
just call to mind how greedy our hearts run."

While he was talking, a demon laid into him
with his whip, and said, "Move along, pander,
there are no women here to sell."

I rejoined my escort, and after a few more steps
we came to a place where a ridge of rock
jutted out from the bank.

We climbed it easily enough,
and turning right along its spine
we left those endless circling files behind.

When we reached the spot where the rock
is hollowed underneath to let the scourged pass through,
my guide said, "Wait, and let the sight

of these other ill-born souls strike your eyes —
the ones whose faces you haven't seen yet,
because they were going the same way as us."

From the old bridge we looked down at the file
coming toward us along the far edge,
driven the same way by the lash.

And the good master, before I could ask,
said, "Look at that tall one coming —
he doesn't seem to shed a tear for his pain.

What a kingly look he still carries!
That is Jason, who by courage and cunning
stripped the Colchians of the Ram.

He passed by the island of Lemnos
after the bold and pitiless women there
had put every one of their men to death.

There, with his tokens and his polished words,
he deceived Hypsipyle, the young woman
who had first deceived all the rest.

He left her there pregnant and abandoned.
That is the guilt that condemns him to this,
and Medea, too, is avenged.

With him go all who cheat the way he did.
Let that be enough to know of the first valley
and the souls it holds in its jaws."

We were already where the narrow path
crosses the second dike and makes of it
a buttress for the next arch.

From here we heard people whimpering
in the next ditch, snorting through their snouts
and pounding themselves with their hands.

The banks were crusted over with a scum
from the reek rising below, which cakes there
and wages war on the eyes and the nose.

The bottom is so deep that there's no seeing it
without climbing to the top of the arch,
where the rock juts out the farthest.

We got up there, and from it, down in the trench,
I saw people sunk in a filth
that looked like it came straight from human privies.

And while my eyes were searching down there
I saw one whose head was so caked with shit
you couldn't tell if he was clergy or layman.

He screamed at me, "Why are you so greedy
to stare at me more than the other filthy ones?"
And I told him, "Because, if I recall,

I've seen you before with your hair dry —
you're Alessio Interminei of Lucca.
That's why I eye you more than the rest."

And he, beating himself on the skull:
"The flatteries dragged me down here,
the ones my tongue could never get its fill of."

Then my guide said to me, "Push your face
a little farther forward, so your eyes
can land squarely on the features

of that filthy, disheveled creature down there,
scratching herself with her shit-caked nails,
now crouching, now back up on her feet.

That's Thaïs, the whore who answered
her lover, when he asked, 'Am I in your good graces?'
— 'Oh, beyond all measure.'

And with that, let our sight be satisfied."
