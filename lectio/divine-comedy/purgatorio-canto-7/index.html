The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto VII

Purgatorio: Canto VII41 of 100

When the glad and gracious greetings
had been exchanged three times and four,
Sordello drew back and said, "Who are you?"

"Before the souls who deserve to climb to God
were ever sent up this mountain,
my bones were buried by Octavian.

I am Virgil; and I lost heaven
for no other fault than this — that I had no faith."
So my Leader answered him.

Like a man who suddenly sees before him
something that astonishes him, and believes
and does not believe, saying, "It is — it isn't" —

so was he; and then he bowed his head
and turned back to him in humility,
and embraced him low, where a lesser man embraces.

"O glory of the Latins," he said,
"through whom our language showed all it could do,
O eternal pride of the place I came from,

what merit, what grace shows you to me?
If I am worthy to hear your words, tell me
whether you come from Hell, and from which cloister."

"Through all the circles of the realm of grief,"
he answered, "I have come this far;
a power from heaven moved me, and with that I come.

Not by doing, but by not doing, I lost
the sight of that high Sun you long for,
the Sun I knew too late.

There is a place down there — not grieved by torments,
but by darkness only, where the laments
are not the sound of wailing but of sighs.

There I live among the little innocents
seized by the teeth of Death
before they were cleared of our human sin.

There I live among those who never put on
the three holy virtues, yet without vice
knew the others and kept them, all of them.

But if you know, and can, give us some sign
by which we may come sooner
to where Purgatory truly begins."

He answered: "No fixed place is set for us;
I am free to go up and around;
as far as I can go, I'll join you as your guide.

But look — the day is already failing,
and we cannot climb by night;
so it is well to think of a fair place to wait.

There are souls off to the right, drawn apart;
if you allow it, I will lead you to them,
and you will be glad to know them."

"How can that be?" came the answer. "If a man
wished to climb by night, would he be stopped
by others — or would he simply have no power?"

And good Sordello drew his finger
across the ground, and said, "See — this single line
you could not cross once the sun is gone;

not that anything else would block
the climb, except the dark of night;
that dark ties up the will by stealing its power.

We could, of course, still go back down,
and wander the hillside all around,
while the horizon keeps the day shut in."

At this my Lord, as if in wonder, said:
"Then lead us there, where you say
we can take some pleasure in waiting."

We had drawn only a little from that place
when I saw the mountain was hollowed out,
the way the valleys here are hollowed.

"Over there," that shade said, "we'll go —
where the hillside makes a lap of itself,
and there we'll wait for the new day."

Between the slope and the level there ran a winding path
that brought us to the rim of that hollow,
where its edge dies away more than halfway down.

Gold and fine silver, scarlet and white lead,
bright Indian wood, clear and serene,
fresh emerald the moment it is split —

set against the grass and flowers in that hollow,
each would be outdone in color,
as the lesser is outdone by the greater.

Nature had not only painted there;
out of the sweetness of a thousand scents
she made one fragrance, mingled and strange.

"Salve, Regina" — there on the green and the flowers
I saw souls seated, singing,
souls you could not see from outside the valley.

"Before what little sun is left goes to its nest,"
began the Mantuan who had led us there,
"do not ask me to bring you down among them.

From this ledge you will make out
the faces and the deeds of all of them better
than if you stood received among them on the floor below.

The one who sits highest, and wears the look
of a man who left undone what he should have done,
and does not move his lips to the others' song —

that was Rudolph the Emperor, who had the power
to heal the wounds that have killed Italy,
so that only slowly, by other hands, she revives.

The other, whose look seems to comfort him,
ruled the land where the waters rise
that the Moldau carries to the Elbe, and the Elbe to the sea.

His name was Ottokar; and even in swaddling-clothes
he was the better man than his bearded son
Wenceslaus, who feeds on idleness and ease.

And the small-nosed one, who seems deep in counsel
with that one of so kind a face,
died in flight, deflowering the lily —

look there, how he beats at his breast!
And see the other, who has made a bed
of his own palm, sighing into his cheek.

They are the father and the father-in-law
of the Plague of France; they know his foul, corrupt life,
and from that comes the grief that pierces them so.

The one who looks so sturdy, and joins his voice,
singing, with that other of the manly nose,
wore girt about him the cord of every worth;

and if the boy who sits behind him
had stayed on as king after him,
the worth would have passed cleanly from vessel to vessel —

which cannot be said of the other heirs.
Frederick and James hold the kingdoms now,
but neither holds the better inheritance.

Rarely does a man's goodness rise again
through the branches; and this He wills
who gives it, so that we may ask it of Him.

My words reach the large-nosed one too,
no less than the other, Pedro, who sings with him;
and so Provence and Apulia grieve already.

The plant is as much beneath its seed
as, more than for Beatrice or Margaret,
Constance still boasts of her husband.

See the king of the simple life,
Henry of England, sitting there alone;
in his branches he has the better growth.

The one seated lowest on the ground among them,
looking upward, is William the Marquis —
for whose sake Alessandria and her war

make Monferrato and the Canavese weep."
