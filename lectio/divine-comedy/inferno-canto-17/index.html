The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XVII

Inferno: Canto XVII17 of 100

"Look at the beast with the pointed tail,
who crosses mountains, shatters walls and weapons —
look at the one whose stench fills the whole world!"

So my guide began to speak to me,
and he beckoned the thing to come to shore,
close to the edge of the trodden marble.

And that filthy image of fraud
came on, and ran its head and chest aground,
but did not drag its tail up onto the bank.

Its face was the face of an honest man,
so kindly was the look of it outside —
and all the rest of its body was a serpent's.

It had two paws, hairy to the armpits.
Its back and breast and both its flanks
were painted over with knots and little shields.

No Turk or Tartar ever wove a cloth
with more colors, ground and pattern both,
nor did Arachne ever lay such webs.

The way boats sometimes lie along the shore,
part of them in the water, part on land,
and the way, off among the guzzling Germans,

the beaver settles in to wage its hunt,
so that vile creature lay against the rim
of stone that fences in the burning sand.

Its tail was thrashing wholly in the void,
twisting its poisoned fork up into the air,
the point of it armed like a scorpion's sting.

My guide said: "Now we have to bend our way
a little, over to that wicked beast
that lies there crouched at the cliff's edge."

So we went down along the right-hand side
and took ten steps out toward the very brink,
to keep well clear of the sand and the flames.

And when we had come up to the beast, I saw
a little farther off, out on the sand,
a crowd of people sitting near the drop.

Then the master said to me: "So you take
the full measure of this ring with you,
go now and see what their condition is.

Keep your talk with them brief. While you're gone
I'll have a word with this one, so he grants us
the loan of his strong shoulders."

So on, still farther, out along the rim
of that seventh circle, all alone
I went, to where the grieving people sat.

Their pain was breaking out through their eyes.
This way and that they kept beating it off
with their hands — now the flames, now the scorching ground.

It's no different from what dogs do in summer,
working now with a paw, now with the muzzle,
when the fleas or the flies or the gadflies bite.

When I had turned my eyes to look at the faces
of some of them the wretched fire was raining on,
I knew none of them — but I saw

that from each one's neck there hung a pouch,
each with its own color, its own crest,
and on these their eyes seemed to be feeding.

And as I came among them, looking around,
I saw, on a yellow pouch, a blue
that had the face and bearing of a lion.

Then, letting the current of my gaze run on,
I saw another, red as blood,
display a goose more white than butter.

And one, who had a blue and pregnant sow
stamped on the white field of his little pouch,
said to me: "What are you doing in this pit?

Get out of here — and since you're still alive,
know that my neighbor Vitaliano
will have his seat right here, at my left side.

I'm a Paduan among these Florentines.
Many a time they go thundering in my ears,
shouting, 'Bring on the sovereign cavalier,

the one who'll bring the pouch with the three goats!'"
At that he twisted his mouth and stuck his tongue out,
like an ox that licks its own nose.

And fearing that a longer stay might irk
the one who'd warned me not to linger long,
I turned my back on those worn-out souls.

I found my guide already mounted up
on the back of that wild animal,
and he said to me: "Now be strong, be bold.

From here on we go down by stairs like these.
Get on in front; I'll ride between,
so the tail can do you no harm."

Like a man so close to the shivering fit
of a quartan fever his nails have already gone blue,
who trembles all over at the sight of shade —

that's what I became at those words of his.
But shame made its threats to me,
as it makes a servant brave before a good master.

I settled myself on those monstrous shoulders.
I wanted to say — but the voice would not come
as I expected — "Be sure to hold me tight."

But he, who other times in other danger
had rescued me, the moment I was up
gathered me in his arms and held me fast,

and said: "Now, Geryon, get moving.
Keep the circles wide, the dropping slow.
Think of the strange new weight you carry."

The way a little boat backs off from shore,
stern first, slowly, slowly, that's how it pulled away —
and when it felt itself fully afloat,

it swung its tail to where its chest had been,
and stretching it out like an eel, it stirred,
and with its paws it gathered in the air.

I don't think there was ever greater fear,
not when Phaethon let the reins go slack
and scorched the sky — the scar of it still shows —

nor when poor Icarus felt the feathers
peeling from his sides as the wax went soft,
his father crying, "You're flying the wrong way!" —

than there was in me, when I saw I was hanging
in the air on every side, and everything
blotted out from view but the beast itself.

On it goes, swimming slowly, slowly on,
wheeling and dropping — though all I can tell of it
is the wind in my face and the wind from below.

Already, off to the right, I could hear the whirlpool
making its horrible roar beneath us,
so I leaned my head out and looked down.

Then I grew even more afraid of the drop,
because I saw fires, and I heard the wailing,
and shaking, I clung all the tighter on.

And then I saw — what I hadn't seen before —
the wheeling and the descent, by the great torments
that were drawing closer on every side.

The way a falcon that's been long on the wing,
seeing neither the lure nor any bird,
makes the falconer groan, "Oh, you're coming down!" —

comes down worn out, after starting so swift,
through a hundred circles, and lands
far from its master, sullen and surly —

just so did Geryon set us at the bottom,
right at the foot of the sheer-cut rock,
and, the moment he was free of our two bodies,

he shot away like an arrow off the string.
