# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXXI

The same tongue that had just wounded me,  
staining first one of my cheeks and then the other,  
was the tongue that held out the cure as well.

So I have heard that Achilles' spear,  
his and his father's both, would deal a wound  
first, and then with a second touch make it whole.

We turned our backs on that wretched valley  
and crossed the bank that rings it all around,  
walking the whole way without a word.

Here it was less than night and less than day,  
so my sight reached only a little ahead;  
but I could hear the blast of a great horn,

so loud it would have made any thunder faint.  
Tracing it back along the path it came,  
I fixed my eyes on the one place it rose from.

After the bitter rout at Roncesvalles,  
when Charlemagne lost the whole holy company,  
Roland's own horn did not sound so terrible.

I had not held my head turned that way long  
when I seemed to see a stand of high towers,  
so I said, "Master, tell me — what city is this?"

And he answered, "Because you strain to see  
so far ahead, and through the dark,  
your mind goes wrong and fills in what's not there.

You'll see well enough, once you arrive,  
how badly distance fools the senses;  
so quicken your step a little now."

Then he took me gently by the hand  
and said, "Before we go any farther,  
so the truth won't seem so strange to you,

know that these are not towers but giants,  
and they stand in the well, around its bank,  
every one of them, from the navel down."

The way that, when a fog is lifting,  
little by little the eye makes out again  
whatever the thick air had hidden away,

so, as I pierced that dense and darkened air,  
drawing nearer and nearer to the edge,  
my error fell away and dread came over me.

For as Montereggione crowns its round wall  
with a ring of towers along the top,  
so here, along the bank that rims the well,

the horrible giants stood up like turrets  
with the upper half of their bodies — the ones  
Jove still threatens from the sky when he thunders.

And I could already make out the face of one,  
the shoulders and the chest, much of the belly,  
and both arms hanging down along his sides.

Nature did well, surely, when she gave up  
making creatures like these, to take away  
from Mars such ready hands to do his work.

And if she has no regret for whales and elephants,  
anyone who reasons it through carefully  
will judge her wiser and more just for that:

for where the power of the mind is added  
to a will set on evil, and to brute strength,  
no people can throw up a wall against it.

His face looked to me as long and broad  
as the bronze pinecone at Saint Peter's in Rome,  
and the rest of his bones were in proportion,

so that the bank, which made an apron of him  
from the waist down, still left so much above it  
that three Frieslanders, one standing on another,

would have boasted in vain of reaching his hair;  
for I could see a good thirty hand-spans of him  
down from the place where a man clasps his cloak.

"Raphèl maì amècche zabì almi,"  
the brute mouth began to bellow out,  
a throat unfit for any sweeter hymn.

And my guide turned on him: "You witless soul,  
stick to your horn, and let that be your vent  
whenever rage or some other passion takes you.

Feel around your neck and you'll find the strap  
that holds it fast, you muddled thing —  
there it is, slung across your great chest."

Then to me: "He accuses himself well enough.  
This is Nimrod, whose mad scheme  
left the world without a single shared tongue.

Let's leave him here and not waste breath;  
for every language is to him  
what his is to everyone — known to no one."

So we made a longer journey of it,  
bearing left, and a crossbow-shot beyond  
we came on another, far fiercer and larger.

Who the master was who bound him, I can't say;  
but he had the right arm pinned behind  
and the left one fixed in front of him,

with a chain that held him so wound about  
from the neck down that on the part left bare  
it coiled itself around to the fifth turn.

"This proud one set out to test his strength  
against the supreme Jove himself,"  
my leader said, "and this is his reward.

His name is Ephialtes. He made his great bid  
the day the giants struck terror in the gods;  
the arms he swung then he will never move again."

And I said to him, "If it were possible,  
I'd want my own eyes to take in  
the sight of measureless Briareus."

He answered, "You will see Antaeus  
close by here — he can speak, and he's unbound,  
and he'll set us down at the floor of all guilt.

The one you want to see is much farther on,  
and he's bound, and shaped like this one here,  
except his face looks even more ferocious."

No earthquake ever shook with force enough  
to rock a tower so violently  
as Ephialtes shook himself just then.

Then I feared death more than ever before —  
the fear alone would have been enough to kill,  
if I had not seen the chains that held him.

We went on, then, a little farther ahead  
and came to Antaeus, who stood up out of the pit  
a full five ells, not counting the head.

"O you who once, in that fortunate valley  
that made Scipio heir to glory,  
when Hannibal and his men turned their backs,

carried off a thousand lions for your prey —  
you who, had you stood with your brothers  
in their high war on heaven, make some still believe

the sons of Earth would have won the day:  
set us down below, and don't think it beneath you,  
there where the cold locks up Cocytus.

Don't send us off to Tityus or Typhon.  
This man can give you what is hungered for here;  
so stoop, and don't curl your lip at us.

He can still restore your fame up in the world,  
for he is alive, and looks to live a long time,  
unless Grace calls him home before his hour."

So spoke the master; and the giant, in haste,  
stretched out the hands whose mighty grip  
Hercules had felt once, and took up my guide.

When Virgil felt himself caught up like that,  
he said to me, "Come here, let me take hold of you" —  
and made the two of us into one bundle.

The way the Garisenda looks, seen from below  
on the side it leans, when a cloud goes over  
so that the tower seems to topple the other way:

that is how Antaeus looked to me  
as I watched him bend — and at that moment  
I could have wished to go by some other road.

But lightly he set us down on the floor  
that swallows Lucifer and Judas;  
nor did he linger, stooped over us, down there,

but rose again like a mast going up in a ship.
