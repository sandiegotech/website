# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto V

I had already left those shades behind  
and was following close in my guide's footsteps  
when, from behind me, one of them pointed his finger

and shouted: "Look — it seems the light  
doesn't reach the ground to the left of the lower one,  
and he moves like a man still alive."

At those words I turned my eyes  
and saw them staring, amazed, at me —  
at me alone, and at the broken light.

"Why does your mind let itself get tangled,"  
the Master said, "so that you slow your step?  
What is it to you, what they whisper here?

Come after me, and let the people talk.  
Stand like a firm tower that never bends  
its top, no matter how the winds may blow.

The man who lets one thought breed another  
drives his own goal further off,  
because each new thought saps the force of the last."

What could I answer but "I'm coming"?  
I said it with a little of that color  
that sometimes earns a man his pardon.

Meanwhile, across the slope ahead of us,  
a crowd was coming, a short way off,  
singing the Miserere, verse by verse.

When they saw that my body gave no passage  
to the light — that it blocked the sun —  
their song changed to a long and ragged "Oh!"

And two of them, like messengers sent ahead,  
ran out to meet us and asked of us:  
"Tell us — what is your condition here?"

And my Master said: "You can go back  
and carry word to those who sent you:  
this man's body is real flesh.

If they stopped, as I suppose, because they saw his shadow,  
that answers them. Let them do him honor —  
it may yet do them good."

I never saw kindled vapors split  
the clear early-evening sky so fast,  
nor August clouds at sundown,

as those two climbed back in less time,  
and once arrived, they wheeled with the rest  
toward us, like a troop charging with no rein.

"These people pressing toward us are many,  
and they come to beg something of you,"  
the Poet said. "Keep going, and listen as you go."

"O soul on your way to blessedness  
with the same body you were born in,"  
they came shouting, "slow your steps a little.

Look — have you ever seen any of us,  
so you might carry word of him back there?  
Why do you go on? Why won't you stay?

Long ago we were all killed by violence,  
and we were sinners to our final hour.  
Then a light from heaven warned us,

so that, repenting and forgiving both,  
we left life reconciled to God,  
who fills our hearts with longing to see him."

And I: "Though I study your faces closely,  
I know none of you. But if there's anything  
I can do, spirits born to better things,

say it, and I'll do it — by that peace  
which, behind the feet of such a guide,  
I go from world to world to find."

And one began: "Each of us trusts  
your good word without an oath,  
so long as "I cannot" does not cut off "I will."

So I, who speak before the others, beg you:  
if you ever see that country lying  
between Romagna and the land of Charles,

be kind enough to ask, in Fano,  
that they pray for me devoutly,  
so I can purge away my heavy faults.

I was from there. But the deep wounds  
that let out the blood I lived in  
were given me among the Antenori,

right where I thought I was most safe.  
The man of Este had it done — he hated me  
far past anything justice could allow.

If I'd run toward the Mira instead,  
when they caught me at Oriaco,  
I'd still be back there, among the breathing.

I ran to the marsh, and the reeds and mud  
so tangled me that I fell, and there I watched  
a lake spread on the ground out of my veins."

Then another said: "Ah — so may the longing  
that draws you up the high mountain be filled,  
as you, in pity, help mine along.

I was of Montefeltro; I am Buonconte.  
Neither Giovanna nor anyone else cares for me;  
so I go among these with my head bowed low."

And I to him: "What violence or chance  
carried you so far from Campaldino  
that no one ever found where you were buried?"

"Oh," he answered, "at the foot of Casentino  
a river runs, called the Archiano,  
rising above the Hermitage in the Apennines.

There, where that name dissolves into another,  
I came — pierced clean through the throat,  
fleeing on foot, bleeding across the plain.

There my sight failed me, and my last words  
died on the name of Mary, and there  
I fell, and left my flesh behind, empty.

I'll speak the truth — carry it to the living:  
the Angel of God took me, and the one from hell  
cried out, "You — from heaven — why do you rob me?

You're carrying off the eternal part of him  
for one small tear, that takes him from me.  
But the rest — the rest I'll handle my own way!"

You know well how, in the air, that wet vapor  
gathers and turns back into water  
the moment it climbs to where the cold can grip it.

He joined that evil will, forever bent on evil,  
to cunning, and he stirred the mist and wind  
by the power his own nature gave him.

Then, when the day was spent, he buried the valley  
from Pratomagno to the great ridge in fog  
and made the sky above hang thick and waiting,

until the loaded air dissolved to water.  
The rain came down, and all the earth  
could not soak up went rushing to the gullies,

and as it gathered into the great torrents,  
it hurtled toward the royal river  
with such speed nothing could hold it back.

Near its own mouth, the swollen Archiano  
found my frozen body and shoved it into the Arno,  
and it pried loose from my chest the cross

I'd made of my arms when the agony took me.  
It rolled me along the banks and the riverbed,  
then wrapped and buried me in its spoils."

"Ah, when you have gone back to the world  
and rested from your long road,"  
after the second, a third spirit began,

"remember me. I am La Pia.  
Siena made me, Maremma unmade me —  
he knows it, the one who first, in marriage,

slipped his jeweled ring onto my finger."
