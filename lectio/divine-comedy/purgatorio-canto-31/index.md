# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XXXI

"You there, beyond the sacred river" —  
turning the point of her words on me,  
which edge-on had already cut so deep —

she went straight on, without a pause:  
"Say it, say if this is true. To a charge like this  
your own confession has to be joined."

Everything in me was in such confusion  
that the voice moved, and yet died  
before my mouth could set it free.

She waited a little; then she said: "What are you thinking?  
Answer me. The waters have not yet  
washed the bitter memory out of you."

Confusion and dismay together  
forced such a Yes out of my mouth  
that the eye was needed to make it out.

The way a crossbow snaps when it goes off  
with string and arm strung past their limit,  
so the bolt strikes the target with less force —

so I broke under that heavy load,  
pouring out a flood of tears and sighs,  
and the voice failed on its way out.

At which she said: "Through those longings of mine  
that led you to the loving of that good  
beyond which there is nothing left to want,

what ditches running across your road, what chains  
did you find, that you should have stripped yourself  
of all your hope of going further on?

And what easy gains, what favors  
showed on the faces of the others,  
that you should turn your steps toward them?"

After the heaving of a bitter sigh,  
I hardly had the voice to answer,  
and my lips shaped it with great effort.

Weeping, I said: "The things in front of me,  
with their false pleasure, turned my steps aside  
the moment your face was hidden from me."

And she: "If you stayed silent, or denied  
what you confess, your fault would be  
no less plain — the Judge who knows it knows.

But when the accusal of the sin  
comes bursting out from a man's own cheeks,  
here in our court the wheel turns back against the blade.

Still, so that you may feel a greater shame  
for going wrong, and another time  
be stronger when you hear the Sirens sing,

put down the seed of weeping now and listen.  
So you will hear how, the opposite way,  
my buried flesh should have led you on.

Never did art or nature set before you  
a pleasure like the lovely body  
that held me once, and now lies scattered in the earth.

And if that highest pleasure failed you so,  
by reason of my death — what mortal thing  
should then have drawn you into wanting it?

At the first arrow of those lying things  
you should have lifted yourself up  
to follow me, who was no longer of them.

You should not have folded your wings down  
to wait for more such blows — some little girl,  
or any other vanity that lasts so short a while.

The new-hatched bird waits for two darts or three,  
but the net is spread, the arrow shot, in vain  
before the eyes of one already fledged."

As children stand silent in their shame,  
eyes on the ground, listening,  
knowing their fault and sorry for it,

so I stood there; and she said: "If hearing  
gives you this much pain, lift up your beard,  
and you will feel a greater pain in seeing."

A sturdy oak is torn up from the ground  
with less resistance, whether by our own wind  
or by the gale that blows from Iarbas' land,

than I lifted my chin at her command;  
and when she named the face by way of the beard,  
I felt the full venom of her meaning.

And as my face came up at last, my eyes  
saw that those beautiful beings had stopped  
their scattering of the flowers;

and still half-blinded, my eyes saw Beatrice  
turned toward the creature  
that is a single person in two natures.

Beneath her veil, beyond the green bank's edge,  
she seemed to me to outshine her old self  
farther than she outshone all others when she was on earth.

The thorn of penitence so stung me then  
that the thing which once had turned me most to love it  
became, of all things, the one I hated most.

Such recognition wrung my heart  
that I fell, overcome — and what I became  
she knows, who was the cause of it.

Then, when my heart gave back my outer sense,  
I found the lady I had seen alone  
standing above me, saying, "Hold me, hold me."

She had drawn me into the stream up to my throat,  
and pulling me along behind her  
she moved across the water light as a shuttle.

When I was near the blessed shore,  
"Asperges me" was sung so sweetly  
I cannot recall it, much less write it down.

The lovely lady opened wide her arms,  
took my head in them, and pushed me under,  
where I had to swallow some of the water.

Then she drew me out, and dripping wet  
led me into the dance of the four beauties,  
and each of them spread an arm over me.

"Here we are Nymphs, and in the sky we are stars.  
Before Beatrice went down into the world  
we were chosen to be her handmaids.

We will lead you to her eyes; but for the joyful  
light inside them, the three beyond us,  
who see more deeply, will sharpen yours."

So they sang; and then they led me with them  
up to the Griffin's breast,  
where Beatrice stood, turned toward us.

"See that you do not spare your eyes," they said.  
"We have set you in front of the emeralds  
where Love once drew his weapons against you."

A thousand longings hotter than any flame  
fastened my eyes to those shining eyes,  
which stayed fixed steadily on the Griffin.

As the sun in a mirror, in just that way  
the twofold creature shone inside her eyes,  
now with the one nature, now with the other.

Think, Reader, how I marveled within myself,  
when I saw the thing itself stand perfectly still  
and keep changing in the image it threw.

And while my soul, amazed and full of joy,  
was tasting of that food which, even as it fills you,  
leaves you hungering after it,

the other three, showing in their bearing  
that they were of the highest rank, came forward,  
singing to their angelic round-dance.

"Turn, Beatrice, oh turn your holy eyes,"  
so ran their song, "upon your faithful one,  
who has come so many steps to see you.

Out of your grace, grant us this grace:  
unveil your mouth to him, so that he may see  
the second beauty that you still keep hidden."

O splendor of the living light eternal!  
Whoever has gone pale in the shade of Parnassus,  
or drunk deep at its spring,

would still seem to have his mind weighed down  
trying to paint you as you appeared just then,  
where the harmonious heaven shadowed you,

when, out in the open air, you let the veil fall?
