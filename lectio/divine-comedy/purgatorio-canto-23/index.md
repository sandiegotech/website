# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XXIII

While I kept my eyes fixed on the green leaves  
the way a man will who throws his whole life away  
chasing after little birds,

the one who was more than a father said to me,  
"Son, come on now — the time we have been given  
should be spent to better purpose than this."

I turned my face, and just as quickly my steps,  
toward the two sages, who were talking in a way  
that made the going cost me nothing at all;

and there — a song and a weeping all at once,  
"Labia mea, Domine," sung in such a strain  
that it gave birth to both delight and grief.

"My sweet father, what is this I'm hearing?"  
I began, and he answered, "Souls who go  
perhaps to loosen the knot of what they owe."

The way a thoughtful pilgrim does on the road  
when he overtakes strangers he doesn't know —  
he turns to look, but does not break his stride;

just so, behind us, moving at a quicker pace,  
coming up and passing on, a crowd of spirits  
gazed at us, silent and devout.

Each of them had eyes dark and sunk in their sockets,  
faces gone pale, and so wasted away  
that the skin took its whole shape from the bones beneath.

I do not think Erysichthon was ever  
withered down to mere rind like this by famine,  
even when his fear of it was at its worst.

Thinking it over, I said to myself, "Look —  
these are the people who lost Jerusalem,  
when Mary sank her teeth into her own son."

Their eye-sockets were like rings with the gems pried out.  
Anyone who reads the word OMO in a human face  
would easily have made out the M right here.

Who would believe the smell of an apple,  
working on their longing, could waste them so —  
and the smell of water — not knowing how?

I was still wondering what made them so starved,  
the reason for their gauntness and grim squalor  
not yet made plain to me,

when, out of the hollow of his head, a shade  
turned his eyes on me and stared hard;  
then cried aloud, "What grace is this, granted to me?"

I never would have known him by his looks;  
but in his voice there came clear through to me  
what his ruined face had buried out of sight.

That spark relit in me, all at once,  
my knowledge of those altered features,  
and I knew again the face of Forese.

"Ah, don't dwell on this dry scab," he begged,  
"that discolors my skin, or on the flesh  
that is missing from me;

but tell me the truth about yourself, and who  
are those two souls escorting you over there —  
don't hold back, speak to me."

"That face of yours, which once I wept over dead,  
gives me no smaller cause for weeping now,"  
I answered him, "to see it changed like this.

But for God's sake, tell me — what strips you down so?  
Don't make me speak while I am still amazed;  
a man speaks badly whose mind is full of something else."

And he to me: "From the eternal counsel  
a power falls into the water and the tree  
we left behind us, and it makes me this thin.

All these people who weep as they sing  
followed their appetite past every limit,  
and here, in hunger and thirst, are made holy again.

The craving to eat and drink is kindled in us  
by the scent that comes off the apple tree  
and off the spray misting down over the leaves;

and not just once, as we go round this ground,  
is our pain renewed —  
I say our pain, and ought to say our solace,

for the same will leads us toward that tree  
that led Christ, glad, to cry out 'Elì'  
when he set us free with the blood of his own veins."

And I to him: "Forese, from the day  
you traded this world for a better life,  
five years have not yet come full circle.

If the power to sin again ran dry in you  
before the hour of that good sorrow came,  
the sorrow that weds us back to God,

how have you climbed up this high already?  
I thought to find you still down there below,  
where time is paid back, time for time."

And he to me: "It is my Nella  
who has led me so soon to drink  
the sweet wormwood of these torments — with her flooding tears.

With her devout prayers and her sighs  
she drew me up from the slope where souls wait,  
and set me free from all the other circles.

So much more dear and pleasing to God  
is my little widow, the one I loved so much,  
the more she stands alone in doing good;

for the Barbagia of Sardinia  
is far more modest in its women  
than the Barbagia I left her behind in.

O sweet brother, what would you have me say?  
A time to come is already in my sight,  
and this present hour will not be old by then,

when from the pulpit it will be forbidden  
to the brazen women of Florence  
to go around showing the breast, nipples bare.

What savages were ever, what Saracen women,  
who had to be made to cover themselves up  
by discipline of the Church or any other?

But if these shameless ones could only know  
what swift Heaven is readying for them,  
they'd already have their mouths open to howl;

for if my foresight here does not deceive me,  
they'll be grieving before the cheeks grow a beard  
on the boy now hushed to sleep with a lullaby.

O brother, don't keep yourself hidden from me now;  
you see that not only I, but all these people  
are staring there, where you blot out the sun."

At that I said to him: "If you bring back to mind  
what you were with me, and I with you,  
the memory will still be hard to bear.

Out of that life he turned me back, the one  
who walks ahead of me — two days ago,  
when the sister of that one up there showed herself full,"

and I pointed to the sun. "Through the deep  
night of the truly dead this man has led me,  
in this real flesh, that follows behind him.

From there his encouragement has carried me up,  
climbing and circling round and round the mountain  
that straightens you, whom the world bent crooked.

He says he will keep me company  
until I am where Beatrice will be;  
there I must go on without him.

This is Virgil, who tells me all of this,"  
and I pointed at him; "and the other is  
that shade for whom, just now, every slope of your realm

shook, as it discharged him from itself."
