The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto I

Paradiso: Canto I68 of 100

The glory of the One who moves all things
penetrates the universe, and shines
in one part more, in another less.

I have been in the heaven that takes
the most of His light, and seen things
no one coming back down knows how to tell, or has the power to;

for as it draws near the thing it longs for,
our mind goes down so deep
that memory cannot follow after.

Yet whatever of that holy kingdom
I had the power to hoard in my mind
shall now become the matter of my song.

Good Apollo, for this last labor
make me so fit a vessel of your power
as you require before you give your beloved laurel.

Until now one peak of Parnassus
has been enough for me; but now
I need them both to enter the ground that's left.

Come into my chest, and breathe through me
the way you breathed the day you drew Marsyas
out of the sheath of his own limbs.

Power of God, if you lend yourself to me
so that I can show even the shadow
of that blessed kingdom stamped in my brain,

you will see me come to your cherished tree
and crown myself there with the leaves
that the theme — and you — will make me worthy of.

So seldom, Father, are they gathered
to mark the triumph of a Caesar or a poet
— the fault and shame of what we want —

that the leaf of Peneus should make any man
thirst for it, and bring joy
to the glad god of Delphi who gives it.

A great flame follows a little spark:
perhaps after me, with stronger voices,
the prayer will rise that Cyrrha may answer.

The lamp of the world rises on mortal men
by different gates; but from the one
that joins four circles with three crosses

it comes out on a better course,
joined to a better star, and stamps and tempers
the wax of the world more after its own kind.

That gate had made it nearly morning there
and evening here, and there the whole
hemisphere was white, the other side was black,

when I saw Beatrice turned to the left
and gazing into the sun:
no eagle ever fixed on it like that.

And just as a second ray will spring
out of the first and climb back up,
like a pilgrim longing to go home,

so from her act, poured through my eyes
into my mind, my own act was made,
and I fixed my eyes on the sun past all our custom —

for much is allowed us there that here
is not, by virtue of the place
made to be the home of humankind.

I could not bear it long, and yet not so short
a while that I did not see it sparkle round
like iron drawn molten from the fire;

and all at once it seemed that day
was added to day, as if the One who can
had decked the heaven with a second sun.

Beatrice stood with all her gaze
upon the eternal wheels, and I
fixed mine on her, drawn back from up there;

and gazing at her I became within
what Glaucus became, tasting the herb
that made him consort of the gods beneath the sea.

To pass beyond the human cannot be told
in words; let the example serve
the one for whom grace keeps the experience.

Whether I was only that part of me
you made last, Love who governs heaven,
you know — you who lifted me with your light.

When the wheel you make turn forever,
spinning in its longing for you, drew my notice
with the harmony you tune and measure,

so much of heaven then seemed to me to blaze
with the sun's fire that no rain, no river,
ever spread a lake so wide.

The newness of the sound and the great light
lit in me a hunger for their cause
sharper than any I had felt before;

so she, who saw me as I saw myself,
to settle my unsettled mind
opened her mouth before I opened mine,

and began: "You make yourself so dull
with false imagining that you can't see
what you would see if you had shaken it off.

You are not on the earth, the way you think;
but lightning, fleeing the place it belongs to,
never ran as you do, climbing home."

If those few words, more smiled than spoken,
freed me of my first doubt,
I was caught the deeper in a new one,

and said: "Already I was at rest
from my great wonder; but now I marvel
how I can rise through these light bodies."

At that, after a pitying sigh,
she turned her eyes on me with the look
a mother gives a child gone out of its mind,

and began: "All things whatever they are
have order among themselves, and this is the form
that makes the universe resemble God.

Here the higher creatures see the trace
of the Eternal Power, which is the end
for which that order was made.

In the order I am speaking of, all natures
incline, each by its different lot,
nearer or less near to where they began;

and so they move toward different harbors
over the great sea of being, each one
carried by an instinct given it to bear it on.

This instinct drives the fire toward the moon;
this is the moving power in mortal hearts;
this binds the earth together and makes it one.

And this bow shoots not only the things
that have no mind, but those as well
that are made with intellect and love.

The Providence that orders all of this
stills with its light the heaven that holds
the sphere that turns with the greatest haste;

and there, as to the place appointed,
the power of that bowstring now bears us,
the one that aims its arrows at a joyful mark.

True, as often the shape
falls short of what the artist meant,
because the matter is deaf and will not answer,

so the creature sometimes swerves
from this course, having the power,
though driven this way, to turn off another —

the way you can watch fire fall
from a cloud — if the first thrust
is wrenched toward earth by some false pleasure.

You should no more wonder at your rising,
if I judge rightly, than at a stream
that falls from a high mountain to the valley floor.

It would be a marvel if you, set free
of every weight, had stayed below —
as if a living flame on earth held still."

And with that she turned her face again to heaven.
