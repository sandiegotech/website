# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto IX

The color that my cowardice had painted  
over my face, seeing my guide turn back,  
made him the quicker to swallow his own.

He stopped and listened hard, like a man straining,  
because his eyes could carry him no distance  
through the black air and the thick fog.

"Still, we have to win this fight," he began —  
"unless . . . But such a one offered her help . . .  
Oh, how I wish that someone would arrive!"

I saw clearly how he covered the beginning  
of what he'd said with what came after it,  
so that the end ran counter to the start —

but his words frightened me all the same,  
because I drew out of the broken phrase  
a worse meaning, maybe, than he meant.

"Down into the bottom of this sad hollow,  
does anyone ever come from the first ring,  
where the only punishment is hope cut off?"

This was my question, and he answered me:  
"It rarely happens that one of us  
makes the journey I am making now.

It's true that once before I came down here,  
conjured by that pitiless Erichtho,  
who called the shades back into their dead bodies.

My flesh had been stripped from me a short while  
when she sent me inside that wall to bring  
a spirit out of the circle of Judas.

That is the lowest place, the darkest,  
the farthest from the heaven that turns all things.  
I know the road well — so set your mind at ease.

This marsh that breathes out such a monstrous stench  
rings the whole city of grief around,  
where now we cannot enter without rage."

And he said more, but I can't hold it in mind,  
because my eyes had drawn me all the way  
to the high tower with its red-flaming top,

where, in an instant, I saw rise straight up  
the three infernal Furies, stained with blood,  
who had the limbs of women and their look,

and were girdled with the greenest water-snakes;  
small serpents and horned vipers were their hair,  
bound around their savage temples.

And he, who knew well the handmaids  
of the queen of everlasting grief,  
said to me: "Look at the fierce Furies.

That is Megaera, there on the left.  
The one weeping on the right is Alecto.  
Tisiphone is between them." Then he was still.

Each one was tearing at her breast with her nails;  
they beat themselves with their palms and shrieked so loud  
that in dread I pressed close against the poet.

"Let Medusa come, and we'll turn him to stone!"  
they all cried, looking down. "It was a bad day  
we failed to avenge the assault of Theseus."

"Turn around, and keep your eyes shut tight,  
because if the Gorgon shows herself and you look,  
there'd be no climbing back up, ever."

So said the master, and he turned me himself,  
and did not trust it to my own hands,  
but sealed my eyes shut with his too.

O you who have sound and steady minds,  
look hard at the teaching that hides itself  
beneath the veil of these strange verses.

And now, across the muddy water, came  
the crash of a sound so full of terror  
that both the banks of the marsh were trembling.

It was just like a wind, turned violent  
by the clash of opposing heats, that strikes  
the forest and, holding nothing back,

rips the branches off, beats them down, sweeps them away,  
and goes on in a proud cloud of dust,  
putting the wild beasts and the shepherds to flight.

He freed my eyes and said: "Now aim the line  
of your sight across that ancient scum,  
over there, where the smoke is thickest."

The way frogs, before the snake that hunts them,  
scatter every which way through the water  
until each one is hunched down in the mud,

I saw more than a thousand ruined souls  
fleeing before one who was crossing the Styx  
on foot, his soles never touching the water.

He fanned that greasy air away from his face,  
waving his left hand over and over in front of him,  
and only that disgust seemed to weary him.

I saw at once that he was sent from Heaven,  
and turned to my master, who made a sign  
that I should stand quiet and bow to him.

Ah, how full of scorn he seemed to me!  
He came to the gate, and with a little wand  
he opened it, and nothing held against him.

"O you cast out of Heaven, despised people,"  
he began on that hideous threshold,  
"where does this arrogance of yours come from?

Why do you kick against that will  
whose end can never be cut short,  
and which has many times piled on your pain?

What good does it do to butt against the fates?  
Your own Cerberus, if you remember,  
still carries his chin and throat rubbed raw for it."

Then he turned back along the filthy road  
and said no word to us, but wore the look  
of a man gripped and driven by another care

than the one of whoever stands before him.  
And we set our feet toward the city,  
sure of ourselves now after those holy words.

We went in without any struggle,  
and I, who was eager to see  
what such a fortress holds inside,

once I was in, threw my eyes all around me,  
and saw on every side a wide plain,  
full of suffering and terrible torment.

The way it is at Arles, where the Rhone goes still,  
or at Pola, near the Quarnaro gulf  
that closes Italy in and bathes its edges,

where the tombs make the whole ground uneven —  
so it was here, on every side,  
except the manner of it was far more bitter,

for flames were scattered in among the tombs,  
and they were heated so completely by them  
that no craft needs iron made any hotter.

All of their lids had been lifted off,  
and out of them came such grievous laments  
they sounded like the truly wretched and tortured.

And I said: "Master, who are all these people  
buried inside these vaults, who make themselves  
heard by their mournful sighing?"

And he to me: "Here are the arch-heretics,  
with their followers from every sect, and the tombs  
are loaded far more full than you would think.

Like is buried here together with like,  
and the monuments burn hotter and less hot."  
And then he turned to the right, and we passed

between the torments and the high parapets.
