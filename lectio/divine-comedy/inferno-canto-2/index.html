The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto II

Inferno: Canto II2 of 100

The day was leaving, and the darkening air
was letting the creatures of the earth
rest from their labors; I alone

was making myself ready for the struggle —
the hard road and the suffering both —
which memory, which does not fail, will trace again.

O Muses, O high genius, help me now.
O memory, that wrote down what I saw,
here your true worth will show.

I began: "Poet, you who guide me,
look at my courage — is it enough,
before you trust me to this brutal pass?

You tell how the father of Silvius,
while still in his mortal body,
went to the immortal world and stood there in the flesh.

But if the enemy of all evil
was gracious to him, thinking of the great effect
that would come of it — who he was, and what —

that doesn't seem unfitting to a thinking man,
for he was chosen, in the highest heaven,
to be the father of great Rome and of her empire;

and that empire, if we want the truth,
was founded as the holy place
where the heir of greatest Peter sits.

On this journey you give him such credit for,
he heard the things that were the cause
both of his victory and of the papal robe.

Later the Chosen Vessel went there too,
to bring back proof for that Faith
which is the first step on salvation's road.

But I — why would I go? Who grants it?
I am not Aeneas, I am not Paul.
Neither I nor anyone thinks me worthy of it.

So if I give myself up to this coming,
I'm afraid the coming may be folly.
You are wise; you understand better than I can say."

And like a man who unwills what he willed,
and on second thought changes his intention
so that he backs out of it completely,

that's what I became on that dark slope:
by thinking I burned up the whole undertaking
that had been so quick to start.

"If I have understood your words rightly,"
answered that shade of the great soul,
"your spirit is touched with cowardice,

which often weighs a man down so
it turns him back from a worthy enterprise,
the way a trick of the eye spooks a shying beast.

To free you from this fear,
I'll tell you why I came, and what I heard
the first moment I felt pity for you.

I was among those who hang in suspense
when a lady called to me, so fair, so holy
that I begged her to command me.

Her eyes shone brighter than the stars,
and she began, gentle and low,
in an angel's voice, in her own tongue:

'O courteous spirit of Mantua,
whose fame still lasts in the world
and will last as long as the world goes on,

a friend of mine — not a friend of fortune —
is blocked on the empty hillside
so badly that he has turned back in terror,

and may already be so lost, I fear,
that I have risen too late to help him,
from what I've heard of him in Heaven.

Go now, and with your polished speech
and whatever else he needs to get free,
help him, so that I am consoled.

I am Beatrice, who send you on this.
I come from the place I long to return to.
Love moved me, and makes me speak.

When I stand again before my Lord,
I'll praise you to him often.'
Then she fell silent, and I began:

'O lady of virtue, you alone
through whom the human race rises above
all that the smallest of the heavens holds,

your command pleases me so much
that to obey, even if already done, would feel late.
You need say nothing more of what you want.

But tell me why you don't shrink
from coming down here into this center
from the wide place you burn to go back to.'

'Since you so deeply want to understand,
I'll tell you briefly,' she answered me,
'why I'm not afraid to enter here.

The only things one ought to fear
are those that have the power to harm.
The rest, no — they are nothing to dread.

God in his mercy made me such
that your misery does not touch me,
nor any flame of this burning reach me.

A gentle lady is in Heaven who grieves
at this hard place I'm sending you to,
so that strict judgment up above is bent.

She called Lucia and made her plea:
"Your faithful one is in need of you now,
and I commend him to your care."

Lucia, the enemy of all that's cruel,
rose and came to where I sat
with ancient Rachel at my side.

"Beatrice," she said, "true praise of God,
why don't you help the man who loved you so
that for you he left the common crowd?

Don't you hear how pitiful his weeping is?
Don't you see the death that fights him
beside a flood the sea itself can't boast of?"

No one in the world ever moved so fast
to seize their good or flee their harm
as I did, after those words were spoken.

I came down here from my blessed seat,
trusting in your honest, dignified speech,
which honors you and all who've listened to it.'

After she had spoken to me this way,
weeping, she turned her shining eyes aside,
and that made me all the quicker to come.

And so I came to you, as she wished.
I took you out of the path of that wild beast
that blocked the short climb up the lovely mountain.

So what is it? Why, why do you hold back?
Why let such cowardice nest in your heart?
Why don't you have the daring and the nerve,

when three such blessed ladies
are caring for you in the court of Heaven,
and my own words promise you so much good?"

Like little flowers, bent down and shut
by the night's chill, that lift themselves
all open on their stems once the sun whitens them,

that's what I became, with my worn-out strength,
and so much courage poured into my heart
that I began, like a man set free of fear:

"O she of such compassion, who came to my aid,
and you, so gracious, who obeyed at once
the words of truth she gave you —

you have set my heart so on fire
for the journey, with these words of yours,
that I've come back to my first resolve.

Go now — one single will moves both of us.
You are my leader, my lord, my master."
So I told him; and when he started off,

I entered on the deep and savage way.
