# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXXIII

He lifted his mouth from its savage meal,  
that sinner, and wiped it clean on the hair  
of the very head he had been gnawing from behind.

Then he began: "You want me to take up again  
a grief so desperate it crushes my heart  
merely to think of, before I even speak.

But if my words can be a seed to bear  
fruit of disgrace for the traitor I devour,  
you'll see me speak and weep in the same breath.

I don't know who you are, or by what way  
you've come down here — but the sound of you,  
when I hear it, tells me you are Florentine.

You should know I was Count Ugolino,  
and this man here was Archbishop Ruggieri.  
Now I'll tell you why I am his neighbor like this.

That through the working of his scheming mind  
I trusted him, was taken, and then killed —  
there's no need to say. You already know it.

But what you cannot have heard — how cruel  
my death was — that you're going to hear from me,  
and then you'll judge whether he wronged me.

A narrow slit high in the prison cell —  
the one that now, because of me, is called  
the Tower of Hunger, and will cage others yet —

had shown me, through its opening, many moons  
go by, when I dreamed the evil dream  
that tore the veil away from what would come.

This man rose in it as lord and master,  
hunting the wolf and the wolf-cubs up the slope  
that blocks the Pisans' view of Lucca's hills.

With lean hounds, trained and ravenous and quick,  
Gualandi, Sismondi, and Lanfranchi  
had been sent out ahead to lead the chase.

After a short run, both the father and sons  
seemed to me worn out, and I thought I saw  
sharp tusks go in and rip their flanks wide open.

When I woke before the morning came,  
I heard my sons, who were locked in there with me,  
moaning in their sleep, crying out for bread.

You're cruel indeed if you don't grieve already,  
thinking what my heart was warning me of —  
and if you don't weep now, what makes you weep?

They were awake by then, and the hour neared  
when our food was usually brought to us,  
and each of us, from his dream, was full of dread.

And down below I heard them nailing shut  
the door of that horrible tower. I said nothing.  
I only looked into my children's faces.

I did not weep — inside I turned to stone.  
They wept; and my little Anselm said to me,  
'Father, you stare so. What is wrong with you?'

Still I shed no tear, and I made no answer  
all that day, and all the night that followed,  
until the next sun rose upon the world.

When a faint thread of light had worked its way  
into that prison of grief, and I made out  
my own face looking back from all four faces,

I bit down on both my hands in my despair;  
and they, thinking I did it out of hunger,  
out of the need to eat, stood up at once

and said: 'Father, it would grieve us less  
if you would eat of us. You dressed us once  
in this poor flesh — now take it off again.'

I steadied myself then, not to grieve them more.  
That day we all stayed silent, and the next.  
Ah, hard earth — why did you not open up?

And when we had come to the fourth day, Gaddo  
threw himself stretched out flat before my feet,  
saying, 'My father, why won't you help me?'

And there he died; and just as you see me,  
I watched the three fall, one by one, between  
the fifth day and the sixth. By then I'd gone

blind, and I took to groping over each,  
and for three days I called them, though they were dead.  
Then fasting did what grief had failed to do."

When he had said this, with his eyes wrenched wide,  
he took the wretched skull back in his teeth,  
which, like a dog's, bit hard against the bone.

Ah, Pisa — shame of all the peoples  
of that fair country where the word for yes is sì —  
since your neighbors are slow to bring you down,

let Capraia and Gorgona shift and come  
and build a dam across the mouth of the Arno,  
so every last soul in you is drowned!

For even if Count Ugolino was said  
to have sold out your fortresses and betrayed you,  
you had no right to rack his sons that way.

Their youth made them innocent, you new Thebes —  
Uguccione and Brigata, and the two  
my song has already named above.

We went on farther, to where the ice  
wraps yet another people in its grip,  
not bent face-down, but every one turned back.

There the weeping itself won't let them weep:  
the grief that hits a wall behind the eyes  
turns back inward and feeds the anguish more,

because the first tears freeze into a knot,  
and, like a visor made of solid crystal,  
fill the whole hollow underneath the brow.

And even though, the way a callus does,  
all feeling had, because of the cold,  
abandoned its post and left my face,

I thought I still could feel a little wind,  
and said: "My master, what is moving this?  
Isn't every warm breath dead down here?"

And he to me: "Soon you'll be where your eye  
itself will give you the answer to that,  
when you see the source that rains the gusts down."

And one of the wretches in the frozen crust  
cried out to us: "O souls so pitiless  
you've been assigned the last and lowest place,

lift these stiff veils off my eyes, and let me  
vent a little of the sorrow choking my heart  
before the tears freeze hard across them again."

So I said: "If you want me to relieve you,  
tell me who you are; and if I don't free you,  
may I have to go to the bottom of the ice."

Then he answered: "I am Friar Alberigo.  
I'm the one who served the fruit of the bad garden,  
and here I'm paid back a date for every fig."

"Oh," I said to him, "are you dead already?"  
And he: "How my body is faring up there  
in the world above, I have no idea.

This Ptolomea has a privilege:  
often enough the soul comes plunging here  
before Atropos has cut it loose.

And so you'll be the readier to scrape  
these glassy tears from off my face, know this:  
the moment a soul commits a betrayal

the way I did, a demon takes its body  
and runs it from then on, governing it  
until its full span of years has spun out.

The soul drops down into a pit like this,  
and maybe the body of the shade behind me,  
who winters here, still walks the world above.

You ought to know him, if you've just come down:  
he is Ser Branca d'Oria — and many years  
have gone by since he was sealed in here like this."

"I think," I said to him, "you're deceiving me.  
Branca d'Oria isn't dead at all —  
he eats, and drinks, and sleeps, and puts on clothes."

"Up in the ditch of the Malebranche," he said,  
"where the sticky pitch is kept forever boiling,  
Michel Zanche had not yet even arrived

when this one left a devil in his place,  
inside his own body — and a kinsman did the same,  
the one who carried out the betrayal with him.

But reach your hand out to me now, and open  
my eyes for me" — and I did not open them,  
and to be rude to him was courtesy.

Ah, Genoese — you people set against  
every virtue, brimming with every vice —  
why haven't you been swept clean off the earth?

For with the foulest spirit of Romagna  
I found one of yours whose deeds have plunged  
his soul already to bathe in the Cocytus,  
while up above his body still seems alive.
