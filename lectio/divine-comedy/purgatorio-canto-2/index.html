The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto II

Purgatorio: Canto II36 of 100

Already the sun had come down to the rim
of the sky whose noon arc stands highest
over Jerusalem, its very crown,

and night, that wheels around opposite the sun,
was rising out of the Ganges with the Scales,
the ones that drop from her hand when the dark grows long;

so that there where I stood, the white and the rose
in lovely Aurora's cheeks
were deepening, with too much age, to orange.

We were still there on the edge of the sea,
like people turning their road over in their minds,
who go in heart while the body stays behind.

And look — as Mars, low in the west
at the first touch of morning, burns red
through the thick vapors above the floor of the sea,

so there appeared to me — and may I see it again! —
a light coming so fast across the water
that no wing in flight could match its speed.

When I had drawn my eyes back from it a moment
to put a question to my guide,
I looked again and it had grown brighter and larger.

Then on either side of it I made out
I could not tell what whiteness, and below
little by little a second whiteness came.

My master had not yet said a word
while the first white unfolded into wings;
but once he clearly knew the pilot for who he was,

he cried: "Down, down on your knees, quickly —
behold the Angel of God! Fold your hands.
From now on you will see such officers as this.

See how he wants nothing of human means —
no oar, no other sail than his own wings
to carry him between shores so far apart.

See how he holds them pointed up to heaven,
fanning the air with his eternal feathers
that do not molt away like mortal hair."

Then, as the Bird of God came nearer
and nearer still, he shone the brighter,
so that up close my eyes could not hold him,

and I cast them down. He came to the shore
in a little boat so swift and light
the water took in none of it at all.

On the stern stood the heavenly pilot,
blessedness itself written on his face,
and more than a hundred souls were seated within.

"In exitu Israel de Aegypto,"
they all sang together, in one voice,
with everything that psalm sets down thereafter.

Then he traced the holy cross above them,
and they flung themselves, every one, onto the strand,
and he was gone, as swiftly as he had come.

The crowd left standing there seemed strangers
to the place, looking all around them,
like someone testing out new things.

On every side the day was breaking now —
the sun, with his bright shafts, had chased
the Capricorn down out of the high middle sky —

when the new people lifted their faces toward us
and said: "If you know it, show us the way
to go up the mountain."

And Virgil answered: "Perhaps you think
we know our way about this place;
but we are strangers here, the same as you.

We came only a little while before you,
by another road, so rough and so steep
that the climb ahead will feel like sport to us."

The souls, who had grasped from watching me breathe
that I was still among the living,
turned pale with astonishment,

and just as a crowd presses in to hear the news
from the messenger who carries the olive branch,
and no one is shy of the crush,

so all those fortunate spirits stood fixed
at the sight of me, every one of them,
as if they had forgotten to go and be made fair.

I saw one of them come forward
with such great longing to embrace me
that it moved me to do the same.

O empty shadows — solid only to the eye!
Three times I clasped my hands behind him,
and three times brought them back against my chest.

Wonder, I think, was painted on my face,
at which the shade smiled and drew back,
and I, chasing it, pressed on ahead.

Gently it told me I should stop;
then I knew who it was, and I begged him
to pause a while and speak with me.

He answered: "As I loved you in the body
that I wore, so I love you now, set free.
So I will stop — but where are you going?"

"Casella, my own friend — to come back
once more to where I am, I make this journey,"
I said. "But you — how was so much time taken from you?"

And he to me: "No wrong has been done me,
if the one who takes whom he wants, and when,
has more than once refused me this crossing.

His will is shaped by a just will.
For three months now, in truth, he has taken
whoever wished to come aboard in peace.

So I, who had just turned to that shore
where the waters of the Tiber go salt,
was kindly gathered in by him.

His wing is pointed now back to that river-mouth,
because that is where they always assemble —
all who do not go down toward Acheron."

And I: "If no new law has stripped from you
the memory or the gift of that song of love
that used to quiet every longing in me,

let it please you to comfort with it a little
this soul of mine, that, coming all this way
clothed in its body, is so worn with grief."

"Love that discourses in my mind" —
so he began, and so sweetly
that the sweetness sounds in me still.

My master, and I, and all that company
who were with him seemed as content
as if nothing else could touch the mind of any of us.

We stood there, all of us, still and intent
upon his notes — and look: the grave old man,
crying out, "What is this, you laggard souls?

What is this carelessness, this standing around?
Run to the mountain and strip off the husk
that keeps God from showing himself to you!"

As doves, gathered together at their feeding,
gleaning the grain or the chaff,
quiet, none of them showing their usual strut,

will all at once leave their food behind
if something appears that frightens them,
seized by a heavier care than hunger —

so I watched that newly come company
break off the song and head for the slope,
like people going who don't know where;
nor was our own leaving any less in haste.
