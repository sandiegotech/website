The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XXVI

Purgatorio: Canto XXVI60 of 100

While we went on this way, one ahead of the other
along the brink, again and again my good Master
warned me: "Take care. Let it be enough that I warn you."

The sun was striking me now on the right shoulder,
and its rays had already begun to wash the whole west
from blue to a pale, even white.

And with my shadow I made the flame
look redder where it fell; and even at so faint a sign
I saw many of the shades, as they walked, take notice.

That was what first set them talking of me,
and they began to say among themselves:
"That looks like no make-believe body."

Then some of them came toward me, as near as they dared,
always careful never to step out
to where they would no longer be in the burning.

"O you who walk behind the others —
not from being slower, perhaps, but out of respect —
answer me, who burn here in thirst and fire.

And it is not only I who need your answer:
all of these thirst for it more
than an Ethiopian or an Indian thirsts for cold water.

Tell us how it is that you make yourself
a wall against the sun, as though
you had not yet stepped into the net of death."

So one of them spoke to me, and I would have shown them
at once what I was, had I not been caught
just then by another strange sight.

For up the middle of that burning road
came a second people, moving face to face against these,
and the wonder of it held me staring.

There I saw the shades on either side
hurrying to meet, each kissing the other,
not pausing, content with a quick greeting —

the way, within their dark brown columns,
one ant will touch its face to another's,
maybe to ask the road, or how the foraging goes.

No sooner is the friendly greeting over,
before the first step carries them apart,
than each strains to out-shout the other:

the new-come crowd cries, "Sodom and Gomorrah!"
and the rest, "Into the cow climbs Pasiphaë,
so the bull will come running to her lust."

Then, like cranes that might fly off in two flights —
part toward the Riphaean mountains, part toward the sands,
these shy of the frost, those shy of the sun —

one company goes and the other comes,
and weeping they return to their first songs
and to the cry that fits each best;

and the same ones who had begged me before
drew close to me again, just as they had,
their faces set to listen.

I, who had twice now seen what they longed for,
began: "O souls assured of reaching,
whenever it comes, a state of peace,

my body has not been left behind on earth,
neither unripe nor ripe with age; it is here with me,
with its own blood and its own joints.

I am going up to be blind no longer.
There is a Lady above who wins me this grace,
and by it I carry my mortal self through your world.

But — and may your greatest longing soon
be filled, so that the Heaven may house you
that is brimming with love and reaches widest —

tell me, so I can write it again in my books,
who you are, and who that crowd is
that goes its way behind your backs."

No more dumbstruck, no more lost for words,
is the mountain man, gone rough and silent,
when he first comes down into the town,

than every shade looked then.
But once they shook off their amazement —
which in high hearts is quickly quieted —

"Blessed are you," said the one who had questioned me first,
starting again, "who, so as to die better,
take on board the knowledge of our borderlands.

The people who do not come with us sinned
in the way for which Caesar, in his triumph,
once heard himself jeered at as 'Queen.'

So they go off crying 'Sodom!' against themselves,
shaming themselves, just as you heard,
and by that shame they feed their fire.

Our own sin was between man and woman;
but because we did not keep human law,
and chased our appetite the way beasts do,

to our own disgrace, as we part,
we call out the name of her
who made herself a beast in the wooden beast.

Now you know our doings, and what our sin was.
If you would like to know us each by name,
there is no time to tell, and I could not anyway.

But mine I will grant you, since you wish it:
I am Guido Guinizelli, and I am cleansed already,
having repented before the final hour."

As, in the grief of Lycurgus, two sons
turned at the sight of their mother again —
so I turned, though I do not rise to such a height,

the moment I heard him name himself,
the father of me and of all my betters
who ever wrote the sweet and graceful rhymes of love;

and without hearing or speaking I went on
a long while, lost in thought, gazing at him —
though for the fire I came no nearer.

When I had fed myself with looking, I offered
myself, entirely, ready to serve him,
with the kind of vow that makes a man believed.

And he to me: "What I hear from you
leaves a mark in me so deep and clear
that Lethe cannot wash it out or blur it.

But if your words just now have sworn the truth,
tell me why it is that your face and your speech
show you hold me so dear."

And I to him: "It is your own sweet songs,
which, as long as this modern way of writing lasts,
will make even their ink precious forever."

"O brother," he said, "this one I single out" —
and he pointed to a spirit just ahead —
"was the better smith of the mother tongue.

In verses of love and tales of romance
he surpassed them all; and let the fools talk on
who think the man of Limoges outdid him.

They turn their faces toward noise more than truth,
and settle their opinion that way,
before they have listened to art or reason.

So the old crowd did with Guittone,
passing the praise from mouth to mouth,
till the truth at last won out with most of them.

Now, if you have so wide a privilege
that you are let into the cloister
where Christ is abbot of the company,

say one Our Father there for me —
as much of it as we still need in this world,
where the power to sin is no longer ours."

Then, perhaps to make room for another
close behind him, he vanished into the fire
the way a fish goes down through water.

I moved a little toward the one he had pointed out
and told him my desire was making ready
an honored place for his name.

Freely he began to speak:
"Tan m'abellis vostre cortes deman,
qu'ieu no me puesc ni voill a vos cobrire.

Ieu sui Arnaut, que plor e vau cantan;
consiros vei la passada folor,
e vei jausen lo joi qu'esper, denan.

Ara vos prec, per aquella valor
que vos guida al som de l'escalina,
sovenha vos a temps de ma dolor!"

Then he hid himself in the fire that refines them.
