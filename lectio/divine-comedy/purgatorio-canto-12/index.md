# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XII

Side by side, like a pair of oxen yoked,  
I walked on with that burdened soul,  
as long as my gentle teacher would allow.

But when he said, "Leave him now, and go ahead —  
for here it's best that each of us drive his boat  
with all the sail and oar that he can muster,"

I drew myself upright again, the way  
walking requires, though all the while  
my thoughts stayed bowed and shamed within me.

I had moved on, glad to follow  
in my Master's footsteps, and the two of us  
were already showing how light our feet had grown,

when he said to me: "Look down.  
It will do you good, to ease the road,  
to study the bed beneath your feet."

The way the tombs above the buried dead  
bear carved upon them what they were in life,  
so that some memory of them may remain —

the kind of thing that makes us weep for them again,  
stung by the prick of remembrance, which spurs on  
only those whose hearts are tender —

so I saw figures here, but cut with far  
finer craft, covering the whole  
broad path that juts out from the mountainside.

I saw the one created nobler  
than any other creature, falling out of heaven  
on one side, streaking down like lightning.

I saw Briareus on the other side,  
struck through by the bolt from heaven,  
lying heavy on the ground in the cold of death.

I saw Thymbraeus, I saw Pallas and Mars,  
still armed and ranged around their father,  
staring at the scattered limbs of the Giants.

I saw Nimrod at the foot of his great work,  
looking lost, gazing at the people  
who had been proud with him in Shinar.

O Niobe, with what grieving eyes  
I saw you carved along that road,  
between your seven and seven children, slain!

O Saul, how you appeared there, fallen  
on your own sword, lifeless on Gilboa,  
which never felt the rain or dew again!

O reckless Arachne, I saw you there,  
already half a spider, mourning on the shreds  
of the work you wove in an evil hour for yourself!

O Rehoboam, your likeness here  
no longer seems to threaten; full of terror,  
a chariot bears it off, with no one in pursuit!

It showed, on that hard pavement, how Alcmaeon  
made his mother pay so dearly  
for the ill-starred necklace she wore.

It showed how his own sons fell  
upon Sennacherib inside the temple,  
and how they left him lying there, dead.

It showed the ruin and the cruel slaughter  
Tomyris dealt, when she said to Cyrus:  
"You thirsted for blood — now drink your fill of it."

It showed how the Assyrians fled, routed,  
once Holofernes had been killed,  
and showed what was left of that bloody field.

I saw Troy there, in ashes and in caves —  
O Ilium, how low, how broken down  
the carving made you, there for all to see!

What master of the brush or stylus ever  
could draw those shades and lines, which there  
would make the finest mind stop and marvel?

The dead looked dead, the living looked alive;  
no one who saw the real thing saw it better  
than I did, walking bowed across that ground.

Go on then, swell with pride, lift up your faces,  
you children of Eve, and never look down  
long enough to see the evil road you're on!

We had circled more of the mountain by now,  
and the sun had run far more of its course,  
than my preoccupied mind had guessed,

when the one who walked ahead, always alert,  
began: "Lift up your head. The time  
for going lost in thought is over.

Look — an angel, coming toward us,  
hurrying; and look, the sixth handmaid  
is returning from her day's service.

Grace your face and bearing with respect,  
so that he'll be glad to send us up;  
remember — this day will never dawn again."

I knew well his warning never to waste time,  
so on that subject, at least, he couldn't  
have spoken to me in any hidden way.

The beautiful being came toward us,  
dressed in white, his face  
bright as the trembling morning star.

He spread his arms, then spread his wings.  
"Come," he said, "the steps are close at hand,  
and from here on the climbing is easy."

Few are the ones who answer that call!  
O human creatures, born to fly upward,  
why do you fall at the slightest breath of wind?

He led us to where the rock was cleft.  
There he brushed my forehead with his wings,  
then promised me a safe way on.

The way the climb up to the church that lords it  
over the well-governed town, above Rubaconte,  
is broken on the right by built-in stairs —

stairs cut in the age when the public ledger  
and the measuring-stave were still kept honest,  
breaking the sheer steepness of the rise —

just so, the bank is eased that drops  
straight down from the second terrace here,  
though the tall rock grazes you on either side.

And as we turned ourselves that way,  
voices were singing "Beati pauperes spiritu" —  
singing it past all power of words to tell.

How different these gateways are  
from the ones in Hell! Here you enter to singing,  
and down there to nothing but wild lament.

Now we were climbing the holy stairs,  
and it seemed to me far easier going  
than it had felt before, down on the level.

So I asked: "Master, tell me — what heavy thing  
has been lifted off me? I feel almost  
no effort now in walking at all."

He answered: "When the P's still left  
faintly marked upon your face  
are wiped clean, as the first one already is,

your feet will be so mastered by good will  
that not only will they feel no weariness —  
being driven upward will become a joy."

Then I did what people do who walk  
with something on their head they don't know about,  
until another's signs make them suspect it,

and the hand reaches up to check, and searches,  
and finds, and does the work the eyes  
can't manage on their own;

and with the fingers of my right hand spread,  
I found only six of the letters left  
that the keeper of the keys had carved into my brow —

and my Leader, seeing it, smiled.
