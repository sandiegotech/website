# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XVI

By now I had come to where I could hear the roar  
of the water falling into the next circle down,  
a sound like the hum the bees make in their hives,

when three shades together broke from the troop  
and came running out from a band that was passing  
beneath the rain of that harsh torment.

They came toward us, and each of them cried out:  
"Stop where you are. By the way you're dressed  
you look to us like one from our twisted city."

Ah, the wounds I saw on their bodies —  
fresh ones and old, all seared in by the flames.  
It still hurts me just to remember it.

My teacher paused, attentive to their cries.  
He turned his face to me and said, "Now wait.  
With these we ought to show some courtesy.

And if it weren't for the fire that this place  
shoots out by its very nature, I would say  
that haste better suited you than them."

As soon as we stood still, they took up again  
their old lament, and once they reached us  
the three of them wheeled into a turning ring.

The way wrestlers do, stripped down and oiled,  
eyeing for their grip and their advantage  
before they close in with blows and thrusts,

so, wheeling, each of them kept his face  
turned toward me, so that his neck  
ran one way while his feet ran the other.

"If the misery of this soft, sinking sand  
brings us and our pleading into contempt,"  
one of them began, "and our scorched, peeled look,

let our renown be reason enough for you  
to tell us who you are, who walk through Hell  
on living feet, and so unafraid.

This man, whose tracks you see me treading in,  
for all that he goes naked now and flayed,  
stood higher in his day than you would think.

He was the grandson of the good Gualdrada.  
Guido Guerra was his name, and in his life  
he did great work with his wisdom and his sword.

The other, who treads the sand beside me,  
is Tegghiaio Aldobrandi, whose voice  
should have been welcome in the world above.

And I, set on the cross here with them,  
was Jacopo Rusticucci; and it's true  
my fierce wife harmed me more than anything."

If I'd had any cover from the fire,  
I would have thrown myself down among them,  
and I think my teacher would have let me;

but since I'd have been scorched and baked alive,  
fear won out over the good will  
that made me hungry to take them in my arms.

Then I began: "It was not contempt but grief  
your condition fixed so deep inside me  
that it will be slow, all of it, to lift —

the moment this lord of mine spoke words  
that made me think to myself that people  
such as you might be drawing near.

I am from your city. Your deeds, your honored names —  
always, with affection, I have traced them  
over again and listened to them told.

I'm leaving the bitter gall for the sweet fruit  
promised me by my truthful guide;  
but first I must drop down to the center."

"So may your soul keep guiding those limbs  
of yours a long while yet," he answered then,  
"and so may your fame shine on after you —

tell us if courtesy and valor still  
live on in our city as they used to,  
or if they've gone clean out of it;

for Guglielmo Borsiere, who has been  
in torment with us only a short time  
and walks there with our band, torments us with his news."

"The newcomers and the quick, easy money  
have bred pride and excess in you,  
Florence, and already you weep for it!"

So I cried out, with my face lifted high.  
And the three of them, taking that for an answer,  
looked at each other the way men do at the truth.

"If it costs you so little, every time,  
to satisfy another the way you have,"  
they all replied, "happy are you, to speak your mind so freely.

So, if you escape these dark places  
and come back to look on the lovely stars,  
when it pleases you to say 'I was down there,'

see that you speak of us to the people."  
Then they broke the ring, and as they fled  
their quick legs seemed to turn to wings.

You could not have said one Amen  
as fast as they had vanished;  
so my master thought it best to move on.

I followed him, and we'd gone only a little way  
before the sound of the water was so close  
we could barely have heard ourselves speak.

Like that river which holds its own course  
first from Monte Viso toward the east,  
down the left-hand slope of the Apennines —

the one called Acquacheta higher up,  
before it drops into its lower bed  
and loses that name at Forlì —

which thunders there above San Benedetto  
of the Alps, coming down in a single leap  
where there should have been room for a thousand;

so, down from a sheer cliff, we found  
that dark-stained water crashing,  
so loud it would soon have hurt the ear.

I had a cord wound around my waist,  
and with it I had once thought  
to catch the leopard with the painted hide.

After I'd unwound it all from me,  
just as my guide had commanded,  
I handed it over to him, knotted and coiled.

At that he turned to his right side,  
and a little out from the edge,  
he flung it down into that deep gorge.

"Surely some new thing must answer,"  
I said to myself, "this new signal  
the master's eye is following so closely."

Ah, how careful a man should be  
around those who don't just see what he does  
but look with their minds clear into his thoughts.

He said to me: "Soon there will rise up  
what I'm waiting for, and what your thought is dreaming  
will soon have to come clear before your eyes."

To a truth that wears the face of a lie  
a man should keep his lips shut as long as he can,  
because through no fault of his it brings him shame;

but here I can't keep silent. And, reader,  
by the lines of this Comedy I swear to you —  
so may they not run dry of lasting favor —

that through that thick, dark air I saw  
a figure come swimming upward,  
a thing to amaze the steadiest heart,

the way a man comes back who has gone down  
now and then to free an anchor snagged  
on a reef or something else hidden in the sea —

reaching upward, drawing in his feet.
