# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto I

To run across better waters now  
the little boat of my genius hoists its sail,  
leaving behind a sea so cruel.

And I will sing of that second kingdom,  
where the human spirit cleanses itself  
and grows worthy to climb to heaven.

But here let dead poetry rise again,  
O holy Muses, since I am yours,  
and here let Calliope lift her voice a little,

carrying my song with that same sound  
whose blow the wretched magpies felt  
so hard they lost all hope of pardon.

Sweet color of an eastern sapphire,  
gathering in the calm clear face  
of the pure air as far as the first circle,

brought delight back to my eyes  
the moment I came out of the dead air  
that had filled my eyes and chest with grief.

The fair planet that calls us to love  
was setting all the east to laughing,  
veiling the Fishes that rode in her escort.

I turned to the right and fixed my mind  
on the other pole, and saw four stars  
seen by no one since the first people.

The sky seemed to rejoice in their small flames.  
O widowed northern country,  
denied the sight of these!

When I had drawn my eyes away from them,  
turning a little toward the other pole,  
there where the Wain had already gone,

I saw beside me an old man, alone,  
so worthy of reverence in his look  
that no son owes his father more.

He wore a long beard streaked with white,  
like the hair upon his head,  
which fell in two folds across his chest.

The rays of the four holy stars  
so lit his face with light  
that I saw him as if the sun stood before him.

"Who are you, you who against the blind river  
have fled the eternal prison?"  
he said, stirring those venerable feathers.

"Who guided you? What lamp lit your way  
as you came up out of the deep night  
that keeps the valley of hell forever black?

Are the laws of the abyss broken like this?  
Or has some new decree changed heaven,  
that the damned should come to my cliffs?"

Then my Leader took hold of me,  
and with his words, his hands, his signs,  
made me bow my knees and brow in reverence.

Then he answered: "I did not come on my own.  
A Lady came down from heaven, and at her prayers  
I helped this man with my company.

But since it is your will that more be told  
of how our true condition stands,  
it cannot be my will to keep it from you.

This man has not yet seen his last evening,  
but by his folly came so near it  
that little time was left to turn him back.

As I have said, I was sent to him  
to rescue him, and there was no other road  
than this one I have taken.

I have shown him all the people of perdition,  
and now I mean to show him those spirits  
who cleanse themselves under your charge.

How I brought him here would take too long;  
a power from on high comes down to help me  
lead him to see you and to hear you.

Now may it please you to welcome his coming.  
He goes in search of freedom, which is precious,  
as anyone knows who gives up life for it.

You know it, since for her sake death in Utica  
was not bitter to you, where you left behind  
the garment that will shine so bright on the great day.

The eternal edicts are not broken by us;  
this man lives, and Minos does not bind me —  
I come from the circle where the chaste eyes

of your Marcia are, who in her looks  
still prays you, O holy heart, to hold her as your own.  
For her love, then, bend toward us.

Let us pass through your seven realms.  
I will carry word of this grace back to her,  
if you will let yourself be named down there."

"Marcia was so pleasing to my eyes  
while I was on the other side," he said,  
"that every grace she asked of me I gave.

Now that she dwells beyond the evil river,  
she can move me no longer, by the law  
that was made when I came out of there.

But if a Lady of Heaven moves and rules you,  
as you say, there is no need to flatter me.  
It is enough that you ask in her name.

Go, then, and gird this man with a smooth rush,  
and wash his face, so that you cleanse away  
every stain that clings to it.

It would not be fitting that the eye,  
clouded by any mist, should come before  
the first angel, who is one of those in Paradise.

This little island, all around its base  
down there, where the waves beat against it,  
bears rushes in the soft, wet mud.

No other plant that puts out leaves  
or hardens its stem can live there,  
because it will not bend to the blows.

Do not come back this way afterward;  
the sun, now rising, will show you  
an easier path to take up the mountain."

With that he vanished, and I rose  
without a word and drew myself close  
to my Guide, and turned my eyes to him.

And he began: "Son, follow my steps.  
Let us turn back, for the plain slopes down  
from here toward its lower edges."

The dawn was overcoming the early hour  
that fled before it, so that from far off  
I could make out the trembling of the sea.

We went along the lonely plain  
like one returning to a lost road,  
who feels he walks in vain until he finds it.

When we came to where the dew  
fights with the sun, and, in a sheltered place,  
is slow to burn away,

my Master spread both his hands, gently,  
open upon the grass; and I,  
who understood what he meant to do,

lifted my tear-stained cheeks to him,  
and there he brought back fully into view  
the color hell had hidden in me.

Then we came down to the deserted shore  
that never saw a man sail its waters  
and afterward come back again.

There, as the other had asked, he girded me.  
And O the wonder — for the moment he plucked  
the humble plant, another sprang up at once,

the very same, there where he had torn it out.
