# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XXI

The natural thirst that nothing ever quiets  
except the water the poor Samaritan woman  
begged of Christ as a gift —

that thirst was working in me, and haste kept goading me  
along the crowded path behind my Leader,  
and I was grieving for that righteous punishment;

when, just as Luke records that Christ appeared  
to the two men walking on the road,  
already risen from the tomb's hollow,

a shade appeared to us, coming up behind,  
his eyes on the souls stretched out before us;  
we did not notice him until he spoke,

saying, "My brothers, may God give you peace."  
We turned at once, and Virgil answered him  
with the sign that fits such a greeting.

Then he began: "In the blessed assembly  
may the truthful court set you at peace —  
the court that binds me in unending exile."

"How is this?" he said, and we kept walking fast,  
"if you are shades God does not welcome above,  
who has led you so far up his stairs?"

And my Teacher said: "If you look at the marks  
this man bears, the ones the Angel traced,  
you will see plainly he belongs among the saved.

But since the one who spins both day and night  
had not yet drawn his thread off the distaff —  
the thread Clotho loads and packs for each of us —

his soul, which is your sister and my own,  
could not come up here on its own,  
because it does not see the way we see.

So I was drawn out of the wide throat of Hell  
to be his guide, and I will guide him on  
as far as my own school has power to lead.

But tell us, if you know, why the mountain  
shook so hard just now, and why every voice  
seemed to cry out as one, down to its wet base."

By asking that, he threaded the very eye  
of my own longing, so that with hope alone  
my thirst already felt a little less starved.

"There is nothing," he began, "that the holy order  
of this mountain can feel without a reason,  
nothing that falls outside its settled custom.

Up here it is free of every change:  
only what heaven takes into itself from itself  
can be the cause of anything, nothing else.

And so no rain, no hail, no snow,  
no dew, no frost falls any higher  
than the short little stairway of three steps.

No clouds appear, thick or thin,  
no lightning, and no daughter of Thaumas —  
the rainbow, that keeps shifting her place below;

no dry vapor rises any farther  
than the top of those three steps I spoke of,  
where the Vicar of Peter sets his feet.

Lower down it may tremble, a little or a lot;  
but from any wind hidden in the earth —  
how, I don't know — up here it has never shaken.

It trembles here only when some soul  
feels itself clean, so that it rises, or stirs  
to climb; and a great cry goes up with it.

The proof of that cleanness is the will alone,  
which, suddenly free to change its dwelling,  
takes the soul by surprise and lifts it to fly.

It wanted to rise before, but the longing forbade it —  
the longing that God's justice turns toward the pain,  
the same will that once turned toward the sin.

And I, who have lain in this suffering  
five hundred years and more, only now  
felt my will go free for a better place.

That is why you felt the earthquake, and why  
the faithful spirits across the mountain  
praised the Lord — may he soon send them upward."

So he told him; and since we take as much  
joy in the drinking as the thirst is deep,  
I couldn't say how much good it did me.

And the wise Leader said: "Now I can see the net  
that tangles you here, and how you slip it,  
why the ground shakes, and why you all rejoice.

Now, if you will, let me know who you were,  
and why you have lain here so many centuries —  
let me gather it from your own words."

"In the days when the good Titus, with the help  
of the highest King, avenged the wounds  
that let out the blood Judas had sold,

I lived on earth," that spirit answered,  
"under the name that lasts and honors most —  
widely known, but not yet a man of faith.

My gift for poetry was so sweet that Rome  
drew me to herself, a man from Toulouse,  
where I earned the myrtle for my brow.

Statius — the people back there still call me that.  
I sang of Thebes, and then of great Achilles,  
but I fell on the road under that second load.

The seeds of my fire were the sparks  
of that divine flame that warmed me,  
from which more than a thousand have been lit —

I mean the Aeneid; it was a mother to me,  
and a nurse to me in poetry; without it  
my work would not have weighed a single dram.

And to have lived on earth in the years  
Virgil lived, I would gladly serve  
one more sun in exile than my sentence asks."

These words made Virgil turn his eyes to me  
with a look that, saying nothing, said "Be still";  
but the will to control cannot do everything,

for laughter and tears follow so closely  
on the feeling that each one springs from  
that in the most truthful they least obey the will.

I only smiled, the way a man drops a hint;  
at which the shade fell silent and looked  
into my eyes, where the truest signs live,

and said, "So may you finish so great a labor —  
but tell me, why did your face just now  
show me the flash of a smile?"

Now I am caught on both sides at once:  
one holds me silent, the other begs me to speak;  
so I sigh, and my Master understands,

and says to me, "Don't be afraid to speak.  
Go ahead and tell him what he asks  
with such longing to know."

So I said: "Maybe you are wondering,  
ancient spirit, at the smile I let slip;  
but I mean to fill you with greater wonder.

This man who guides my eyes on high  
is that Virgil — the one from whom you drew  
the strength to sing of men and of the gods.

If you imagined any other cause for my smile,  
let it go as false, and trust instead  
those very words you spoke about him."

He was already stooping to embrace the feet  
of my Teacher, but Virgil said: "Brother,  
don't — you are a shade, and a shade is what you see."

And rising, he answered: "Now you can grasp  
how great the love is that warms me toward you,  
when I forget how empty we both are

and treat a shadow as a solid thing."
