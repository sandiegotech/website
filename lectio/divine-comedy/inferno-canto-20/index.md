# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XX

Now I must make my verses out of a new pain  
and give the matter for this twentieth canto  
of the first song, the one about the drowned.

I was already leaning out, intent  
on staring down into the open depth  
that washed itself with tears of agony.

And through the round of that valley I saw people  
coming, silent, weeping, at the pace  
a slow procession keeps in our own world.

As my eyes dropped lower along their bodies,  
each one seemed strangely wrenched and turned around  
between the chin and where the chest begins —

the face was twisted round toward the small of the back,  
and they had to walk along backward,  
since looking ahead had been taken from them.

Maybe the violence of a stroke has once  
twisted a man around like that, completely —  
but I never saw it, and I doubt it can be.

Reader — so God may let you take some good  
from what you read — judge for yourself now  
how I could keep my own face dry,

when I saw our human shape so close to me  
wrenched out of true, the tears the eyes wept  
running down the cleft to bathe the buttocks.

I wept, for certain, leaning on a spur  
of the hard rock, until my guide said to me,  
"Are you, too, like the other fools?

Here pity lives when it is wholly dead.  
Who is more of a criminal than the one  
who lets his feeling argue against God's judgment?

Lift your head up, lift it, and see the man  
for whom the earth split open before the Thebans,  
so that they all cried, 'Where are you rushing,

Amphiaraus? Why are you quitting the war?'  
And he kept falling, down and down the valley  
to Minos, who lays his hands on every soul.

Look — he has made a chest out of his shoulders.  
Because he wanted to see too far ahead,  
he looks behind him now and walks backward.

See Tiresias, who changed his very form  
when, out of a man, he turned into a woman,  
every part of his body transformed;

and afterward he had to strike once more  
the two entangled serpents with his staff  
before he got his manhood back again.

That one who backs against the other's belly  
is Aruns, who up in the hills of Luni —  
where the Carrarese who lives below works the ground —

made his home in a cave among the white  
marble, and from there his view of the stars  
and of the sea was never cut off from him.

And she there, covering her breasts — which you  
cannot see — with her loosened hair,  
and with all the hairy skin turned to that side,

was Manto, who searched through many lands  
and then settled where I myself was born;  
so let me tell you a little about that.

After her father had departed from life  
and the city of Bacchus had been enslaved,  
she wandered the world a long, long while.

Up in lovely Italy there lies a lake  
at the foot of the Alps that wall off Germany  
above the Tyrol — its name is Benaco.

A thousand springs and more, I think, between  
Garda and Val Camonica, bathe the Pennine ridge  
with water that comes to rest within that lake.

There's a place midway where the pastor of Trent,  
the one of Brescia, and the one of Verona  
could each give blessing, if his road ran there.

Peschiera sits there, a fine and strong stronghold  
to face the men of Brescia and Bergamo,  
at the point where the shore around it dips down lowest.

There everything must spill out that cannot  
be held in the lap of Benaco any longer,  
and it becomes a river through green pastures.

The moment the water starts to run, it is  
no longer called Benaco but the Mincio,  
as far as Governo, where it falls into the Po.

It does not run far before it finds a flat,  
where it spreads itself out and turns to marsh,  
and in summer it often grows foul and sickly.

Passing that way, the pitiless virgin saw  
land in the middle of the fen, untilled  
and bare of any people living there.

There, to keep clear of all human company,  
she stayed with her servants to practice her arts,  
and lived, and left her empty body there.

Afterward the men who were scattered around  
gathered together in that place, made strong  
by the swamp it had on every side of it;

they built their city over those dead bones,  
and for the one who first chose out the spot  
they named it Mantua, with no other omen.

Its people once were more crowded inside it,  
before the foolishness of Casalodi  
was taken in by Pinamonte's treachery.

So I warn you: if you ever hear my city's  
beginning told some other way than this,  
let no lie cheat the truth of what it is."

And I said, "Master, your words are so certain  
to me, and so completely hold my faith,  
that anything else would be burnt-out coals.

But tell me about these people going by —  
if you see any one of them worth noting,  
because that is the only thing my mind is on."

Then he said to me, "That one whose beard  
spreads from his cheeks across his dark brown shoulders  
was, back when Greece was so emptied of men

that there was hardly one left in the cradle,  
an augur; with Calchas he fixed the moment  
at Aulis to cut the first cable loose.

Eurypylus was his name, and that is how  
my high tragedy sings him, somewhere in it —  
you know that well, who know the whole of it.

The next, the one so thin across the flanks,  
was Michael Scot, who truly knew the game  
of magic and its sleights and illusions.

See Guido Bonatti; see Asdente, who now  
wishes he had stuck to his leather and his thread —  
but his regret has come too late.

See the wretched women who left the needle,  
the spool and the distaff, to tell fortunes;  
they worked their spells with herbs and waxen images.

But come now — Cain and his bundle of thorns  
already holds the line between both hemispheres  
and touches the wave below Seville;

and the moon was already full last night.  
You ought to remember that well — it did you  
no harm, more than once, in the deep dark wood."

He said this to me, and meanwhile we walked on.
