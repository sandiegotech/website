The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto I

Inferno: Canto I1 of 100

Midway along the journey of our life
I came to myself in a dark wood,
for the straight road had been lost.

How hard it is to say what it was like —
that wood, so wild and rough and unrelenting
that the fear comes back the moment I think of it.

It was so bitter that death is hardly worse.
But to tell of the good I found there too,
I'll speak of the other things I saw.

How I came to enter it I can't say clearly,
so heavy was I with sleep at the moment
I wandered off from the true way.

But once I had reached the foot of a hill,
there where the valley came to an end
that had pierced my heart with dread,

I looked up and saw its shoulders
already wrapped in that planet's rays
that lead us straight down every road.

Then the fear grew a little quieter
that had lasted through the lake of my heart
all that night I'd spent in such misery.

And like a man who, gasping for breath,
drags himself out of the sea onto the shore
and turns to stare back at the dangerous water,

so my mind, still racing in flight,
turned back to look again at that pass
no living person has ever left.

After I had rested my worn-out body
I took up the climb again on the empty slope,
my lower foot always the steady one.

And there, almost where the climb began,
a leopard — quick and very light on its feet,
its coat all covered in spots —

would not move out of my path.
It blocked my way so completely
that again and again I turned to go back.

It was the early hour of morning,
and the sun was climbing with the same stars
that rode beside it when Divine Love

first set those beautiful things in motion.
So the hour of the day and the sweet season
gave me good reason to hope

even against that beast with its bright pelt —
but not enough to keep me from fear
when a lion came into view.

It looked like it was coming straight at me,
head high, in a fury of hunger,
until the very air seemed afraid of it.

And a she-wolf, who in her gauntness
seemed loaded down with every craving
and has made so many live in grief —

she laid such a weight on me,
such terror came off the sight of her,
that I gave up all hope of the height.

And like a man who gladly piles up gains,
but when the time comes that he loses them
weeps and despairs in all his thoughts,

that restless beast did the same to me:
coming on, pushing me back step by step
toward the place where the sun is silent.

While I was sliding down toward the low ground,
before my eyes someone appeared
who seemed faint from long silence.

When I saw him there in the vast waste,
"Have pity on me," I cried out to him,
"whatever you are — a shade, or a real man."

He answered: "Not a man. I was one once.
My parents both came from Lombardy,
Mantuans, both of them, by birth.

I was born under Julius, though late in his day,
and lived at Rome under the good Augustus,
in the time of the false and lying gods.

I was a poet, and I sang of that just
son of Anchises who came out of Troy
after proud Ilion was burned to the ground.

But you — why turn back to all that misery?
Why not climb the mountain of delight,
the source and cause of every joy?"

"Then are you that Virgil, that fountain
that pours out so wide a river of speech?"
I answered him, my head bowed in shame.

"You, the honor and the light of all the other poets —
let the long study and the great love
that drove me to your book stand for me now.

You are my master, you are my author.
You alone are the one from whom I took
the noble style that has earned me my name.

Look at the beast that turned me back.
Save me from her, famous sage,
for she sets my veins and pulses shaking."

"You have to go another way,"
he answered, when he saw me weeping,
"if you want to escape this wild place,

because this beast that makes you cry out
lets no one pass along her road,
but harries him until she kills him.

Her nature is so cruel, so vicious,
that she never fills her greedy appetite —
after feeding she's hungrier than before.

Many are the animals she mates with,
and more there'll be, until the Greyhound comes
who will hunt her down to a painful death.

He won't feed on land or money,
but on wisdom, on love and courage,
and his birthplace will lie between felt and felt.

He'll be the salvation of that humble Italy
for which the maiden Camilla died,
and Euryalus, Turnus, and Nisus, of their wounds.

Through every city he'll drive her down,
until he's chased her back into Hell,
out of which envy first turned her loose.

And so, for your own good, I judge it best
that you follow me. I'll be your guide,
and lead you out of here through an eternal place,

where you'll hear the cries of the hopeless,
where you'll see the ancient spirits in their grief,
each one screaming for a second death.

And then you'll see those who are content
within the fire, because they hope to come,
whenever it may be, among the blessed.

And if you want to climb up to them after that,
a soul worthier than I am will be there for it;
I'll leave you with her when I go,

because that Emperor who reigns above —
since I rebelled against his law —
wills that none come through me into his city.

He governs everywhere, but he rules from there.
There is his city and his high throne.
Happy the one he chooses for it!"

And I said to him: "Poet, I beg you,
by that same God you never knew,
so that I escape this evil and worse,

lead me to the place you spoke of,
so I may see the gate of Saint Peter
and those you call so wretched."

Then he set out, and I followed behind.
