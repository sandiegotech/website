The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto III

Purgatorio: Canto III37 of 100

Their sudden flight had scattered them
across the plain, faces turned toward the mountain
where reason drives us on,

and I drew close against my faithful guide.
Without him, how could I have held my course?
Who would have led me up the slope?

He seemed to me at odds with himself,
ashamed. O noble conscience, clean and unstained,
how sharp a sting the smallest fault is to you.

When his feet let go of the haste
that strips the grace from any act,
my mind, until then drawn tight,

opened again, as if released into delight,
and I turned my eyes up to the hill
that lifts itself highest toward the sky.

The sun was blazing red behind us,
and in front of me it broke against my shape —
my body stopped its rays.

I wheeled to the side, gripped by the fear
of being left alone, when I saw
the ground darkened in front of me, and only there.

"Why do you still doubt?" my comfort said,
turning full around to face me.
"Don't you believe I'm with you, that I guide you?

It's already evening where the body lies
that I once cast a shadow with —
taken from Brindisi, now Naples keeps it.

And if no shadow falls in front of me now,
don't wonder at that any more than at the sky,
where one ray does not block another.

Bodies like this — made to suffer torment,
the cold and the heat — that Power shapes,
and wills that how it works stay hidden from us.

He is a fool who hopes our reason
can walk the whole unmeasured road
that one Substance in three Persons travels.

Be content, humankind, with knowing that it is.
For if you could have seen the whole of it,
Mary would not have needed to give birth,

and you have seen men long for it in vain —
men whose longing would have found its rest —
the very longing that is now their grief forever.

I mean Aristotle, and Plato,
and many more." Here he bent his head,
said nothing further, and stayed troubled.

By now we had come to the mountain's foot.
The rock rose up so sheer
that the nimblest legs would have been useless there.

Between Lerici and Turbia, the loneliest,
most broken track is an easy open stair
set beside that one.

"Who knows now on which side the slope
eases enough," my master said, his steps halting,
"that a man without wings could climb?"

And while he kept his eyes on the ground,
studying what the path was made of,
and I was looking up around the cliff,

on the left a crowd of souls appeared,
moving their feet toward us —
and seeming not to move, they came so slowly.

"Lift your eyes," I said to my master.
"Look — here are people who can advise us,
if you can't find the way yourself."

He looked at me, and with an open face
answered: "Let's go to them, since they come slowly.
And you — hold on to hope, sweet son."

Even after we had gone a thousand steps,
that people was still as far from us
as a good arm could throw a stone,

when they all pressed against the hard mass
of the high bank and stood there, still and close,
the way a man stops to stare when he's unsure.

"O you who died well, spirits already chosen,"
Virgil began, "by that peace
I believe is waiting for you all,

tell us where the mountain slopes
gently enough to climb,
for losing time stings most the man who knows the most."

As sheep come out from the fold
by ones and twos and threes, and the rest stand
timid, eyes and noses to the ground,

and what the first does the others do,
bunching against her if she stops,
meek and quiet, and not knowing why —

so I saw the leader of that fortunate flock
move forward to come near us,
modest in face, steady in his walk.

But when those in front saw the light
broken on the ground at my right side,
so that my shadow reached the rock,

they stopped, and drew back a little,
and all the others coming behind them,
not knowing why, did the same.

"Without your asking, I'll tell you plainly:
what you see is a human body,
which is why the sunlight is split on the ground.

Don't be amazed at it. Believe instead
that it is not without a power from Heaven
that he tries to climb this wall."

So my master spoke, and those worthy souls said,
"Turn back, then, and go on ahead of us,"
motioning with the backs of their hands.

And one of them began: "Whoever you are,
turn your eyes as you walk, and think hard —
did you ever see me in the other world?"

I turned toward him and looked closely.
He was blond, and beautiful, and noble to look at,
but a blow had split one of his eyebrows.

When I had humbly said no, I had never
seen him before, he said, "Now look,"
and showed me a wound high on his chest.

Then he said, smiling: "I am Manfred,
grandson of the Empress Constance.
So when you go back, I beg you,

go to my beautiful daughter, the mother
of the pride of Sicily and of Aragon,
and tell her the truth, if any other tale is told.

After my body had been torn open
by two mortal stabs, I gave myself,
weeping, to the One who gladly pardons.

My sins had been horrible.
But Infinite Goodness has arms so wide
that it takes in whatever turns to it.

If Cosenza's pastor — sent by Clement
then to hunt me down — had read this page
of God with any understanding,

the bones of my dead body would still be
at the bridgehead near Benevento,
guarded under the heavy cairn of stones.

Now the rain washes them, the wind shifts them,
beyond the kingdom, almost on the Verde,
where he carried them with the candles snuffed out.

Their curse does not so lose a soul
that Eternal Love can't still come back,
as long as hope keeps anything green.

It's true: whoever dies cut off
from Holy Church, though he repents at last,
must wait outside, here on this bank,

thirty times as long as the years he lived
in his defiance — unless that sentence
is cut shorter by faithful prayers.

See now if you can make me glad,
by letting my good Constance know
how you have seen me, and of this ban as well —

for those on earth can do much for us here."
