The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XI

Purgatorio: Canto XI45 of 100

Our Father, you who dwell in the heavens —
not bound there, but because you love
your first creation in the heights most of all —

praised be your name and your power
by every creature you have made,
as it is right to thank your sweet breath.

Let the peace of your kingdom come to us,
for we cannot reach it on our own,
not with all our wit, unless it comes.

As your own angels offer up their will
to you, singing Hosanna,
so let all people offer up theirs.

Give us this day our daily bread,
for without it, in this hard desert,
the one who strains hardest only falls behind.

And as we pardon, each of us, the wrong
we have suffered, so pardon us
in kindness, and do not weigh what we deserve.

Do not put our strength, so easily broken,
to the test against the old Enemy —
from the one who goads it, deliver us.

This last request, dear Lord, we do not make
for ourselves, who no longer need it,
but for the sake of those we left behind."

So those shades, praying that the road go well
for themselves and for us, moved on beneath
a weight like the kind we sometimes bear in dreams,

each under his own measure of pain,
circling, worn out, along that first ledge,
scrubbing away the grime of the world.

If good words are always said for us up there,
what may be said and done for them down here
by those whose will is rooted in good ground?

Surely we can help them wash away the marks
they carried from the world, so that clean and light
they may climb at last to the wheeling stars.

"Ah — so may mercy and justice soon
lift your burden, and give you the strength
to raise the wing that bears you where you long to go —

show us the way toward the stairs, the side
where the climb is shortest; and if there's more than one
pass, point us to the one that falls least steeply.

For the man who comes with me still carries
the weight of Adam's flesh, and so, against
his own wish, he is slow to climb."

These words came back in answer to the words
spoken by the one I was following,
but it was not clear which shade had said them.

Yet someone said: "Come with us to the right
along the bank, and you'll find a pass
a living man can manage to ascend.

And were I not held down by this stone
that tames the pride of my neck and forces
my face toward the ground, I'd lift my eyes

to the man who still lives and won't say his name,
to see if I might know him, and move him
to pity for this burden that I bear.

I was a Latian, born of a great Tuscan:
Guglielmo Aldobrandeschi was my father —
though I doubt his name ever reached you.

The old blood and the brave deeds of my line
made me so arrogant that, forgetting
the mother we all share, the common earth,

I held every man in such contempt
that it killed me — as the Sienese know,
and every child in Campagnatico.

I am Omberto. And pride did its harm
not to me alone: it dragged my whole house,
all my kin, down into ruin with it.

And for that I must carry this weight here
until God is satisfied — paying among
the dead what I would not pay among the living."

Listening, I bent my face toward the ground;
and one of them — not the one who'd spoken —
twisted himself beneath the stone that cramps him,

and saw me, and knew me, and called out,
holding his eyes on me with effort
as I went bowed all the way down with them.

"Oh," I said to him, "aren't you Oderisi,
Gubbio's pride, and the pride of that art
they call illuminating, up in Paris?"

"Brother," he said, "the pages laugh brighter now
under the brush of Franco of Bologna.
The honor is all his now — mine in part.

I'd never have been so generous, I admit,
while I was alive, so fierce was the hunger
for mastery that my whole heart was set on.

Here the price of that pride is paid in full;
and I'd not even be here yet, except
that I turned to God while I still could sin.

Oh, the empty glory of what we can do!
How briefly the green stays at the top —
unless a cruder age comes after it.

Cimabue thought he held the field
in painting; now it's Giotto people cry up,
and the older man's fame has gone dim.

And so one Guido has taken from the other
the glory of our tongue — and one is born,
perhaps, who'll chase them both out of the nest.

Worldly fame is nothing but a breath
of wind, that blows now from here, now from there,
and changes its name as it changes its quarter.

What more fame will you have, if you strip off
your old flesh, than if you'd died a child,
before you left off baby-talk for words —

once a thousand years have passed? And that
is a shorter span, against the eternal,
than a blink against the slowest wheel in heaven.

That man creeping so slowly down the road
ahead of me — all Tuscany rang with his name,
and now it's barely whispered in Siena,

where he was lord, back when she crushed
the raving pride of Florence — Florence, as haughty
then as she is for sale today.

Your fame is the color of grass: it comes,
it goes, and the same sun that draws it green
out of the earth is the one that burns it pale."

And I: "Your true words fill my heart
with good humility, and ease a swelling
in me. But who is the man you spoke of just now?"

"That," he answered, "is Provenzano Salvani.
He is here because he reached too far —
he tried to take all Siena into his hands.

So he has gone, and goes without rest,
ever since he died: this is the coin
paid back by the man who dared too much on earth."

And I: "If every spirit who puts off
repentance to the very edge of life
must wait below, and may not climb up here

(unless good prayers come to his aid)
for as long a time as he lived on earth —
how was the climb granted him so freely?"

"When he was living in his greatest splendor,"
he said, "he set himself, of his own will,
in the Campo of Siena, all shame put aside,

and there, to free his friend from the suffering
he bore in Charles's prison, he brought himself
to tremble in every vein.

I'll say no more, and I know I speak in the dark;
but not much time will pass before your neighbors
act in a way that lets you read it clear.

That act is what released him from those bounds."
