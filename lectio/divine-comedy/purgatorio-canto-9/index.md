# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto IX

Now the mistress of old Tithonus  
was glowing white on the balcony of the east,  
slipped free of the arms of her sweet lover.

Her forehead blazed with gems  
set in the shape of that cold creature  
whose tail comes lashing down on the nations,

and in the place where we were, Night  
had climbed two of the stairs by which she rises,  
and the third was already folding its wings,

when I — who still had something of Adam in me —  
gave in to sleep and lay down on the grass,  
there where the five of us were sitting.

Right at the hour when the little swallow  
begins her mournful song, near morning,  
perhaps remembering the old griefs she suffered,

and when the mind, gone wandering  
farther from the flesh and less held down by thought,  
sees almost like a prophet in its dreams,

I dreamed I saw an eagle hanging  
in the sky, its feathers made of gold,  
wings spread wide, poised to drop —

and it seemed to be the place where Ganymede  
had left his own behind, his kin,  
when he was carried up to the high council.

I thought to myself: maybe it strikes here  
out of habit only, and from anywhere else  
scorns to lift a body in its claws.

Then, wheeling a little, it seemed to me,  
terrible as lightning it came down  
and snatched me up as far as the fire.

There it seemed both of us were burning,  
and the imagined flames so scorched me  
that my sleep had no choice but to break.

No differently did Achilles startle awake,  
turning his roused eyes all around,  
with no idea where he was —

the time his mother stole him sleeping from Chiron  
and carried him in her arms to Scyros,  
the place the Greeks would later draw him from —

than I came bolting up, the moment  
sleep fled my face; I went pale  
like a man turned cold with fright.

Only my comfort was there at my side.  
The sun stood already more than two hours high,  
and my face was turned toward the sea.

"Don't be afraid," my lord said.  
"Take heart — things stand well for us.  
Don't hold back; throw your whole strength into it.

You've reached Purgatory at last.  
Look — there's the cliff that walls it in;  
look there, where it seems to break open, the way through.

Earlier, in the dawn that runs ahead of day,  
while your spirit lay asleep inside you  
on the flowers that adorn the land below,

a Lady came and said, 'I am Lucia.  
Let me take up this sleeping man;  
I'll make his road easier for him.'

Sordello and the other noble shapes stayed.  
She lifted you, and as the day grew bright  
she climbed, and I came after, in her steps.

She set you here; first her lovely eyes  
showed me that open entrance,  
then she and sleep together went away."

Like a man whose doubts are settled,  
who turns his fear to confidence  
once the truth has been laid bare to him,

so I changed; and when my leader saw me  
free of unease, he started up the cliff,  
and I went behind him, toward the height.

Reader, you can see how I lift  
my subject higher — so don't be amazed  
if I shore it up now with greater art.

We drew nearer, and reached a place  
where what had first looked to me like a gap,  
a crack splitting a wall apart,

showed itself a gate, with three steps under it,  
each a different color, leading up,  
and a keeper who said not a word yet.

And as I opened my eyes wider and wider,  
I saw him seated on the topmost step,  
his face such that I could not bear it.

In his hand he held a naked sword  
that threw the sunlight back at us  
so hard I kept lifting my eyes in vain.

"Speak from where you stand: what do you want?"  
he began. "Where is your escort?  
Watch that your coming here does you no harm."

"A Lady of Heaven, who knows these things well,"  
my master answered him, "told us just now,  
'Go that way; the gate is there.'"

"And may she speed your steps toward all good,"  
the courteous gatekeeper said again.  
"Come forward, then, to these stairs of ours."

We went up. The first step  
was white marble, so polished, so clear,  
I saw myself mirrored in it as I am.

The second was darker than the deepest blue,  
a rough, scorched, brittle stone,  
cracked through its whole length and across.

The third, that rests its mass on top,  
looked to me like porphyry, flaming red  
as blood that spurts up from a vein.

On this one God's Angel kept  
both his feet planted, seated on the threshold,  
which seemed to me a stone of diamond.

Up the three steps, with a willing heart,  
my guide drew me, saying: "Ask him,  
and humbly, to undo the lock."

I threw myself down at the holy feet,  
begged for mercy that he would open,  
but first I struck my breast three times.

Seven P's he cut into my forehead  
with the point of his sword, and said:  
"Be sure you wash these wounds once you're inside."

Ashes, or earth dug dry from the ground,  
would be the same color as his robe,  
and from beneath it he drew two keys.

One was gold, the other silver.  
First with the white, then with the yellow,  
he worked the gate, and I was satisfied.

"Whenever one of these keys fails,  
so that it won't turn true in the lock,"  
he told us, "this entrance does not open.

One is more precious; but the other  
takes more skill and judgment to unlock,  
for it's the one that loosens the knot.

I have them from Peter, and he told me  
to err in opening sooner than in keeping shut,  
so long as people fall down at my feet."

Then he pushed the panels of the sacred door,  
saying: "Enter — but I warn you,  
whoever looks back goes back out."

And when the pivots of that holy gate  
turned on their hinges —  
which are of heavy, ringing metal —

the Tarpeian rock did not roar so loud  
or sound so harsh, the day good Metellus  
was wrenched from it and it was left the poorer.

I turned, listening hard, at the first peal,  
and I seemed to hear "Te Deum laudamus"  
in voices woven through a sweet music.

What I was hearing gave me exactly  
the impression we get when people  
are singing along with an organ —

now you catch the words, now you don't.
