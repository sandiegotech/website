# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XXVIII

Eager now to search it through, inside and around —  
the divine forest, thick and green and living,  
which softened the new day for my eyes —

I left the bank without waiting any longer  
and took the open ground at a slow, slow pace,  
over soil that breathed out fragrance everywhere.

A gentle, steady air, with no change in it,  
brushed against my forehead, no harder  
than the touch of a mild wind,

and at it the branches, trembling lightly,  
all bowed the same way, toward the side  
where the Holy Mountain throws its first shadow —

yet not so far bent from standing straight  
that the little birds among their tops  
had to break off from any of their arts.

No — full of joy, they took the early hours  
singing within the leaves, which kept  
a steady undertone beneath their songs,

the kind that gathers branch to branch  
through the pine wood on the shore at Chiassi  
when Aeolus lets the Sirocco loose.

My slow steps had already carried me  
so deep into the ancient wood that I  
could no longer see where I had come in.

And there — a stream cut off my way ahead,  
bending the grass that grew along its edge  
to the left with its small waves.

The clearest waters anywhere on earth  
would look like they had something mixed in them  
beside this one, which hides nothing at all,

though it moves on dark, dark,  
under a shade that never breaks —  
no ray of the sun gets in, none of the moon.

I stopped where I stood, but with my eyes I crossed  
beyond the little stream, to take in  
the wide variety of fresh new May.

And there appeared to me — the way a thing appears  
all at once and turns aside,  
in sheer wonder, every other thought —

a lady, all alone, who walked along  
singing, picking flower after flower  
from the path her feet had painted bright.

"Ah, lovely lady, warming yourself  
in the rays of love — if I can trust your looks,  
which are usually the heart's true witnesses —

may you feel the wish," I said to her,  
"to come a little closer to this bank,  
so that I can hear what you are singing.

You make me remember where Proserpina was,  
and what she was, the moment she was lost —  
her mother losing her, and she the Spring."

The way a lady turns when she is dancing,  
feet pressed together and close to the ground,  
barely setting one before the other,

she turned toward me over the red and yellow  
flowers — no differently  
than a girl who lowers her modest eyes —

and granted what I asked, drawing  
so near that the sweet sound  
reached me now together with its meaning.

As soon as she had come to where the grass  
is bathed by that beautiful river's waters,  
she gave me the gift of lifting up her eyes.

I don't believe so great a light ever shone  
under the lids of Venus, the day her own son  
pierced her past his usual aim.

She smiled, standing straight on the far bank,  
her hands full of the many colors  
that high ground grows without any seed.

The river held us three paces apart;  
but the Hellespont, where Xerxes crossed —  
still a check on all human pride —

drew no more hatred from Leander  
for rolling between Sestos and Abydos  
than this stream did from me, for not opening then.

"You are newcomers, and because I smile,"  
she began, "here in this place  
chosen as the nest for human nature,

some puzzlement may be holding you in wonder;  
but the psalm Delectasti gives a light  
that can clear the cloud from your minds.

And you in front, who asked me — speak,  
if you want to hear more; I have come ready  
for all your questions, as far as you need."

"The water," I said, "and the sound of the forest  
are fighting against a new belief in me,  
something I heard that runs against this."

And she: "I'll tell you how the thing  
that makes you wonder comes from its cause,  
and clear away the cloud that troubles you.

The Supreme Good, who delights in himself alone,  
made man good, and gave him this good place  
as a pledge of everlasting peace.

By his own fault he stayed here only a while;  
by his own fault he traded his innocent  
laughter and sweet play for weeping and toil.

So that the turbulence raised down below  
by the breath of the land and the water —  
which follow after heat as far as they can —

would wage no war on humankind,  
this mountain climbed so high toward heaven  
and stands free of it, from the gate up.

Now, since the whole atmosphere  
turns in a circle with the first motion,  
unless that circle is broken somewhere,

here at this height, fully open  
in the living air, that motion strikes  
and makes the forest sound, for it is dense;

and the struck plant has so much power  
that with its strength it charges the air,  
and the air, wheeling, scatters it around;

and the ground over there, each according  
to its own worth and climate, conceives and bears  
the many trees of their many kinds.

So it should be no marvel, back on earth,  
once this is understood, when some plant  
takes root there with no seed in sight.

And you should know: this holy upland  
you are standing in is full of every seed,  
and holds fruit no one there has ever picked.

The water you see does not spring from a vein  
fed by mist the cold condenses,  
like a stream that gains and loses its breath;

it issues from a fountain sure and steady,  
which by the will of God takes back  
as much as it pours out, open on two sides.

On this side it flows down with the power  
to take away all memory of sin;  
on that, it gives back the memory of every good deed.

Here it is called Lethe; over there,  
Eunoë; and neither one does its work  
unless it is tasted on both sides first.

Its taste is finer than any other taste;  
and though your thirst by now  
might be slaked enough that I tell you no more,

I'll give you one more thing, freely added —  
and don't think my words will be less dear to you  
if they run on past what I promised.

Those who long ago set the Age of Gold to song,  
and its happy state,  
were perhaps dreaming, on Parnassus, of this place.

Here the root of humankind was innocent;  
here it was always Spring, with every fruit;  
this is the nectar everyone has meant."

Then I turned myself all the way around  
to my Poets, and saw that they had stood  
listening with a smile to these last words;

then I turned my eyes back to the lovely lady.
