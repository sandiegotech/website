The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XVII

Purgatorio: Canto XVII51 of 100

Remember, reader, if a mist in the Alps
ever closed over you, so that you saw
no more than a mole sees through its skin —

how, when the damp and crowded vapors start
to thin and break apart, the sun's round face
comes feebly filtering through —

and then your mind will leap to grasp at once
how I, too, came to see the sun again,
the sun that even now was going down.

So, matching my own steps to my Master's
faithful ones, I walked out of that cloud
into the rays already dead on the low shore.

O Imagination, you who at times
so steal us from ourselves that a man hears
nothing, though a thousand trumpets sound around him —

who moves you, if the senses give you nothing?
A light moves you, formed in heaven on its own,
or by a will that sends it down to us.

Of her cruelty — the one who changed her shape
into the bird that most delights in singing —
the trace appeared inside my imagining;

and at this my mind drew so far back
into itself that nothing from outside
could reach it now or be received by it.

Then into my lifted fantasy there came
one crucified, scornful and savage still
in the look on his face, and dying even so.

Around him stood the great Ahasuerus,
Esther his wife, and Mordecai the just,
a man so whole in everything he said and did.

And as this image burst of its own accord —
the way a bubble breaks the moment
the water it was made of gives way beneath it —

there rose up in my vision a young girl
bitterly weeping, and she said: "O queen,
why did your anger wish you into nothing?

You killed yourself so as not to lose Lavinia;
now you have lost me. I am the one who mourns,
Mother, your ruin before another's comes."

As sleep is shattered when a sudden light
strikes in against the eyelids that are shut,
and flickers, broken, before it dies away —

so this imagining of mine fell off
the instant a brightness struck across my face,
far greater than any we are used to here.

I turned to see where I had come to be,
when a voice said, "Here is the way up" —
a voice that swept all other thoughts away,

and made my wish so full of eagerness
to look and see who it was that spoke,
it could not rest until we stood face to face.

But just as, looking at the sun, which beats
the eyes back and veils its own shape in its blaze,
so here my power of sight fell short.

"This is a spirit of God, who on the way
of going up directs us without our asking,
and hides himself inside his own light.

He does for us what a man does for himself:
for he who sees the need and waits to be asked
is already leaning, in malice, toward no.

Let our feet answer such an invitation;
let us press on to climb before the dark,
for once it falls we cannot, till day returns."

So said my Guide; and he and I together
turned our steps toward a stairway,
and I, the moment I had reached the first step,

felt near me something move, as of wings,
and a fanning across my face, and a voice: "Beati
pacifici, who are free of evil anger!"

Already the last sunbeams, with the night
close on their heels, were lifted up so high
above us that on many sides the stars came out.

"O my strength, why are you draining away?"
I said within myself, for I could feel
the power in my legs called to a halt.

We had come to where the stairway climbs
no higher, and we stood there, fixed in place,
like a ship that has just touched the shore;

and I listened a little, to catch whatever
there might be to hear on this new ledge,
then turned to my Master and I said:

"Tell me, sweet Father, what wrong is purged
here in the circle where we stand?
Our feet may rest — let your speech not rest."

And he to me: "The love of good, that fell
short of what it should have done, is made good here;
here the slow-pulled, late oar is plied again.

But so that you may grasp it still more clearly,
turn your mind to me, and you will gather
some useful fruit out of this pause of ours.

Neither Creator nor created thing, my son,"
he began, "was ever without love —
either of nature or of the mind; and this you know.

Natural love can never go astray;
but the other can, by fixing on a wrong
object, or by too much, or too little force.

While it is aimed straight at the highest goods,
and in the lesser keeps itself in measure,
it cannot be the cause of any guilty joy;

but when it bends toward evil, or chases good
with more drive than it should, or less,
the creature works against its own Creator.

From this you can understand that love must be
the seed in you of every virtue,
and of every act that earns a punishment.

Now since love can never turn its gaze
away from the good of the one who holds it,
all things are safe from hating their own selves;

and since no being can be conceived
as standing alone, cut off from the First,
every desire to hate Him is ruled out.

So, if I divide it rightly, the evil
that a man loves is his neighbor's harm,
and in your clay this love is born three ways.

There are those who, by their neighbor's fall,
hope to rise, and so for that alone
long to see him toppled from his height;

there are those who fear to lose their power,
grace, honor, fame, because another climbs,
and grieve so they come to love the opposite;

and there are those whom an injury so galls
that it leaves them hungry for revenge,
and such men must go shape another's hurt.

This threefold love is wept for down below.
Now hear me on the other kind, the love
that runs toward good with measure gone wrong.

Each of us dimly conceives some good
in which the mind might rest, and longs for it;
and so each one strives to reach it.

If a slack love draws you to look on it,
or to attain it, this terrace, after
just repentance, is where you suffer for it.

There is another good that does not make us happy;
it is not happiness, not the good Essence,
the fruit and root of every good there is.

The love that gives itself too far to that
is wept for in three circles up above us;
but how it falls in three, and why,

I will not say — so that you seek it out yourself."
