# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXVII

The flame was standing straight and still by now,  
done speaking, and it had already left us,  
with the gentle Poet's leave to go,

when another one, coming up behind it,  
made us turn our eyes toward its tip  
because of a garbled sound that came from there.

As the Sicilian bull — which bellowed first  
with the cries of the man who shaped it with his file,  
and that was only right —

would bellow so with the voice of the one in agony  
that, for all it was made of brass,  
it seemed pierced through with pain;

so here, having no way out, no opening  
at first from inside the fire, the suffering words  
were turned into the fire's own language.

But once they had worked their way upward  
to the point, and given it the same quivering  
the tongue had given them on their way out,

we heard it say: "You there, at whom I aim  
my voice, who were just now speaking Lombard,  
saying, 'Go on now, I won't keep you' —

though I come, it may be, a little late,  
don't let it bother you to stop and speak with me.  
You see it doesn't bother me, and I am burning.

If you have only just dropped into this blind world  
from that sweet Italian land  
where I brought all my guilt from,

tell me — do the people of Romagna have peace or war?  
I was from the mountains there, the country  
between Urbino and the ridge where the Tiber breaks loose."

I was still bent downward, listening close,  
when my Guide touched me on the side  
and said: "You speak. This one is Italian."

And I, who already had my answer  
ready, began at once to speak:  
"O soul, hidden away down there,

your Romagna is not now, and never was,  
without war in the hearts of its tyrants;  
but I left no open war there when I came.

Ravenna stands as it has stood for years.  
The Eagle of Polenta broods over it,  
and spreads her wings to cover Cervia too.

The city that once stood the long siege  
and made a bloody heap of the French  
lies again beneath the Green Paws.

Verrucchio's old Mastiff and the young one,  
who dealt so foully with Montagna,  
still make augers of their teeth where they always did.

The cities on the Lamone and the Santerno  
are ruled by the young Lion of the white den,  
who switches sides between summer and winter.

And the one whose flank the Savio washes —  
just as it lies between the plain and the mountains —  
lives caught between tyranny and freedom.

Now I beg you, tell us who you are.  
Don't be more stubborn than the others were,  
and may your name hold its place up in the world."

After the fire had roared a little longer  
in its own way, the sharp point swayed  
this way and that, and then breathed out these words:

"If I thought I was answering someone  
who would ever go back up to the world,  
this flame would stand still and stop its flickering.

But since no one has ever gone back  
from this deep place alive, if what I hear is true,  
I answer you with no fear of disgrace.

I was a man of arms, then a Franciscan,  
believing the cord would make my amends;  
and my belief would have come true in full

but for the High Priest — bad luck go with him —  
who dragged me back into my old sins;  
and I want you to hear how, and why.

While I still wore the shape of flesh and bone  
my mother gave me, the things I did  
were not a lion's work, but a fox's.

Every trick, every hidden, winding way —  
I knew them all, and worked their craft so well  
that word of it went out to the ends of the earth.

When I saw I had reached that part of life  
where every man ought to lower his sails  
and coil away his ropes,

what had pleased me before now sickened me;  
I repented, I confessed, I gave myself up —  
and oh, it would have saved me!

But the Prince of the modern Pharisees,  
with a war on his hands near the Lateran —  
and not against Saracens or Jews,

for every one of his enemies was Christian,  
and none had been at the taking of Acre  
or traded in the Sultan's lands —

he gave no thought to his high office or holy orders,  
nor in me to that cord  
which used to keep those who wore it lean;

but the way Constantine sought out Sylvester  
on Soracte to cure his leprosy,  
this man sought me out as his physician

to cure the fever of his pride.  
He asked me for counsel, and I kept silent,  
because his words sounded like a drunk man's.

Then he said: 'Don't be afraid in your heart.  
From this moment I absolve you — now teach me  
how to level Palestrina to the ground.

I have the power to lock Heaven and unlock it,  
as you know; that is why the keys are two —  
the keys my predecessor did not prize.'

Then his heavy arguments drove me  
to the point where silence seemed the worst counsel,  
and I said: 'Father, since you wash me clean

of the sin I am about to fall into —  
a long promise kept short on delivery  
will make you win your high seat.'

Francis came for me afterward, when I was dead;  
but one of the black cherubim  
said to him: 'Don't take him. Do me no wrong.

He has to come down among my servants,  
because he gave that fraudulent counsel —  
and ever since, I have had him by the hair.

You cannot absolve a man who does not repent,  
nor can a man repent and will a thing at once —  
the contradiction forbids it.'

Oh, how I shook, in my misery,  
when he seized me and said: 'You didn't think,  
maybe, that I was a logician!'

He carried me down to Minos, who wound  
his tail eight times around his stiff back,  
and after he had bitten it in a great rage,

said: 'This one's for the thieving fire.'  
And so, where you see me, I am lost,  
and dressed in this flame I go grieving."

When it had finished telling its story this way,  
the flame moved off, full of lament,  
wringing and shaking its sharp horn.

We went on, my Guide and I together,  
up over the ridge to the next arch,  
the one that spans the ditch where the toll is paid

by those who, sowing discord, earn their load.
