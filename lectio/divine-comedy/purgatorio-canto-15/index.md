# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XV

As much of its course as the sun has left  
between the close of the third hour and daybreak —  
that sphere forever playing like a child —

just so much of its day, toward nightfall now,  
was left for the sun above us to run.  
There it was evening; here it was midnight.

And the rays were striking us full in the face,  
for we had circled so far around the mount  
that we were walking straight into the west,

when I felt my forehead overwhelmed  
by a brightness far beyond what shone before,  
and the unknown thing of it left me dazed.

So I raised both hands above my brows  
and made of them a visor for my eyes,  
to cut the glare that was too much to bear.

The way a sunbeam, leaping from water  
or from a mirror, springs back to the other side,  
climbing again at the very same angle

by which it fell, and bends as far away  
from the straight drop a stone would take —  
as art and experiment can show —

just so it seemed a light, reflected there  
in front of me, was striking at my sight,  
and my eyes were quick to flinch and turn aside.

"What is that, gentle Father, that I cannot  
shield my eyes from no matter how I try,"  
I said, "and that seems to be moving toward us?"

"Don't be amazed if the household of heaven  
still dazzles you," he answered me. "It is  
an angel, come to invite us higher up.

Soon it will be that seeing such things  
will not be hard for you, but a delight,  
as great as nature shaped you to receive."

When we had reached the blessed Angel, he  
with a joyful voice said: "Enter here,  
to a stairway far less steep than were the rest."

We were climbing now, already past that place,  
and "Beati misericordes" rose  
in song behind us — "Rejoice, you who overcome!"

My Master and I, the two of us alone,  
were going upward, and I thought that I  
might gain some profit from his words as we went;

so I turned to him and put this question:  
"What did the spirit from Romagna mean,  
speaking of being shut out, and of sharing?"

And he to me: "He knows the harm that comes  
from his own worst failing — so don't be surprised  
if he warns us off it, to spare us the grief.

Because your longing fastens on those goods  
where having a partner means each share is less,  
envy works the bellows under your sighs.

But if the love of the highest sphere instead  
turned your desire upward toward the heights,  
that fear would never be there in your chest.

For there, the more there are who say 'our own,'  
the more of good each single soul possesses,  
and the more love burns bright in that cloister."

"I'm hungrier now to have this satisfied,"  
I said, "than if I'd held my tongue at first,  
and I'm gathering more doubt inside my mind.

How can it be that a good, shared out,  
makes its many owners richer in it  
than if it were held by only a few?"

And he to me: "Because you keep your mind  
fixed wholly still on things of the earth,  
you pull down darkness out of the very light.

That good, infinite and beyond all words,  
which is up there, runs toward love  
as a sunbeam comes to a body that is bright.

It gives itself as much as it finds warmth,  
so that however far love reaches out,  
the eternal worth grows greater over it.

And the more souls who aspire toward that height,  
the more there are to love, and the more they love,  
and each, like a mirror, gives the others back.

And if my reasoning doesn't ease you yet,  
you will see Beatrice; and she will lift  
from you this longing and every other one.

Only work to have soon erased — as two  
already are — the five wounds still remaining,  
the ones that close again by being grieved."

I was just about to say, "You set me at peace,"  
when I saw I had come to another ring,  
so my hungry eyes made me fall silent.

There it seemed to me that, all at once,  
I was caught up into a trance, and in it  
I saw a crowd of people in a temple;

and at the door a woman, with the sweet  
bearing of a mother, who was saying: "Son,  
why have you treated the two of us like this?

Look — your father and I have been searching  
for you in sorrow." And as she fell silent there,  
what had first appeared was gone.

Then another woman came before me, her cheeks  
wet with the tears that grief distills  
when it is born of deep resentment at someone else,

and she was saying: "If you are the lord  
of the city over whose name the gods  
so quarreled, the fountain of all learning,

take revenge, Pisistratus, on those bold arms  
that dared to lock our daughter in their hold."  
And the lord, it seemed, was gentle and mild,

answering her with a calm and even face:  
"What shall we do to those who wish us harm,  
if we condemn the one who loves us?"

Then I saw people on fire with rage,  
stoning a young man to death, and shouting  
over and over to each other, "Kill him! Kill him!"

And I saw him sinking toward the ground,  
because death already weighed him down,  
but he made his eyes forever gates to heaven,

praying to the high Lord, in such an agony,  
that He would pardon those who hunted him,  
with a face of the kind that unlocks pity.

When my soul came back outward, to the things  
outside it that are real, I recognized  
the errors I had seen — true ones, not false.

My Leader, who could see me carry myself  
like a man shaking himself awake from sleep,  
said: "What's the matter? You can't keep your feet.

You've come more than half a league like this,  
eyes half shut, your legs tangling beneath you,  
like someone weighed down by wine or sleep."

"O my sweet Father, if you'll listen," I said,  
"I'll tell you what appeared before my eyes  
when my legs were taken out from under me."

And he: "Even if you wore a hundred masks  
across your face, the smallest of your thoughts  
would still not be hidden from me.

What you saw was meant so you would not refuse  
to open your heart to the waters of peace  
that pour from the eternal fountain down.

I did not ask 'What's wrong?' the way a man  
asks who looks with eyes that cannot see  
when the body lies there, emptied of its soul,

but I asked it to put strength back in your feet.  
This is how we must goad the sluggish on,  
slow to use their waking when it returns."

We went on, peering through the failing light  
as far ahead as the eye could reach,  
straining against the late and brilliant sun;

and look — by slow degrees a smoke came on  
toward us, as dark as the night itself,  
and there was no place to take shelter from it.

It robbed us of our eyes and the clean air.
