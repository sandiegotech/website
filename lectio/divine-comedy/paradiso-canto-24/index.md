# The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto XXIV

"O chosen fellowship at the great supper  
of the blessed Lamb, who feeds you so  
that your desire is filled forever full,

if by the grace of God this man tastes  
beforehand something of what falls from your table,  
before death sets the hour for him,

turn your minds to his measureless longing  
and let some dew of it fall on him: you drink  
forever from the spring that feeds his thought."

So Beatrice; and those glad souls  
turned themselves to spheres on fixed poles,  
flaming as they wheeled, like comets in the sky.

And as the wheels inside a clockwork turn  
so that the first, to one who watches,  
seems to stand still, and the last to fly,

so those dancing rounds, each moving  
in its own measure, let me gauge  
the wealth within them, swift or slow.

From the one I marked as loveliest  
I saw a fire come forth so full of joy  
that it left none there of greater brightness;

and three times round about Beatrice  
it wheeled, with a song so divine  
that my imagining cannot say it again.

So the pen leaps ahead and I do not write it:  
for our fancy, let alone our speech,  
is too crude a color for such folds.

"O holy sister of mine, who pray to us  
so devoutly — by your burning love  
you unbind me from that lovely sphere."

Then, having stopped, the blessed fire  
turned its breath toward my Lady,  
and spoke in the way I have set down.

And she: "O eternal light of the great man  
to whom our Lord left the keys,  
which He carried down, of this miraculous joy,

test this one on points light and grave,  
as pleases you, about the Faith  
by which you once walked upon the sea.

Whether he loves well, hopes well, and believes,  
is not hidden from you, for your sight is fixed  
where everything is seen as in a picture;

but since this kingdom has won its citizens  
by the true Faith, it is right — to glorify it —  
that he be given the chance to speak of it."

As a bachelor of arts arms himself and stays silent  
until the master sets the question,  
to argue it, not to settle it,

so I armed myself with every reason  
while she was speaking, to be ready  
for such a questioner and such a profession.

"Speak, good Christian; show yourself plainly:  
what is Faith?" At this I raised my brow  
toward that light from which the words were breathed;

then I turned to Beatrice, and she  
gave me quick signs that I should pour  
the water out from my inward spring.

"May the grace that lets me make confession,"  
I began, "to the great captain here  
make all my thoughts come clearly into words."

And I went on: "As the truthful pen  
of your dear brother wrote of it, Father —  
he who with you set Rome on the good road —

faith is the substance of things hoped for,  
and the evidence of things not seen;  
and this seems to me to be its essence."

Then I heard: "You see it rightly,  
if you grasp well why he placed it  
first among the substances, then the evidences."

And I then: "The deep things  
that grant me here their showing  
are so hidden from the eyes below

that their being is there in belief alone,  
on which the high hope is founded;  
and so it takes the nature of substance.

And from this belief we are bound  
to reason without other sight;  
and so it takes the nature of evidence."

Then I heard: "If everything acquired  
below by doctrine were grasped like this,  
no sophist's cunning would find room there."

So it was breathed from that kindled love;  
then it added: "The alloy and weight  
of this coin have been gone over well;

but tell me — do you have it in your purse?"  
And I: "Yes — so bright and so round  
that in its minting I have no doubt at all."

Then from the depth of light that shone there  
came the words: "This precious jewel,  
on which every virtue is founded,

where did you get it?" And I: "The broad rain  
of the Holy Spirit, poured out  
over the old parchments and the new,

is a syllogism that proved it to me  
so sharply that, set beside it,  
every other proof seems blunt to me."

Then I heard: "The old and the new  
premises that so convince you —  
why do you take them for the word of God?"

And I: "The proof that opens the truth to me  
is the works that followed, for which Nature  
never heated iron nor beat the anvil."

The answer came: "Say, who assures you  
that those works ever happened? The very thing  
that needs proving, and nothing else, attests them."

"If the world turned to Christianity  
without miracles," I said, "this one alone  
is such that the rest are not its hundredth part;

for you went into the field poor and fasting  
to sow there the good plant  
that once was a vine and now is grown a thorn."

When this was finished, the high holy Court  
rang through the spheres with "We praise one God,"  
in the melody they sing up there.

And then that lord, who branch by branch,  
examining, had led me onward  
until we neared the outermost leaves,

began again: "The Grace that courts  
your mind has opened your mouth  
until now just as a mouth should open,

so that I approve what has come forth;  
but now you must set out what you believe,  
and from where your belief was offered to you."

"O holy father, spirit who now sees  
what you believed so surely that, toward the tomb,  
you outran feet far younger than your own,"

I began, "you want me here to make plain  
the form of my ready belief,  
and you also ask the cause of it.

And I answer: I believe in one God,  
sole and eternal, who moves all the heavens  
with love and longing, himself unmoved;

and for such belief I have not only proofs  
physical and metaphysical — they are given me  
also by the truth that rains down from this place

through Moses, through the Prophets and the Psalms,  
through the Gospel, and through you who wrote  
after the burning Spirit made you holy;

and I believe in three eternal Persons, and these  
I believe one essence, so one and three  
that they bear both 'are' and 'is' together.

Of the deep and divine condition  
I touch on now, the teaching of the Gospel  
stamps my mind again and again.

This is the beginning, this the spark  
that widens afterward into living flame  
and, like a star in heaven, burns in me."

As a lord who hears what pleases him  
embraces his servant on the spot, rejoicing  
at the good news the moment he falls silent,

so, blessing me and singing,  
three times it circled me, when I had ceased —  
the apostolic light at whose command

I had spoken: so well had my speaking pleased him.
