# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto X

Once we were over the threshold of the gate  
that the misdirected love of souls leaves shut,  
the love that makes the crooked road look straight,

I heard it close again behind us, ringing.  
And if I had turned my eyes back toward it then,  
what decent excuse could I have made for the lapse?

We climbed up through a cleft in the rock,  
which swung now this way and now that,  
like a wave that pulls back and rolls in again.

"Here we'll have to use a little skill,"  
my guide began, "and lean ourselves  
now toward this side, now toward the side that gives."

That slowed our steps so much that the moon,  
worn down to its last sliver, had time  
to sink back into its bed and rest once more

before we worked free of that needle's eye.  
But when we were out, and in the open at last,  
up where the mountain draws itself back,

I was worn out, and the two of us, unsure  
which way to go, stopped on a level shelf  
lonelier than any road across a desert.

From its edge, where it borders on the drop,  
in to the foot of the cliff that keeps on rising,  
would measure three times the length of a man;

and as far as my eye could carry in its flight,  
first on the left and then on the right,  
this terrace looked the same the whole way round.

Our feet had not yet moved out onto it  
when I noticed that the bank circling us,  
which left no possible foothold for the climb,

was made of white marble, and so worked  
with carvings that not only Polyclitus  
but Nature herself would be shamed beside them.

The angel who came down to earth with word  
of the peace men had wept for so many years,  
the peace that opened Heaven after its long ban,

stood there before us, carved so true,  
set in so gracious a pose,  
he did not seem an image that keeps silent.

You'd have sworn he was saying "Ave";  
for she was pictured there as well,  
the one who turned the key to the highest love,

and stamped into her bearing was this answer,  
"Ecce ancilla Dei," as plainly  
as a seal presses its shape into wax.

"Don't fix your mind on one spot only,"  
my gentle master said — and he had me  
standing on the side where people keep their hearts.

So I moved my eyes, and there beyond Mary,  
on the same side as the one  
who was guiding me, I saw

another scene cut into the rock.  
I crossed past Virgil and drew close,  
so that it stood full in front of my eyes.

Carved there in the very same marble  
were the cart and oxen drawing the holy ark —  
for which men dread an office not given them to hold.

People came on in front, the whole crowd  
split into seven choirs; and of my two senses  
one said "No, they don't," the other, "Yes, they sing."

The same with the smoke of the incense  
shown rising there: my eyes and my nose  
were left at odds, one yes, the other no.

Ahead of the blessed vessel danced  
the humble Psalmist, his robe hitched up,  
and in that moment he was both more and less than king.

Across from him, framed in the window  
of a great palace, Michal looked down,  
like a woman full of scorn and grief.

I shifted my feet from where I stood  
to study, close up, one more story  
that shone white at me past Michal.

There the high glory of the Roman prince  
was set down — the one whose great goodness  
moved Gregory to win his great victory.

I mean the Emperor Trajan. At his bridle  
a poor widow had taken her stand,  
her whole bearing one of weeping and grief.

Around him it looked crowded, packed  
with horsemen, and the eagles worked in gold  
above them seemed to stir in the wind.

The wretched woman, in the thick of them,  
seemed to be saying: "Lord, avenge me  
for my dead son. My heart is breaking."

And he, answering: "Wait until I get back."  
And she, like someone whose grief  
will not be put off: "My lord — and if

you don't come back?" And he: "Whoever holds  
my place will see to it." And she: "What good  
is another man's good deed to you,

if you forget your own?" So he: "Take comfort.  
I must clear this debt before I move on.  
Justice demands it, and pity holds me here."

He to whom nothing is ever new  
made this visible speech —  
strange to us, since we have nothing like it.

While I stood there taking pleasure  
in those images of such humility,  
precious to look on for the sake of Him who made them,

"Look — over here, though they come on slowly,  
many people," the poet murmured.  
"These will point us up toward the higher stairs."

My eyes, so intent on taking in  
new things, things they're always hungry for,  
were not slow to turn his way.

But I don't want you, reader, to lose heart  
in your good resolve just because you hear  
how God ordains the debt be paid.

Don't dwell on the shape of the punishment;  
think of what comes after — think that at worst  
it cannot outlast the final sentence.

"Master," I started, "what I see  
moving toward us doesn't look like people,  
and I can't tell what it is — my sight keeps wavering."

And he to me: "The crushing weight  
of what they carry bends them down to the ground  
so far that my own eyes fought it at first.

But look hard, and pick apart  
with your eyes what's coming under those stones.  
You can already see how each one is struck low."

O you proud Christians — worn out, miserable —  
who, sick in the eye of the mind,  
put your faith in steps that carry you backward,

don't you see that we are worms,  
born to become the angelic butterfly  
that flies toward justice with nothing to shield it?

Why does your spirit float so high in the air?  
You are no more than insects half-formed,  
like the grub that dies before it takes its shape.

The way a figure is sometimes set,  
to hold up a ceiling or a roof in a corbel's place,  
with its knees drawn up against its chest,

so that out of something unreal a real  
ache rises in whoever sees it —  
that is how I saw these souls, once I looked closely.

True, they were bent down more or less,  
each according to the load on his back;  
and the one whose face held the most patience

seemed, through his weeping, to be saying: "I can't go on."
