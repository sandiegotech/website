# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXIV

In that early part of the young year  
when the sun cools his rays beneath Aquarius  
and the nights already shrink toward half the day,

when the hoarfrost copies onto the ground  
the likeness of her white sister, the snow —  
though her pen's ink does not hold for long —

the farmer, whose feed has run out,  
gets up, looks out, and sees the fields  
all gleaming white, and slaps his thigh,

goes back inside, and paces, and grieves  
like a poor wretch who has no idea what to do;  
then he comes out again, and hope returns,

seeing how the face of the world has changed  
in so little time, and he takes up his crook  
and drives the little lambs out to graze.

That was how my master filled me with alarm  
when I saw the trouble on his brow —  
and just as fast the cure came to the wound.

For as we reached the broken bridge,  
my guide turned to me with that gentle look  
I first saw at the foot of the mountain.

He opened his arms, after some thought,  
weighing the choice within himself, studying  
the rubble closely first — and took hold of me.

And like a man who works while he plans,  
so that he always seems to look ahead,  
he lifted me toward the top of one boulder

while his eye picked out another crag,  
saying, "Grapple onto that one next,  
but test it first — make sure it holds your weight."

This was no road for a man in a heavy cloak,  
for even we — he weightless, and I shoved from below —  
could barely haul ourselves from ledge to ledge.

And had it not been that on this side  
the bank was shorter to climb than the other,  
he might have managed, but I'd have been finished.

But since all of Malebolge tilts down  
toward the mouth of the deepest pit,  
the shape of every ditch is the same:

one bank rises while the other sinks.  
Still, we came at last to the very point  
where the last stone juts out over the drop.

The breath had been so milked out of my lungs  
by the time I was up that I could go no farther,  
and I sat down the moment I arrived.

"Now you have to shake off this sloth,"  
my master said. "No one wins fame  
sitting on feather cushions, or tucked under a quilt —

and without fame, whoever burns up his life  
leaves the same trace of himself on earth  
as smoke leaves in the air, or foam on water.

So get up. Beat down your exhaustion  
with the spirit that wins every fight,  
so long as the body's weight doesn't drag it down.

A longer stairway is still ahead of you;  
it's not enough to have left these behind.  
If you understand me, put it to use."

Then I stood up, making myself look  
better supplied with breath than I felt,  
and said, "Go on — I'm strong, I'm ready."

We took our way up along the ridge,  
which was jagged and narrow and hard to climb,  
and far steeper than the one before.

I kept talking as I went, not to seem worn out,  
when a voice came up from the next ditch —  
a voice not shaped well enough for words.

I couldn't tell what it said, though by now  
I stood on the back of the arch that crosses there;  
but whoever spoke seemed driven by anger.

I leaned down, but my living eyes  
could not reach the bottom through the dark,  
so I said, "Master, get us to the next ring,

and let's go down the wall, because from here  
I hear and can't make sense of it,  
and I look down and can't make out a thing."

"I'll give you no answer," he said, "but the deed,  
for a reasonable request should be met  
with the doing, in silence."

We came down off the bridge at its end,  
where it joins the eighth bank,  
and then the ditch opened up plain before me;

and inside it I saw a terrible swarm  
of serpents, of so monstrous a kind  
that the memory still freezes my blood.

Let Libya stop boasting of her sand —  
for though she breeds the chelydri, the jaculi,  
the phareae, the cenchri, the amphisbaena,

she never showed so many plagues, so vile,  
not with all of Ethiopia thrown in,  
nor with everything that lives by the Red Sea.

Through this cruel, miserable swarm  
naked people were running, terrified,  
with no hope of a hiding-hole or charm.

Their hands were bound behind them with serpents;  
these drove their head and tail through the loins  
and were knotted in a tangle out in front.

And look — at one of them, right on our side,  
a serpent lunged and ran him through  
there where the neck is tied to the shoulders.

No 'O' or 'I' was ever written so fast  
as he caught fire and burned, and had to fall  
to the ground entirely turned to ash.

And once he lay there, wrecked on the ground,  
the ash drew itself together on its own  
and in an instant he was himself again.

Just so, the great sages tell us,  
the Phoenix dies and then is born once more  
as it nears its five-hundredth year:

in its life it eats no herb or grain,  
only the tears of incense and amomum,  
and nard and myrrh are its last winding-sheet.

And like a man who falls and can't say how —  
dragged to the ground by the force of demons,  
or by some other seizure that locks the body —

who, when he rises, looks around him  
utterly dazed by the great anguish  
he has been through, and gazes, and sighs:

that was the sinner once he got to his feet.  
O justice of God — how harsh it is,  
that rains down blows like these for vengeance!

My guide then asked him who he was,  
and he answered, "I rained down from Tuscany  
not long ago, into this savage gorge.

A bestial life pleased me, not a human one,  
mule that I was. I am Vanni Fucci,  
the beast — and Pistoia was a fitting den for me."

And I said to my guide, "Tell him to hold still,  
and ask what crime has shoved him down here,  
for I once knew him as a man of blood and rage."

The sinner heard, and did not pretend not to;  
he turned his mind and his face toward me,  
and a wretched shame spread across him.

Then he said, "It hurts me more that you've caught me  
down in the misery where you see me now  
than it did to be torn from the other life.

I can't refuse you what you ask. I'm sunk  
this low because I robbed the sacristy  
of its beautiful sacred ornaments —

and the blame was once laid falsely on another.  
But so you won't enjoy the sight of me,  
if you ever climb out of these dark places,

open your ears to my announcement, and hear:  
first Pistoia thins itself of the Blacks,  
then Florence renews her people and her ways.

Mars draws a vapor up from Val di Magra,  
wrapped around in turbulent cloud,  
and in a savage, bitter storm

there will be a battle over Campo Piceno,  
when the vapor suddenly splits the fog,  
and every White will be struck down by it.

And I've told you this to make you grieve."
