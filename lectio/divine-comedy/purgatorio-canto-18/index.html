The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XVIII

Purgatorio: Canto XVIII52 of 100

My noble Teacher had brought his reasoning
to a close, and was watching my face closely
to see whether I looked satisfied;

and I, whom a new thirst was already goading,
was silent on the outside, but said within:
"Perhaps all my asking is wearing on him."

But that true father, who had understood
the shy wish I had not let speak for itself,
by speaking first gave me the nerve to speak.

So I said: "Master, my sight is so quickened
in your light that I see clearly now
all that your words carry or set out.

Then I beg you, dear and gentle father,
teach me about love, since you trace back to it
every good action and its opposite."

"Turn on me," he said, "the keen eyes
of your mind, and the error will be plain to you
of the blind men who set up as guides.

The soul, created quick to love,
moves toward anything that pleases it
the moment pleasure wakes it into action.

Your perception draws an image from
some real thing, and unfolds it inside you,
so that it turns the soul to face it.

And if, so turned, the soul leans toward it,
that leaning is love — it is nature,
bound in you afresh by pleasure.

Then, the way fire climbs upward
by its own form, born to rise
to where it lasts longest in its element,

so the captured soul enters into desire,
a motion of the spirit, and never rests
until the thing it loves is its own.

Now you can see how far the truth lies hidden
from those people who insist
that every love is, in itself, a good thing —

because the matter of love may well
look good; and yet not every imprint is good,
even when the wax is good."

"Your words, and my mind following close behind,"
I answered him, "have made love clear to me;
but they have only left me fuller of doubt.

For if love is offered to us from outside,
and the soul walks on no other foot than that,
then whether it goes straight or crooked is no merit of its own."

And he to me: "What reason can see here,
I can tell you; past that, you must wait
for Beatrice, for it is a matter of faith.

Every substantial form, set apart
from matter and yet bound up with it,
holds a specific power gathered in itself,

which is not felt while it lies still,
and shows itself only by its effects,
as life shows in a plant by its green leaves.

So no one knows where the understanding
of the first ideas comes from, or the pull
toward the first things we want —

which are in you as the instinct in the bee
to make its honey; and this first leaning
holds no praise in it, and no blame.

Now, so that every other wish may fall in with this one,
there is born in you the power that counsels,
whose task is to keep the threshold of consent.

This is the principle from which is drawn
the ground of your deserving, as it gathers in
and sifts the loves that are good and the loves that are guilty.

Those who reasoned their way to the bottom of it
saw this freedom, born in us,
and so they left the world its moral teaching.

Grant, then, that every love kindled in you
springs up by necessity:
the power to hold it back is still your own.

This is the noble power Beatrice means
by free will — so keep it in mind,
in case she speaks to you of it."

The moon, held back almost to midnight now,
made the stars look thinner to us,
shaped like a bucket all in flames,

and ran against the sky down those tracks
the sun sets blazing, when the man in Rome
sees it go down between Sardinia and Corsica.

And that noble shade, for whom Pietola
is named above any town of Mantua,
had set down the load I had laid on him;

so I, who had been given clear and open
reasoning in answer to my questions,
stood like a man drifting in a half-sleep.

But this drowsiness was suddenly taken
from me by a crowd of people who
had already swung up behind our backs.

And as, long ago, Ismenus and Asopus
saw along their banks at night the rush and press
whenever the Thebans needed Bacchus,

so these came rounding the curve of that ledge —
from what I saw of those drawing near —
spurred on by good will and rightful love.

They were on us in an instant, because
that whole vast crowd moved at a run,
and two in front cried out, weeping:

"Mary ran in haste up into the hill country,
and Caesar, to bring Ilerda to its knees,
struck at Marseilles, then ran on into Spain."

"Faster, faster! Don't let the time be lost
for want of love!" the others cried at once,
"for eagerness in doing good revives grace!"

"O people in whom a burning eagerness now
makes up, perhaps, for the delay and neglect
you paid to doing good through lukewarmness,

this man here is alive — and I'm telling the truth —
and wants to climb when the sun lights us again;
so tell us where the opening is, the nearest way."

These were the words of him who was my guide;
and one of those spirits said: "Come on
behind us, and you'll find the gap;

we are so full of longing to keep moving
that we cannot stop; so forgive us
if you take our justice for rudeness.

I was the Abbot of San Zeno at Verona
under the rule of good Barbarossa,
of whom Milan still speaks with grief.

And there is a man with one foot in the grave
already, who will soon mourn that monastery
and be sorry he ever held power there,

because in place of its true shepherd he set
his son — sick in his whole body,
worse in his mind, and base from birth."

Whether he said more, or fell silent, I don't know:
by then he had run so far past us.
But I heard this much, and was glad to keep it.

And he who was my help in every need
said: "Turn this way; look — there are two of them
sinking their teeth into sloth."

At the back of all of them they were shouting:
"The people for whom the sea was opened
died before their heirs could see the Jordan;

and those who would not bear the hardship
to the end with Aeneas, the son of Anchises,
gave themselves to a life without glory."

Then, when those shades had pulled so far
from us that they could no longer be seen,
a new thought found its way into me,

and out of it many others, all different, were born;
and I drifted so from one into the next
that, wandering, I let my eyes fall shut,

and turned my thinking into dream.
