The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXXIV

Inferno: Canto XXXIV34 of 100

"Vexilla regis prodeunt inferni
toward us. Look straight ahead," my Master said,
"and see if you can make him out."

The way a mill, far off, will seem to turn
when a thick fog comes breathing across the land,
or when our half of the world goes dark toward night —

I thought I saw a structure like that now;
and then, against the wind, I drew myself
behind my Guide, since there was no other shelter.

By now I had come — I set it down with dread —
to where the shades lay wholly buried in the ice,
showing through it like straws sealed inside glass.

Some of them lie flat, some stand upright,
one on his head, another on the soles of his feet,
and one bent like a bow, his face curved to his feet.

When we had gone so far ahead that it
now pleased my Master to reveal to me
the creature who was once so beautiful,

he stepped aside from in front of me and stopped me,
saying, "Look — there is Dis, and there's the place
where you must arm yourself with all your courage."

How frozen I went then, how the strength drained out of me,
don't ask me, Reader — I will not write it down,
because no words I have would be enough.

I did not die, and I did not stay alive.
Think for yourself now, if you have any wit,
what I became, stripped of both the one and the other.

The emperor of the kingdom of all grief
rose from the ice, out of his mid-chest up;
and I come closer in size to a giant

than the giants do to those great arms of his.
See for yourself how vast the whole must be
that answers to a part on such a scale.

If he was once as beautiful as he is now foul,
and raised his brow in rebellion against his Maker,
then rightly does all sorrow flow from him.

Oh, what a marvel it was to me to see
three faces set upon that single head!
The one in front, and that one was blood-red;

the other two were joined to this first face
above the middle of each shoulder,
and all three met together at the crown.

The right-hand face seemed something between white and yellow;
the left was such to look on as the men
who come from where the Nile runs down to the valley.

Beneath each face came two enormous wings,
as large as fit a bird of such a size —
no sails at sea were ever spread so wide.

They had no feathers; their make was like a bat's;
and he kept beating them, so that three winds
went streaming outward from him through the dark.

By these the whole of Cocytus was frozen solid.
With six eyes he was weeping, and down three chins
ran the tears mixed with bloody drool.

In each of the three mouths he was grinding a sinner
between his teeth, the way a brake crushes flax,
and so he kept three of them in torment at once.

For the one in front the biting was nothing
beside the clawing — there were times the spine
was left with all the skin torn off it.

"That soul up there who suffers the worst of all,"
my Master said, "is Judas Iscariot —
head inside the mouth, his legs kicking out.

Of the other two, who hang there head-down,
the one who dangles from the black face is Brutus —
look how he writhes and never says a word —

and the other, who looks so powerfully built, is Cassius.
But night is climbing back, and it is time
that we set out, for we have seen it all."

As he thought best, I clasped him round the neck,
and he watched for the moment and the place,
and when the wings had opened wide enough,

he took firm hold upon the shaggy flanks;
then down from tuft to tuft he climbed, between
the matted hair and the frozen crust of ice.

When we had reached the point where the thigh turns,
right at the swelling thickness of the hip,
my Guide, with great effort and hard-drawn breath,

swung his head around to where his legs had been,
and gripped the hair like a man who climbs upward,
so that I thought we were going back into Hell.

"Hold tight to me, for it is by stairs like these,"
my Master said, panting like a man worn out,
"that we must take our leave of so much evil."

Then he passed out through the opening of a rock
and set me down to sit upon its rim;
then he stepped carefully across to me.

I raised my eyes, expecting I would see
Lucifer the way I'd left him — and instead
I saw him holding his legs up in the air.

And if I stood there baffled then, let dull minds
think it through, the ones who fail to grasp
what point it was that I had just passed through.

"Get up onto your feet," my Master said.
"The way is long, the road is hard to travel,
and already the sun is climbing toward mid-morning."

It was no palace hallway where we stood,
but a rough cave the earth itself had made,
with a broken floor and almost no light at all.

"Before I tear myself out of this pit,
my Master," I said, once I was on my feet,
"set me straight on something — say a few words.

Where has the ice gone? And how is he fixed
upside down like this? And how, in so short a time,
has the sun crossed over from evening to morning?"

And he answered me: "You still imagine you are
on the far side of the center, where I gripped
the hair of the vile worm that bores through the world.

On that side you were, the whole time I climbed down;
but when I turned myself around, you passed
the point that all weight is drawn toward from every side.

Now you have come beneath the hemisphere
opposite the one that arches over
the great dry land — beneath whose height was killed

the Man who was born and lived without a sin.
Your feet are set upon the little sphere
that forms the other face of the Judecca.

Here it is morning when it is evening there;
and the one whose hair we made a ladder of
is still fixed fast, just as he was before.

On this side he fell headlong out of heaven;
and the land that once stood up above the surface here,
out of terror of him, made the sea its veil

and fled to our hemisphere; and maybe it was
to escape from him that what shows on this side
left its place here empty, and rushed back up."

There is a place down there, as far from Beelzebub
as the limit of his tomb, made known to no one
by sight, but only by the sound

of a little stream that trickles down there
through a hollow in the rock it has worn away
in its winding course, and it falls gently.

My Guide and I went onto that hidden road
to make our way back into the bright world;
and without troubling to rest at all,

we climbed — he first and I behind him second —
until I saw, through a round opening,
some of the lovely things the sky carries above us.

And from there we came out, to see again the stars.
