# The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto XIX

Now it appeared before me, wings spread wide —  
that lovely image whose sweet feast of joy  
made glad the souls woven into one.

Each one of them appeared a little ruby  
in which a ray of sun was burning so bright  
that each flung it back, kindled, into my eyes.

And what I now must trace for you again  
no voice has ever told, no ink has written,  
nor has the mind imagined it.

For I saw — and heard — the beak itself speak,  
and sound, in its voice, both "I" and "mine,"  
when in the thought behind it lay "we" and "ours."

And it began: "Because I was just and merciful  
I am lifted here into that glory  
which no desire can ever outreach;

and on earth I left a memory of myself  
so made that even the wicked there  
praise it — though they do not follow its example."

So a single heat is felt from many embers,  
and so, out of that image, from many loves  
there issued a single sound.

Then I: "O everlasting flowers  
of the eternal gladness, you who make  
your many fragrances seem one to me,

breathe now, and break this great fast in me  
that has held me hungry through a long season,  
finding no food for it on earth.

Well I know that if Divine Justice  
makes any other realm in heaven its mirror,  
yours takes it in without a veil.

You know how intently I make myself ready  
to listen; you know the very doubt  
that has been so old a fast in me."

Like a falcon let out from the hood,  
who lifts his head and claps his wings,  
showing his hunger, making himself fine —

so I saw that ensign become, woven  
out of the praise of grace divine,  
with songs known only to those who rejoice up there.

Then it began: "He who turned the compass  
round the world's outer edge, and within it  
set out so much, hidden and shown,

could not so stamp his power on all  
the universe but that his Word  
stayed, infinitely, in excess of it.

And this makes certain that the first proud one —  
who was the summit of all created things —  
fell unripe, for not waiting for the light.

And from this it shows that every lesser nature  
is too small a vessel for that good  
which has no end, and is its own measure.

So your sight, which must be no more  
than one ray of that Mind  
with which all things whatever are filled,

cannot by its own nature be so strong  
that it should not perceive its source  
to lie far past whatever it can see.

And so the vision your world is given  
sinks into the eternal justice  
the way the eye sinks into the sea:

from the shore it can make out the bottom,  
but on the open deep it cannot —  
and the bottom is there, only the depth conceals it.

No light exists but what comes from that calm  
which is never troubled; all the rest  
is dark — the shadow of the flesh, or its poison.

Now the hiding place is opened wide to you  
that kept the living justice out of your sight,  
the justice you questioned again and again.

For you would say: 'A man is born on the bank  
of the Indus, and there is no one there  
to speak of Christ, or read of him, or write;

and all his wishes and his deeds are good,  
as far as human reason can see,  
without a sin in his life or in his speech.

He dies unbaptized, and without faith.  
Where is the justice that condemns him?  
Where is his fault, if he never believed?'

Now who are you, to take the judge's bench  
and rule on what stands a thousand miles off  
with a sight that reaches barely a span?

Surely, for the man who splits hairs with me,  
if Scripture were not set over you,  
there would be marvelous cause for doubt.

O creatures of the earth! O thick-witted minds!  
The first Will, which is good in itself,  
has never moved from itself, the Highest Good.

Only what falls into accord with it is just.  
No created good draws it toward itself;  
it, raying out, is the cause of every good."

As the stork wheels above her nest  
once she has fed her young, and the one she fed  
looks up at her —

so the blessed image lifted, and I lifted  
my own brow with it, while it moved  
its wings, driven by so many counsels.

Wheeling, it sang, and said: "As my notes are  
to you, who cannot understand them,  
so is the eternal judgment to you mortals."

Then those shining fires of the Holy Spirit  
grew quiet — still within the standard  
that once made Rome revered across the world —

and it began again: "Into this kingdom  
no one ever climbed who had no faith in Christ,  
before or after he was nailed to the tree.

But look: many cry out, 'Christ, Christ!'  
who at the judgment will stand far less near  
to him than some who never knew Christ;

and the Ethiopian will condemn such Christians  
when the two companies are parted —  
one rich forever, the other poor.

What could the Persians say to your kings  
when they see that volume thrown open  
in which all their disgraces stand written?

There, among the deeds of Albert,  
will be the one that soon sets the pen moving,  
for which the realm of Prague is laid waste.

There will be seen the grief he brings the Seine  
by debasing the coin — he who will die  
from the blow of a wild boar.

There will be seen the pride that breeds such thirst  
it drives the Scot and the Englishman mad,  
so neither can keep within his own border.

Seen too the lust and the soft life  
of him of Spain and of Bohemia,  
who never knew valor and never wished to.

For the Cripple of Jerusalem, his good  
will be marked down with a single I,  
while an M will stand for the opposite.

Seen too the greed and the cowardice  
of the one who guards the Island of Fire,  
where Anchises ended his long life;

and to show how little the man amounts to,  
his record will be set in shrunken letters  
that note much in a narrow space.

And plain to all will be the filthy deeds  
of the uncle and the brother who dishonored  
so famed a nation, and two crowns.

And he of Portugal, and he of Norway,  
will be known there, and he of Rascia too,  
who in an evil hour aped the coin of Venice.

O happy Hungary, if only she will let  
herself be wronged no more! And happy Navarre,  
if she armed herself with the hills that gird her!

And let everyone believe that even now,  
as first installment, Nicosia and Famagosta  
wail and rage because of their own beast,

who never strays from the others' flank."
