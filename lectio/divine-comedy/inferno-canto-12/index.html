The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XII

Inferno: Canto XII12 of 100

The place we came to, where we had to climb down the bank,
was all jagged rock, and what was waiting there as well
was a thing every eye would shrink from.

Like the landslide that struck the flank of the Adige
on this side of Trento — whether it was an earthquake
or the ground simply gave way underneath —

where the cliff broke off from the top of the mountain
and crashed all the way down to the plain,
leaving a kind of rough path for anyone coming from above:

that was what the way down into this ravine was like.
And there, stretched out along the edge of the broken gorge,
lay the shame of Crete,

the one conceived inside the counterfeit cow.
When he caught sight of us, he bit into himself,
like a creature eaten alive by its own rage.

My sage called out to him: "Maybe you think
this is the Duke of Athens come again,
the man who killed you up in the world above.

Get away, beast. This one isn't here
schooled by your sister.
He comes to see what your kind suffer."

The way a bull breaks loose the very instant
it takes the killing blow and can't walk straight,
but lurches and staggers this way and that —

that was the Minotaur, the way I saw him reel.
And my watchful guide cried: "Run for the gap.
While he's blind with fury, this is the time to go down."

So we picked our way down over that spill of stones,
which kept shifting and sliding under my feet
because they had never felt a living weight.

I went on deep in thought, and he said: "You're wondering,
maybe, about this ruin, guarded by the brute fury
I just now put out.

I want you to know: the last time I came down
this far into the lower Hell,
this cliff had not yet fallen.

But if I read it right, it was a little
before He came who carried off from Dis
the great spoils of the highest circle,

that this deep and reeking valley shook so hard
on every side that I thought the universe
had felt the pull of love — the love that some believe

has turned the world back into chaos more than once.
And it was at that moment this ancient crag
came down, here and in other places too.

But look down now, because the river of blood
is getting close — the one that boils
whoever harmed others by violence."

O blind greed, O insane wrath,
goading us so hard through this short life of ours,
and then steeping us so cruelly in the one that never ends!

I saw a wide moat curved like a bow,
the kind that loops around to take in the whole plain,
just as my guide had told me.

And between it and the foot of the bank,
Centaurs were running single file, armed with arrows,
the way they used to hunt in the world above.

Seeing us coming down, every one of them stopped,
and three broke off from the troop,
arrows already chosen and bows ready.

And one called from a distance: "You there, coming down
the slope — what punishment are you headed for?
Tell me from where you stand, or I let the arrow fly."

My master answered: "We'll give our answer
to Chiron, there beside you. Your will was always
too quick for your own good."

Then he touched me and said: "That one is Nessus,
who died for the lovely Deianira
and made his own revenge out of his own death.

The one in the middle, staring down at his chest,
is the great Chiron, who raised Achilles.
The other is Pholus, who was so full of rage.

By the thousands they circle the moat,
shooting any soul that lifts itself out of the blood
farther than its guilt allows."

We came up close to those swift creatures.
Chiron took an arrow, and with the notch
he pushed his beard back clear of his jaws.

When he had uncovered his huge mouth,
he said to his companions: "Have you noticed
that the one behind moves whatever he touches?

The feet of the dead don't do that."
And my good guide, standing now at his breast,
where the two natures join,

answered: "Yes, he's alive, and he is all alone;
it's my task to show him the dark valley.
Necessity brings us here, not pleasure.

Someone left off singing Hallelujah
to hand me this new charge.
He is no thief, and I'm no thieving spirit.

But by the power that moves my steps
along this savage road,
give us one of your own to go with us,

to show us where the ford is
and carry this one across on his back,
since he's no spirit that can walk on air."

Chiron wheeled to his right and said to Nessus:
"Turn back and guide them, and if you meet
another band, warn them off."

So with our trusted escort we moved on
along the edge of the boiling red,
where the boiled were screaming out loud.

I saw people sunk in it up to the eyebrows,
and the great Centaur said: "These are tyrants,
who dealt in blood and plunder.

Here they weep for their merciless wrongs.
Here is Alexander, and fierce Dionysius,
who gave Sicily its years of grief.

That forehead there with the hair so black
is Ezzelino. The fair-haired one beside him
is Obizzo d'Este, who — and this is the truth —

was murdered up in the world by his own stepson."
Then I turned to the poet, and he said:
"Let him lead now; I come second."

A little farther on the Centaur stopped
above some people who seemed to rise
out of the boiling stream only as far as the throat.

He pointed out one shade off by itself, and said:
"That one split open, in the very lap of God,
the heart that is still honored on the Thames."

Then I saw others who lifted their heads
and all their chest above the river,
and many of them I knew.

Bit by bit the blood grew shallower,
until it covered nothing but their feet,
and there was where we crossed the moat.

"Just as on this side, where you're looking,
you see the boiling stream getting lower and lower,"
the Centaur said, "I want you to understand

that on the other side it sinks deeper and deeper,
until it comes back around to the place
where tyranny is made to groan.

Divine justice is goading, on that side,
that Attila who was a scourge on the earth,
and Pyrrhus, and Sextus; and forever it draws out

the tears that the boiling unlocks
from Rinier da Corneto and Rinier Pazzo,
who made such war on the open roads."

Then he turned back and recrossed the ford alone.
