# The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto XVII

Like the one who came to Clymene to be sure  
of what he had heard said against himself —  
he who still makes fathers wary of their sons —

such was I, and so I was perceived  
by Beatrice and by the holy light  
that had already shifted its place for me.

And so my Lady said: "Send out the flame  
of your desire, and let it come forth  
stamped clearly with the seal that is inside you —

not so our knowledge can be made the greater  
by your speaking, but so you grow used  
to telling your thirst, and we can pour you drink."

"O my dear root, lifted so high  
that just as earthly minds can see  
no triangle can hold two obtuse angles,

so you behold all things that may or may not be  
before they are themselves, by fixing your eyes  
on the point where every time is now —

while I was still joined with Virgil,  
climbing the mountain that heals the soul,  
and going down into the world of the dead,

grave words were spoken to me about  
the life ahead — though I feel myself  
squared like a tower against the blows of chance.

And so my wish would be content  
to learn what fortune is coming toward me:  
an arrow seen in advance falls slower."

So I spoke to that same light  
that had spoken to me before; and just  
as Beatrice willed, I confessed my own will.

Not in the cloudy riddles that once  
ensnared the foolish crowd, before  
the Lamb of God was slain who lifts our sins,

but in clear words and plain, unclouded speech  
that fatherly love gave its answer,  
hidden and shown at once within its own smile:

"Chance, which does not reach beyond  
the pages of your material world,  
is painted whole in the eternal sight —

yet it draws no necessity from that,  
any more than a ship that rides the current down  
is driven by the eye that watches it.

From there, the way sweet music reaches the ear  
from an organ, the time that is being readied  
for you comes into my sight.

As Hippolytus was driven out of Athens  
by his cruel and lying stepmother,  
so you must leave Florence. This is willed already;

already it is plotted, and soon  
it will be done by the one who schemes it  
there where Christ is bought and sold each day.

The blame, as always, will follow the side  
that was wronged, in the loud public cry; but the vengeance  
will bear witness to the truth that deals it out.

You shall leave behind everything you love  
most dearly. This is the first arrow  
the bow of exile lets fly.

You shall learn how salt is the taste  
of another man's bread, and how hard the path  
going down and climbing up another man's stairs.

And what shall weigh heaviest on your shoulders  
will be the vile and witless company  
you will fall with into this valley;

for they will turn ungrateful, mad, and faithless  
against you — but before long it is they,  
not you, whose brows will burn red with the shame.

Their own conduct will prove their brute folly,  
and it will stand well for you  
to have made yourself a party of one.

Your first refuge and your first inn  
will be the great Lombard's courtesy —  
the one who bears the holy bird on the ladder.

He will look on you so kindly  
that between you two, in giving and in asking,  
what comes last with others will come first.

With him you shall see one so stamped at birth  
by this strong star that his deeds  
will be worth remembering.

The people do not yet know him,  
because of his young years — these wheels  
have turned only nine times around him.

But before the Gascon cheats the noble Henry,  
some sparks of his worth will show:  
he will care nothing for money or for toil.

His greatness will become so known  
in time to come that even his enemies  
will not be able to hold their tongues.

Look to him, and to what he gives.  
Through him many lives will be transformed,  
rich and poor changing places;

and you shall carry written in your mind  
what I say of him — but not repeat it." And he told  
things past belief to those who would live to see them.

Then he added: "Son, this is the gloss  
on what was said to you — these are the snares  
hidden behind a few turnings of the years.

Yet do not envy your neighbors for it,  
since your life will reach far into the future,  
beyond the punishment of their treachery."

When that holy soul showed by its silence  
that it had finished laying the woof  
into the web I had set up warped for it,

I began, like a man who longs,  
in his doubt, for counsel from someone  
who sees, and wills uprightly, and loves:

"I see well, my father, how time  
spurs toward me to deal me a blow  
that falls heaviest on the one who yields most.

So it is good I arm myself with foresight,  
so that if the dearest place is taken from me,  
I do not lose the rest through my own songs.

Down through the world of endless bitterness,  
and over the mountain from whose lovely summit  
my Lady's eyes lifted me,

and afterward through heaven, from light to light,  
I have learned what, if I tell it again,  
will taste like bitter herbs to many.

And if I am a timid friend to truth,  
I fear I'll lose my life among those  
who will one day call this time the old days."

The light in which my found treasure was smiling  
flashed first, the way a golden mirror  
catches and throws back the sun;

then it answered: "A conscience darkened  
by its own shame or another's  
will surely find your words harsh.

But all the same — let every lie fall away,  
make your whole vision plain,  
and let them scratch wherever it may itch.

For if your voice is bitter at the first taste,  
it will leave vital nourishment behind  
once it has been digested.

This cry of yours will do as the wind does,  
striking hardest at the highest peaks —  
and that is no small mark of honor.

That is why, within these wheels,  
upon the mountain and in the grieving valley,  
only the souls already known to fame are shown you;

for the hearer's mind will not be settled,  
nor will it ground its faith on an example  
whose root lies hidden and unknown,  
nor on any other proof that does not show."
