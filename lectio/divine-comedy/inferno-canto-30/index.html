The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXX

Inferno: Canto XXX30 of 100

Back when Juno was burning with rage
at the blood of Thebes, on Semele's account,
as more than once she let the city feel,

Athamas went so wholly out of his mind
that, seeing his wife come walking toward him,
a child held heavy on either arm,

he cried, "Spread out the nets! Let me trap
the lioness and her cubs at the pass" —
and he stretched out his merciless claws,

snatched up the first, the one called Learchus,
and swung him round and smashed him on a rock;
and she, with the other load, drowned herself.

And when Fortune turned and brought low
the pride of Troy, that had dared everything,
so that the king was crushed with his kingdom,

Hecuba — wretched, broken, taken captive —
once she had seen her Polyxena dead
and, there on the seashore, made out at last

the body of her Polydorus, the grieving woman
lost her wits and barked like a dog,
so far had the pain wrenched her mind awry.

But no fury of Thebes or of Troy
was ever seen so savage in any soul —
goring brute beasts, much less human limbs —

as the two I saw, pale shades and naked,
biting and running the way a hog runs
the moment the gate of its pen springs open.

One reached Capocchio and sank its teeth
in the nape of his neck, then dragged him off
so his belly scraped along the hard floor.

And the Aretine, who had stayed behind
shaking, said to me, "That mad imp is Gianni
Schicchi, and he goes raging, mauling people."

"Oh," I said to him, "as you hope the other one
never sets its teeth in you, be so kind,
tell me who it is before it darts away."

And he to me: "That is the ancient soul
of Myrrha, the wicked one, who made herself
her father's lover, against all rightful love.

She came to sin with him by this same trick —
falsifying herself in another's shape,
just as that one going off over there dared

to win for himself the prize mare of the herd:
he counterfeited himself as Buoso Donati,
drawing a will and giving it legal form."

And once the two rabid ones had run past —
the pair I had been holding in my eye —
I turned it to look at the other ill-born souls.

I saw one shaped like a lute, if only
he had been cut off at the groin,
there where a man divides into two.

The heavy dropsy, which throws the body
so out of true with its ill-digested fluid
that the face no longer answers to the belly,

kept his lips forced apart from each other
the way a fevered man's are, who in his thirst
curls one toward his chin, the other up.

"O you who go without any torment
through this grim world — and I don't know why —"
he said to us, "look, and pay attention

to the misery of Master Adam.
Alive, I had all I could wish for;
now, God help me, I beg one drop of water.

The little brooks that run from the green hills
of the Casentino down into the Arno,
making their channels cool and moist —

they stand before me always, and not for nothing:
their image dries me out far worse
than the disease that strips the flesh from my face.

The rigid justice that scourges me
draws on the very place where I sinned
to set my sighs flying all the faster.

Romena lies there — where I falsified
the coin that bore the stamp of the Baptist,
for which I left my body burned above.

But if I could see down here the wretched soul
of Guido, or Alessandro, or their brother,
I'd not trade the sight for Fonte Branda's spring.

One is in already, if the raving
shades that go round about are telling the truth —
but what's the use, with my limbs tied like this?

If I were only still light enough
that in a hundred years I could move one inch,
I'd have set out already down the road,

hunting for him through this disfigured crowd,
even though the ring runs eleven miles around
and is no less than half a mile across.

It's because of them I'm in such a family:
they talked me into striking the florins
that carried three carats of dross."

And I to him: "Who are the two poor wretches
lying close against your right-hand border,
steaming like a wet hand in winter?"

"I found them here," he answered, "when I rained
down into this pit, and they've not turned since,
nor will they, I think, for all eternity.

One is the false woman who accused Joseph;
the other is Sinon, the lying Greek of Troy:
the sharp fever makes them reek like that."

And one of them, stung at being named
so darkly, perhaps, took offense,
and punched him on his hardened paunch.

It boomed like a drum; and Master Adam
struck him back, full in the face,
with an arm that seemed no less hard,

saying, "Even if I've lost the use
of my legs, weighed down as they are,
I've got an arm free for work like this."

To which he answered, "When you marched
into the fire, you didn't have it so ready —
though you had it, and more, when you were minting."

The dropsical one: "There you tell the truth;
but you weren't half so true a witness
back when they questioned you for the truth at Troy."

"If I spoke false, you falsified the coin,"
said Sinon. "I'm here for a single sin —
you for more than any other devil!"

"Remember the horse, you perjurer,"
the one with the swollen belly shot back,
"and may it gall you the whole world knows it!"

"And may the thirst that cracks your tongue gall you,"
the Greek said, "and the rotten water
that walls your gut up there before your eyes!"

Then the coiner: "So your mouth gapes wide
to speak its evil, the way it always does;
for if I've got thirst, and the fluid stuffs me,

you've got the burning and the splitting head —
and to lap up the mirror of Narcissus
you'd hardly need much coaxing to begin."

I was wholly fixed on listening to them
when the Master said to me, "Just keep staring —
and it won't take much before I quarrel with you!"

When I heard him speak to me in anger
I turned toward him with such shame
that it still goes circling through my memory.

And like a man who dreams some harm to himself
and, dreaming, wishes it were only a dream,
so that he longs for what is, as if it weren't —

that's what I became, unable to speak:
I wanted to excuse myself, and all the while
was doing it, and never thought I was.

"Less shame than yours would wash a greater fault
than this of yours has been," the Master said;
"so unburden yourself of all this sadness,

and remember I am always at your side,
should it ever happen that Fortune brings you
where there are people in a brawl like this:

for the wish to listen to it is a low wish."
