The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto II

Paradiso: Canto II69 of 100

You who are out in a little boat,
hungry to listen, who have followed
behind my ship as it sails along singing —

turn back now and look for your own shores.
Don't push out onto the open sea: lose me,
and you may find yourselves lost too.

These waters I sail have never been crossed.
Minerva fills my sail, Apollo steers,
and the nine Muses point me to the Bears.

But you few who lifted your necks early
toward the bread of the angels — the bread
one lives on here, and never has enough of —

you may put your vessel out on the deep salt
without fear, holding to my wake
while the water it cuts smooths flat again.

Those heroes who crossed the sea to Colchis
were not as struck with wonder as you will be,
the day they saw Jason turned into a plowman.

The thirst born in us with our birth, the thirst
for that God-shaped kingdom, carried us up
almost as fast as you watch the sky turn.

Beatrice was gazing upward, and I at her;
and in about the time an arrow takes
to strike, to fly, to leave the string —

I saw I had come to where a wonder
pulled my eyes to itself; and she, from whom
no turn of my mind could ever be hidden,

swung toward me, as glad as she was lovely,
and said: "Lift your mind to God in thanks —
he has brought us up to the first star."

It seemed a cloud had closed around us, bright
and dense, packed solid and shining hard
as a diamond struck by the sun.

Into itself the eternal pearl
took us in, the way water takes in
a ray of light and stays unbroken.

If I was still a body — and down here
we can't conceive how one dimension makes room
for another, as it must when body enters body —

all the more should the longing burn in us
to see that being in which we can watch
how God and our own nature were made one.

There we will see what we now take on faith,
not proved out but self-evident,
like the first truth a person simply knows.

I answered: "Lady, as devoutly
as I am able, I give thanks to him
who has lifted me out of the mortal world.

But tell me — what are the dark markings
on this body, that make people back on earth
tell that old fable about Cain?"

She gave a slight smile, then: "If the opinion
of mortals goes wrong," she said, "there
where the key of the senses can't unlock the door,

then the arrows of surprise should not be piercing you
now — you can see for yourself how reason,
when it trails the senses, flies on short wings.

But tell me what you yourself make of it."
And I: "What looks to us, up here, like difference,
is caused, I think, by matter dense and thin."

And she: "You'll see your belief sunk deep
in error, if you'll only listen well
to the argument I'm about to set against it.

The eighth sphere shows you lights past counting,
and you can mark in them, by their quality
and their quantity, faces that differ.

If thin and dense alone were the cause,
then one single power would run through them all,
shared out more, or less, or evenly.

Different powers have to be the fruit
of formative principles — and by your reasoning
all but one of those would be destroyed.

Besides: if thinness were the cause
of this dimness you ask about, then either
this planet is starved of matter clean through,

or else, the way a body parcels out
its fat and its lean, this one would trade
thick layers and thin across its bulk.

If the first were true, it would show in an eclipse
of the sun — the light would shine straight through,
the way it passes anything sheer.

It does not. So we turn to the other case;
and if I take that one apart as well,
then your opinion stands proven false.

If this thinness does not run clean through,
there has to be a limit, past which
its opposite blocks any further passage,

and there the borrowed light bounces back,
the way a color returns to you from glass
that hides a backing of lead behind it.

Now you'll say the sunbeam shows itself
dimmer there than it does in the other parts,
because it's thrown back from farther in.

From that reply experiment can free you,
if you ever try one — experiment, the spring
that feeds the rivers of all your arts.

Take three mirrors. Set two of them
the same distance from you; let the third,
farther off, meet your eyes between the two.

Turning toward them, have a light set up
behind your back to fill the three mirrors
and come back to you reflected from them all.

The farthest image won't be as large,
but you will see it shine back at you
just as bright as the nearer ones do.

Now: as the ground beneath the snow comes bare
under the touch of the warm rays, stripped
of the snow's old color and its cold —

so I will inform your mind, left bare like that,
with such a living light
that it will tremble as you look at it.

Inside the heaven of the divine repose
a body turns, and in its power lies
the being of all the heaven holds.

The next heaven down, the one with so many eyes,
divides that being among its many essences,
distinct from it, yet held within it.

The other spheres, by their various differences,
order the distinctions each one carries
toward their own ends and their own effects.

So these organs of the world go on,
as you can see now, grade by grade:
taking from above, acting below.

Watch closely how I work my way through here
to the truth you want, so that from now on
you'll know how to keep this ford alone.

The power and the motion of the holy spheres
must breathe down from the blessed movers,
as the craft of the hammer comes from the smith.

The heaven that so many lights make lovely
takes its image from the deep Mind that turns it,
and of that image makes itself a seal.

And just as the soul inside your dust
spreads itself out through different organs,
each shaped to a different faculty,

so this Intelligence unfolds its power,
multiplied among the stars,
while it keeps revolving on its own oneness.

A different power makes a different alloy
with the precious body it brings to life,
bound into it as life is bound in you.

And out of the glad nature it comes from,
the mingled power shines through the body,
the way gladness shines through a living eye.

From this comes all that, from light to light,
looks different — not from dense and thin.
This is the formative principle that makes,

out of its own goodness, the dark and the bright."
