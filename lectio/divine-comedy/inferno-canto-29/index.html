The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXIX

Inferno: Canto XXIX29 of 100

So many people, and such ragged wounds,
had left my eyes so drunk with what they saw
that I wanted only to stand still and weep.

But Virgil said: "What are you staring at?
Why does your sight keep fastening down there
among the mournful, mutilated shades?

You didn't do this at the other ditches.
If you mean to count them, think: this valley
winds around for twenty-two full miles.

And now the moon is already under our feet.
The time we're given grows short from here on,
and there is more to see than what you see."

"If you had paid attention," I replied,
"to the reason I was looking down so hard,
you might have let me stay a little longer."

My guide had already moved on, and I went
behind him, still making my reply,
and adding to it: "In that hollow

where I held my eyes so fixed just now,
I think a spirit of my own blood mourns
the sin that costs so much down in that place."

Then the master said: "Don't let your thoughts
break themselves on him any longer.
Attend to other things; let him stay there.

I saw him below the little bridge,
pointing at you, threatening hard with his finger,
and heard him called Geri del Bello.

You were so taken up at that moment
with the one who once held Hautefort
that you never looked his way; so he left."

"O my guide, it was his own violent death,
still not avenged," I said to him,
"by anyone who shares in the disgrace,

that made him scornful — and so he went off,
as I imagine, without speaking to me,
and by that he made me pity him the more."

We talked like this until we reached the first place
on the ridge that would have shown the next valley
clear to its floor, if there had been more light.

When we stood right over the last cloister
of Malebolge, and its lay brothers
could begin to show themselves to our sight,

strange wails came piercing through and through me,
their arrows barbed with pity,
and I covered my ears with both my hands.

Imagine the pain if all the sick
in the hospitals of the Valdichiana, between
July and September, and of Maremma

and Sardinia, were heaped in one ditch together —
that was the suffering here, and a stench rose from it
like the smell that comes off rotting limbs.

We climbed down onto the last bank
of the long ridge, keeping to the left,
and then my sight grew sharper, reaching

down toward the bottom, where the minister
of the high Lord, unerring Justice,
punishes the forgers she records here.

I don't think it was sadder to see
the whole people of Aegina sick —
when the air was so thick with plague

that the animals, down to the smallest worm,
all dropped, and afterward the ancient people
(so the poets have told us)

were remade from the seed of ants —
than it was to watch, through that dark valley,
the spirits languishing in heap after heap.

One lay on his belly, one on the back
of another, and some were dragging themselves
on hands and knees along the dismal track.

Step by step we went on without a word,
looking at and listening to the sick
who hadn't the strength to lift their bodies.

I saw two propped against each other,
the way one pan leans on another to warm,
blotched with scabs from head to foot.

I never saw a stable-boy work a currycomb
so fast, with his master standing there waiting,
or a man kept up against his will,

as each of these kept driving the bite
of his nails into himself, in the fury
of an itch that had no other relief.

And the nails dragged the scabs down off them
the way a knife scrapes the scales from a bream,
or any fish with scales still larger.

"You there, unscaling yourself with your fingers,"
my guide began to one of them,
"and making pincers of them now and then,

tell me if any Italian is among
those down here — and may your nails hold out
at this work for you through all eternity."

"We're Italians, both of us, the two
you see so wasted here," one answered, weeping.
"But who are you, asking about us?"

And my guide said: "I am one who goes down
with this living man from ledge to ledge,
and I mean to show him Hell."

Then their mutual prop broke apart,
and each turned to me, trembling, with others
who had caught the word as it rebounded.

The good master drew close to me, saying,
"Tell them whatever you want to ask."
And I began, since he wished it so:

"So may your memory not fade away
out of the minds of men in the first world,
but live on under many suns,

tell me who you are, and of what people.
Don't let your foul and loathsome punishment
make you afraid to show yourselves to me."

"I was from Arezzo," one of them replied,
"and Albert of Siena had me burned;
but what I died for isn't what brought me here.

It's true I told him, half in jest,
that I knew how to lift myself through the air —
and he, with curiosity and little wit,

wanted me to show him the trick. And only
because I couldn't make him a Daedalus,
he had a man who took him for a son

burn me. But to the last ditch of the ten,
for the alchemy I practiced in the world,
Minos, who never errs, condemned me."

And I said to the poet: "Was there ever
a people as vain as the Sienese?
Not even the French come close."

At which the other leper, who had heard me,
answered my words: "Except for Stricca,
who knew how to spend with such restraint;

and Niccolò, who was first to discover
the lavish use of cloves
in the garden where such seed takes root;

and except for the club where Caccia d'Ascian
threw away his vineyards and his great woods,
and Abbagliato showed off his wit!

But so you'll know who backs you up like this
against the Sienese, sharpen your eye
toward me, so my face can answer you,

and you'll see I am the shade of Capocchio,
who falsified the metals by alchemy;
and you must remember, if I read you right,

what a good ape of nature I was."
