The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto XIII

Paradiso: Canto XIII80 of 100

Let anyone who wants to truly grasp
what I saw now hold the image fast
while I speak, steady as a rock,

and let him imagine the fifteen stars
that light the sky from their scattered regions
so brightly no haze of air can dim them;

let him imagine the Wain, the Plough,
for which our vault of heaven is room enough
night and day, so that wheeling its pole it never sets;

let him imagine the mouth of the Horn
that opens at the tip of the axis
the first wheel turns around —

and picture all of these stars composing
two signs in the sky, like the crown that Minos'
daughter shaped the moment she felt death's cold;

one with its rays inside the other's,
both of them wheeling in such a way
that one runs forward, the other back —

and he will have some shadow of the true
constellation and the double dance
that circled the point where I was standing;

for it is as far beyond anything we know
as the swiftest heaven, that outruns them all,
outruns the sluggish current of the Chiana.

There they sang no Bacchus, no Apollo,
but Three Persons in the divine nature,
and in one person the divine and human.

The singing and the dancing kept their measure,
and those holy lights turned their care toward us,
growing in joy from one bright care to the next.

Then the silence of those saints in concord
was broken by the light that had told me
of God's own beggar, the wonder of his life,

and it said: "Now that one sheaf is threshed,
now that its grain is already gathered in,
sweet love invites me to thresh out the other.

You believe that into the breast from which
the rib was drawn to shape the lovely face
whose taste is costing all the world so dear,

and into the one the lance pierced through,
which made, for sins before it and since,
such amends it outweighs the whole scale of wrong —

you believe that whatever light human nature
is allowed to hold was poured into them both
by the same Power that created each;

and so you wonder at what I said above,
when I told you that nothing ever rivaled
the good locked in the fifth light here.

Now open your eyes to what I answer,
and you will see your belief and my words
meet in the truth like a center in a circle.

What can die and what cannot die
are nothing but the radiance of the Idea
our Lord, in his love, brings into being;

for that living Light, that streams so bright
from its source it is never cut off
from Him, nor from the Love that makes them Three,

through its own goodness gathers its rays
into nine subsistences, as into a mirror,
remaining forever and wholly One.

From there it descends to the last powers,
step by step, act after act, until at the end
it makes only the briefest, passing things —

and by passing things I mean what is generated,
what the wheeling heavens bring to birth
out of seed, and without seed too.

Neither their wax nor the hand that stamps it
stays the same; and so beneath the ideal
seal the imprint shines through more, or less.

That is why a single tree of one kind
will bear worse fruit and better, and why
you are each born with your own grain.

If the wax were drawn out to perfection
and the heaven stood at its highest power,
the whole brilliance of the seal would show;

but nature hands it down always short,
working the way the artist works
whose hand trembles though his craft is sure.

Yet where the burning Love and the clear Vision
of the first Power lay down the seal themselves,
there absolute perfection is achieved.

So once the earth was made worthy to hold
the whole perfection of a living creature;
so the Virgin was made to carry her Son.

And so I grant you what you believe —
that human nature never was, nor will be,
what it was in those two persons.

Now, if I stopped here and went no further,
'Then how was Solomon without a peer?'
would be the first words out of your mouth.

So that what isn't clear may come clear,
think who he was, and what moved him to ask
when he was told, 'Ask whatever you want.'

I have not spoken so that you can't see
plainly: he was a king, and he asked for wisdom
enough to be the king he needed to be —

not to count the number of the movers
turning up here, or whether a necessary
and a contingent make a necessary,

not whether you can grant a first uncaused motion,
or whether a triangle can be drawn
inside a half-circle without a right angle.

So if you weigh this with what I said before,
that peerless seeing is a king's own prudence —
that is the mark my arrow was aimed at.

And if you turn a clear eye on the word 'rose,'
you'll see it points to kings, and only kings —
there are many of them, and good ones are rare.

Take what I said with that distinction drawn,
and it can stand beside what you believe
of our first father and of our Delight.

And let this always be lead at your feet,
to make you, like a tired man, move slowly
toward the Yes and the No you cannot see;

for he ranks low among the fools
who affirms or denies without distinction,
in the one case as much as in the other,

because it happens, again and again,
that hasty opinion leans the wrong way,
and then feeling ties down the mind.

Worse than uselessly he leaves the shore —
for he does not come back the man he was —
who fishes for the truth and has no skill.

And the open proofs of this in the world
are Parmenides, Melissus, Bryson,
and all the rest who set out not knowing where;

so too Sabellius, Arius, and those fools
who were like swords held up to Scripture,
reflecting its straight face back twisted.

And let no one be too sure of himself
in judging — like the man who counts the grain
in the field before it is ripe;

for I have seen the thornbush all winter
show itself stiff and savage to the touch,
and then carry the rose on its crown,

and I have seen a ship run straight and fast
across the sea the whole length of its course,
only to go down at the harbor's mouth.

Don't let Dame Bertha or Sir Martin think,
because they watch one man steal and one give alms,
that they have seen them inside the divine counsel;

for the one may yet rise, and the other fall."
