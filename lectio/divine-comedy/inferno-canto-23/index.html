The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXIII

Inferno: Canto XXIII23 of 100

Silent, alone, with no one near, we walked,
one in front, the other behind,
the way the Minor Friars go down a road.

The brawl we'd just seen turned my mind
to Aesop's fable, the one
where he tells of the frog and the mouse;

for "now" and "this instant" are no closer a match
than the one tale is to the other,
if you hold beginning and end side by side.

And as one thought springs straight from another,
so out of this one a second was born,
and it doubled the fear already in me.

I was thinking: these demons are mocked
because of us, and hurt as well,
and it has to be galling enough to enrage them.

If their malice takes on a coat of anger,
they'll come after us more savagely
than a dog on the hare it snaps up.

I could already feel my hair standing on end
with terror, and I kept glancing back as I said:
"Master, unless you hide the two of us,

and fast, I'm afraid of the Malebranche —
they're right behind us now. I picture them
so clearly I can already feel them coming."

And he: "If I were a mirror
I couldn't take in your outward look
faster than I take in the one inside you.

Just now your thoughts arrived among mine,
with the same bearing, the same face,
so that out of both I made one plan.

If the right bank lies at enough of a slope
that we can climb down to the next ditch,
we'll escape the chase you imagine."

He hadn't finished laying out the plan
when I saw them come, wings spread wide,
not far off, and bent on seizing us.

All at once my Guide snatched me up,
the way a mother woken by noise
sees the flames already burning beside her

and grabs her child and runs, never stopping,
caring more for him than for herself,
not even pausing to throw on more than a nightgown;

and down from the top of the hard bank's edge
he gave himself, flat on his back, to the sloping rock
that walls one side of the next ditch.

Water never ran so fast down a sluice
to drive the wheel of a land-built mill,
even where it crowds hardest against the paddles,

as my Master rode down that bank,
carrying me along on his chest
like his own son, not a companion.

His feet had barely touched the floor of the ravine
when the demons reached the ridge
directly above us — but now he had nothing to fear,

for the high Providence that set them
as keepers of the fifth ditch
had stripped from them all power to leave it.

Down there we found a painted people,
circling with very slow steps,
weeping, and worn out, and beaten in their faces.

They wore cloaks with the hoods pulled low
over their eyes, cut in the same fashion
they make the monks' robes in Cologne.

Outside, they're gilded so it dazzles you;
but inside, all lead, and so heavy
that the cloaks Frederick used were straw beside them.

What an exhausting robe to wear forever!
We turned again, still bearing left,
keeping pace with them, listening to their misery;

but the weight made that weary crowd
so slow that with every swing of the hip
we found ourselves beside new company.

So I said to my Guide: "Look for someone
known by name or by deed,
and keep your eyes moving as we go."

And one of them, who caught the Tuscan speech,
cried out from behind us: "Hold your feet,
you two, running so fast through the dim air!

Maybe you'll get from me what you're after."
At that my Guide turned and said: "Wait,
and then go on at his own pace."

I stopped, and saw two whose faces showed
how much their spirits hurried to reach me;
but the load and the narrow path slowed them down.

When they came up, they eyed me a long while,
sidelong, without a word.
Then they turned to each other and said:

"By the working of his throat, this one's alive;
and if they're dead, by what privilege
do they walk uncovered by the heavy stole?"

Then to me: "Tuscan, you who've come
to the college of wretched hypocrites,
don't disdain to tell us who you are."

And I told them: "I was born and grew up
in the great city on the fair Arno,
and I'm here with the body I've always had.

But who are you, with such grief
running down your cheeks as I see now?
And what is the pain on you, that glints like that?"

And one answered: "These orange cloaks
are made of lead so thick that the weight
makes us creak like overloaded scales.

We were Jovial Friars, both Bolognese —
I Catalano, and he Loderingo
by name — chosen together by your city,

the way it used to choose one man alone
to keep its peace; and what we were
is still plain to see around the Gardingo."

"O Friars," I began, "the evil you—"
but I said no more, for there before my eyes
lay one crucified to the ground with three stakes.

When he saw me he writhed his whole body,
blowing into his beard with his sighs;
and Friar Catalano, who noticed it,

said to me: "This nailed-down man you're looking at
counseled the Pharisees that it was fitting
to put one man to torture for the people.

He lies across the path, naked,
as you can see, and he has to feel
the weight of everyone who passes, first.

And his father-in-law is racked the same way
in this ditch, and the rest of that council
that was an evil seed for the Jews."

And then I watched Virgil stand amazed
over the one stretched out on the cross
so shamefully in eternal exile.

After that he turned this question to the Friar:
"Don't be displeased, if it's allowed, to tell us
whether some passage slopes off to the right

by which the two of us can get out of here
without forcing any of the black angels
to come and haul us up from this pit."

He answered: "Closer than you'd hope
there's a ridge that runs out from the great wall
and crosses every one of these cruel ditches —

except that here it's broken, and bridges nothing;
you'll be able to climb up the rubble,
which slopes along the side and piles up below."

My Guide stood a while with his head bowed,
then said: "He gave us a bad account of it,
that one back there who hooks the sinners."

And the Friar: "Once at Bologna I heard
a good many of the Devil's vices listed,
and one was that he lies, and fathers all lies."

At that my Guide went on with great strides,
his face somewhat disturbed with anger;
and I left the heavy-laden behind

to follow the prints of his beloved feet.
