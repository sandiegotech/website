The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto XVI

Paradiso: Canto XVI83 of 100

O our poor little nobility of blood —
if you can make us glory in you down here,
where our affection runs thin and weak,

it will never strike me as a wonder again;
for up there, where appetite is never warped —
I mean in Heaven — even I boasted of you.

Truly you are a cloak that quickly shortens,
so that, unless we add to you day by day,
Time keeps circling with his shears.

With "you" — the formal address that Rome was first
to allow, though her own people keep it least —
I began my words to him once more;

and Beatrice, standing a little apart,
smiled, and looked like the lady who coughed
at the first slip of Guinevere's the old tale records.

I began: "You are my own ancestor.
You give me all the boldness I have to speak.
You lift me so high I am more than myself.

So many streams of gladness fill my mind
that it makes a joy of itself, simply
because it can hold all this and not burst.

So tell me, my beloved root and stock,
who your forefathers were, and what the years were
that marked themselves down in your boyhood.

Tell me about the sheepfold of Saint John —
how large it was back then, and who the people
were within it who deserved the highest seats."

As a coal quickens into flame when the wind
blows over it, so I watched that light
grow radiant under my words of love;

and as it grew more beautiful to my eyes,
with a voice sweeter and gentler — but not in
this modern speech of ours — it said to me:

"From the day 'Ave' was spoken to the birth
in which my mother, who is a saint now,
was eased of me, the burden she had carried,

this fire of Mars had come back to its Lion
five hundred and fifty times, and thirty more,
to blaze again beneath the Lion's paw.

My fathers and I were born where the runners
in your yearly race first reach the last ward
of the city, at the close of the course.

Let it be enough to hear that of my elders.
Who they were, and where they came from to settle there —
silence becomes that better than speech.

All who, in those days, lived between Mars
and the Baptist, fit to bear arms, were
a fifth of the number living there now;

but the citizen body, which today is mixed
with blood from Campi, Certaldo, and Figline,
ran pure then, down to the lowest craftsman.

How much better it would be to have those
as your neighbors — and to set your boundary out
at Galluzzo and Trespiano —

than to keep them inside the walls, and stomach
the stink of the boor from Aguglione, and that man
from Signa whose eye is already sharp for fraud!

Had the people who, more than any on earth,
degenerate, not played stepmother to Caesar,
but been a kind mother to her son,

then certain men now turned Florentine —
who trade and lend at interest — would have gone
back to Simifonte, where their grandfathers begged.

Montemurlo would still belong to the Counts,
the Cerchi would be in Acone's parish,
the Buondelmonti, maybe, in Valdigreve.

The mixing of one people with another
has always been the sickness of a city,
the way too much food sickens the body;

and a blind bull goes down more headlong
than a blind lamb; and very often one sword
cuts better and deeper than five.

If you look hard at Luni and Urbisaglia,
how they have passed away, and how Chiusi
and Sinigaglia are passing after them,

then to hear how whole bloodlines wear away
will seem to you no strange thing, and no hard one —
since even cities come to their end.

Everything of yours has its own death,
just as you do; but in some it stays hidden,
because they last long and your lives are short.

And as the turning of the heaven of the moon
covers and bares the shorelines without rest,
so fortune does with Florence.

So it should not seem any marvel to you,
what I am about to say of the great Florentines
whose fame lies buried now in the past.

I saw the Ughi, and I saw the Catellini,
the Filippi, the Greci, the Ormanni, the Alberichi —
illustrious citizens even as they fell;

and I saw, as mighty as they were ancient,
with the man of La Sannella the man of Arca,
and the Soldanieri, the Ardinghi, the Bostichi.

Near the gate that is now weighed down
with a fresh treachery, a load so heavy
it will soon be thrown from the ship,

the Ravignani lived — from whom came down
Count Guido, and everyone since who has taken
the name of the great Bellincione.

The man of La Pressa already knew
the art of governing; and already Galigaio
had gilded hilt and pommel in his house.

Already the Column of Vair was mighty;
the Sacchetti, the Giuochi, Fifanti, Barucci,
and Galli, and the men who blush for the bushel.

The stock the Calfucci sprang from was
already great; and already the Sizii and Arrigucci
had been drawn up into the curule chairs.

O how I saw them — those now undone
by their own pride! And how the Balls of Gold
flowered Florence into all her mighty deeds!

So too did the forefathers of those
who, every time your church stands empty,
grow fat by lingering in the consistory.

That insolent breed, which plays the dragon
to whoever runs, but to the man who shows
his teeth or his purse turns lamb-gentle,

was already rising — but up from low stock;
so it did not please Ubertin Donato
that his wife's father made them kin to him.

Already Caponsacco had come down
to the Market from Fiesole; and already
Giuda and Infangato were good burghers.

I'll tell you a thing past belief, but true:
you entered the small old ring of walls
by a gate that took its name from the Della Pera!

Every man who bears the lovely arms
of the great baron whose name and renown
the feast of Thomas keeps alive each year

had his knighthood and his rank from him —
though the man who rings that shield with a border
sides with the common people today.

Already the Gualterotti and the Importuni
were there; and the Borgo would be quieter still
if it had gone unfed by these new neighbors.

The house your grief is born from — through the just
resentment that brought death among you
and put an end to your glad days —

was honored then, it and its kin.
O Buondelmonte, in what an evil hour
you fled that marriage at another's urging!

Many would be glad who now are grieving,
if God had given you up to the Ema
the first time you ever rode toward the city.

But it was owed to that battered stone
that guards the bridge — Florence had to give
a victim, there, in her last hour of peace.

With all these houses, and others beside them,
I saw Florence in such deep repose
she had no cause to weep for anything;

with all these houses I saw her people
so just and so glorious that the lily
was never set reversed upon the staff,

nor turned to red by faction."
