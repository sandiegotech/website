The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXI

Inferno: Canto XXI21 of 100

So on from bridge to bridge we came, talking of other things
my Comedy does not care to sing,
and we had reached the top of the arch, when

we stopped to look down into the next cleft
of Malebolge, into more useless weeping;
and I saw it astonishingly dark.

The way, in the Arsenal of the Venetians,
the sticky pitch boils all winter long
for caulking their unsound ships again —

for they cannot sail, and so instead
one builds his boat anew, one packs the seams
in the ribs of a hull worn out by many voyages;

one hammers at the prow, one at the stern,
this one shapes oars, that one twists the rope,
another patches the mainsail and the jib —

just so, not by fire but by a divine art,
a thick pitch boiled down there in the ditch
and caked every face of the bank with glue.

I saw it, but inside it I saw nothing
except the bubbles the boiling threw up,
the whole mass swelling and sinking back, packed tight.

While I was staring fixedly down there,
my Leader cried out, "Watch it — watch out!"
and pulled me to him from where I stood.

Then I spun around, like a man on edge
to see the thing he has to run from,
a sudden terror draining the nerve from him,

who looks but does not slow his leaving —
and behind us I saw a black devil
come running up along the ridge of rock.

Ah, how savage he looked in the face!
And how cruel he seemed to me in his moves,
with his wings spread and light on his feet!

Across his shoulders — sharp-pointed and high —
he had a sinner slung by both hips,
and he gripped him hard by the tendons of the feet.

From our bridge he shouted, "Malebranche!
Here's one of the Elders of Saint Zita —
shove him under, while I go back for more

to that town of his, so well stocked with them.
Every man there is a barrator but Bonturo;
there, for cash, a No is turned to Yes."

He pitched him down, then wheeled on the hard crag,
and no mastiff was ever slipped its leash
in such a hurry to run down a thief.

The sinner went under, then surfaced face-up;
but the demons crouched beneath the bridge
yelled, "No Holy Face does any good down here!

This is not how you swim back in the Serchio!
So unless you want a taste of our hooks,
don't go lifting yourself above the pitch."

Then they jabbed him with a hundred grappling-hooks
and said, "Down here you dance under cover —
so filch on the sly, if you've still got the knack."

No differently, the cooks have their scullions
plunge the meat down into the middle of the pot
with their forks, so none of it floats to the top.

The good Master said to me, "So they don't
spot that you're here, hunker down
behind a jag of rock, get yourself some cover;

and whatever outrage they do to me,
don't be afraid — I know how this goes;
I've been in this kind of brawl before."

Then he crossed beyond the head of the bridge,
and the moment he set foot on the sixth bank
he needed all the steadiness he had.

With the same fury, the same uproar,
as dogs that come tearing out at a beggar
who stops short and starts begging on the spot,

they rushed out from under the little bridge
and turned all their hooks against him at once;
but he cried, "None of you get vicious!

Before those hooks of yours lay hold of me,
let one of you step up and hear me out,
and then decide whether to gaff me."

They all yelled, "Send Malacoda!" — so
one of them came forward, the rest held still,
and he came up saying, "What good will this do him?"

"Do you think, Malacoda," my Master said,
"that you see me arrived this far in spite
of all your skill at arms,

without the will of Heaven and a favoring fate?
Let me pass — for it is willed above
that I show another this savage road."

At that his arrogance fell so flat in him
that he let the hook drop at his feet
and told the others, "Now — nobody strike him."

And my Guide called to me, "You there, crouched down
among the splinters of the bridge —
it's safe now, come back to me."

So I roused myself and came to him quick;
and all the devils lunged forward at once,
so I was afraid they'd break the truce.

I had seen fear like it once — the soldiers
marching out under safe-conduct from Caprona,
finding themselves surrounded by so many foes.

I pressed myself, every inch of me,
close against my Leader, and never took
my eyes off their faces — and the faces were bad.

They leveled their hooks, and "Want me to nick him
on the rump?" they said to one another,
and answered, "Sure — give him a good one."

But the same demon who had been parleying
with my Guide turned around fast
and said, "Easy, easy, Scarmiglione!"

Then he told us, "There's no going forward
any farther along this crag, because
the sixth arch lies in pieces at the bottom.

But if you still want to go on ahead,
follow the line of this rocky ridge —
there's another crag nearby that makes a path.

Yesterday, five hours past this very hour,
one thousand two hundred sixty-six years
came full since the road here was broken through.

I'm sending some of mine off that way
to see if anyone's airing himself above —
go along with them; they won't play dirty.

Step up, Alichino and Calcabrina,"
he started shouting, "and you, Cagnazzo;
Barbariccia, you take charge of the ten.

Come on, Libicocco and Draghignazzo,
and tusked Ciriatto and Graffiacane,
and Farfarello and crazy Rubicante.

Range all around the boiling pitch;
keep these two safe as far as the next crag
that runs unbroken over all the dens."

"Oh no — Master, what is it I'm seeing?"
I said. "Please, let's go without an escort,
if you know the way — I'm not asking for one.

If you're as watchful as you usually are,
don't you see how they're grinding their teeth,
threatening us trouble with their brows?"

And he to me, "I won't have you afraid;
let them grind away to their hearts' content —
they do it for those wretches boiling in the pitch."

They wheeled off along the left-hand bank;
but first each one had pressed his tongue
between his teeth at their leader, for a signal —

and he had made a trumpet of his rear.
