The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XIX

Purgatorio: Canto XIX53 of 100

It was the hour when the heat left over from the day
can no longer warm the coldness of the moon,
overpowered by the earth, or sometimes by Saturn —

the hour when the diviners of the earth
see their Greater Fortune rise in the east,
before the dawn, on a path soon lit with day —

when there came to me, in dream, a stammering woman,
her eyes asquint, her feet turned crookedly,
her hands lopped short, her color a sallow gray.

I looked at her; and as the sun brings back
the frozen limbs the night has dulled with cold,
just so my gaze unbound her stumbling tongue,

and then, in a little while, set her wholly straight,
and brought into her lost and faded face
the very flush that love would wish to see.

And once her speech was freed in this way,
she began to sing, so that it would have cost me
an effort to pull my thoughts away from her.

"I am," she sang, "I am the sweet Siren,
the one who strands the sailors out at sea —
so full am I of pleasure to the ear.

I drew Ulysses from his wandering course
with my song; and whoever takes up with me
rarely leaves, so deeply do I content him."

Her mouth had not yet closed again before
a lady appeared beside me — holy, alert —
right at my side, to throw the Siren into shame.

"Virgil, O Virgil! who is this?" she said,
her voice severe; and he came toward us,
his eyes still fixed upon that decent one.

She seized the other and laid her open in front,
ripping her clothes, and showed me her belly —
and the stench that poured from it woke me up.

I turned my eyes, and good Virgil said:
"Three times at least I've called you. Rise and come;
let us find the opening you are to enter by."

I rose; and already the broad light of day
filled all the circles of the Sacred Mountain,
and we went on with the new sun at our backs.

Following behind him, I carried my forehead
bent like a man weighed down with thought,
who makes of himself the half-arch of a bridge,

until I heard a voice say, "Come, the passage is here,"
spoken in a way so gentle and so kind
as we never hear within this mortal country.

With open wings that looked to be a swan's,
the one who spoke to us turned us upward,
between the two walls of the solid granite.

Then he moved his pinions and fanned us,
declaring "Qui lugent" to be blessed,
for their souls shall be filled with comfort.

"What troubles you, that you keep staring at the ground?"
my Guide began to say to me, once we both
had climbed a little way beyond the Angel.

And I: "What sends me on so uneasily
is a new vision, which bends me to itself,
so that I cannot pull my mind from it."

"Did you see," he said, "that old enchantress,
the one for whom alone, above us, they now weep?
Did you see how a man is freed from her?

Let it be enough — strike the earth with your heels,
and lift your eyes up to the lure that the Eternal King
spins around through the vast turning of the heavens."

As the hawk first looks down at its own feet,
then turns toward the call and stretches forward,
drawn by the hunger for the food held out,

so I became; and so, as far as the rock
is cleft to give a path to one who climbs,
I went on up to where the circling begins.

When I had come out onto the fifth terrace,
I saw people upon it, weeping,
stretched face-down upon the ground, all turned to earth.

"Adhaesit pavimento anima mea,"
I heard them say, with sighs so deep
that the words could barely be made out.

"O chosen of God, whose sufferings
both Justice and Hope make less harsh to bear,
direct us toward the high ascents."

"If you come here safe, exempt from lying prone,
and want to find the quickest way up,
keep your right hands always to the outside."

So the Poet asked, and so the answer came
from one a little ahead of us; and from
what was said I made out the rest, unspoken,

and turned my eyes to meet my Lord's;
and with a glad sign he gave his consent
to what the look of my longing was asking.

When I could do as I wished, at last,
I drew myself above that creature
whose words had first made me take notice,

and said: "O spirit, in whom weeping ripens
that without which there is no turning back to God,
set aside your greater care a moment for me.

Who were you, and why are your backs turned upward?
Tell me — and tell me whether you would have me win you
anything back there, where I set out alive."

And he to me: "Why heaven turns our backs
up toward itself, you shall learn; but first
scias quod ego fui successor Petri.

Between Sestri and Chiavari there comes down
a lovely river, and from its name
my family takes the title of its rank.

A month and a little more I tried how heavy
the great cloak weighs on one who keeps it clear of mud —
beside it, every other burden feels like down.

Late, alas, was my conversion;
but once I had been made the Roman Shepherd,
then I found out how life is a lie.

I saw that the heart finds no rest there,
and that in that life one can climb no higher;
and so the love of this life caught fire in me.

Until that hour I was a wretched soul,
cut off from God, and wholly given to greed;
now, as you see, I am punished here for it.

What greed does is laid bare here,
in the cleansing of these souls who have turned;
the Mountain has no more bitter pain than this.

Just as our eyes never lifted themselves
on high, but stayed fastened on earthly things,
so Justice here has sunk them into the earth.

As greed put out our love
for every good, and so our doing came to nothing,
so Justice here holds us in its grip,

bound and made fast by the feet and the hands;
and for as long as it pleases the just Lord,
we shall lie here, motionless and prone."

I had gone down on my knees, and meant to speak;
but just as I began, and he became aware,
by the listening alone, of my reverence,

"What has bent you down like this?" he said.
And I to him: "For your own dignity
my conscience stung me to stand no longer upright."

"Straighten your legs, rise up, brother,"
he answered. "Make no mistake: I am a fellow-servant
with you and with the rest, under one Power.

If you have ever taken in the holy gospel sound
that says they neither marry nor are given in marriage,
you can see plainly why I speak this way.

Now go; I would not have you stay here longer,
for your staying gets in the way of my weeping,
with which I ripen what you spoke of.

Back on earth I have a niece named Alagia,
good in herself — unless, indeed, our house
should by its bad example make her otherwise —

and she is all that is left to me on earth."
