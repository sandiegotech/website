The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXVI

Inferno: Canto XXVI26 of 100

Rejoice, Florence, since you are so great
that over sea and land you beat your wings,
and your name is spread through Hell itself!

Among the thieves I found five of your citizens —
five, and the shame of it comes back on me,
and you rise to no great honor by it either.

But if the dreams that come near morning are true,
you'll feel before long what Prato craves for you,
and others too — and it won't be too soon.

If it had come already, it wouldn't be early.
Let it come, then, since come it must —
for it will weigh on me more, the older I grow.

We left, and up along the jutting stones
that had carried us down before,
my guide remounted, and drew me after him.

And following that lonely path
among the splinters and ridges of the crag,
the foot got nowhere without the help of the hand.

I grieved then, and I grieve again now
when I turn my mind back to what I saw,
and I rein in my gift more than I'm used to,

so that it won't run on unless virtue guides it —
so that if a good star, or something better,
has granted me good, I won't begrudge myself the having.

As many as the fireflies the farmer sees
resting on the hillside in the season
when the one who lights the world hides his face the least,

in the hour when the fly gives way to the gnat —
as many as he sees flickering down the valley,
there, maybe, where he plows and gathers his grapes,

with that many flames the eighth ditch
was glittering all over, as I saw
the moment I came to where the bottom showed.

And the way the one avenged by the bears
saw Elijah's chariot at its leaving,
when the horses reared and climbed straight up to heaven,

and he could not follow it with his eyes,
could make out nothing but the flame alone
rising like a little cloud into the sky —

just so each flame moved along the throat of the ditch,
not one of them showing the soul it stole,
and every flame had a sinner hidden inside.

I stood on the bridge, leaning so far out to see
that if I hadn't caught hold of a rock,
I'd have gone over without a push.

And my guide, who saw me straining like that,
said: "Inside those fires are the spirits.
Each is wrapped in the flame that burns him."

"My master," I said, "now that I've heard you
I'm sure of it — but I'd already guessed
it might be so, and I wanted to ask you:

who is in that fire, the one that comes
so split at the top it seems to rise
from the pyre where Eteocles burned with his brother?"

He answered: "In there, tormented together,
are Ulysses and Diomedes — paired in their pain
as once they were paired in their wrath.

And inside their flame they groan now
for the ambush of the horse, that made the gate
through which Rome's noble seed came out;

in there they weep the trick that still,
even in death, makes Deidamia mourn Achilles,
and there they pay for the Palladium too."

"If they can still speak inside those sparks,"
I said, "master, I beg you, and beg again —
let my asking be worth a thousand askings —

don't refuse to wait until that horned flame
comes near to us. You see how I lean toward it,
bent the whole way with wanting."

And he to me: "Your asking deserves
all praise, and so I grant it — only
keep a guard on your own tongue.

Leave the speaking to me. I understand
what you want to know. They were Greeks,
and might hold themselves above your words."

When the flame had come to where my guide
judged the time and place were right,
I heard him speak to it like this:

"You two who move as one inside that fire —
if I earned anything from you while I lived,
if I earned from you anything, much or little,

when I wrote my high verses in the world,
don't go on. Let one of you tell
where, having lost himself, he went to die."

Then the taller horn of that ancient flame
began to shudder, murmuring,
the way a flame does when the wind wears at it.

And working its tip back and forth
as if it were a tongue that spoke,
it flung out a voice and said: "When I

broke away from Circe, who'd kept me hidden
more than a year near Gaeta —
before Aeneas had given the place that name —

not my fondness for my son, not duty
to my old father, not the love I owed
that should have made Penelope glad,

could put down in me the burning to go out
and know the world, to learn it whole,
the worth and the worthlessness of men.

So I set out on the deep open sea
with one ship only, and the small company
that had never once deserted me.

I saw both shores, as far as Spain,
as far as Morocco, the island of Sardinia,
and the others that sea washes round.

We were old by then, and slow, my crew and I,
when we came at last to that narrow throat
where Hercules set up his warning markers

so that no man should sail past that point.
Seville fell away behind me on the right;
on the other hand I had already left Ceuta.

'Brothers,' I said, 'who through a hundred thousand
dangers have made it here, to the West —
to this last little watch our senses keep,

to the small waking time we have left,
don't choose to deny yourselves the knowledge
of the unpeopled world beyond the sun.

Consider where you came from. You were not made
to live like beasts,
but to follow after virtue and knowledge.'

With this short speech I left my companions
so hungry to be on their way
that after it I could hardly have held them back.

And turning our stern toward the morning,
we made wings of our oars for the reckless flight,
bearing always to the left as we went.

Night already saw all the stars
of the other pole, and ours so low
they never lifted clear of the ocean floor.

Five times the light beneath the moon
had kindled and gone dark again
since we had entered on that deep crossing,

when a mountain rose before us, dim
with distance, and it looked to me so high
I had never seen the like of it.

We were glad — and the gladness turned soon to weeping,
for out of that strange land a whirlwind came
and struck the ship hard on the bow.

Three times it spun her round with all the water;
on the fourth it heaved the stern up high
and drove the prow down, as Another willed,

until the sea had closed over us again."
