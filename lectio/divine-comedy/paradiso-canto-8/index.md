# The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto VIII

The world, in its old peril, used to believe  
that lovely Venus, wheeling in the third  
epicycle, rayed down mad love on men;

so the ancient peoples in their ancient error  
gave her not only the honor of their cries  
and sacrifices;

they worshipped Dione too, as her mother,  
and Cupid as her son, and told  
how he had once sat in Dido's lap;

and from her, with whom I now begin, they took the name  
of the planet's light that courts the sun,  
sometimes trailing behind it, sometimes running on before its face.

I never felt us rise into that sphere,  
but I believed we had arrived, because  
I saw my Lady grow more beautiful.

And as a spark is seen within a flame,  
and as one voice is heard inside another  
when one holds steady and one comes and goes,

so in that light I saw still other lights  
circling, some swifter and some slow, each one,  
I think, by the measure of its inner sight.

No wind, seen or unseen, has ever fallen  
from a cold cloud so fast that it would not  
seem stalled and laboring to anyone

who had seen those divine lights come toward us,  
breaking off the great ring they had begun  
among the high Seraphim, to greet us.

And from the ones that came in front there rose  
a "Hosanna!" so sweet that ever since  
I have not stopped longing to hear it again.

Then one drew nearer to us than the rest,  
and it alone began: "We are all ready,  
eager to serve your pleasure, that you may rejoice in us.

We wheel here with the heavenly Princes, all  
in one ring and one turning and one thirst,  
the ones to whom you said, once, in the world,

'You who, with your minds, move the third heaven' —  
and we are so full of love that, to please you,  
a little stillness will be just as sweet."

After my eyes had lifted to my Lady  
in reverence, and she had made them sure  
and satisfied of her own steady will,

they turned back to the light that promised me  
so much of itself; and "Tell me, who are you?"  
was all I said, stamped with deep affection.

Oh, how I saw it brighten and grow large  
with the new gladness added to its joy  
the very moment that I finished speaking!

So changed, it said to me: "The world below  
held me a short time only; had it been longer,  
much coming evil never would have been.

My gladness keeps me hidden from your eyes,  
for it rays out around me and conceals me  
the way a creature wraps itself in silk.

You loved me deeply, and you had good cause;  
for had I stayed below, I would have shown you  
far more than the first leaves of all my love.

That left bank, which the river Rhone bathes  
once it has mingled with the Sorgue, awaited  
me in due time to be its rightful lord;

and so did that horn of Italy, walled with towns —  
Bari, and Gaeta, and Catona — down  
to where the Tronto and the Verde reach the sea.

Already on my brow there flashed the crown  
of the land the Danube waters, once  
it has left the German borders far behind;

and lovely Sicily — Trinacria, dark  
with smoke between Pachino and Peloro,  
along the gulf that Eurus lashes hardest,

not from Typhoeus, but from rising sulphur —  
would still be waiting for her own true kings,  
born through me from Charles and from Rudolph,

if a hard misrule, which always embitters  
the peoples it bears down on, had not driven  
Palermo to scream its outcry, 'Death! Death!'

And if my brother could have foreseen this,  
he would already flee the grasping want  
of Catalonia, before it cost him dear;

for truly someone must take care, through him  
or through another, that no further weight  
be loaded on a boat already overladen.

His nature, fallen from a generous line  
to a covetous one, would need a kind of men  
who do not care to stuff their coffers full."

"My Lord, because I believe the deep delight  
your words pour into me is seen by you  
there, where each good thing begins and ends,

just as I see it here, it is more welcome;  
and this too I hold dear — that you discern it  
while gazing straight upon the face of God.

You have made me glad; now make this clear to me,  
for in your speaking you have stirred my doubt:  
how can a sweet seed bring forth bitter fruit?"

This I asked him; and he: "If I can show you  
one truth, then what you now hold at your back  
you'll have in front of you, before your eyes.

The Good that turns and fills the whole bright realm  
you are ascending makes its providence  
a working power within these mighty spheres;

and not the natures only are foreseen  
within that Mind which is perfect in itself,  
but, with the natures, all that keeps them whole.

So whatever this bow looses into being  
falls toward a foreseen and fitting end,  
like an arrow aimed straight at its mark.

Were it not so, the heaven you walk would shape  
its effects in such a way that they would be  
not works of art at all, but only ruins;

and that cannot be, unless the Intelligences  
that move these stars are flawed — and flawed as well  
the First Mind that has failed to make them perfect.

Would you have this truth made still clearer to you?"  
And I: "No need; for I can plainly see  
that nature never tires in what is needful."

And he again: "Now tell me — would it be worse  
for a man on earth if he lived as no part of a city?"  
"Yes," I said, "and here I ask no reason why."

"And can he be one, unless men live below  
in different ways, for offices that differ?  
No — not if your own master writes it true."

So step by step his reasoning arrived,  
and then he closed: "It follows, then, that the roots  
of what you each become must be diverse:

so one man is born a Solon, one a Xerxes,  
one a Melchizedek, and one the man  
who flew through the air and lost his son in falling.

The wheeling heavens, the seal on mortal wax,  
practice their art and practice it with skill,  
but do not tell one cradle from another;

so it comes about that Esau differs  
in the seed from Jacob, and Quirinus springs  
from a father so base he is given to Mars.

A nature, once begotten, would always run  
the road its parents ran before it, if  
the providence of God did not prevail.

Now what was at your back stands plain before you;  
but so that you may know I take delight  
in you, I'll wrap you in a corollary:

always, when a nature meets a fortune  
out of tune with it, like any seed  
far from its native ground, it comes to no good.

And if the world below would only fix its mind  
on the foundation nature lays,  
and follow that, its people would be good.

But you wrench toward the cloister  
one born to buckle on the sword,  
and make a king of one fit for the pulpit;

and so the track you walk runs off the road."
