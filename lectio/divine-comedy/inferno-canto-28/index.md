# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXVIII

Who could ever, even in words set free of meter,  
tell in full of the blood and of the wounds  
I saw now, however many times he told it?

Every tongue would surely fall short,  
because our speech and our memory  
have too little room to hold so much.

If you gathered all the people again  
who once, on Apulia's fated ground,  
mourned for the blood that the Romans spilled,

and for the long-drawn war that made  
such towering spoils of fallen rings —  
as Livy records, and he does not err —

with those who felt the agony of blows  
standing against Robert Guiscard, and all the rest  
whose bones are gathered to this day

at Ceperano, where every Apulian turned traitor,  
and there at Tagliacozzo, where old Alardo  
won the field without lifting a weapon —

and if each one showed his pierced or severed limb,  
it would still be nothing beside  
the foul condition of the ninth ditch.

No cask, its stave or its end-piece knocked loose,  
was ever split open the way I saw one shade  
torn from the chin to the place a man breaks wind.

Between his legs his entrails hung;  
you could see his heart, and the wretched sack  
that turns to filth whatever is swallowed.

While I stood there caught up in staring,  
he looked at me, and with his hands  
he pulled his chest open, saying: "See how I rip myself!

See how mangled Mohammed is!  
In front of me Ali goes weeping,  
his face split open from the forelock to the chin.

And all the others you see here  
were, in life, sowers of scandal and of schism,  
and for that they are cleft this way.

A devil stands behind us and carves us  
this cruelly, putting each one of us  
back to the edge of his blade

once we have rounded the road of grief;  
because our wounds have closed again  
by the time we come around in front of him.

But who are you, dawdling there on the ridge,  
putting off, perhaps, the punishment  
pronounced upon your own confessions?"

"Death has not reached him yet, nor does guilt  
bring him here to be tormented,"  
my master answered, "but to give him full experience;

and it falls to me, who am dead, to lead him  
down here through Hell, from circle to circle —  
and this is as true as that I speak to you."

More than a hundred of them, hearing this,  
stopped in the ditch to look at me,  
so amazed they forgot their torture.

"Now, you who will likely see the sun before long,  
tell Fra Dolcino, if he doesn't want  
to follow me down here soon,

to stock himself with food, so that no siege of snow  
hands the victory to the men of Novara,  
which otherwise would not come easy."

After he had raised one foot to leave,  
Mohammed said these words to me,  
then set the foot back down and went.

Another, whose throat had been pierced through,  
his nose hacked off right up to the brows,  
and who had only a single ear left,

stayed behind, staring in wonder with the rest,  
and before the others he opened his windpipe,  
which on the outside ran red everywhere,

and said: "O you whom no guilt damns,  
and whom I once saw up in the Italian land —  
unless too close a likeness deceives me —

remember Pier da Medicina,  
if you ever see again that lovely plain  
that slopes from Vercelli down to Marcabò.

And let the two best men of Fano know —  
Messer Guido, and Angiolello too —  
that, unless the foresight here proves empty,

they will be flung from their ship  
and drowned near La Cattolica,  
betrayed by a vicious tyrant.

Between the islands of Cyprus and Majorca  
Neptune never saw so great a crime,  
not from pirates, not from the men of Argos.

That traitor who sees with only one eye,  
and holds the land that one here with me  
wishes he had never set eyes on,

will summon them to come and parley with him,  
then will so arrange it that against Focara's wind  
they'll have no need of vow or prayer."

And I said to him: "If you want me  
to carry news of you above, show me, tell me,  
who is the man so bitter at the sight of that land."

Then he laid his hand on the jaw  
of one of his companions, forced the mouth open,  
and cried: "This is the one, and he cannot speak.

Driven into exile, he drowned every doubt  
in Caesar, swearing that a man prepared  
always pays a price for delay."

O how stricken he looked to me,  
his tongue slit through inside his throat —  
Curio, who once was so bold in speech!

And one who had both his hands lopped off  
lifted the stumps through the murky air  
so the blood smeared and fouled his face,

and cried out: "Remember Mosca too,  
who said — God help me — 'A thing done has an end!'  
which was the seed of ruin for the Tuscan people."

"And death to your own line," I added;  
and he, piling grief upon grief,  
went off like a man gone sick and mad.

But I stayed on to watch the crowd,  
and saw a thing I would be afraid  
even to tell of, with no further proof,

were it not that conscience reassures me —  
that good companion that gives a man courage  
under the armor of its own clean feeling.

I truly saw it, and I seem to see it still:  
a trunk without a head, walking along  
just like the others in that miserable herd.

And it held the severed head by the hair,  
swinging it from its hand like a lantern,  
and the head looked at us and said: "Oh me!"

Of itself it made a lamp for itself,  
and they were two in one, and one in two —  
how that can be, He knows who ordains it.

When it had come right to the foot of the bridge,  
it raised its arm high, head and all,  
to bring its words up closer to us,

which were: "Now look at this, my brutal punishment,  
you who go breathing through the realm of the dead —  
see if any is as heavy as mine.

And so you may carry word of me,  
know that I am Bertran de Born, the one  
who gave the Young King his evil counsel.

I set the son and the father against each other.  
Ahithophel did no worse to Absalom  
and David with his wicked goading.

Because I parted people so closely joined,  
I carry my brain, alas, parted from its root,  
which is here in this trunk.

So in me you see the counterpass fulfilled."
