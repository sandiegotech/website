The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XV

Inferno: Canto XV15 of 100

Now one of the hard stone banks carries us on,
and the mist off the brook hangs over it,
shielding the water and the dikes from the fire.

The way the Flemings, between Cadsand and Bruges,
dreading the flood that hurls itself toward them,
throw up their walls to drive the sea back,

and the way the Paduans along the Brenta
build to protect their towns and their estates
before Chiarentana feels the thaw —

in that same fashion these banks had been raised,
though not so high and not so thick,
whoever the master was who made them.

By now we were so far from the wood
that I could not have made out where it lay
even if I had turned around to look.

Then we came on a company of souls
coming along beside the bank, and each one
eyed us the way men eye one another

at dusk under a new moon, and they
sharpened their gaze toward us, narrowing it
the way an old tailor squints at the needle's eye.

Stared at so hard by that whole crowd,
I was recognized by one of them, who caught
me by the hem and cried, "What a wonder!"

And when he reached his arm out toward me,
I fixed my eyes so hard on his baked face
that the scorched skin did not keep

my mind from knowing who he was;
and bending my own face down to his,
I answered: "Are you here, ser Brunetto?"

And he: "Don't take it amiss, my son,
if Brunetto Latini turns back a little
with you, and lets the file go on ahead."

I said to him: "With all my heart I ask it;
and if you want me to sit down with you,
I will — if he allows it, for I go with him."

"My son," he said, "whoever of this herd
stops for a moment lies a hundred years
without a hand to fan the fire that strikes him.

So keep moving. I'll come along at your skirts,
and afterward rejoin my band, who go
weeping over their everlasting loss."

I did not dare step down from the bank
to walk level with him, but I held my head
bowed, like a man going in reverence.

And he began: "What fortune, what fate
brings you down here before your last day?
And who is this who shows you the way?"

"Up there above, in the bright life,"
I answered him, "I lost myself in a valley
before my years had run their full course.

Only yesterday morning I turned my back on it.
This one appeared to me as I was sliding back,
and he leads me home along this road."

And he to me: "If you follow your star,
you cannot fail to reach a glorious harbor,
if I read you right in that beautiful life.

And if I had not died so soon,
seeing Heaven so kind to you,
I would have stood by you in the work.

But that thankless, malignant people,
who came down long ago from Fiesole
and still smack of the mountain and the rock,

will turn against you for your good deeds —
and rightly so: among bitter sorb-trees
it is no place for the sweet fig to bear fruit.

Old rumor in the world calls them blind:
a grasping people, envious and proud.
See that you keep yourself clean of their ways.

Your fortune holds such honor in store
that both parties will be hungry for you —
but the grass will be kept far from the goat.

Let the beasts of Fiesole make litter
of their own carcasses, and keep their hands
off the plant — if any still grows on their dunghill —

in which the holy seed may live again
of those Romans who stayed behind when
it was made the nest of so much malice."

"If my prayer were granted in full,"
I answered him, "you would not yet
be cast out from human life;

for fixed in my mind, and aching in me now,
is your dear, good, fatherly face
as you were in the world, when hour by hour

you taught me how a man makes himself eternal;
and how grateful I am for it, while I live,
must be heard in the words I speak.

What you tell me of my road I'm writing down,
and keeping it to be read beside another text
by a Lady who can do that, if I reach her.

This much I want you to know plainly:
so long as my conscience does not reproach me,
I am ready for whatever Fortune brings.

Such a forecast is nothing new to my ears.
So let Fortune turn her wheel as she likes,
and the peasant swing his mattock."

At that my master turned his head
back over his right cheek and looked at me,
then said: "He listens well who marks it."

Still, I kept walking and talking with
ser Brunetto, and asked him who were
the best-known and highest of his companions.

And he to me: "Of some it is good to know;
of the rest it would be better to keep silent,
for the time is short for so much talk.

Know in brief that they were all clerics
and great men of letters, of great fame,
fouled in the world by the one same sin.

Priscian goes there with that wretched crowd,
and Francesco d'Accorso; and if you'd had
any appetite for such scurf, you could have seen

the one whom the Servant of the Servants
moved from the Arno to the Bacchiglione,
where he left his sin-strained body behind.

I would say more, but the walking and talking
can't go on, for I see new smoke
rising over there from the sand.

People are coming I must not be with.
Let my Treasure be commended to you,
the book I still live in — I ask nothing more."

Then he turned back, and looked like one of those
who race for the green cloth across the open field
at Verona; and of them he seemed

the one who wins, not the one who loses.
