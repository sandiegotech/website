# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto VIII

It was now the hour that turns the longing back  
in those who sail the sea, and melts the heart  
the day they've said goodbye to the friends they love;

the hour that pierces the new traveler with longing  
if, far off, he hears a bell  
that seems to mourn the dying of the day —

when I let my hearing go for nothing  
and began to watch a soul that had risen,  
lifting a hand to ask us for our silence.

It pressed both palms together and raised them,  
fixing its eyes upon the east,  
as if it said to God, "I want nothing else."

"Te lucis ante" came from its lips  
so devoutly, in notes so sweet,  
they carried me clean out of myself.

And then the rest, devout and sweet,  
followed it through the whole hymn,  
their eyes fixed on the turning heavens.

Here, Reader, hold your eyes hard on the truth,  
for now the veil has worn so thin  
that to see straight through it is easy.

I saw that company of gentle souls  
fall silent then, and gaze upward,  
pale and humble, as if waiting;

and from on high, descending toward us,  
I saw two angels with two flaming swords —  
broken short, their points cut away.

Green as the youngest leaf just born,  
their robes were, and behind them streamed and tossed,  
beaten by the wind of their green wings.

One came to take his post just above us,  
and one came down to the opposite bank,  
so the souls were held between the two.

I could make out the gold of their hair,  
but my eyes failed at their faces —  
sight overwhelmed by too much light.

"Both have come from the bosom of Mary,"  
Sordello said, "to guard the valley  
against the serpent, which will come any moment now."

And I, not knowing by which way it would come,  
turned all around, and froze, and drew myself  
close against those shoulders I trusted.

And Sordello again: "Now let us go down  
among the great shades and speak with them;  
it will give them real joy to see you."

Only three steps, I think, I had gone down  
before I was below — and saw one watching,  
staring at me alone, as if to know me.

By now the air was darkening,  
but not so much that, between his eyes and mine,  
it hid what the distance had hidden before.

He moved toward me, and I toward him.  
Noble Judge Nino — what joy it gave me  
to find you here, and not among the damned!

No fair greeting went unspoken between us.  
Then he asked: "How long is it since you came  
across the far waters to the mountain's foot?"

"Oh," I said to him, "through the places of grief  
I came this morning — and I am in the first life still,  
though by this journey I am winning the other."

The moment my answer was heard,  
he and Sordello both drew back from me  
like men struck suddenly bewildered.

One turned to Virgil, and the other  
to a soul seated there, calling, "Up, Currado!  
Come and see what God in his grace has willed!"

Then, turning to me: "By the singular grace  
you owe to him who hides his own first cause  
so deep that no mind can ford to it —

when you are back across the wide waters,  
tell my Giovanna to pray for me  
there where the innocent are heard.

I do not think her mother loves me now,  
since she laid aside the white widow's veil —  
which, poor woman, she will wish she had kept.

Through her you can easily see  
how long the fire of love lasts in a woman  
if eye and touch don't keep relighting it.

The Viper that leads the Milanese to war  
will not make her so fair a grave-crest  
as Gallura's Cock would have done."

So he spoke, and on his face was stamped  
the mark of that righteous zeal  
which burns in the heart, but within measure.

My greedy eyes kept climbing to the sky,  
to that one point where the stars move slowest,  
like the spoke of a wheel nearest the axle.

And my guide: "Son, what are you watching  
up there?" And I to him: "Those three torches  
that set this whole near pole on fire."

And he to me: "The four bright stars  
you saw this morning have sunk low,  
and these have risen where those once stood."

As he spoke, Sordello drew him close  
and said, "Look there — our Adversary!"  
and pointed a finger to where we should look.

On the side where the little valley  
has no wall, there was a serpent — perhaps  
the very one that gave Eve the bitter food.

Through grass and flowers came the evil streak,  
turning its head now and again to lick  
its back, like a beast grooming itself smooth.

I did not see — so I cannot say —  
how the heavenly hawks first stirred,  
but I saw clearly that both were moving.

Hearing the air split by their green wings,  
the serpent fled, and the angels wheeled around,  
flying back, each one, to his post.

The shade that had drawn near the Judge  
when he called had not, through the whole assault,  
loosed its eyes from me for a moment.

"So may the light that leads you upward  
find in your own free will as much wax  
as it needs to reach the highest blue,"

it began, "if you have any true word  
of Valdimagra or the country near it,  
tell it to me — I was once great there.

I was called Currado Malaspina.  
Not the old Currado, but descended from him;  
the love I bore my own is purified here."

"Oh," I said to him, "I have never passed  
through your lands. But where in all of Europe  
is there a house where they are not known?

That fame which does your line such honor  
proclaims its lords and proclaims its country,  
so that a man knows them who was never there.

And, as I hope to climb, I swear to you:  
your honored family loses nothing  
of the glory of the purse and of the sword.

Custom and nature have so blessed it  
that though a guilty head misleads the world,  
your house alone goes straight and scorns the wrong road."

And he: "Now go. The sun will not lie  
seven times in the bed the Ram  
covers and straddles with all four feet,

before this courteous opinion of yours  
will be nailed into the middle of your head  
with heavier nails than another's words —

unless the course of justice comes to nothing."
