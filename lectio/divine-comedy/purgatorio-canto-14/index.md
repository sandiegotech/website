# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XIV

"Who is this one who circles round our mountain  
before Death has yet given him the power of flight,  
and opens and shuts his eyes as he pleases?"

"I don't know who he is, but I know he isn't alone.  
Ask him yourself — you're closer to him —  
and greet him gently, so he'll want to speak."

So two spirits, leaning toward each other,  
discussed me there along the right-hand side;  
then they tipped their faces up to speak to me.

And one of them said: "Soul, still fastened  
inside your body, climbing toward heaven,  
for charity's sake, console us — tell us

where you come from, and who you are. You make us  
marvel as much at this grace given to you  
as one would marvel at a thing that never was."

And I: "Through the middle of Tuscany there runs  
a little stream, born up on Falterona,  
and not a hundred miles is enough to spend it.

It's from its banks I bring this body of mine.  
To tell you my name would be wasted breath,  
since it makes no great noise in the world yet."

"If I read the sense of your words rightly,  
following it down to the bone," he answered me,  
the one who'd spoken first, "you mean the Arno."

And the other said to him: "Why did he hide  
the name of that river, the way a man  
will hide the name of something horrible?"

And the shade who'd been asked cleared himself of it  
like this: "I don't know. But it's only right  
the name of such a valley should perish —

because from its source (where the Alpine ridge  
is so swollen, the one torn off to make Peloro,  
that in few places does it rise any higher)

all the way down to where it gives itself back,  
restoring to the sea what heaven dries from it  
so the rivers have what runs along with them,

there everyone shuns virtue like an enemy,  
as they'd shun a snake — whether from the bad luck  
of the place itself, or some habit that drives them.

It's why the people in that wretched valley  
have so twisted their own nature out of shape  
you'd think Circe had them grazing in her pen.

Among filthy hogs, fitter for acorns  
than for any food made for human use,  
the river first directs its starveling way.

Coming down, it finds curs after that —  
snarling far more than their strength can back up —  
and it turns its muzzle from them in disdain.

It keeps falling, and the more it swells,  
the more it finds those dogs turning to wolves,  
this cursed and luckless ditch of a river.

Then, dropped down through one deep gorge after another,  
it comes on foxes so stuffed full of fraud  
they fear no cunning clever enough to catch them.

Nor will I stop just because someone else hears me;  
and it'll be good for this man if he keeps in mind  
what a truthful spirit is laying bare for me.

I see your grandson there, the one who turns  
hunter of those wolves along the bank  
of that wild stream, and leaves them all terrified.

He sells their flesh while they're still alive;  
then butchers them like old worn-out cattle.  
Many he robs of life, himself of honor.

He comes out blood-soaked from the wretched wood,  
and leaves it so that a thousand years from now  
it won't have grown its trees back as they were."

The way a man's face clouds at the warning  
of some disaster bearing down on him,  
no matter which side the danger comes from,

so I watched that other soul, who'd turned  
to listen, grow troubled and downcast  
once he had taken the words into himself.

The speech of the one and the look on the other  
had made me want to know their names,  
and I asked it, half question and half prayer.

At which the spirit who had spoken first  
began again: "You want me to do for you  
what you yourself won't do for me.

But since God wills that so much of his grace  
shine out in you, I won't be stingy with you.  
Know, then, that I am Guido del Duca.

My blood ran so on fire with envy  
that if I'd seen another man rejoicing,  
you'd have watched me go livid with the pallor of it.

From what I sowed, that's the straw I reap.  
O human race — why do you set your heart  
where there's no sharing the thing you want?

This is Rinieri — this is the pride and honor  
of the house of Calboli, where no one since  
has made himself the heir of what he was.

And it isn't only his own blood that's been stripped,  
between the Po and the mountains, the shore and the Reno,  
of the good a man needs for truth and for ease;

for the whole stretch inside those bounds is choked  
with poisonous roots, so thick that now  
no amount of tending would thin them out in time.

Where is good Lizio? Arrigo Manardi?  
Pier Traversaro, and Guido di Carpigna?  
O men of Romagna gone to bastard stock!

When will a Fabbro take root in Bologna again?  
When, in Faenza, a Bernardin di Fosco —  
the noble shoot off an ignoble seed?

Don't be amazed, Tuscan, if I weep  
when I remember, alongside Guido da Prata,  
Ugolin d'Azzo, who once lived among us,

Federigo Tignoso and his company,  
the house of Traversara, and the Anastagi —  
the one line and the other, both run out;

the ladies and the knights, the labors and the leisure  
that once filled our souls with love and courtesy  
there, where the hearts have grown so full of malice.

O Bertinoro, why don't you flee,  
now that your family is gone  
along with so many who wouldn't be corrupted?

Bagnacavallo does well, having no more sons;  
and Castrocaro does badly, and Conio worse,  
taking such pains to breed more counts like those.

The Pagani will do well once their Devil  
has finally gone — though even then no clean  
record of them will ever be left behind.

O Ugolino de' Fantolini, your name  
is safe, since no one's left to be awaited  
who, going to seed, could darken it now.

But go on your way, Tuscan; it gives me more  
pleasure now to weep than to keep talking —  
so much has this conversation wrung my mind."

We knew those dear souls heard us going,  
and so, by their staying silent,  
they made us sure we'd taken the right path.

Once we were alone and moving on,  
a voice came toward us, splitting the air  
like thunder when it cracks the sky, and cried:

"Whoever finds me will kill me!" —  
then fled the way an echo dies away  
when the cloud is suddenly torn open.

No sooner had our hearing eased from that  
than another came, with so great a crash  
it was like one thunderclap hard on the next:

"I am Aglauros, who was turned to stone!"  
And then, to press myself close to the Poet,  
I stepped backward instead of ahead.

By now the air had gone quiet on every side,  
and he said to me: "That was the hard bit  
meant to hold a man inside his bounds.

But you all swallow the bait, so the hook  
of the old Adversary draws you in,  
and then curb and call do little good.

Heaven calls to you, wheels around you,  
holding up its eternal beauties for you,  
and still your eye stays fixed on the ground —

and so He who discerns it all strikes you."
