# The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto XXXI

So in the form of a pure white rose  
the sacred host was shown to me —  
the bride Christ made his own in his own blood.

But the other host, that as it flies still sees  
and sings the glory of the One who fills it  
with love, and the goodness that made it so noble,

the way a swarm of bees will sink into  
the flowers one moment, and the next return  
to where their labor is turned into sweetness,

came down into the great flower, adorned  
with all those many petals, and rose again  
to where its love forever has its home.

Their faces were all of living flame,  
their wings of gold, and the rest of them so white  
no snow on earth has ever reached that white.

Down through the flower, from rank to rank, they brought  
something of the peace and burning love  
they gathered by the fanning of their sides.

And all that throng of flying shapes between  
the flower and what rose above it never  
blocked the splendor, never dimmed the sight,

because the light of God so pierces through  
the universe, each part as it deserves,  
that nothing anywhere can stand against it.

This kingdom, sure of itself and full of joy,  
crowded with people ancient and new,  
turned all its looking and its love one way.

O threefold Light, that as a single star  
sparkling upon their sight contents them so,  
look down upon our storm here far below!

If the barbarians, coming from a land  
that lies each day beneath Callisto's wheel,  
as she turns with the son she so delights in,

were struck with wonder seeing Rome and all  
her mighty works, back when the Lateran  
rose higher than all things that mortals build —

then I, who from the human reached the divine,  
from time into eternity had come,  
from Florence to a people just and sane,

with what amazement must I have been filled!  
Between that wonder and the joy of it,  
I was glad to hear nothing, and stay still.

And like a pilgrim, drinking in delight  
as he stands gazing round the shrine he vowed,  
already aching to recount how it was,

so, moving through the living light, I sent  
my eyes out over all the ranks of souls —  
now up, now down, now searching all around.

I saw faces that love itself made pleading,  
lit by His light and by their own glad smiles,  
and every bearing graced past telling.

By now my glance had taken in the whole  
broad shape of Paradise, the form of it,  
without my eyes yet fixing anywhere,

and I turned back, my longing freshly kindled,  
to ask my Lady about the things  
that still hung in suspense within my mind.

I meant to ask one thing; another answered.  
I thought that I would see Beatrice there,  
and saw an old man robed like the glorious people.

His eyes, his cheeks, were overflowing with  
a kind, benevolent joy; his whole bearing  
tender, the way a loving father's is.

"And she — where is she?" I burst out at once.  
And he: "To bring your longing to its end  
Beatrice sent me down out of my place.

Look to the third tier of the highest rank  
and there you will behold her once again,  
upon the throne her merits have assigned her."

I lifted up my eyes without a word  
and saw her, crowning herself with a crown  
that caught and threw back the eternal rays.

No mortal eye, sunk to the deepest floor  
of any sea, lies half so far removed  
from that high region where the thunder breaks

as my eyes were from Beatrice — and yet  
it did not matter, for her image came  
down to me through no blurring medium.

"O Lady, in whom my hope stands strong and whole,  
who for the saving of my soul allowed  
your footprints to be left behind in Hell,

of all the many things I have now seen,  
I know it is your power and your goodness  
from which their worth and grace have come to me.

You led me up from slavery to freedom  
by every road, by every means at hand  
that lay within your power to set me free.

Keep your great gift alive in me, so that  
my soul, which you have healed and made your own,  
may please you when it breaks loose from the body."

So I prayed; and she, so far away,  
looked down at me once more, and seemed to smile —  
then turned again to the eternal fountain.

And the holy old man said: "So that you may  
finish your journey perfectly to its end —  
which prayer and holy love have sent me for —

let your eyes fly all round about this garden;  
for taking it in will train and clear your sight  
to climb still higher along the ray of God.

And she, the Queen of Heaven, for whom I burn  
entirely with love, will grant us every grace,  
because I am her faithful Bernard."

As one who comes, perhaps, from far Croatia  
to look upon our Veronica, and stands  
never quite sated by its ancient fame,

but thinks, the whole time it is held on high,  
"My Lord, Christ Jesus, true God of true God,  
was this, then, what your living face was like?" —

just so was I, gazing upon the living  
love in that man, who while still in this world  
had tasted of that peace through contemplation.

"Son of grace," he began, "this joyous life  
will not be known to you while you keep staring  
down here below, at this, the lowest place.

Look instead to the rings, out to the farthest,  
until you see enthroned at last the Queen  
to whom this kingdom bows and is devoted."

I raised my eyes; and as at break of day  
the eastern reaches of the sky outshine  
the quarter where the sun goes down to rest,

so, as if traveling with my eyes from valley  
up to a peak, I saw one far-off region  
outblaze in splendor all the rest of the rim.

And just as there, where we look for the pole  
that Phaethon steered so badly, the light burns  
brightest, then dims to either side of it,

so that peaceful oriflamme, that war-banner  
of peace, blazed brightest at its very center,  
and on each side the flame in equal measure failed.

And at that center, with their wings spread wide,  
I saw more than a thousand angels reveling,  
each one distinct in brightness and in kind.

I saw, there in their play and in their song,  
a beauty smiling — the very gladness shining  
within the eyes of all the other saints;

and even if my words were as rich as  
my power to imagine, I would not dare  
attempt the smallest fraction of its joy.

Bernard, the moment that he saw my eyes  
fixed and intent on her for whom he burned,  
turned his own to her with such great love

that he made mine more ardent still to gaze.
