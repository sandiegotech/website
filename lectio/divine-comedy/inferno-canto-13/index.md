# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XIII

Nessus had not yet reached the other side  
when we had pushed our way into a wood  
that no path anywhere had ever marked.

No green leaves, only the color of soot.  
No smooth branches, but gnarled and snarled together.  
No fruit, only thorns full of poison.

No thickets so wild and matted hold  
the savage beasts that hate the cleared land  
between the Cecina and Corneto.

Here the foul Harpies make their nests,  
the ones who drove the Trojans from the Strophades  
with their grim warning of the grief to come.

They have wide wings, and human necks and faces,  
feet with claws, and great feathered bellies,  
and they wail from the monstrous trees.

And the good master: "Before you go on,  
know that you are in the second ring,"  
he began, "and will be, until you come

out onto the horrible sand.  
So look hard around you, and you will see  
things that would make you doubt my word if I only told them."

I heard cries of grief on every side  
and saw no one at all who could be making them,  
so that I stopped, completely lost.

I think he thought that I was thinking  
so many voices came out of those trunks  
from people hiding themselves from us;

so the master said: "If you break off  
any small twig from one of these trees,  
what you're thinking will fall apart at once."

Then I reached my hand out a little way  
and snapped a small branch from a great thorn-bush,  
and the trunk cried out: "Why are you tearing me?"

Then, after it had gone dark with blood,  
it cried again: "Why are you ripping me?  
Have you no pity in you anywhere?

We were men once, and now we are made into trees.  
Your hand should have shown more mercy than this,  
even if we had been the souls of snakes."

The way a green branch, burning at one end,  
drips sap from the other and hisses there  
with the air forcing its way out —

so from that broken splinter, together,  
came words and blood at once; and I let the tip  
fall, and I stood like a man afraid.

"If he had been able to believe sooner,"  
my sage answered, "O wounded soul,  
what he had only seen inside my verses,

he would never have laid his hand on you.  
But the thing was past believing, and that drove me  
to urge him to a deed that grieves me now.

But tell him who you were, so that to make  
some amends he can revive your name  
up in the world, where he's allowed to return."

And the trunk said: "Your sweet words draw me out  
so that I can't stay silent — and don't be vexed  
if I'm tempted to talk a little while.

I am the one who held both of the keys  
to Frederick's heart, and turned them, locking  
and unlocking, so gently

that I shut almost everyone out of his secrets.  
I was so faithful to that glorious office  
that I lost my sleep and my strength keeping it.

The whore who never takes her sluttish eyes  
off the house of Caesar — the death of everyone,  
the vice that rots the courts —

turned all minds against me, and those minds,  
on fire, set Augustus on fire too,  
so that my glad honors curdled into grief.

My spirit, in its scornful exultation,  
thinking that dying would let me escape that scorn,  
made me unjust against my own just self.

By the strange roots of this tree I swear to you  
that I never once broke faith  
with my lord, who so deserved the honor.

And if either of you goes back to the world,  
let him lift up my memory, which still lies  
flat under the blow that envy gave it."

He waited a moment, and then: "Since he's silent,"  
the poet said to me, "don't waste the time —  
speak, ask him, if there's more you'd like to know."

And I said to him: "You ask him yourself  
about whatever you think would satisfy me,  
for I cannot. Such pity fills my heart."

So he began again: "So may this man  
do freely for you what your words ask,  
imprisoned spirit, please be willing

to tell us how the soul gets bound  
inside these knots; and tell us, if you can,  
whether any soul is ever freed from such limbs."

Then the trunk blew hard, and afterward  
the wind was turned into a voice like this:  
"You'll have my answer briefly.

When the savage soul departs  
from the body it has torn itself away from,  
Minos sends it down to the seventh pit.

It falls into the wood — no part is chosen  
for it, but wherever Fortune flings it,  
there it sprouts like a grain of spelt.

It shoots up a sapling, then a wild tree.  
The Harpies, feeding then upon its leaves,  
give it pain, and an open wound for the pain.

Like the rest, we will go for our bodies,  
but not so any soul may put one back on —  
it isn't right to have what you threw away.

We will drag them here, and through this dismal wood  
our bodies will hang, each one of them  
on the thorn of the shade that did it wrong."

We were still listening hard to the trunk,  
thinking it might have more it wanted to say,  
when a sudden uproar caught us off guard —

the way a hunter at his post can tell  
the boar and the chase are coming on,  
hearing the beasts and the branches crash —

and look: on our left, two of them,  
naked and scratched, fleeing so wildly  
that they snapped off every branch of the wood.

The one in front: "Come now, Death, come quick!"  
And the other, who felt he was lagging behind,  
was shouting: "Lano, your legs were never

this fast at the jousting by the Toppo!"  
And then, maybe because his breath was failing,  
he folded himself into a bush.

Behind them the wood was full of black  
bitches, ravenous, running as fast  
as greyhounds slipped loose from the chain.

On the one who had crouched down they set their teeth  
and tore him apart, piece by piece,  
and carried those aching limbs away.

Then my guide took me by the hand  
and led me to the bush, which was weeping,  
all in vain, out of its bleeding wounds.

"O Jacopo da Sant'Andrea," it said,  
"what good did it do you to use me for a screen?  
What blame do I bear for your wicked life?"

When the master had stopped beside it, he said:  
"Who were you, that through so many wounds  
are blowing out your sorrowing words with blood?"

And it to us: "O souls who have come  
to look at the shameful butchery  
that has torn all my leaves away from me,

gather them up at the foot of this sad bush.  
I was of the city that traded its first patron  
for the Baptist — and for that he will use his art

to keep it grieving forever.  
And if it weren't that on the crossing of the Arno  
some trace of him still remains,

the citizens who later rebuilt it  
on the ashes that Attila left behind  
would have done all that work for nothing.

I made my own house into my gallows."
