The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XIII

Purgatorio: Canto XIII47 of 100

We had come out at the top of the stairs,
where the mountain is carved back a second time —
the mountain that, by climbing, strips you clean.

There a ledge binds the slope all the way around
just as the first one did, except that this arc
bends a little sooner to its turn.

No shade is there, no carving to be seen:
the bank looks bare, the road looks bare and smooth,
nothing but the livid, bruise-dark stone.

"If we wait here to ask the way of someone,"
the poet said, "I'm afraid that we may lose
too much time before we make our choice."

Then he fixed his eyes steadily on the sun,
made his right side the pivot of his motion,
and swung the left side of himself around.

"Sweet light," he said, "I step out trusting you
along this strange new road — so lead us now
as one ought to be led through a place like this.

You warm the world, you shine down over it;
unless some other reason says otherwise,
your rays should always be the ones we follow."

As far as a mile is reckoned here below,
that far already we had gone up there
in little time, our will being quick and ready;

and we heard them flying toward us — though no shape
was visible — spirits, as they passed, calling out
gracious invitations to the table of love.

The first voice, sweeping by us on its flight,
said out loud, "Vinum non habent" —
and kept on saying it as it fell behind.

And before it had faded out of hearing
with distance, another passed us, crying,
"I am Orestes!" — and it too did not stop.

"Father," I said, "what voices are these?"
And even as I asked, here came the third,
saying, "Love those at whose hands you've suffered harm."

And my good Master: "This circle whips the sin
of envy, and that is why the cords
that make the lash are drawn from love itself.

The bridle has to be the opposite sound;
you'll hear it, I expect — or so I judge —
before you reach the Pass of Pardon above.

But aim your eyes steadily out through the air,
and you'll see people sitting ahead of us,
each one settled close against the cliff."

Then I opened my eyes wider than before;
I looked ahead, and saw shades wrapped in cloaks
no different in color from the stone.

And when we had gone a little farther on,
I heard the cry, "Mary, pray for us!" —
a cry of "Michael, Peter, and all the Saints!"

I don't think there walks the earth today
a man so hard he wouldn't have been pierced
with pity at what I went on to see.

For when I had come so near to them at last
that their bearing grew plain and clear to me,
heavy grief wrung the tears out of my eyes.

They seemed to me wrapped in coarse, vile haircloth,
and each one propped the next up with his shoulder,
and all of them were propped against the bank.

So the blind, when they have no way to live,
stand at church doors on pardon-days for alms,
and one lets his head sink down upon the next,

so that pity will spring up the sooner in us,
not only at the sound of what they say,
but at the sight, which begs no less than words.

And as the sun never reaches the blind,
so to the shades I am speaking of just now
the light of heaven will not give itself;

for an iron wire runs through the lids of all of them
and sews them shut — the way it's done
to a wild hawk that will not hold still.

It seemed to me, walking on, that I did them wrong,
seeing the others while I went unseen;
and so I turned to my wise counselor.

He knew well what the mute one meant to say,
and so he did not wait for me to ask,
but said, "Speak — be brief, and to the point."

Virgil was walking on that side of me,
the edge of the ledge from which a man could fall,
since no parapet runs round to garland it;

and on my other side were the devout
shades, who through the horrible sewn seam
were pressing tears out till they bathed their cheeks.

I turned to them and said, "You souls, made certain
of one day seeing the high light above,
the one your longing keeps as its sole care,

so may grace soon dissolve the scum that lies
upon your conscience, and let the river of the mind
flow down through it clear and clean —

tell me, for it would be dear and kind to me,
if any soul among you here is Italian;
perhaps it will do him good if I should learn it."

"My brother, every one of us is a citizen
of one true city; but what you mean to say
is, who lived as a pilgrim down in Italy."

This was the answer I seemed to hear come back
from a little farther on than where I stood,
so I made myself heard still farther up the line.

Among the rest I saw a shade that waited,
by the look of it — and if you ask me how,
it lifted its chin upward, like a blind man.

"Spirit," I said, "who school yourself by climbing,
if you're the one who answered me just now,
make yourself known to me by place or name."

"I was Sienese," it answered, "and with these
others here I cleanse my guilty life,
weeping to Him to lend himself to us.

Wise I was not, though Sapìa was the name
they called me; and I took far more delight
in others' ruin than in my own good luck.

And so you won't believe I'm deceiving you,
hear whether I was the fool I say I was,
the arc of my years already bending down.

My fellow townsmen had met near Colle,
drawn up in the field against their enemies,
and I was praying God for what He willed.

Routed they were there, and turned to the bitter
passes of flight; and watching them run down,
I felt a joy unmatched by any other,

so that I lifted up my brazen face
and cried to God, "From now on I don't fear you!" —
like the blackbird at the first thin spell of sun.

Peace with God was what I wanted at the very
end of my life; and even then my debt
would not yet have been paid down by penance,

had it not been that Pier Pettinaio held me
in mind in his holy prayers — a man
who, out of pure charity, grieved for me.

But who are you, that go among us asking
about our state, with your eyes unsewn —
or so I think — and breathing as you speak?"

"My eyes," I said, "will yet be taken from me
here too, but only briefly; for small
is the wrong they did by being turned in envy.

Far greater is the fear that holds my soul
in dread — the torment on the terrace below;
even now that load down there weighs on me."

And she to me: "Who led you, then, up here
among us, if you mean to go back down?"
And I: "The one who is with me, and says nothing.

And I am alive; so ask it of me freely,
chosen spirit, if you would have me move
my mortal feet down there again for you."

"Oh, this is such a strange thing to be told,"
she answered, "that it is a great sign God loves you;
so help me sometimes with a prayer of yours.

And I beg you, by whatever you most long for:
if you ever walk the soil of Tuscany again,
restore my good name with my kin.

You'll see them among that empty-headed people
who put their hope in Talamone, and will lose
more hope there than in the hunt for the Diana;
but it's the admirals who'll lose the most."
