The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto XXI

Paradiso: Canto XXI88 of 100

My eyes were fixed again on my Lady's face,
and with them my whole mind, drawn back from
everything else and held to that alone;

and she did not smile. "If I were to smile,"
she said to me, "you would become like Semele
the moment she was burned to ash.

For my beauty, which along the stairs
of this eternal palace burns the brighter
the higher we climb — as you have seen —

if it were not held in check, would blaze so fierce
that all your mortal strength before its light
would be a leaf the thunderbolt has crushed.

We have been lifted to the seventh splendor,
which now, beneath the burning Lion's breast,
sends its rays downward mingled with his force.

Set your mind in the line of your eyes,
and make of them a mirror for the figure
that in this mirror will appear to you."

Anyone who knew what pasture my sight took
in that blessed face, when I turned myself
away from it to another care,

would know, weighing the one side against the other,
how glad I was to obey
my guide from heaven.

Inside the crystal that, circling the world,
still carries the name of its beloved king,
under whom no wickedness was left alive,

the color of gold with sunlight flashing on it,
I saw a stairway rising to such a height
my eye could not pursue it to the top.

And I saw, coming down those steps, so many
splendors that I thought the whole of heaven's
light had poured itself out there.

And the way that, true to their own nature,
the rooks at the break of day will stir
together to warm their cold feathers —

some of them flying off and not returning,
some circling back to where they started from,
and some still wheeling above the nest —

just such a motion seemed to be at work
in that glittering host that came down together,
as soon as it struck on one certain step.

And the one that stopped the nearest to us
grew so bright that in my thought I said,
"I see clearly the love you offer me.

But she from whom I wait to learn the how
and when of speaking and of silence holds still;
so, against my own desire, I do well not to ask."

Then she, who saw my silence in the sight
of Him who sees all things, said to me,
"Let your warm longing go."

And I began: "No merit of my own
makes me worth an answer from you;
but for her sake, who grants that I may ask —

blessed soul, hidden away inside
your own gladness — make known to me
the cause that draws you so close to my side;

and tell me why the sweet music of Paradise
falls silent here in this one wheel,
when through the rest below it sounds so devout."

"Your hearing is mortal, like your sight,"
it answered me. "There is no singing here,
for the same reason Beatrice did not smile.

I have come down this far on the holy stairway's
steps for one thing only — to welcome you
with words and with the light that wraps me round;

and it was not more love that made me readier,
for up above as much love burns and more,
as the flaming makes plain to you.

But the high love that makes us prompt
to serve the will that governs all the world
assigns us here, just as you see."

"I see it well," I said, "O sacred lamp,
how love set free in this court is enough
to follow the eternal Providence;

but this is what seems hard for me to grasp:
why you alone, out of all your fellows,
were chosen beforehand for this task."

I had not yet come to the last word
when the light made its own middle a center
and spun itself like a swift millstone.

Then the love within it answered me:
"A light divine is aimed straight at me,
piercing through this one that holds me close,

and its power, joined to my sight, lifts me
so far above myself that I can see
the supreme essence this light is drawn from.

From that comes the gladness that makes me blaze,
for I make the brightness of my flame
equal to the brightness of my sight.

But even the soul in heaven that is most pure,
the seraph who fixes his eye most fast on God,
could not give you the answer you are asking;

for what you ask sinks down so deep
into the abyss of the eternal decree
that it is cut off from all created sight.

And when you go back to the mortal world,
carry this with you: let it not presume
to move its feet toward such a goal.

The mind that shines clear here is smoke on earth.
So think — how could it do below
what it cannot do once heaven has taken it in?"

Its words set such a limit on me
that I let the question go, and held myself
to asking, humbly, who it was.

"Between two shores of Italy there rise
cliffs, not far from your own native place,
so high the thunders sound far down below them;

they make a ridge, and Catria is its name,
beneath which a hermitage stands consecrated,
once set apart for worship and nothing else."

So the third speech began to me again,
and then, going on, it said: "There, in that place,
I grew so steadfast in the service of God

that, fed on nothing but the juice of olives,
I passed lightly through the heats and frosts,
content in my contemplative thoughts.

That cloister used to send up to these heavens
a rich harvest, and now it has run so empty
that soon the truth of it must come to light.

There I was Peter Damian; and Peter the Sinner
I was, in the house of Our Lady
on the shore of the Adriatic Sea.

Little of mortal life was left to me
when I was called and dragged out to the hat
that only ever passes from bad to worse.

Cephas came, and the great Vessel of the Holy
Spirit came — both of them lean and barefoot,
taking their food at whatever door they found.

Now the shepherds of our day need someone
to prop them up on either side, someone to lead,
someone behind to lift the train — so heavy are they.

They drape their cloaks over their horses too,
so that two beasts go forward under one hide.
O Patience, that you bear so much!"

At this voice I saw many little flames
descending and revolving from step to step,
and every turn of them made them more lovely.

They came and gathered round this one and stopped,
and gave a cry of so enormous a sound
that nothing here could match it; nor could I

make it out, the thunder so overwhelmed me.
