# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto IV

A heavy crash of thunder broke the deep  
sleep in my head, and I came to with a start,  
like someone shaken awake by force.

I stood up straight, my body rested now,  
and turned my eyes around, fully awake,  
to make out where it was I had come to.

The truth is, I found myself on the very edge  
of the valley of grief, that bottomless abyss  
where the thunder of endless wailing gathers.

It was so dark, so deep and full of mist  
that however hard I fixed my eyes on its floor,  
I could make out nothing down there at all.

"Now let us go down into the blind world,"  
the poet began, and his face had gone white.  
"I will go first, and you will come behind."

And I, who saw the color drain from him,  
said: "How am I to go, if you're afraid —  
you, who have always steadied me in my fear?"

And he to me: "It's the anguish of the people  
down here that paints my face the way you see,  
and you have taken that pity for terror.

Let's go. The long road won't wait for us."  
And so he went in, and led me in with him,  
to the first circle that rings the abyss.

There, as far as I could tell by listening,  
there was no weeping, no — only sighs,  
that set the everlasting air trembling.

And this came out of grief without torment,  
the grief of the crowds — and they were many, and great —  
of little children, and women, and men.

The good master said to me: "You don't ask  
who these spirits are that you're looking at?  
I want you to know, before you go on,

that they didn't sin; and whatever good they did,  
it isn't enough, because they had no baptism,  
which is the gateway to the faith you hold.

And those who came before Christianity  
did not worship God in the right way.  
And I myself am one of them.

For that lack, and for no other guilt,  
we are lost, and our only punishment is this:  
with no hope, we go on living in longing."

Great grief took hold of my heart when I heard him,  
because I knew some people of real worth  
who hung suspended in that Limbo.

"Tell me, my master — tell me, my lord,"  
I began, wanting to be certain now  
of the faith that overcomes every error,

"did anyone ever leave here — by his own merit,  
or by another's — and go on to be blessed?"  
And he, who caught the thing I couldn't say outright,

answered: "I was still new to this place  
when I saw a Mighty One come down to us,  
crowned with the sign of victory.

He drew out the shade of our first father,  
and of his son Abel, and of Noah,  
of Moses the lawgiver, the obedient

Abraham, the patriarch, and David the king,  
Israel, with his father and his sons,  
and Rachel, the one he had labored so long for,

and many more, and he made them blessed.  
And you should know that before these souls,  
no human spirit had ever been saved."

We did not stop walking while he spoke,  
but kept moving on the whole time through the forest —  
the forest, I mean, of crowded ghosts.

Our way had not yet carried us far  
this side of where I'd slept, when I saw a fire  
that won back a hemisphere from the dark.

We were still some distance off from it,  
but not so far that I couldn't partly see  
that people of honor held that place.

"You who honor every art and science,  
who are these, with such great honor  
that it sets them apart from all the rest?"

And he to me: "The honor of their name,  
which still resounds up there in your life,  
wins grace in Heaven, which raises them like this."

Just then I heard a voice ring out:  
"All honor to the greatest of the poets!  
His shade, which had gone, is coming back."

After the voice had stopped and gone still,  
I saw four great shades coming toward us.  
Their faces were neither sorrowful nor glad.

My gracious master began to speak to me:  
"Look at the one with the sword in his hand,  
who comes ahead of the three, like their lord.

That is Homer, sovereign poet of them all.  
The next to come is Horace, the satirist.  
The third is Ovid, and the last is Lucan.

Because each one of them shares with me  
the name that single voice just cried aloud,  
they do me honor, and they do well in it."

And so I saw them gather, the fair school  
of that lord of the highest song,  
who soars above the others like an eagle.

After they had talked among themselves a while,  
they turned to me with a sign of welcome,  
and my master smiled to see it.

And they did me still greater honor than that:  
they made me one of their own company,  
so that I was the sixth among such minds.

So we went on toward the light, and talked  
of things it's good to keep silent about now,  
as good as it was to speak of them there.

We came to the foot of a noble castle,  
ringed seven times with its high walls,  
and guarded all around by a lovely stream.

We crossed over this as if on solid ground.  
Through seven gates I entered with those sages,  
and we came out on a meadow of fresh green.

There were people there with calm, slow eyes,  
great authority in the way they looked,  
who spoke seldom, and in gentle voices.

We drew off together to one side,  
into an open place, bright and high,  
so that all of them could be seen.

There, straight ahead, on the green enamel,  
the great spirits were pointed out to me,  
and the sight of them still lifts me up inside.

I saw Electra with many companions,  
and among them I knew Hector and Aeneas,  
and Caesar, armed, with his hawk's keen eyes.

I saw Camilla and Penthesilea  
on the other side, and I saw King Latinus,  
seated with his daughter Lavinia.

I saw that Brutus who drove out Tarquin,  
Lucretia, Julia, Marcia, and Cornelia,  
and, alone and apart, I saw Saladin.

When I raised my eyes a little higher,  
I saw the master of those who know,  
sitting among his family of philosophers.

All of them look to him, all do him honor.  
There I saw both Socrates and Plato,  
who stand nearer to him than the rest;

Democritus, who lays the world to chance,  
Diogenes, Anaxagoras, and Thales,  
Zeno, Empedocles, and Heraclitus.

I saw the one who gathered the powers of things,  
Dioscorides, I mean; and I saw Orpheus,  
Tully and Livy, and the moralist Seneca,

Euclid the geometer, and Ptolemy,  
Galen, Hippocrates, and Avicenna,  
and Averroes, who wrote the great Commentary.

I can't give the full account of all of them,  
because the long theme drives me on so hard  
that again and again the words fall short of the facts.

The company of six splits into two.  
My wise guide leads me off another way,  
out of the stillness into the trembling air,

and I come to a place where nothing shines.
