# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XXIV

Our talking did not slow the walk,  
nor the walk the talk; we pressed on, speaking,  
like a ship driven by a fair wind.

And the shades, who looked like things twice dead,  
drew the wonder up through the pits of their eyes,  
realizing that I was alive.

And I, keeping the thread of our talk,  
said: "He climbs more slowly, perhaps,  
than he would, for another's sake.

But tell me, if you know, where Piccarda is;  
and tell me whether I see anyone worth noting  
among this crowd that stares at me so."

"My sister, who was so good and so lovely  
I cannot say which won out, rejoices  
already, crowned, on high Olympus."

So he said first, and then: "There is no rule  
against naming each other here, since our faces  
have been so milked away by the fasting.

This one," he said, and pointed, "is Bonagiunta,  
Bonagiunta of Lucca; and that face past him,  
more pinched and stitched than the rest,

once held the Holy Church in its arms.  
He came from Tours, and starves off here  
the eels of Bolsena and the Vernaccia wine."

He named me many more, one by one,  
and each seemed glad enough to be named,  
so that I saw not one dark look.

I saw, biting at empty air for hunger,  
Ubaldino della Pila, and Boniface,  
whose crook had shepherded so many.

I saw Messer Marchese, who once had room  
at Forlì to drink without so dry a throat,  
and even so was never a man filled up.

But as a man will study a crowd and single out  
one above the others, so I singled out  
the one from Lucca, who seemed to mark me most.

He was murmuring, and some word like "Gentucca"  
is what I caught, there where he felt the wound  
of the justice that wears them down so.

"O soul," I said, "who seem so eager  
to speak with me, speak so that I can hear,  
and with your words content us both."

"A girl is born, and does not yet wear the veil,"  
he began, "who will make my city please you,  
however men may run it down.

You will carry this foreknowing on your way;  
and if my murmur has misled you at all,  
the truth itself will make it plain in time.

But tell me — do I see before me the man  
who drew out the new rhymes, the ones that open  
'Ladies who have intelligence of love'?"

And I said to him: "I am one who, when Love  
breathes in me, takes note, and in the way  
he dictates within, I set it down."

"O brother, now I see," he said, "the knot  
that held the Notary, and Guittone, and me  
short of the sweet new style I hear in you.

I see clearly enough how your pens  
follow close behind the one who dictates —  
something ours never managed to do;

and the man who looks to go further than that  
sees no difference between the one style and the other."  
And, as if satisfied, he fell silent.

As birds that winter along the Nile  
will sometimes gather into a single rank,  
then fly off faster, strung out in a line,

so all the people who were standing there  
turned their faces and quickened their steps,  
made light by their leanness and their longing.

And as a man worn out with running  
lets his companions go on ahead, and walks  
until the heaving in his chest dies down,

so Forese let the holy flock  
pass by, and came on behind it with me,  
saying, "When will I see you again?"

"How long I may live, I don't know," I answered;  
"yet my coming back will not be so soon  
but that, in longing, I'll be here first;

because the place where I was set to live  
is stripped of good a little more each day,  
and seems marked out for a dismal ruin."

"Go on now," he said; "for the man most to blame  
I see dragged at the tail of a beast  
toward the valley where there is no repenting.

Faster at every stride the beast goes,  
building speed until it dashes him down  
and leaves the body shamefully torn.

Those wheels have not far to turn" — and he raised  
his eyes to heaven — "before what my words  
can say no further will be clear to you.

Now stay behind; for time in this kingdom  
is so precious that I lose too much  
walking beside you, stride for stride."

As a horseman will sometimes break into a gallop  
out of a troop that rides together,  
to take for himself the honor of first clash,

so he left us with longer strides,  
and I was left on the road with those two  
who had been such great marshals of the world.

And when he had drawn so far ahead  
that my eyes followed him only as far  
as my mind had followed his words,

there came in sight, with branches green and laden,  
another apple tree, not far off,  
since I had only just then turned that way.

I saw people beneath it lifting their hands  
and crying I don't know what up toward the leaves,  
like little children, eager and put off,

who beg, and the one they beg of gives no answer,  
but, to keep their craving at its sharpest,  
holds what they want up high and does not hide it.

Then they went off, as if undeceived;  
and now we came up to the great tree  
that turns away so many prayers and tears.

"Pass on by without coming near.  
Higher up is the tree Eve ate from,  
and this plant was raised out of that one."

So said a voice among the boughs, I don't know whose;  
at which Virgil, Statius, and I  
drew together and kept to the rising side.

"Remember," the voice said, "the cursed ones  
formed out of the cloud, who, drunk,  
fought Theseus with their double chests;

and the Hebrews who showed themselves soft at the drinking,  
so that Gideon would not have them with him  
when he came down the hills toward Midian."

So, pressed close against one of the two banks,  
we went on, hearing of the sins of gluttony,  
and the wretched winnings that came of them;

then, given the open road again, alone,  
we walked a thousand steps and more,  
each lost in thought, none of us speaking.

"What are you turning over, the three of you alone?"  
a voice said, all at once, and I startled  
the way a frightened, skittish animal does.

I raised my head to see who it might be;  
and never in a furnace was there seen  
metal or glass so glowing and so red

as the one I saw, who said: "If it please you  
to climb, here is the place you must turn;  
this is the way for whoever goes after peace."

His brightness had robbed me of my sight,  
so I turned back toward my Teachers,  
like a man who walks by what his ears tell him.

And as, herald of the coming dawn,  
the May air stirs and breathes out fragrance,  
steeped through with grass and with flowers,

so I felt a breeze strike the middle  
of my brow, and felt the stirring of feathers  
that breathed around me a scent of ambrosia;

and I heard it said: "Blessed are they whom grace  
so lights from within that the love of taste  
kindles no excess of longing in their breasts,  
hungering always only as much as is just."
