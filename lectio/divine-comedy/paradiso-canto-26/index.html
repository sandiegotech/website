The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto XXVI

Paradiso: Canto XXVI93 of 100

While I stood doubting, my sight put out,
out of the radiant flame that had quenched it
breathed a voice that made me listen,

saying: "Until you recover the seeing
you have spent here in me,
it is good that you make up for it by speaking.

Begin, then, and say where your soul
takes its aim — and be certain of this:
your sight is dazzled, not destroyed,

for the Lady who leads you through this
divine country holds in her look
the power the hand of Ananias had."

I said: "As pleases her, soon or late,
let the cure come to these eyes — the gates
she entered with the fire I burn with still.

The Good that gives this Court its peace
is the Alpha and the Omega
of all the writing love reads to me, low or loud."

The same voice that had taken from me
the terror of the sudden blaze
set it in my mind to speak still further,

and said: "You must sift it through a finer sieve.
You must say who aimed your bow
at such a target."

And I: "By the arguments of philosophy,
and by the authority that comes down from here,
such love must stamp itself in me.

For the good, just as it is good, once understood
kindles love at once, and the more so
the more goodness it holds in itself.

So to that Essence — which holds such advantage
that every good found outside it
is only a ray of its own light —

the mind must move, more than anywhere else,
in every one who loves and sees
the truth this proof rests on.

That truth is shown to my understanding
by the one who proves to me the first love
of all the eternal beings.

It is shown by the voice of the truthful Author,
who says to Moses, speaking of Himself,
'I will make all my goodness pass before you.'

You show it to me too, in the opening
of your great Gospel, which proclaims
heaven's secret to earth above every other word."

And I heard him say: "By human reason
and by the authority that agrees with it,
keep the highest of your loves for God.

But tell me again if you feel other cords
drawing you toward Him — say plainly
with how many teeth this love bites you."

The holy intent of the Eagle of Christ
was not hidden from me; I saw clearly
where he meant to lead my confession.

So I began again: "All the bites
that have the power to turn the heart to God
have worked together in my love.

The being of the world, and my own being,
the death He suffered that I might live,
and the hope that holds all the faithful, as it holds me,

together with that living awareness I spoke of,
have drawn me out of the sea of crooked love
and set me on the shore of the right one.

The leaves that fill the whole garden
of the Eternal Gardener — these I love
in the measure of the good He has granted them."

The moment I fell silent, a song most sweet
rang through the heaven, and my Lady
sang with the rest: "Holy, holy, holy!"

And as a sharp light wakes a man from sleep —
the sight running out to meet the brightness
that passes from coat to coat of the eye —

and the one who wakes recoils from what he sees,
so blank is his sudden waking,
until his judgment comes to help him:

just so Beatrice chased every speck
from before my eyes with the light of her own,
which shone a thousand miles and more.

So that I saw better after than before,
and, half in wonder, I asked about
a fourth light I now saw with us.

And my Lady said: "Within those rays
the first soul that the first Power ever made
gazes with love upon its Maker."

As the bough that bends its crown down
when the wind passes, and then lifts again
by the strength in it that draws it upright,

so did I while she was speaking —
first struck with awe, then made bold again
by a desire to speak that burned in me.

And I began: "O fruit, you who alone
were brought forth ripe, O ancient father
to whom each wife is daughter and daughter-in-law,

as devoutly as I can I beg you:
speak to me. You see what I wish,
and to hear you the sooner, I leave it unsaid."

Sometimes an animal under a cloth
will stir so that its mood shows through,
the covering moving with what moves beneath —

and just so that first soul made plain to me,
through the light it was wrapped in,
how gladly it came to give me joy.

Then it breathed: "Without your saying it,
I read your wish more clearly than you read
whatever you hold most certain;

for I see it in the truthful mirror
that makes itself the likeness of all things,
while nothing can make it a likeness of itself.

You would hear how long ago God set me
in the high garden where this Lady
made you ready for so long a stair,

and how long it was a delight to my eyes,
and the true cause of the great anger,
and the speech I used and the speech I made.

Now, my son, the tasting of the tree
was not in itself the cause of so long an exile,
but only the stepping past the bound.

There, in the place from which your Lady sent Virgil,
I longed for this Council four thousand
three hundred and two turnings of the sun;

and I watched him return to every light
along his road nine hundred and thirty times
while I was lingering on the earth.

The tongue I spoke was wholly dead
before the people under Nimrod
set their hands to the work that could not be finished;

for nothing reason ever makes — since human liking,
following the turning heavens,
keeps changing — has ever lasted long.

That man speaks at all is the work of nature;
but whether this way or that, nature leaves
to your own choosing, as it suits you best.

Before I went down to the anguish of hell,
'I' was the name on earth of the Highest Good,
whose joy now wraps me round;

later He was called 'El' — and that is right,
for the ways of men are like a leaf
on a branch: one goes, another comes.

On the mountain that rises highest from the sea
I lived, in innocence and then in sin,
from the first hour to the hour that follows,

as the sun shifts its quarter, to the sixth."
