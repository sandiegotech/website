The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto VI

Purgatorio: Canto VI40 of 100

When the game of hazard breaks up,
the one who lost is left behind, low,
replaying his throws, learning the bitter way;

the crowd goes off with the winner —
one walks in front, one tugs him from behind,
and one at his side keeps bringing himself to mind.

He doesn't stop; he hears this one and that;
the ones he reaches a hand to press no closer,
and so he works his way clear of the mob.

That was me in that thick press of souls,
turning my face to them this way and that,
and by promising I worked myself free.

There was the man of Arezzo, who took his death
from the savage arms of Ghino di Tacco,
and the other, drowned as he fled the chase.

There Federico Novello prayed with hands
stretched out, and that one from Pisa
who made good Marzucco look so strong.

I saw Count Orso, and the soul cut loose
from its body by spite and by envy —
so it claimed, and not for any crime —

Pierre de la Brosse, I mean; and let the Lady
of Brabant see to it while she's still here,
so she doesn't end in a worse flock.

As soon as I was free of all those shades
whose only prayer was that others pray for them,
to speed their becoming holy,

I began: "It seems you flatly deny,
O my light, somewhere in your own text,
that prayer can bend a decree of heaven;

and yet these people pray for exactly that.
So is their hope empty, after all —
or have I not understood what you said?"

And he to me: "My writing is plain,
and the hope of these souls does not fail,
if you look at it with a sound mind.

For the height of judgment does not bend down
because the fire of love fulfills in an instant
what the one paying his debt here owes;

and there, where I set down that point,
a prayer could mend no fault, because
the prayer was cut off from God.

But on a question this deep, don't settle it —
not unless she tells you, the one
who will be light between truth and your mind.

I don't know if you follow: I mean Beatrice.
You'll see her above, on the very top
of this mountain, smiling and glad."

And I: "My lord, then let's move faster,
for I'm not tiring now the way I was,
and look — the hill already throws its shadow."

"We'll go on with this day," he answered,
"as far as we still can — but understand,
the truth of it is not what you think.

Before you're up there, you'll see him come back,
the sun, who now hides himself behind the slope,
so that you no longer break his rays.

But look over there — a soul, set down
all alone, gazing toward us:
that one will point us the quickest way."

We came up to it. O Lombard soul,
how you stood there, proud and aloof,
slow and grave in the moving of your eyes!

It said nothing at all to us,
but let us come on, only watching,
like a lion at rest, couched and still;

Virgil still drew close to it, asking
that it show us the best way up;
and it gave no answer to his question,

but asked us instead about our country
and our lives; and my sweet guide began
"Mantua —" and the shade, drawn into itself,

rose toward him from the place it had been,
saying: "O Mantuan, I am Sordello,
from your own land!" — and the two embraced.

Slave Italy, hostel of grief,
ship without a pilot in a great storm,
no lady of provinces — a brothel!

That noble soul was so quick to rise,
only at the sweet sound of his own city,
to give his fellow townsman welcome there;

and now the ones alive in you can't live
without war, and they tear at each other —
those shut inside a single wall and moat.

Search, wretched one, around your coasts,
your shorelines, and then look into your heart:
is there one part of you at peace?

What good is it that Justinian mended
your bridle, if the saddle sits empty?
Without that, the shame would be less.

You people, you who ought to be devout,
who ought to let Caesar keep the saddle,
if you understood what God writes for you —

look how this beast has turned savage
for want of the spur to correct her,
since you laid your own hand on the reins.

O German Albert, you abandon her —
she's gone wild now, unbroken —
when you ought to be mounted on her saddle.

May a just judgment fall from the stars
upon your blood, and let it be strange and plain,
so your successor learns to fear it!

For you and your father both stood by,
held back by greed for the lands up north,
and let the garden of the empire run to waste.

Come see the Montagues and the Capulets,
the Monaldi and the Filippeschi, you careless man —
those already broken, these in dread!

Come, cruel one, come see the crushing
of your own nobles, and tend their wounds —
you'll see how safe Santafiora is!

Come see your Rome, who weeps,
widowed and alone, crying day and night:
"My Caesar, why have you left me?"

Come see how the people love each other!
And if no pity for us can move you,
come and be ashamed of your own good name.

And if it's allowed me to ask — O highest Jove,
you who were crucified on earth for us,
are your just eyes turned somewhere else?

Or is this some preparation, in the deep
of your counsel, working toward a good
wholly cut off from anything we can see?

For the cities of Italy are all packed
with tyrants, and every clownish peasant
turns into a Marcellus the moment he takes a side.

My Florence — you can rest content
with this digression, since it doesn't touch you,
thanks to your people, so full of foresight!

Many hold justice in their hearts, but loose it slowly,
so it won't leave the bow without thought;
but your people have it right on their lips.

Many refuse the common burden;
but your people, so eager, answer
before they're asked, and shout: "I'll shoulder it!"

Now be glad — you've every reason:
you, rich; you, at peace; you, so wise!
If I'm telling the truth, the facts won't hide it.

Athens and Sparta, who drew up
the ancient laws and were so civilized,
made only the faintest gesture toward living well

next to you — you who spin out
rulings so fine that what you spin in October
doesn't last to the middle of November.

How many times, in the span you can remember,
have you remade your laws, your coinage,
your offices, your customs, your own limbs!

And if you remember rightly, and see clearly,
you'll find you're like a sick woman
who can find no rest on her own down,

but with all her tossing fends off the pain.
