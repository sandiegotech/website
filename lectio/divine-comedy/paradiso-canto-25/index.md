# The Divine Comedy, Paradiso: Canto XXV

If it should ever happen that this sacred poem —  
to which both heaven and earth have set their hand,  
so that for years it has worn me thin —

should overcome the cruelty that bars me  
from the fair fold where I slept as a lamb,  
an enemy to the wolves that make war on it,

then with another voice now, another fleece,  
I will return a poet, and at the font  
of my baptism I will take the crown of laurel;

for there I entered the Faith that makes  
all souls known to God, and for its sake  
Peter, afterward, encircled my brow.

Then toward us moved a light  
out of that company from which came the first  
of the vicars Christ left behind him,

and my Lady, full of joy, said to me:  
"Look, look — there is the great lord  
for whose sake, below, they crowd to Galicia."

As when a dove settles beside its mate  
and the two of them, circling and murmuring,  
pour out the love each feels for the other,

so I saw one great glorified prince  
welcomed by the other with that same greeting,  
praising the food that is eaten up here.

But when their glad salute was done,  
each fell silent before me, coram me,  
blazing so bright it overcame my sight.

Smiling then, Beatrice said:  
"Illustrious life, who set down in writing  
the largesse of our Basilica,

make Hope ring out at this height.  
You can — for as often as Jesus gave  
the three more light, you were its figure."

"Lift your head, and put your mind at rest,  
for whatever comes here from the mortal world  
must ripen in our radiance."

That comfort came to me from the second fire,  
so I raised my eyes to the heights  
that their too-great weight had bent down before.

"Since by his grace our Emperor wills  
that you should come, before your death,  
face to face with his counts in the inmost room,

so that, having seen the truth of this court,  
you may strengthen in yourself and others  
the Hope that, down below, rightly draws love,

say what it is, and how your mind  
flowers with it, and from where it came to you."  
So the second light went on.

And she, the compassionate one who had guided  
the feathers of my wings in flight so high,  
answered ahead of me, like this:

"The Church Militant has no child  
of greater hope, as is written  
in the Sun that lights our whole band;

and so it is granted him to come  
out of Egypt to see Jerusalem  
before his soldiering is done.

The other two points — asked not for knowledge,  
but so that he may report  
how much this virtue pleases you —

I leave to him; he will not find them hard,  
nor cause for self-praise. Let him answer,  
and may the grace of God help him in it."

As a student answers his teacher,  
ready and willing, in what he knows well,  
so that his command of it may show —

"Hope," I said, "is the sure expectation  
of glory to come, and it is the work  
of grace divine and merit that goes before.

This light comes to me from many stars,  
but he first poured it into my heart  
who was chief singer to the highest captain.

'Let them hope in you,' he says in his high song,  
'who know your name' — and who does not know it,  
if, like me, he holds the faith?

Then you poured into me, with his pouring,  
in your Epistle, until I am full,  
and I rain your rain again on others."

While I was speaking, deep in the living heart  
of that fire a brightness quivered,  
sudden and quick, like a flash of lightning;

then it breathed: "The love that still burns in me  
toward the virtue that followed me  
to the palm and the end of the field

wills that I breathe again to you who delight  
in her; and it pleases me that you tell  
whatever things Hope promises you."

And I: "The Scriptures, old and new,  
set down the sign, and the sign points me  
to what awaits the souls God has made his friends.

Isaiah says that each one, clothed  
in his own land, shall wear a double garment —  
and his own land is this sweet life.

And your brother, far more plainly,  
where he treats of the robes of white,  
makes this revelation clear to us."

And first, near the ending of these words,  
"Sperent in te" was heard above us,  
and all the singing rings answered it.

Then one light among them grew so bright  
that, had the Crab one crystal like it,  
winter would have a month of a single day.

And as a glad girl rises, steps in,  
and joins the dance — only to do honor  
to the new bride, and from no fault of her own —

so I saw that brightened splendor  
come to the two who were wheeling round  
as befitted their burning love.

It entered into the song and the music,  
and my Lady kept her gaze fixed on them,  
still and silent, like a bride.

"This is the one who lay on the breast  
of our Pelican; this is he  
chosen from the cross for the great office."

So my Lady; yet for all that  
she did not move her watching eyes  
before those words of hers, or after.

As a man who strains and tries  
to see a little of the sun's eclipse,  
and by the looking goes blind,

so I became before that last fire,  
while it was said: "Why do you dazzle yourself  
to see what has no being here?

Earth in the earth my body is, and shall be  
with all the others there, until our number  
matches the eternal reckoning.

With the two garments, in the blessed cloister,  
are the two lights alone that have risen —  
and this you shall carry back to your world."

At these words the flaming circle  
grew quiet, with the sweet mingling  
of the sound the threefold breath had made,

the way oars that beat the water hard  
are all held still at a single whistle,  
to escape some danger or to rest.

Ah, how it troubled my mind  
when I turned to look at Beatrice  
and could not see her, though I stood

close at her side, and in the world of joy!
