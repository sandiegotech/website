# The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXII

I have watched horsemen breaking camp before,  
opening an assault, lining up for review,  
and sometimes pulling back to save themselves;

I have seen the scouts ride out across your country,  
O men of Arezzo, and the raiding parties go,  
the tournaments fought, the jousts run hard,

now with trumpets, now with the ringing of bells,  
with drums, with the signal-fires up on the towers,  
with our own sounds and with foreign ones —

but I never yet saw horsemen or foot soldiers  
set out to so strange a bugle as this,  
nor any ship steer by a star or a landmark.

We went along with the ten demons.  
What savage company! But that's how it goes:  
in church with the saints, in the tavern with the gluttons.

My whole attention stayed fixed on the pitch,  
to take in everything about that ditch  
and the people who were burning down inside it.

The way dolphins arch their backs to send  
a signal to sailors — a sign to take care  
and make ready to save their ship —

so now and then, to ease his own pain,  
one of the sinners would show his back  
and hide it again faster than lightning.

And the way frogs, at the edge of a ditch,  
stand with only their muzzles poking out,  
keeping their feet and the rest of them under,

that was how the sinners stood on every side;  
but the moment Barbariccia came near,  
they would duck back down beneath the boil.

I saw — and my heart still shudders to recall it —  
one of them wait too long, the way it happens  
that one frog stays while another dives;

and Graffiacane, who was closest to him,  
hooked him by his pitch-matted hair  
and hauled him up — he looked like an otter dragged out.

By now I knew all of their names,  
since I'd marked them when they were first picked,  
and listened to how they called one another.

"O Rubicante, see that you sink  
your claws in him and strip the skin off!"  
all the cursed ones shouted at once.

And I said: "Master, find out if you can  
who that poor wretch is, the one who's fallen  
into the hands of his enemies."

My guide stepped up to the man's side,  
asked him where he was from, and he answered:  
"I was born in the kingdom of Navarre.

My mother put me in service to a lord,  
for she had borne me to a worthless wastrel  
who destroyed himself and everything he had.

Then I joined the household of good King Thibault.  
There I set myself to working graft,  
and I'm paying for it now in this heat."

And Ciriatto, from whose mouth a tusk  
jutted out on each side, the way a boar's does,  
let him feel how just one of them could rip.

The rat had landed among vicious cats;  
but Barbariccia locked him in his arms  
and said: "Stand off while I fork him myself."

Then he turned his head toward my master:  
"Ask him more," he said, "if there's anything else  
you want to know, before someone tears him apart."

My guide: "Then tell us — among the others  
under the pitch, do you know any  
who are Italian?" And he: "A moment ago

I left one who came from a country bordering it.  
I wish I were still down there, covered with him —  
then I'd have no fear of claw or hook!"

And Libicocco: "We've put up with enough."  
He sank his grapple into the man's arm  
and, ripping, tore a whole tendon loose.

Draghignazzo wanted his turn too,  
going for the legs; so their squad-leader  
wheeled around on them with an ugly look.

When they had quieted down a little,  
my guide, without waiting, asked the man,  
who was still staring at his wound:

"Who was it you say you made that unlucky  
parting from, when you came up to shore?"  
And he answered: "That was Friar Gomita,

the one from Gallura, a vessel of every fraud,  
who held his lord's enemies in his hands  
and worked them so smoothly each one still thanks him.

He took their money and let them slip free,  
as he puts it; and in his other dealings  
he was no petty grafter but a king of them.

Don Michel Zanche of Logodoro  
keeps him company down there; and of Sardinia  
their tongues never once grow tired of talking.

But look — see that one grinding his teeth!  
I'd tell you more, but I'm afraid  
he's getting ready to scratch my hide."

And the great marshal, turning to Farfarello,  
who was rolling his eyes as if to strike,  
said: "Back off, you vicious bird."

"If you'd like to see or hear," the terrified man  
started up again, "some Tuscans  
or Lombards, I'll make them come.

But let the Malebranche pull back a little,  
so the others won't be afraid of their reprisals,  
and I, sitting right here in this spot,

for the one of me that I am, will bring up seven  
when I whistle — the way we always do  
whenever one of us gets out of the pitch."

At that, Cagnazzo lifted his snout,  
shaking his head, and said: "Just listen  
to the trick he's cooked up to throw himself back in!"

And the man, who had no shortage of schemes,  
answered: "Oh, I'm tricky, all right —  
rigging up worse misery for my own kind."

Alichino couldn't hold back. Against  
the others he said: "If you dive,  
I won't come after you at a gallop —

I'll just beat my wings above the pitch.  
We'll give up the rim; let the bank be our cover,  
and we'll see if you alone can outmatch us."

Reader, you're about to hear a new game.  
All of them turned their eyes to the far side —  
he first who'd been most against it.

The Navarrese picked his moment well.  
He set his feet, and in a single instant  
leaped and was free of all their plans.

At that, each one was stung with shame,  
but most of all the one who'd caused the blunder;  
so he launched off, shouting: "You're caught!"

It did no good. Wings couldn't outrun  
the other's terror; the sinner went under,  
and the demon, flying, pulled his chest up sharp —

exactly the way a duck dives down  
the instant the falcon comes close,  
and the falcon climbs back up, beaten and spent.

Calcabrina, furious at being made a fool,  
flew after Alichino, glad enough  
the sinner had escaped — it gave him his fight.

And the moment the grafter had vanished,  
he turned his talons on his own comrade  
and grappled with him right over the ditch.

But the other was a seasoned hawk himself,  
ready to claw him back; and the two of them  
fell together into the middle of the boiling pond.

The heat broke up the fight at once —  
but there was no climbing out of it now,  
their wings were so gummed and limed with pitch.

Lamenting with the rest, Barbariccia  
sent four of them flying to the far side  
with all their gaffs, and quick about it;

down they went to their posts on either bank  
and reached their hooks out toward the limed pair,  
who were already baked inside the crust.

And we left them tangled up there like that.
