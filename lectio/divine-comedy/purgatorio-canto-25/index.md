# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XXV

Now was no time to slow our climbing down,  
for the sun had handed his noon circle  
to Taurus, and the night had passed it on to Scorpio.

So, like a man who will not stop  
but keeps on walking, whatever he meets,  
once need has driven its goad into him,

we went up through the cleft that way,  
taking the stair one behind the other,  
since its narrowness divides the climbers.

And as the little stork lifts its wing  
with a longing to fly, yet does not dare  
to leave the nest, and lets it sink again,

so was I, with my wish to ask  
kindled and quenched at once, going as far  
as the parted lips of a man about to speak.

Quick as our pace was, my gentle father  
did not let it hold him back, but said:  
"Loose the bow of speech you've drawn to the head."

Then I opened my mouth with confidence  
and began: "How can a thing grow thin  
where there is no need to take in food?"

"If you would call to mind how Meleager  
was wasted as a brand burned down,"  
he said, "this would not taste so bitter to you;

and if you'd think how, at your least trembling,  
your image trembles inside the mirror,  
what seems hard would soften for you.

But so your wish may be put to rest,  
here is Statius; I call on him, I ask him  
to be the healer now of your wounds."

"If I unfold the eternal workings to him  
with you standing here," Statius answered,  
"let my excuse be that I can deny you nothing."

Then he began: "Son, if your mind  
takes in these words of mine and holds them,  
they will be your light on the How you ask.

The perfect blood, which is never drunk  
down by the thirsting veins, but stays behind  
like food you lift away from the table,

takes on, within the heart, the forming power  
for all the human parts — the power  
that travels the veins to become them.

Refined once more, it goes down where it is  
better to keep silent than to speak; and from there  
it drips onto another's blood, in the natural vessel.

There the one mingles with the other,  
one made to receive, the other to act,  
by virtue of the perfect place it springs from;

and joined, it sets to work, first thickening,  
then quickening into life the matter  
it had made firm enough to hold.

The active power, having become a soul  
like a plant's — except that this one  
is still on the way, and that one has arrived —

then works until it stirs and feels,  
like a sea-sponge, and goes on  
to shape the faculties whose seed it is.

Now, Son, the power swells, now it spreads  
out from the heart of the one who fathered it,  
where nature is set on forming all the limbs.

But how it turns from animal to human  
you do not yet see; this is the point  
that once led a wiser man than you astray,

so far that in his teaching he set  
the soul apart from the mind that thinks,  
because he saw no organ it could use.

Open your heart to the truth now coming,  
and know that, the moment the brain  
is fully formed and jointed in the unborn child,

the First Mover turns to it, glad  
at such great craft of nature, and breathes  
into it a new spirit, full of power,

which draws whatever it finds at work there  
into its own being, and becomes one soul,  
that lives, and feels, and turns upon itself.

And that my words may amaze you less,  
look at the sun's heat, which becomes wine  
when it joins the juice that drips from the vine.

When Lachesis has no more thread to spin,  
the soul lets go of the flesh, and carries off  
in itself, in power, the human and the divine;

the lower faculties all fall silent,  
but memory, intelligence, and will  
act far more keenly than they did before.

Without a pause it falls of its own weight,  
in a marvelous way, on the one shore or the other;  
there it first learns the road it is to take.

As soon as the place there closes it round,  
the forming power rays out from it,  
as it did, and as fully, in the living limbs.

And as the air, when it is full of rain,  
through rays cast back and crossing inside it  
shows itself decked in many colors,

so here the neighboring air takes on  
the very form the soul stamps upon it,  
by its power, once the soul has come to rest.

And then, the way a little flame  
follows the fire wherever it shifts,  
the spirit follows its new shape.

Since from this it takes on its look,  
it is called a shade; and out of this  
it then forms every sense, even sight.

From this we speak, and from this we laugh;  
from this we shape the tears and sighs  
you may have heard along the mountain.

As our longings press upon us,  
and our other feelings, so the shade is shaped —  
and this is the cause of what you wondered at."

And now we had come to the last  
of all the circles, and turned to the right,  
and our care was bent on something new.

There the bank throws out flames of fire,  
and the ledge breathes upward a blast  
that bends them back and keeps them off itself.

So we had to keep to the open edge  
and go one by one; and I feared the fire  
on this side, and the long fall on that.

My Leader said: "Along this stretch  
you must hold a tight rein on the eyes,  
for here a man could slip so easily."

"Summae Deus clementiae" — within  
the heart of that great burning I heard it sung,  
and it made me no less eager to turn and look;

and I saw spirits walking through the flame;  
so I kept watch, parceling out my sight  
between my own steps and theirs, again and again.

When the close of that hymn was reached,  
they cried aloud, "Virum non cognosco" —  
then took the hymn up softly once more.

And when that too was done, they called:  
"To the wood Diana ran, and drove out Helice,  
who had felt the poison of Venus."

Then they went back to their singing; then they cried  
the names of wives, and of husbands who stayed chaste,  
as virtue and the marriage vow require.

And I believe this is enough for them  
the whole time the fire is burning them;  
with such care, and such food, is there need

that the last wound of all should close and heal.
