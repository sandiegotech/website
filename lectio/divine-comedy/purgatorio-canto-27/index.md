# The Divine Comedy, Purgatorio: Canto XXVII

As when he trembles out his first rays  
over the land where his Maker shed his blood  
— with the Ebro under the high scales of Libra,

and the Ganges scorched by noon —  
so stood the sun; the day was leaving us  
when the glad Angel of God appeared.

Outside the flame he stood, on the edge,  
and sang "Beati mundo corde,"  
in a voice far more alive than ours.

Then: "No one goes farther, holy souls,  
until the fire has bitten. Enter it,  
and do not be deaf to the singing on the other side."

That is what he said when we drew close,  
and hearing it I became  
like a man being lowered into his grave.

I clasped my hands and stretched up tall,  
staring into the fire, calling back hard  
the human bodies I had once watched burn.

My good escorts turned to me,  
and Virgil said: "My son,  
there may be torment here, but not death.

Remember — remember! If I brought you  
safely down on Geryon's back,  
what will I do now, this much nearer God?

Believe it for certain: if you stood  
a thousand years in the heart of this flame,  
it could not singe a single hair of you.

And if you think that I am tricking you,  
step up to it and test it for yourself —  
hold the hem of your robe against the fire.

Lay it down now, lay down all your fear.  
Turn this way and come on, safe."  
And I stood still — against my own conscience.

Seeing me planted there, stiff and stubborn,  
he said, a little troubled: "Look, my son —  
between Beatrice and you stands this wall."

As the dying Pyramus, at the name of Thisbe,  
lifted his eyelids and looked at her,  
the hour the mulberry turned dark red —

so my stubbornness melted, and I turned  
to my wise Guide, hearing the name  
that wells up in me and never stops.

At which he shook his head and said: "Well, then —  
are we to stay on this side?" and smiled  
the way you smile at a child won over by an apple.

Then he stepped into the fire ahead of me,  
asking Statius — who for a long stretch  
had walked between us — to come behind.

Once I was in it, I would have flung myself  
into boiling glass to cool down,  
the burning there was so far past all measure.

And my sweet Father, to steady me,  
kept talking of Beatrice as he walked,  
saying: "I think I see her eyes already."

A voice that was singing on the far side  
guided us, and fixed wholly on it  
we came out where the climb began.

"Venite, benedicti Patris mei"  
sounded from inside a brightness there  
so great I could not bear to look.

"The sun is going," it added, "and night is coming.  
Do not stop — press your steps onward  
while the west is not yet dark."

Straight up through the rock the path ran on,  
and it ran in such a line that I cut off  
the rays of the sun in front of me, now low.

We had tried only a few of the stairs  
when, by the loss of my shadow, my Sages and I  
felt the sun set behind us.

And before the whole vast reach  
of the horizon had become one color,  
and Night held her boundless sway,

each of us made his bed of a stair —  
for the nature of the mountain took from us  
not the will to climb, but the power.

The way goats grow quiet, chewing the cud,  
that had been quick and reckless on the peaks  
before they fed,

hushed now in the shade while the sun beats down,  
watched by the goatherd leaning on his staff,  
and leaning, tending them —

and the way the shepherd, lodging out of doors,  
passes the night beside his quiet flock,  
guarding that no wild thing scatters it —

such, at that hour, were the three of us,  
I like the goat, and they like the herdsmen,  
walled in on this side and on that by rock.

Little of the open could be seen,  
but through that little I saw the stars  
brighter and larger than they usually are.

Chewing it all over, gazing at them,  
sleep took me — sleep that often, before  
a thing is done, has word of it already.

It was the hour, I think, when Citherea,  
who always seems to burn with the fire of love,  
first beamed on the mountain from the East;

and in my dream I seemed to see  
a lady, young and lovely, walking a meadow,  
gathering flowers, and singing as she said:

"Let anyone who asks my name know  
that I am Leah, and I move my lovely hands  
all round, to make myself a garland.

To please myself at the glass, I deck myself here.  
But my sister Rachel never leaves  
her mirror, and sits before it all day long.

She is as eager to gaze at her own fair eyes  
as I am to adorn myself with my hands.  
Looking fills her; doing fills me."

And now, before the first glimmers of dawn  
— which rise the more welcome to pilgrims  
whose homeward lodging is the nearer —

the dark was fleeing on every side,  
and my sleep with it; so I rose,  
seeing the great Masters already up.

"That sweet apple, which mortals chase  
branch after branch in all their care,  
shall put your hungers at peace today."

Those were the words Virgil used,  
speaking to me; and there were never gifts  
that could match these for sheer delight.

Longing on longing came over me  
to be above, so that with every step  
I felt the feathers growing in me for flight.

When the whole stairway had been run  
beneath us, and we stood on the highest step,  
Virgil fixed his eyes on me

and said: "You have seen the temporal fire  
and the eternal, my son, and reached a place  
beyond which I myself see nothing.

By intellect and craft I have brought you here.  
From now on, take your own pleasure as your guide.  
You are past the steep ways, and past the narrow.

Look at the sun, shining on your brow;  
look at the grass, the flowers, the young trees  
this ground brings forth all on its own.

Until they come rejoicing — the lovely eyes  
whose weeping sent me to you —  
you may sit among them, or walk.

Expect no more word, no more sign from me.  
Your will is free, upright, and whole,  
and it would be wrong not to do as it bids —

so over yourself I crown and mitre you."
