The Divine Comedy, Inferno: Canto XXV

Inferno: Canto XXV25 of 100

When he had finished speaking, the thief
raised both his hands with the fig thrust out,
shouting: "Take that, God — I aim them straight at you."

From that moment the serpents were my friends,
for one of them coiled itself around his neck
as if to say, "You'll speak no more,"

and another wound about his arms and bound him,
clinching itself so tight across his front
that he could not so much as twitch.

Pistoia, ah, Pistoia! Why not resolve
to burn yourself to ashes and be done,
since in evildoing you outdo your own seed?

In all the dark circles of this Hell
I saw no spirit so defiant toward God,
not even the one who fell from the walls at Thebes.

He fled, and said no further word.
Then I saw a Centaur, raging,
come on crying, "Where is he, where is the insolent one?"

I don't think Maremma has as many
snakes as he carried all along his back,
up to where our human shape begins.

On his shoulders, just behind the nape,
a dragon lay with its wings spread wide,
and it sets fire to everything it meets.

My master said: "That one is Cacus,
who beneath the rock of Mount Aventine
made a lake of blood again and again.

He does not travel the same road as his brothers
because of the fraud and the theft he worked
on the great herd that grazed close by him —

and so his crooked dealings came to an end
under the club of Hercules, who likely
landed a hundred blows; he felt not ten."

While he was still speaking, Cacus passed on,
and three spirits came up below us,
noticed by neither me nor my guide

until they shouted out: "Who are you?"
At that our talk broke off,
and we turned all our attention to them.

I didn't know them, but it happened —
as one thing leads to another by chance —
that one of them was made to name another,

saying, "Where has Cianfa gotten to?"
So, to make sure my guide would notice,
I laid a finger from my chin to my nose.

If you are slow now, Reader, to believe
what I am about to tell, it's no wonder —
for I, who saw it, can barely accept it myself.

As I held my brows lifted toward them,
look! — a serpent with six feet shoots out
in front of one of them and grips him whole.

With its middle feet it clamped him round the belly,
with the front ones it seized both his arms,
then sank its teeth through one cheek and the other.

Its hind feet it stretched along his thighs,
thrust its tail up between the two of them,
and spread it out behind, along his loins.

Ivy was never rooted to a tree
so fast as that horrible reptile
wove its own limbs through the other's.

Then they clung together as if made
of hot wax, and their colors ran into one;
neither one nor the other looked like what it was —

the way, ahead of the flame, a brown stain
creeps up along a sheet of paper,
not black yet, while the white dies.

The other two looked on, and each
cried out: "Agnello, oh — how you change!
Look, you're already neither two nor one."

Already the two heads had become one,
when two faces appeared to us, blended
into a single face where both were lost.

Out of four strips the two arms were made;
the thighs and legs, the belly and the chest
turned into parts no eye had ever seen.

Every original feature was erased.
The warped image looked like two and like neither,
and off it went at a slow, dragging pace.

As a lizard, under the great whip
of the dog-day heat, darting from hedge to hedge,
flashes like lightning if it crosses the road,

so there appeared, making toward the bellies
of the other two, a small burning serpent,
livid and black as a peppercorn.

And in that part where we first take
our nourishment, it pierced one of them through,
then dropped down stretched out before him.

The pierced one stared at it but said nothing —
no, he stood there, feet planted, and yawned
as if sleep or a fever had come over him.

He gazed at the serpent, it at him;
one through its wound, the other through its mouth
smoked hard, and the two smokes met and mingled.

Now let Lucan fall silent, there where he tells
of wretched Sabellus and Nasidius,
and let him wait to hear what comes next.

Let Ovid be silent on Cadmus and Arethusa —
for if he turns the one to a snake in his verse
and her to a spring, I don't begrudge it him;

because two natures, face to face, he never
transmuted so that both their forms
stood ready to trade the matter they were made of.

These two answered each other so exactly
that the serpent split its tail into a fork,
and the wounded man drew his feet together.

His legs, along with the thighs, fused
so completely that in no time the seam
left not the faintest sign that anyone could see.

The cloven tail took on the shape
the other one was losing, and its skin
turned soft while the man's went hard.

I saw the arms draw in at the armpits,
and the reptile's two feet, which had been short,
lengthen by as much as the arms shrank.

Then the hind feet, twisted together,
became the part a man keeps hidden,
and the wretch's own had split into two feet.

While the smoke veils them both
in a new color, and breeds hair
on the one and strips it from the other,

the one rose up, the other sank down,
but neither turned aside its pitiless eyes,
beneath which each was changing its muzzle.

The one now standing drew his muzzle back toward the temples,
and from the surplus of matter gathering there
the ears came out of the smooth cheeks;

what did not run back but stayed in place,
out of that surplus, made the face a nose
and thickened the lips as much as was right.

The one lying flat thrusts his muzzle forward
and draws the ears back into his head
the way a snail draws in its horns;

and the tongue, which had been whole and fit
for speech, splits in two, while the forked tongue
in the other closes up — and the smoke stops.

The soul that had been turned into a reptile
fled hissing away down the valley,
and the other, talking, spat after it.

Then he turned his new shoulders on him
and said to the third: "I'll have Buoso run
on all fours down this road, the way I did."

So I watched the seventh ballast
shift and shift again — and let the strangeness of it
be my excuse, if my pen has slipped.

And though my eyes were somewhat dazed by then,
and my mind dismayed, those souls
could not slip off so secretly

that I failed to make out Puccio Sciancato clearly —
he was the one, alone of the three companions
who came at first, who was not changed.

The other was the one you weep for, Gaville.
