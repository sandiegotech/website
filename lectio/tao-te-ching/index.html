Lectio Edition · 2026

The Tao Te Ching

Laozi

  • 400–301 BCE
  • Eastern tradition
  • Philosophy
  • Lectio Edition 2026

Eighty-one short chapters, part poem and part proverb, ascribed to Laozi. They teach the Way that cannot be named and the power that works by yielding. It is the founding text of Taoism.

Why this editionGoal and purpose

A book this short should not need brackets.

James Legge's translation of 1891 is exact and hard to read. He explains inside the line, in brackets: "The highest excellence is like (that of) water." He reads the famous first line as a path one can walk, which loses its argument about speech and names.

The Chinese is spare, and it hedges where it is unsure. A translation that explains and decides for it is a different book.

The Lectio Edition gives each chapter in a few plain sentences, with no gloss inside the line, and keeps the hedges where the Chinese has them. Legge stays in the app beside it.

The openingFrom the Lectio Edition

How it begins.

Chapter 1 · The Way and the Name

The Way that can be spoken is not the eternal Way. The name that can be named is not the eternal name.

Nameless, it is the origin of heaven and earth. Named, it is the mother of all things.

So if you are always without desire, you see its hidden depths; but if desire is always in you, you see only its outer edges.

These two spring from the same source, though we call them by different names. Together they are the Mystery — mystery within mystery, the gate of all that is subtle and wonderful.

Chapter 2 · The Mutual Arising of Opposites

When everyone in the world recognizes beauty as beauty, ugliness has already appeared. When everyone recognizes good as good, what is not good has already appeared.

So it is that being and non-being give rise to each other; the difficult and the easy complete each other; the long and the short measure each other; the high and the low lean on each other; note and voice harmonize with each other; before and after follow each other.

The first 172 words of the Lectio Edition, as they stand in the bundle the app reads.

The choicesPolicies and divergences

What this edition decided.

The review set these rules. The first is the one a reader will notice.

  • Te, the second word of the title, is "power" everywhere. "Virtue" is retired, because in current English it means moral goodness and te does not. The one exception is in Chapter 63, where "repay resentment with power" would read as revenge. There it is "kindness". The editor may overrule this.
  • Wu wei is acting without forcing, not doing nothing.
  • No explanation inside the line. What a term means goes in a note.
  • Hedges stay hedged. Where the Chinese says "seems" or "perhaps", so does the English.
  • The ideal person is "he", as the sage is throughout. A general maxim may say "you".
  • The base is the received text of Wang Bi. A variant reading is used only where a note records it.
  • A phrase the Chinese repeats is repeated. "He lets that go and takes hold of this" closes Chapters 12, 38 and 72 in the same words.

Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 169 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 8. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

道可道，非常道

Chapter 1

Older
"The Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao." (Legge)
Lectio
The Way that can be spoken is not the eternal Way.

The second dao is a verb, and in the oldest usage it means to say. The whole chapter is about speaking and naming. Legge's "trodden" is possible grammar and loses the point.

為無為，則無不治

Chapter 3

Older
"When there is this abstinence from action, good order is universal." (Legge)
Lectio
Act without forcing, and nothing is left ungoverned.

The Chinese is an active paradox: do the not-doing. It is not idleness. The edition keeps it as a command.

芻狗

Chapter 5

Older
"the dogs of grass" (Legge)
Lectio
straw dogs

A straw dog was made for a sacrifice, handled with honor during the rite, and thrown away after. Heaven and earth treat all things so, without malice and without favor.

上善若水

Chapter 8

Older
"The highest excellence is like (that of) water." (Legge)
Lectio
The highest good is like water.

Four characters, five English words. The same word for "good" then runs down the chapter's list, and the reader should hear it as one idea.

道常無為而無不為

Chapter 37

Older
"The Tao in its regular course does nothing (for the sake of doing it), and so there is nothing which it does not do." (Legge)
Lectio
The Way never forces anything, yet nothing is left undone.

The book's central paradox. Legge hedges it with a bracket and it reads as inaction.

含德之厚，比於赤子

Chapter 55

Older
"He who has in himself abundantly the attributes (of the Tao) is like an infant." (Legge)
Lectio
One full of power is like a newborn child.

The fullest power is held by the weakest creature. Legge unpacks te into a phrase and hides the one word the chapter turns on.

報怨以德

Chapter 63

Older
"to recompense injury with kindness" (Legge)
Lectio
Repay resentment with kindness.

The edition's one exception to "power" for te. Confucius answers this same proverb in the Analects, and the Lectio Analects says "kindness" there. The two books should let the reader hear one saying.

天下莫柔弱於水

Chapter 78

Older
"There is nothing in the world more soft and weak than water" (Legge)
Lectio
Nothing in the world is softer or weaker than water

Legge goes on to add a bracketed "(so effectual)". The Chinese closes more simply: nothing can take water's place.
