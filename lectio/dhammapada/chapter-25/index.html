The Dhammapada, Chapter XXV

The Monk

Chapter XXV25 of 26

360. Restraint of the eye is good; good is restraint of the ear; good is restraint of the nose; good is restraint of the tongue.

361. Restraint of the body is good; good is restraint of speech; good is restraint of the mind; good is restraint in all things. The monk who is restrained in all things is freed from all suffering.

362. The one who controls his hands, who controls his feet, who controls his speech, who is master of himself; who takes his delight inwardly, who is composed, who is content alone—him they call a monk.

363. Sweet is the speech of the monk who masters his tongue, who speaks with wisdom and without pride, who makes plain the meaning and the teaching.

364. The monk who dwells in the teaching, who delights in the teaching, who meditates on the teaching, who keeps the teaching in mind, will never fall away from the true teaching.

365. Let him not despise what he himself has received, nor go about envying what others receive. The monk who envies others never comes to stillness of mind.

366. Though a monk receives little, if he does not despise what he has received, even the gods will praise him—so long as his life is pure and he is not slack.

367. The one who never takes name and form to be himself, and does not grieve over what has passed away—he indeed is called a monk.

368. The monk who lives in loving-kindness, who has faith in the teaching of the Buddha, will reach the place of peace, where all that is conditioned is stilled, and there find happiness.

369. Empty this boat, O monk! Once emptied, it will move swiftly. Cut off craving and hatred, and you will come to Nirvana.

370. Cut off five, let go of five, and cultivate five more. The monk who has risen above the five attachments is called one who has crossed the flood.

371. Meditate, O monk, and do not be heedless. Do not let your mind whirl after the pleasures of the senses. Be heedless, and you may have to swallow the iron ball in hell, and cry out as you burn, "This is suffering!"

372. There is no meditation for one without wisdom, and no wisdom for one who does not meditate. The one in whom both meditation and wisdom are found is close to Nirvana.

373. The monk who has gone into an empty house, whose mind is at peace, who sees the teaching clearly, knows a joy that is more than human.

374. As soon as he grasps how the elements of body and mind rise and pass away, he finds the happiness and gladness that belong to those who know the deathless.

375. And here is where the wise monk begins: guarding the senses, contentment, restraint within the rule of the teaching. Let him keep noble friends whose lives are pure and who are not slack.

376. Let him be generous and warm-hearted, faultless in how he conducts himself; then, filled with gladness, he will make an end of suffering.

377. As the jasmine sheds its withered flowers, so, O monks, shed craving and hatred.

378. The monk whose body is stilled, whose speech is stilled, whose mind is stilled, who is composed, who has spat out the lures of the world—he is rightly called one who is at peace.

379. Rouse yourself by yourself; examine yourself by yourself. Guarding yourself and keeping watch, O monk, you will live in happiness.

380. For you are your own master, you are your own refuge. So hold yourself in check, as a horse-dealer reins in a fine horse.

381. The monk who is full of gladness, who has faith in the teaching of the Buddha, will reach the place of peace, where all that is conditioned is stilled, and there find happiness.

382. The monk who, though still young, gives himself to the teaching of the Buddha lights up this whole world, as the moon does when it breaks free of the clouds.
