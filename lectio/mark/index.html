Lectio Edition · 2026

The Gospel of Mark

Anonymous · attributed to Mark

  • c. 65–75 CE
  • Western tradition
  • Scripture
  • Lectio Edition 2026

The earliest of the four Gospels and the shortest. It has no birth story and no long sermon. It opens with a voice in the wilderness and moves fast, in the everyday Greek of markets and letters, to an empty tomb.

Why this editionGoal and purpose

Mark is rough and in a hurry. English Bibles have made him dignified.

The King James Version of 1611 gave English its sacred register, and Mark does not write in a sacred register. He piles clause on clause with "and". He says "right away" more than forty times in sixteen chapters. Translators have varied the word for elegance since the beginning.

The smoothing also hides what Mark built. At the baptism the sky is torn, the same verb he uses for the temple curtain at the crucifixion. The King James has "opened".

The Lectio Edition keeps the pace and the repetition, and keeps the hard words hard. It ends where the oldest manuscripts end. The King James stays in the app beside it.

The openingFrom the Lectio Edition

How it begins.

Chapter 1 · The Beginning of the Good News

The beginning of the good news of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. As it stands written in the prophet Isaiah — “Look, I am sending my messenger ahead of you; he will prepare your road. A voice shouts in the wilderness: Get the road ready for the Lord. Make his paths straight.”

John appeared, baptizing in the wilderness and announcing a baptism of turning back, for the forgiveness of sins. The whole Judean countryside went out to him, and all the people of Jerusalem; they were baptized by him in the Jordan River, confessing their sins. John wore camel's hair with a leather belt at his waist, and he ate locusts and wild honey.

And this is what he announced: “After me comes one stronger than I am. I'm not fit to stoop down and untie the strap of his sandals. I baptized you with water. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit.”

The choicesPolicies and divergences

What this edition decided.

These rules were set before drafting, in a charter for the biblical books, and the review held the text to them.

  • Euthys is "right away", every time. The review checked all 41 places and corrected 13 that had been varied.
  • Biblical units and hours stay: denarii, the third hour, the fourth watch. They are not converted.
  • Hard words stay hard: "rebuking", "deny himself", "eternal sin".
  • Parabole is "parable" wherever it names the form.
  • Where Jesus is moved by a crowd, "his heart went out". At 1:41, where manuscripts differ between compassion and anger, the edition has "Moved to the depths" and records the variant.
  • The base Greek text is Westcott and Hort's of 1881, which is in the public domain.

Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 13 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 8. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

εὐαγγέλιον (euangelion)

Throughout

Older
"gospel" (King James)
Lectio
good news

Before it became a church word it was a herald's word, for news of a victory. "Good news" is what a first hearer heard.

εὐθύς (euthys)

Throughout

Older
"straightway", "immediately", "anon", "forthwith" (King James)
Lectio
right away

Mark repeats it on purpose. A translator who varies it is correcting an author who made no mistake.

σχιζομένους

1:10

Older
"he saw the heavens opened" (King James)
Lectio
torn open

The verb is the one for tearing cloth. Mark uses it again of the temple curtain. The tear frames the book.

σπλαγχνισθείς or ὀργισθείς

1:41

Older
"moved with compassion" (King James)
Lectio
Moved to the depths

A few important manuscripts read "moved with anger". The Greek word behind "compassion" names the inward organs. The edition gives the depth and records the variant in a note.

σιώπα, πεφίμωσο

4:39

Older
"Peace, be still" (King James)
Lectio
Quiet. Be muzzled.

Jesus does not soothe the storm. He silences it with the verb he used on an unclean spirit in chapter 1. The King James line is beautiful and hides the connection.

ὁ Χριστός

8:29

Older
"Thou art the Christ" (King James)
Lectio
You are the Messiah

It is a title being claimed. Modern readers hear "Christ" as a surname, and the scene loses its weight.

γέεννα

9:43 to 9:48

Older
"hell" (King James)
Lectio
Gehenna

Gehenna is a place, the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem. "Hell" brings in a later picture of the afterlife.

ἐφοβοῦντο γάρ

16:8

Older
verses 9 to 20 printed as part of the Gospel (King James)
Lectio
Verse 8 is the ending. Verses 9 to 20 are printed and marked as a later addition.

The oldest manuscripts stop with the women running from the tomb, afraid, saying nothing. The longer ending was written later in a different style. The edition prints it and says what it is.
