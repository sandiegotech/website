# Meditations, Book X

Will you ever, my soul, be good and simple and single and bare, more visible than the body that surrounds you? Will you ever taste a loving and contented disposition? Will you ever be full and lacking nothing, longing for nothing more, craving nothing—neither living thing nor object—for the enjoyment of pleasures? Will you stop craving more time in which to enjoy them longer, or a particular place, or a pleasant climate, or the company of people you can live with in harmony? Instead, will you be satisfied with your present condition, pleased with everything around you, and convince yourself that you have everything you need, that it all comes from the gods, that everything is well for you and will be well whatever they choose to give for the preservation of that perfect living being—the good and just and beautiful—which generates and holds all things together, and contains and embraces all things that dissolve so that other like things can be produced? Will you ever be such that you can live in community with gods and with people, never finding fault with them and never being condemned by them?

Observe what your nature requires insofar as you are governed by nature alone; then do it and accept it, provided your nature as a living being is not made worse by it. Next, observe what your nature requires insofar as you are a living being, and allow yourself all of that, provided your nature as a rational creature is not made worse by it. But a rational creature is by consequence also a social creature. So use these rules, and trouble yourself about nothing else.

Everything that happens either happens in a way you are naturally formed to bear, or in a way you are not. If it happens in a way you are formed to bear, do not complain, but bear it as you are formed to bear it. But if it happens in a way you are not formed to bear, still do not complain, for it will perish after it has consumed you. Remember, though, that you are naturally formed to bear everything that your own judgment can make endurable and tolerable, by thinking that it is either in your interest or your duty to do so.

If someone is mistaken, instruct him kindly and show him his error. But if you cannot, blame yourself—or not even yourself.

Whatever happens to you was prepared for you from all eternity; the chain of causes was, from eternity, spinning the thread of your existence and of everything that befalls it.

Whether the universe is a chance gathering of atoms, or a system governed by nature, let this first be settled: that I am a part of the whole which nature governs; and next, that I am in some way intimately related to the other parts of the same kind as myself. Remembering this, then, as a part I will be discontented with none of the things assigned to me out of the whole; for nothing that benefits the whole is harmful to the part. The whole contains nothing that is not for its own advantage. All natures share this principle, but the nature of the universe has this besides: that no external cause can ever compel it to generate anything harmful to itself. Remembering, then, that I am a part of such a whole, I will be content with everything that happens. And since I am intimately related to the parts of the same kind as myself, I will do nothing unsocial, but rather direct myself toward those who are akin to me, turn all my efforts toward the common good, and turn them away from the opposite. If things are done this way, life must flow on happily—just as you would expect the life of a citizen to be happy who pursues a course of action that benefits his fellow citizens and is content with whatever the state assigns to him.

The parts of the whole—everything, I mean, that is naturally contained in the universe—must necessarily perish; but understand this in the sense that they must undergo change. Now if change were by nature both an evil and a necessity for the parts, the whole could not continue in good condition, with its parts subject to change and so constituted as to perish in various ways. For did Nature herself deliberately set out to do evil to her own parts, making them subject to evil and bound to fall into it of necessity—or did such results come about without her knowing? Both suppositions are unbelievable. But suppose someone drops the term Nature as an active power and merely calls these things natural: even then it would be absurd to say that the parts of the whole are by nature subject to change, and at the same time to be surprised or annoyed as though something were happening contrary to nature—especially since each thing dissolves into the very elements of which it is composed. For there is either a scattering of the elements out of which everything was compounded, or a change from the solid into the earthy and from the airy into the aerial, so that these parts are taken back into the universal reason—whether the universe is consumed by fire at fixed periods or renewed by eternal changes. And do not imagine that the solid and the airy parts of you have belonged to you since birth. All of it took on its growth only yesterday and the day before, so to speak, from the food you eat and the air you breathe. So it is this—what has grown—that changes, not what your mother brought forth. But suppose what your mother brought forth is closely bound up with that other part, the one that has the peculiar quality of changing: that is no objection at all to what I have said.

Once you have taken on these names—good, modest, truthful, rational, even-tempered, high-minded—take care never to exchange them for others; and if you ever lose them, return to them quickly. Remember that "rational" was meant to signify a discriminating attention to each particular thing and a freedom from carelessness; that "even-tempered" means the willing acceptance of whatever the common nature assigns to you; and that "high-minded" means the elevation of the thinking part above the pleasant or painful sensations of the flesh, above that poor thing called fame, and above death and all such things. If, then, you keep hold of these names without craving to be called by them by others, you will be a different person and will enter a different life. For to go on being what you have been until now—torn to pieces and defiled in such a life—is the mark of a very stupid man, far too fond of his life, like those half-devoured fighters in the arena who, though covered in wounds and gore, still beg to be kept until the next day, when they will be thrown out in the same state to the same claws and teeth. So plant yourself firmly in these few names, and if you are able to abide in them, abide as if you had been carried off to the Islands of the Blessed. But if you find that you are falling away from them and cannot keep your hold, then go bravely off into some corner where you can keep them—or even depart from life altogether, not in passion, but with simplicity and freedom and modesty, having at least done this one praiseworthy thing in your life, to have left it in this way.

To keep these names in mind, it will help you greatly to remember the gods, and that they do not wish to be flattered but wish all reasonable beings to become like themselves; and to remember that what does the work of a fig tree is a fig tree, what does the work of a dog is a dog, what does the work of a bee is a bee, and what does the work of a man is a man.

Farce, war, panic, torpor, slavery: day by day these will wipe out those sacred principles of yours, all those you entertain without studying nature and then let slip. Yet it is your duty to look at and to do everything in such a way that, at one and the same time, your power to deal with circumstances is brought to perfection, your contemplative faculty is exercised, and the confidence that comes from knowing each particular thing is kept—not displayed, but not concealed either. For when will you ever enjoy simplicity, when gravity, when the knowledge of each particular thing—what it is in substance, what place it holds in the universe, how long it is formed to last, what it is made of, to whom it can belong, and who can both give it and take it away?

A spider is proud when it catches a fly, one man when he catches a hare, another when he nets a little fish, another when he takes wild boars, another bears, and another Sarmatians. If you examine their principles, are these last not robbers too?

Acquire the contemplative way of seeing how all things change into one another; attend to it constantly and train yourself in this part of philosophy. Nothing is so apt to produce greatness of mind. Such a man has set aside the body, and seeing that he must—no one knows how soon—depart from among men and leave everything behind, he gives himself up entirely to acting justly in all that he does and to resigning himself, in everything else that happens, to the universal nature. As for what anyone may say or think about him or do against him, he never even gives it a thought, being content with these two things: to act justly in what he is now doing, and to be satisfied with what is now assigned to him. He lays aside all distracting and busy pursuits, and desires nothing else than to run the straight course according to the law, and by running the straight course to follow God.

What need is there for anxious suspicion, when it is in your power to inquire what ought to be done? If you see clearly, then go this way contentedly, without turning back; if you do not see clearly, then stop and take the best advice. But if other things stand in your way, go on as your powers allow, with careful consideration, holding to what seems just. For it is best to reach that goal, and if you fail, let your failure be in the attempt to reach it. The man who follows reason in all things is at once tranquil and active, cheerful and composed.

As soon as you wake from sleep, ask yourself whether it will make any difference to you if another person does what is just and right. It will make no difference.

You have not forgotten, I trust, that those who put on arrogant airs in dealing out their praise or blame to others are just what they are at bed and at table; you have not forgotten what they do, what they avoid, what they pursue, and how they steal and rob—not with hands and feet, but with their most valuable part, the part by which a man, when he chooses, produces fidelity, modesty, truth, law, and a good divinity within.

To Nature, who gives all things and takes them back, the man who is well taught and modest says: Give what you will; take back what you will. And he says this not out of pride, but in obedience, and well pleased with her.

Short is the little that remains of your life. Live as on a mountain. For it makes no difference whether a man lives here or there, if he lives everywhere in the world as in a city. Let people see, let them know, a real man who lives according to nature. If they cannot endure him, let them kill him—for that is better than to live as men do.

No longer talk at all about the kind of man a good man ought to be. Be one.

Constantly contemplate the whole of time and the whole of substance, and consider that, in substance, every individual thing is but a grain of a fig, and in time but the single turn of a drill.

Look at everything that exists and observe that it is already breaking up and changing—a kind of decay or scattering—and that everything is so constituted by nature as to die.

Consider what men are when they are eating, sleeping, mating, relieving themselves, and so on. Then consider what kind of men they are when they are domineering and arrogant, or angry and scolding from their lofty positions. Yet a little while ago, to how many were they slaves, and for what things—and after a little while, consider the condition they will be in.

What the universal nature brings to each thing is for the good of that thing—and it is for its good at the very time nature brings it.

"The earth loves the rain," and "the majestic sky loves"—and the universe loves to bring about whatever is about to be. So I say to the universe: I love along with you. And is this not also why we say that a thing philei, "loves," to happen, meaning that it tends to?

Either you go on living here and have grown accustomed to it; or you are going away, and that was your own wish; or you are dying and have discharged your duty. Beyond these there is nothing. So take heart.

Always keep it clear to yourself that this patch of land is like any other, and that everything here is the same as on a mountaintop, or by the seashore, or wherever you choose to be. For you will find exactly what Plato describes: "penned in a fold on the mountain and milking his bleating flock."

What is my ruling faculty to me right now? What sort of thing am I now making it? For what purpose am I now using it? Is it empty of understanding? Is it cut loose and torn away from social life? Is it melted and mixed into this poor flesh, so that it moves along with it?

He who runs away from his master is a runaway slave. The law is our master, and whoever breaks the law is a runaway. So too is the man who is grieved or angry or afraid, because he is dissatisfied that something has been, is, or will be among the things appointed by the one who rules all things—and that ruler is Law, who assigns each man his due. The man, then, who fears or grieves or rages is a runaway.

A man deposits seed in a womb and goes away, and then another cause takes it up, works on it, and makes a child. What a thing from such material! Again, the child sends food down through its throat, and then another cause takes it and makes perception and movement, and in the end life and strength and all the rest—so many things, and so strange! Observe, then, the things produced in this hidden way, and see the power behind them, just as we see the power that carries things downward and upward—not with our eyes, yet no less plainly.

Constantly consider that all things, just as they are now, also were in time past, and that they will be the same again. Set before your eyes whole dramas and scenes of the same pattern, whatever you have learned from your own experience or from older history—for example, the whole court of Hadrian, the whole court of Antoninus, the whole court of Philip, Alexander, Croesus. All of those were just such dramas as we see now, only with different actors.

Imagine every man who is grieved or discontented at anything to be like a pig being sacrificed, kicking and squealing.

Like this pig, too, is the man who, lying on his bed in silence, laments the bonds in which we are held. And consider that to the rational creature alone is it given to follow willingly what happens; merely to follow is a necessity imposed on all.

On the occasion of each thing you do, pause and ask yourself whether death is a dreadful thing because it deprives you of this.

When you are offended at another man's fault, turn at once to yourself and reflect how you commit the same sort of error—for example, in thinking that money is a good thing, or pleasure, or a bit of reputation, and the like. By attending to this, you will quickly forget your anger, especially if you also consider that the man is under compulsion—for what else could he do? Or, if you are able, remove the compulsion from him.

When you have seen Satyron the Socratic, think of Eutyches or Hymen; when you have seen Euphrates, think of Eutychion or Silvanus; when you have seen Alciphron, think of Tropaeophorus; when you have seen Xenophon, think of Crito or Severus; and when you have looked at yourself, think of one of the other Caesars—and in every case do the same. Then let this thought come to mind: Where, then, are those men now? Nowhere, or nobody knows where. For in this way you will continually see human things as smoke and nothing at all, especially if you also reflect that what has once changed will never exist again in all the infinite span of time. And you—in how brief a stretch of time is your own existence? Why, then, are you not content to pass through this short time in an orderly way? What material and opportunity for your activity are you turning away from? For what else are all these things but exercises for the reason, once it has examined carefully and by close inquiry into their nature the things that happen in life? Persevere, then, until you have made these things your own—as a strengthened stomach makes all things its own, as a blazing fire turns whatever is thrown into it into flame and brightness.

Let it never be in any man's power to say truly of you that you are not simple or that you are not good; let whoever thinks anything of the kind about you be a liar. And this is entirely in your power. For who is there to stop you from being good and simple? Only resolve to live no longer if you cannot be such—for reason itself does not allow you to live if you are not.

In dealing with the material of our life, what can be done or said in the way most in keeping with reason? Whatever it may be, it is in your power to do it or to say it; so do not make excuses that you are hindered. You will not stop your lamenting until your mind reaches a state where doing the things in keeping with man's constitution, in whatever matter is set before you, is to you what luxury is to those who pursue pleasure. For a man ought to consider as an enjoyment everything it is in his power to do according to his own nature—and that is in his power everywhere. Now a cylinder is not given the power to move everywhere by its own motion, nor is water, nor fire, nor anything else governed by nature or by an irrational soul, for the things that check them and stand in their way are many. But intelligence and reason are able to make their way through everything that opposes them, in the manner they are formed by nature and as they choose. Set before your eyes this ease with which reason will be carried through all things, as fire moves upward, a stone downward, a cylinder down a slope—and seek nothing further. For all other obstacles either affect only the body, which is a dead thing, or—except through opinion and the yielding of reason itself—they do not crush us or do any harm at all; for if they did, the one who felt it would at once become bad. Indeed, in everything that has a certain constitution, whatever harm befalls it makes the thing that suffers it correspondingly worse; but in a man's case, he becomes better, if one may say so, and more worthy of praise by making right use of such accidents. And in the end, remember that nothing harms the man who is truly a citizen unless it harms the state, and nothing harms the state unless it harms law and order; and of the things called misfortunes, not one harms law. What, then, does not harm law harms neither state nor citizen.

To the man steeped in true principles, even the briefest reminder is enough, any common precept, to recall him to a life free from grief and fear. For example:

"Leaves—some the wind scatters on the ground—so is the race of men."

Leaves, too, are your children; leaves are those who cry out as if they deserved to be believed and bestow their praise, or on the contrary curse, or secretly blame and sneer; and leaves likewise are those who will receive and hand down a man's fame to later ages. For all such things "are produced in the season of spring," as the poet says; then the wind casts them down; then the forest brings forth other leaves in their place. A brief existence is common to all things, and yet you avoid and pursue them all as though they would last forever. In a little while you will close your eyes, and the man who carried you to your grave will soon be mourned by another.

A healthy eye ought to look at everything visible and not say, "I want only green things"—for that is the condition of a diseased eye. Healthy hearing and smell ought to be ready to perceive all that can be heard and smelled. A healthy stomach ought to be toward all food just as a mill is toward all that it is made to grind. And so a healthy understanding ought to be ready for everything that happens; but the understanding that says, "Let my dear children live, and let all men praise whatever I do," is an eye that wants only green things, or teeth that want only soft food.

There is no man so fortunate that, when he is dying, there will not be some standing by who are pleased at what is about to happen. Suppose he was a good and wise man: will there not be at least someone to say to himself, "Let us at last breathe freely, now that we are relieved of this schoolmaster? True, he was harsh to none of us, but I sensed that he silently condemned us"? That is what is said of a good man. But in our own case, how many other reasons are there why so many wish to be rid of us? Consider this, then, when you are dying, and you will depart more contentedly by reflecting thus: I am leaving a life in which even my own companions—those for whom I strove so much, prayed, and cared—themselves wish me gone, hoping perhaps to gain some small advantage by it. Why, then, should a man cling to a longer stay here? Yet do not on that account leave them any less kindly disposed; keep your own character, friendly and benevolent and gentle, and do not depart as if you were being torn away. Rather, just as when a man dies a quiet death the poor soul is easily separated from the body, so too should your departure from men be. For nature bound you to them and joined you with them—and now she dissolves the union. I am separated as from kinsmen, yet not dragged off resisting, but going without compulsion; for this too is one of the things in keeping with nature.

Train yourself, as much as you can, whenever anyone does anything, to ask yourself: For what purpose is this man doing this? But begin with yourself, and examine yourself first.

Remember that what pulls the strings is the thing hidden within: this is the power of persuasion, this is life, this, one might say, is the man. In picturing yourself, never include the vessel that surrounds you and these instruments attached to it. For they are like an axe, differing only in that they have grown onto the body. Indeed, these parts are of no more use without the cause that moves and checks them than a weaver's shuttle is without the weaver, a pen without the writer, or a whip without the driver.
