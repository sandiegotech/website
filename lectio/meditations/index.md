# Meditations

Marcus Aurelius

- 170–180 CE
- Western tradition
- Philosophy
- Lectio Edition 2026

The private notebook of a Roman emperor. Marcus Aurelius wrote it in Greek, at night and on campaign, to remind himself how to live: with reason, with justice, and without fear of death. He did not mean anyone to read it.

## A man talking to himself should sound like one.

Most readers meet Marcus in George Long's translation of 1862. It is accurate and it is stiff. "Begin the morning by saying to thyself" is not how anyone speaks to himself, and the book is nothing but a man speaking to himself.

The older English also carries its own furniture. Long's brackets, footnotes and conjectures sit in and around the text, and a reader cannot always tell where Marcus stops and his translator begins.

The Lectio Edition gives the twelve books in plain current English, with nothing in the text that the Greek does not have. Long, Casaubon's English of 1634 and the Greek itself stay in the app beside it. The aim is that you read Marcus straight through, and go to the older versions when a line makes you curious.

## How it begins.

> **Book I · What I Learned, and From Whom**

> From my grandfather Verus, I learned to be good-natured and to keep my temper under control.

> From everything I heard and remembered about my father, I learned modesty and a strong, manly character.

> From my mother, I learned reverence and generosity; to hold back not only from doing wrong but even from thinking it; and to live simply, far from the habits of the rich.

> From my great-grandfather, I learned the value of not relying on public schools but having good teachers at home—and that this is something worth spending freely on.

> From my tutor, I learned not to back the Greens or the Blues at the races, or the small-shield or the big-shield fighters in the arena; to endure hard work, to want little, to do things with my own hands, to mind my own business, and to refuse to listen to gossip.

> From Diognetus, I learned not to waste myself on trivial things; not to believe the claims of magicians and quacks about spells and exorcisms; and not to keep quails for fighting, or get excited about such things. He taught me to put up with plain speaking and to take philosophy seriously; he led me to hear the philosophers Bacchius, Tandasis, and Marcianus, to write dialogues as a boy, and to choose the simple camp-bed and animal skins of the Greek discipline.

## What this edition decided.

The review of November 2026 set these rules for the whole work and applied them. The authority is the Greek. Long and Casaubon are witnesses to it, not sources of text.

- Long's apparatus stays out. Nothing from his square brackets, footnotes, conjectures or ellipses is printed unless the Greek has it. The review found two places where a footnote of Long's had been printed as Marcus's own words: a line of Homer in Book X and Cicero's Latin in Book VII. Both are gone.
- The inner *daimon* is "the divinity within you" every time, so a reader can see it is one thing. Only in VII.17, where the sentence turns on the Greek word, is the word itself named.
- A generic person is "a man" and "he", with "people" for the plural, as the Greek has it. Book VIII had drifted to "a person" and "they" and was brought back.
- Where Marcus quotes the same verse twice, the English is the same twice.
- Where a sentence depends on a Greek pun that English cannot carry, the Greek word is named once inside the sentence. Where English can carry the play, it stays English.
- Quoted speech takes double quotation marks. Quoted verse runs on as prose, because this is a prose edition.

### Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 3 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. These are all of them, with 1 correction from the review added. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

### Τὰ εἰς ἑαυτόν

The title

**Older:** "Meditations", the traditional title

**Lectio:** Kept as "Meditations", read as "To Himself"

Marcus gave the work no title. "Meditations" is a later editor's label. The Greek means "to himself". The edition keeps the familiar name and treats the text as what it is, a private notebook.

### δαίμων (*daimon*)

Books II, III, V and elsewhere

**Older:** "the daemon" (Long)

**Lectio:** the divinity within you

"Daemon" sends a modern reader toward "demon". For the Stoics it is the share of divine reason each person carries, and Marcus treats it as a god. One phrase throughout lets the reader follow it, and the phrase keeps the word divine.

### τὸ ἡγεμονικόν (*to hegemonikon*)

Throughout

**Older:** "the ruling faculty" (Long)

**Lectio:** the ruling faculty

This is the Stoic name for the part of the self that reasons and commands. Here the edition agrees with Long and keeps his term as the standing one, varying it only where the sentence needs it. The editor has still to decide whether an agreement belongs in a list of divergences.

### τῇ παλλακῇ τοῦ πάππου

Book I, section 17

**Older:** "my grandfather's concubine" (Long)

**Lectio:** my grandfather's mistress

The first draft had "my father's mistress". The Greek says grandfather, and so does Long. It is one of the errors of meaning the review caught, and an example of why every section is read against its sources.

## The other editions on hand.

The Lectio Edition is what opens by default. Three other editions of Meditations are in the bundle, and switching keeps your place.

- **George Long, 1862.** The classic Victorian standard: clear, dignified, widely read.
- **Méric Casaubon, 1634.** The first English Meditations: ornate, archaic, devotional.
- **The original Greek.** The original Koine Greek: Marcus's private notebook (Τὰ εἰς ἑαυτόν).
