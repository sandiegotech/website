Meditations, Book VII

Book VII7 of 12

What is wickedness? It is something you have seen many times before. With everything that happens, keep this in mind: it is something you have seen many times before. Up and down, everywhere, you will find the same things, the things that fill the old histories, the histories of past ages and of our own day, the things that fill cities and households now. There is nothing new. All of it is both familiar and short-lived.

How can your principles die, unless the thoughts that match them are snuffed out? But it is in your power to keep fanning these thoughts back into flame. I am able to hold whatever opinion I ought to hold about anything. If I am able, why am I troubled? The things outside my mind have no bearing on my mind at all. Once your feelings reach this state, you stand upright. To recover your life is in your power. Look at things again the way you used to look at them, for in this lies the recovery of your life.

The pointless business of pageantry, plays on the stage, flocks of sheep, herds of cattle, sparring with spears, a bone tossed to puppies, a crumb of bread dropped into fishponds, the toil and load-hauling of ants, the scurrying of frightened little mice, puppets jerked on strings, all of it the same. It is your duty, then, in the midst of such things, to be good-humored and not put on airs, while understanding that every person is worth exactly as much as the things he busies himself with.

In conversation you must pay attention to what is being said, and in every action you must watch what is being done. In what is done, look at once to the end it aims at; in what is said, watch carefully for what is meant.

Is my mind equal to this task or not? If it is equal to it, I use it for the work as an instrument given me by universal nature. But if it is not equal to it, then either I step back from the work and yield to someone better able to do it, unless there is some reason I ought not, or I do it as well as I can, calling in to help me the person who, with the guidance of my ruling mind, can do what is now fitting and useful for the common good. For whatever I do, whether by myself or with another, ought to be aimed at this alone: at what is useful and well suited to society.

How many who were celebrated by fame have been handed over to oblivion, and how many who celebrated the fame of others have themselves long been dead.

Do not be ashamed to be helped, for it is your business to do your duty like a soldier storming a town. So what if, being lame, you cannot scale the battlements alone, but with another's help it can be done?

Do not let things to come trouble you, for you will meet them, if you must, carrying with you the same reason you now use for the things at hand.

All things are bound up with one another, and the bond is sacred; hardly anything is unconnected to anything else. For things have been arranged together, and they combine to make up one and the same universe. There is one universe made of all things, and one God who pervades all things, and one substance, and one law, one common reason in all intelligent creatures, and one truth, if indeed there is also one perfection for all creatures that share the same origin and partake of reason.

Everything material soon vanishes back into the substance of the whole; everything that gives form is very soon taken back into the universal reason; and the memory of everything is very soon swallowed up in time.

For the rational creature, the same act is both according to nature and according to reason.

Stand upright, or be set upright.

Just as it is with the parts of a single united body, so it is with rational beings, which exist separately yet have been made for one shared work. The awareness of this will become clearer to you if you often say to yourself: I am a limb of the body of rational beings. But if you say you are merely a part, you do not yet love your fellow human beings from the heart; doing good does not yet delight you for its own sake; you still do it merely as something proper, and not yet as doing good to yourself.

Let whatever will fall from outside fall upon the parts that can feel its impact. Those parts that have felt it may complain, if they choose. But I, unless I judge what has happened to be an evil, am not harmed. And it is in my power not to judge it so.

Whatever anyone does or says, I must be good, just as if the gold, or the emerald, or the purple were forever saying: whatever anyone does or says, I must be emerald and keep my color.

The ruling faculty does not disturb itself; I mean, it does not frighten itself or cause itself pain. But if anyone else can frighten or pain it, let him try. For the faculty itself will not, by its own opinion, turn itself toward such states. Let the body itself take care, if it can, that it suffers nothing, and let it speak up if it does suffer. But the soul itself, the part that is subject to fear and pain and has full power to form an opinion about these things, will suffer nothing, for it will never slide into such a judgment. The ruling principle in itself wants nothing, unless it creates a want for itself; and so it is free from disturbance and unobstructed, as long as it does not disturb and obstruct itself.

Happiness—eudaimonia—is a good daimon, a good divinity within, or something good and divine. So what are you doing here, imagination? Go away, I beg you by the gods, the same way you came, for I have no need of you. But you have come in your old habitual way. I am not angry with you; only go away.

Is anyone afraid of change? Why, what can happen at all without change? What is more pleasing or more natural to universal nature? Can you take a bath unless the wood undergoes change? Can you be nourished unless the food undergoes change? Can anything useful at all be accomplished without change? Do you not see, then, that for you to change is just the same thing, and equally necessary to universal nature?

Through the universal substance, as through a raging torrent, all bodies are swept along, united with the whole by their nature and working together with it, like the parts of our body with one another. How many a Chrysippus, how many a Socrates, how many an Epictetus has time already swallowed up! And let the same thought come to you about every person and every thing.

Only one thing troubles me: that I might do something the constitution of man does not allow, or in a way it does not allow, or that it does not allow now.

Near is your forgetting of all things, and near is your being forgotten by all.

It is peculiar to man to love even those who do wrong. This happens when it occurs to you, as they do wrong, that they are your kin, that they do wrong through ignorance and without meaning to, that soon you will both be dead, and above all that the wrongdoer has done you no harm, for he has not made your ruling faculty any worse than it was before.

Universal nature, out of the universal substance as if it were wax, now molds a horse, and when it has broken that up, uses the material for a tree, then for a man, then for something else; and each of these lasts only a very short time. But it is no hardship for the vessel to be broken up, just as there was none in its being put together.

A scowling look is entirely unnatural; when it is often put on, the result is that all its grace dies away, and at last is so completely extinguished that it cannot be rekindled at all. Try to draw from this very fact the conclusion that it is contrary to reason. For if even the awareness of doing wrong should leave you, what reason is there to go on living?

The nature that governs the whole will soon change everything you see, and out of their substance will make other things, and again other things out of the substance of those, so that the world may be forever new.

When someone has wronged you, consider at once what notion of good or evil led him to do wrong. For once you have seen this, you will pity him, and you will neither be amazed nor angry. For either you yourself hold the same thing to be good that he does, or something else of the same kind, in which case it is your duty to forgive him. But if you do not regard such things as good or evil, you will all the more readily be kindly toward someone who is in error.

Think not so much of what you do not have as of what you do have, and of the things you have, pick out the best, and then reflect how eagerly you would have hunted for them if you did not have them. At the same time, take care that you do not, by being so pleased with them, get into the habit of overvaluing them, so that you would be upset if you ever lost them.

Withdraw into yourself. The rational principle that rules has this nature: it is content with itself when it does what is just, and so secures its own tranquility.

Wipe out the imagination. Stop the pulling of the strings. Confine yourself to the present. Understand well what is happening, whether to you or to another. Divide and break down every object into its cause and its matter. Think of your last hour. Let the wrong done by another stay there, where the wrong was done.

Direct your attention to what is being said. Let your understanding enter into the things being done and into the things doing them.

Adorn yourself with simplicity and modesty, and with indifference toward the things that lie between virtue and vice. Love humankind. Follow God. That man says, "All things are by law, and in truth there are only the elements." It is enough to remember that all things are by law. Very little now remains.

About death: whether it is a scattering, or a dissolution into atoms, or annihilation, it is either an extinguishing or a change.

About pain: pain that is unbearable carries us off, but pain that lasts a long time is bearable; and the mind keeps its own tranquility by withdrawing into itself, and the ruling faculty is not made any worse. As for the parts that are harmed by pain, let them, if they can, speak their opinion about it.

About fame: look at the minds of those who chase it, observe what they are, and what sort of things they avoid, and what sort of things they pursue. And consider that, just as heaps of sand piled on one another bury the earlier sand, so in life the events that come before are soon buried by those that come after.

From Plato: "Do you suppose it possible for a man with an elevated mind, who takes in the whole of time and the whole of substance, to think human life is anything great?" "It is not possible," he said. "Then will such a man think death anything to fear?" "Not at all."

From Antisthenes: "It is kingly to do good and to be spoken ill of."

It is a disgraceful thing for the face to be obedient, arranging and composing itself as the mind commands, while the mind does not arrange and compose itself.

"We must not be angry at things, for they care nothing about it."

"To the immortal gods and to us give joy."

"Life must be reaped like a ripe ear of grain."

"One stands, and another falls."

"If the gods care nothing for me and my children, there is a reason for this too."

"For the good is with me, and the just."

"No joining others in their wailing, no violent passion."

From Plato: "But I would give this man a sufficient answer, which is this: you speak wrongly if you think that a man worth anything at all ought to weigh the risk of life or death, and not rather look to this one thing in everything he does: whether he is doing what is just or unjust, the work of a good man or of a bad one."

"For the truth is this, men of Athens: wherever a man has stationed himself, thinking it the best place for him, or has been stationed by his commander, there, in my opinion, he ought to stay and face the danger, reckoning nothing, neither death nor anything else, ahead of disgrace."

"But, my good friend, consider whether what is noble and good is not something other than saving your life and being saved. As for a man's living a certain length of time, at least if he is truly a man, consider whether this is not something to be put out of mind; there must be no clinging to life. A man must entrust these matters to the Deity and believe what the women say, that no one can escape his destiny, and the next question is how he may best live the time he has to live."

Look around at the courses of the stars, as if you were traveling along with them, and constantly consider how the elements change into one another, for such thoughts wash away the grime of life on earth.

Whoever is discussing human beings should also look at earthly things as if viewing them from some higher place, looking at them in their gatherings, armies, farm labor, marriages, treaties, births, deaths, the uproar of the law courts, lonely places, the various tribes of barbarians, festivals, mournings, marketplaces, a mixture of all things and an orderly weaving together of opposites.

Consider the past, all those great shifts of political power; you can foresee the things to come as well. For they will certainly take the same form, and they cannot possibly stray from the pattern of what happens now. And so to have observed human life for forty years is the same as to have observed it for ten thousand. For what more will you see?

"What has grown from the earth returns to the earth; but what has sprung from heavenly seed returns to the realms of heaven."

This is either a breaking apart of the atoms woven together, or a like scattering of the senseless elements.

"With food and drink and cunning magic arts they turn the channel's course, to escape death." "The breeze that heaven has sent we must endure, and toil without complaint."

Another may be more skilled at throwing his opponent, but he is no more sociable, no more modest, no better trained to meet whatever happens, no more considerate of his neighbors' faults.

Wherever a work can be done in keeping with the reason common to gods and men, there we have nothing to fear; for where we can gain benefit through activity that succeeds and proceeds according to our constitution, there no harm is to be suspected.

Everywhere and always it is in your power to accept your present condition with reverence, to behave justly toward those around you, and to apply your skill to your present thoughts, so that nothing slips into them without being well examined.

Do not look around you to discover other people's ruling principles, but look straight to this: to what nature is leading you, both universal nature through the things that happen to you, and your own nature through the acts you must perform. Every being ought to do what accords with its constitution. All other things have been made for the sake of rational beings, just as among irrational things the lower exists for the sake of the higher; but rational beings exist for the sake of one another.

The chief principle, then, in man's constitution is the social one. The second is not to give in to the urgings of the body, for it is the special office of the rational and intelligent motion to set its own limits and never to be overpowered by the motion of the senses or of the appetites, since both of these are animal; the intelligent motion claims supremacy and does not allow itself to be overpowered by the others. And rightly so, for it is formed by nature to make use of them all. The third thing in the rational constitution is freedom from error and from deception. Let the ruling principle, then, hold fast to these and go straight on, and it has what is its own.

Consider yourself dead, and that you have completed your life up to the present; and live the rest that is allowed you according to nature.

Love only what happens to you and is spun for you by the thread of your destiny. For what could suit you better?

In everything that happens, keep before your eyes those to whom the same things happened, and how they were vexed, and treated them as strange, and found fault with them. And now, where are they? Nowhere. Why, then, do you choose to act the same way? Why not leave these agitations, which are foreign to nature, to those who stir them up and those who are stirred by them? Why not be wholly intent on the right use of the things that happen to you? For then you will use them well, and they will be material for you to work on. Only attend to yourself, and resolve to be a good man in everything you do; and remember both: that what you do matters, and that the material you work on does not.

Look within. Within is the fountain of good, and it will keep bubbling up, if you will keep digging.

The body ought to be steady and to show no awkwardness in motion or posture. For what the mind shows in the face by keeping there an expression of intelligence and good order, that should be asked of the whole body as well. But all of this should be done without affectation.

The art of living is more like wrestling than dancing, in that it should stand ready and firm to meet whatever comes at it, sudden and unexpected.

Constantly observe who the people are whose approval you want, and what ruling principles they have. For then you will neither blame those who offend without meaning to, nor crave their approval, once you look to the sources of their opinions and desires.

"Every soul," the philosopher says, "is robbed of the truth against its will"; and in the same way it is robbed of justice and self-control and kindness and everything of the sort. It is most necessary to keep this constantly in mind, for then you will be gentler toward everyone.

In every pain, let this thought be present: that there is no dishonor in it, and that it does not make the governing intelligence any worse, for it does no damage to the intelligence either as rational or as social. In most pains, too, let this remark of Epicurus help you: that pain is neither unbearable nor everlasting, if you keep in mind that it has its limits and add nothing to it in your imagination. And remember this as well, that many things which annoy us are really the same as pain, though we do not notice it, such as drowsiness, being scorched by heat, and having no appetite. So whenever you are discontented over any of these, say to yourself that you are giving in to pain.

Take care not to feel toward the inhuman the way they feel toward other people.

How do we know that Telauges was not finer in character than Socrates? It is not enough that Socrates died a more noble death, argued more skillfully with the sophists, endured a night in the cold with greater toughness, and that, when ordered to arrest Leon of Salamis, judged it nobler to refuse, and that he walked the streets with a swagger, though one may well doubt whether that last point is even true. What we ought to ask is what sort of soul Socrates had, and whether he was able to be content with being just toward men and reverent toward the gods, neither pointlessly vexed at men's villainy, nor making himself a slave to any man's ignorance, nor treating anything that fell to his lot from the universe as strange, nor enduring it as unbearable, nor letting his understanding share in the feelings of the poor flesh.

Nature has not so blended the intelligence with the body's makeup as to deny you the power of setting your own limits and bringing under your control all that is your own; for it is quite possible to be a godlike man and to be recognized as such by no one. Always bear this in mind, and this too: that very little indeed is needed to live a happy life. And just because you have given up hope of becoming a logician and an expert in the knowledge of nature, do not for that reason give up the hope of being free and modest and social and obedient to God.

It is in your power to live free from all compulsion, in the greatest tranquility of mind, even if the whole world shouts against you all it likes, even if wild beasts tear apart the limbs of this lump of matter that has grown up around you. For what prevents the mind, in the midst of all this, from keeping itself in tranquility, in a just judgment of all that surrounds it, and in a ready use of whatever is set before it? So that the judgment may say to the thing it observes: This is what you are in reality, though in men's opinion you may seem to be something else. And the use may say to whatever comes to hand: You are the thing I was looking for; for to me whatever presents itself is always material for virtue, both rational and civic, and, in a word, for the practice of an art that belongs to man or God. For everything that happens is related either to God or to man, and is neither new nor hard to handle, but familiar and fit material to work on.

The perfection of character lies in this: in passing every day as if it were your last, neither violently agitated nor sluggish nor playing the hypocrite.

The gods, who are immortal, are not annoyed that for so long a time they must put up with men as they are, and so many of them bad; and besides this, they even take care of them in every way. But you, who are bound to die so soon, are you tired of putting up with the bad, and this when you are one of them?

It is absurd for a man not to flee from his own badness, which is in fact possible, but to flee from other men's badness, which is impossible.

Whatever the rational and social faculty finds to be neither intelligent nor social, it rightly judges to be beneath itself.

When you have done a good deed and another has been the better for it, why do you still go looking for some third thing besides, as fools do, either to get the reputation of having done a good deed or to be repaid?

No one tires of being helped; and help is action according to nature. So do not tire of being helped, when it is by helping others that you are helped.

The nature of the All set itself in motion to make the universe. Now, either everything that happens follows by way of consequence and connection, or else even the chief things toward which the ruling power of the universe directs its own movement are governed by no rational principle. If you remember this, it will make you more tranquil in many situations.
