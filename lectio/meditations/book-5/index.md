# Meditations, Book V

When you wake reluctantly in the morning, keep this thought ready: I am rising to do the work of a human being. Why should I be unhappy, if I am going to do the very things I exist for, the things I was brought into the world to do? Or was I made for this, to lie under the blankets and keep myself warm? "But this is more pleasant." So were you made to take your pleasure, and not at all for action and effort? Don't you see the little plants, the little birds, the ants, the spiders, the bees all working together to put their own parts of the universe in order? And you are unwilling to do the work of a human being, and don't hurry to do what your own nature calls for? "But I need rest too." You do. Yet nature has set limits to that, just as she has set limits to eating and drinking; and still you go past those limits, past what is enough, while in your work you do the opposite and stop short of what you could do. So you don't really love yourself, for if you did, you would love your nature and what it wants. People who love their crafts wear themselves out working at them, forgetting to wash or eat; but you value your own nature less than the metalworker values his craft, or the dancer his dancing, or the money-lover his money, or the vain man his scrap of glory. When such people are gripped by a passion for something, they would rather not eat or sleep than fall short of perfecting what they care about. Are the acts that serve society so worthless in your eyes, so much less worthy of your effort?

How easy it is to push away and wipe out every impression that is troubling or out of place, and at once to be completely at peace.

Judge that every word and action which is in keeping with nature is fitting for you, and don't be turned aside by the blame that may follow from others, or by their talk. If a thing is good to do or say, don't consider it beneath you. Those people have their own guiding principle and follow their own impulse; pay no attention to that, but go straight ahead, following your own nature and the common nature, for the path of both is one and the same.

I make my way through the things that happen according to nature until I fall and come to rest, breathing out my breath into the same element I draw it from each day, and falling onto the same earth from which my father gathered the seed, my mother the blood, and my nurse the milk; the earth that for so many years has supplied me with food and drink, and that bears me up as I tread on it and misuse it in so many ways.

You say men cannot admire the sharpness of your wit. So be it. But there are many other qualities about which you cannot say, I am not formed by nature for them. So display those qualities that are entirely within your power: sincerity, dignity, endurance of hard work, indifference to pleasure, contentment with your lot and with little, kindness, frankness, no taste for excess, no triviality, greatness of soul. Don't you see how many qualities you could show right now, for which there is no excuse of natural incapacity or unfitness, and yet you still choose to stay below the mark? Or are you forced by some defect of nature to grumble, to be stingy, to flatter, to blame your poor body, to fawn on people, to make a great show of yourself, and to be so restless in your mind? No, by the gods, you could have been free of all this long ago. If you can truly be charged only with being somewhat slow and dull in understanding, then work at this too, instead of neglecting it or taking comfort in your dullness.

One man, when he has done another a service, is quick to put it on the books as a favor owed to him. Another doesn't go that far, but still privately counts the man as his debtor and is well aware of what he has done. A third, in a way, isn't even aware of what he has done; he is like a vine that has produced grapes and looks for nothing more once it has yielded its proper fruit. Like a horse that has run its race, a dog that has tracked its game, a bee that has made its honey, so a man who has done a good deed doesn't call out for others to come and see, but moves on to the next deed, as the vine moves on to bear grapes again in season. So we must be among those who do this, in a way, without noticing it. "Yes," he says, "but one has to be aware of this very thing, for it is the mark of the social creature to perceive that it is acting for society, and indeed to want its fellow to perceive it too." What you say is true, but you misunderstand the point being made, and for that very reason you will become one of those I described earlier, for even they are led astray by a certain show of reason. But if you will choose to grasp the real meaning of what is said, don't be afraid that you will therefore neglect any social act.

A prayer of the Athenians: Rain, rain, dear Zeus, on the plowed fields and plains of the Athenians. In truth, either we should not pray at all, or we should pray in just this simple and noble way.

Just as we understand what it means when we say that Asclepius prescribed for a man horseback riding, or cold baths, or going barefoot, so we must understand it when we say that the nature of the universe prescribed for a man disease, or maiming, or loss, or anything else of the kind. In the first case, prescribed means roughly this: he ordered this for this man as something suited to bring about health; and in the second it means that what happens to each man is in some way fixed for him to fit his destiny. This is what we mean when we say that things suit us, just as builders say of the squared stones in walls or pyramids that they suit one another, when they fit together in some connection. For there is altogether one fitness, one harmony. And just as the universe is made up out of all bodies to be the body it is, so out of all the existing causes destiny is made up to be the cause it is. Even people who understand nothing of this grasp my meaning, for they say, Fate brought this on him. So this was brought to him, this was prescribed for him. Let us then accept these things as we accept what Asclepius prescribes. Many of his prescriptions, too, are unpleasant, yet we accept them in hope of health. Let the carrying-out and completion of the things that the common nature judges good be regarded by you as on the same footing as your health. And so accept everything that happens, even if it seems unpleasant, because it leads to this: the health of the universe and the well-being and good fortune of Zeus. For he would not have brought on anyone what he has brought, if it were not useful for the whole. For not even an ordinary nature brings about anything unsuited to what it governs. So for two reasons you should be content with what happens to you: first, because it was done for you, prescribed for you, and in a way had reference to you, spun together with your destiny from the most ancient causes; and second, because even what comes to each man separately is, for the power that administers the universe, a cause of its well-being and completion, and indeed of its very continuance. For the integrity of the whole is maimed if you cut off anything at all from the connection and continuity of its parts or of its causes. And you do cut it off, as far as you can, when you are discontented, and in a way you try to do away with it.

Don't be disgusted, discouraged, or dissatisfied if you don't manage to do everything by sound principles. When you fail, go back and try again, and be content if most of what you do is consistent with human nature, and love the thing you return to. Don't go back to philosophy as if it were a stern master, but like a man with sore eyes who reaches for a bit of sponge and egg, or like another who applies a plaster, or a soak of water. That way you will not fail to obey reason, and you will find rest in it. Remember too that philosophy asks only for what your nature asks; it is you who want something else that is not according to nature. But, you may object, what is more agreeable than what I am doing now? Yet isn't this the very way pleasure deceives us? Consider whether greatness of soul, freedom, simplicity, calm, and devotion are not more agreeable still. And what is more agreeable than wisdom itself, when you think of the sureness and easy flow of everything that depends on the powers of understanding and knowledge?

Things are so wrapped up and obscure that they have seemed to philosophers, and not a few of them, nor commonplace ones, altogether unintelligible; indeed even to the Stoics themselves they seem hard to understand. And every judgment we make is liable to change, for where is the man who never changes his mind? Turn your thoughts, then, to the objects themselves, and consider how short-lived and worthless they are, and how they may be owned by a debauchee, a prostitute, or a thief. Then turn to the characters of those who live with you, and you can hardly bear even the most agreeable of them, let alone bear yourself. In such darkness and filth, then, and in such a ceaseless flow of substance and time, of motion and things moved, I cannot imagine what there is worth prizing highly or even pursuing seriously. On the contrary, a man's duty is to comfort himself and wait for his natural dissolution without fretting at the delay, resting only in these principles: first, that nothing will happen to me that is not in keeping with the nature of the universe; and second, that it is in my power never to act against my god and the divinity within me, for no one can force me to do so.

What am I now using my soul for? On every occasion I must ask myself this, and examine what I now have in this part of me that they call the ruling principle, and whose soul I now have: a child's, a young man's, a weak woman's, a tyrant's, a tame animal's, or a wild beast's?

What sort of things the many consider good, we can learn even from this. If a man conceived of certain things as truly good, such as practical wisdom, self-control, justice, and courage, then having first grasped these he could not bear to hear anything that did not square with what is truly good. But if a man has first conceived as good the things the many call good, he will listen and gladly accept as very apt the line of the comic poet. In this way even the many sense the difference. For if it were otherwise, the saying would give offense and be rejected when applied to true goods, while we accept it as fitting and witty when it is said of wealth and of the means to luxury and fame. So go on and ask whether we should value, and judge to be good, the very things to which, once conceived in the mind, the comic poet's words could aptly be applied, that the man who has them has, through sheer abundance, nowhere even to relieve himself.

I am made up of the formal and the material; and neither of these will perish into nothing, just as neither came into being out of nothing. Every part of me, then, will be changed into some part of the universe, and that in turn into another part of the universe, and so on forever. By such a change I too came to exist, and so did those who begot me, and so on back forever in the other direction. For nothing stops us from saying this, even if the universe is administered in fixed cycles.

Reason and the art of reasoning are powers sufficient for themselves and for their own works. They set out from a starting point of their own and make their way to the goal set before them; and that is why such acts are called katorthoseis, "straight-goings," a word that signifies the straightness of their road.

None of these things should be called a man's, which do not belong to a man as man. They are not required of a man, nor does man's nature promise them, nor are they the means by which his nature reaches its end. Therefore man's end does not lie in these things, nor does what helps toward that end, and what helps toward it is what is good. Besides, if any of these things did belong to man, it would not be right for him to despise them and set himself against them; nor would a man deserve praise for showing that he had no need of them, nor would a man who went without any of them be good, if these things really were good. But in fact, the more a man strips himself of these things, or others like them, or the more patiently he bears being deprived of any of them, by just so much is he the better man.

Such as are your habitual thoughts, such also will your mind's character be, for the soul is dyed by its thoughts. Dye it, then, with a continuous run of thoughts like these: that wherever a man can live, there he can also live well. But suppose he must live in a palace; well then, he can live well in a palace too. And consider further that whatever a thing has been made for, that is what it has been made for, and that is what it is carried toward; and its end lies in what it is carried toward; and where the end is, there too lie the advantage and the good of each thing. Now the good for a reasoning creature is society, for it has been shown above that we are made for society. Isn't it plain that the inferior exists for the sake of the superior? And living things are superior to lifeless ones, and among living things the superior are those that have reason.

To seek what is impossible is madness; and it is impossible for the bad not to do things of this kind.

Nothing happens to any man that he is not formed by nature to bear. The same things happen to another man, and either because he doesn't notice that they have happened, or because he wants to show greatness of spirit, he stands firm and remains unharmed. It is a disgrace, then, that ignorance and conceit should be stronger than wisdom.

Things in themselves do not touch the soul, not in the slightest; they have no access to the soul, and they cannot turn or move it. The soul alone turns and moves itself, and whatever judgments it sees fit to make, it makes for itself out of the things that present themselves to it.

In one respect a man is the thing nearest to me, in that I must do good to others and put up with them. But insofar as some men set themselves in the way of my proper actions, a man becomes to me one of the things that are indifferent, no less than the sun, the wind, or a wild beast. These may hinder my action, but they are no hindrance to my impulses and my disposition, which can act conditionally and adapt; for the mind turns and changes every obstacle to its activity into an aid, so that what was a hindrance becomes a help to action, and what blocks the road actually advances us along it.

Revere what is best in the universe, that which makes use of all things and directs all things. And in the same way revere what is best in yourself, for it is of the same kind. In you too, this is the thing that makes use of everything else, and by this your life is directed.

What does no harm to the state does no harm to the citizen. Whenever something appears harmful, apply this rule: if the state is not harmed by it, neither am I. But if the state is harmed, do not be angry with the one who harms it; instead, show him where his error lies.

Think often of how swiftly things pass by and vanish, both the things that exist and the things that are being produced. For substance is like a river in continual flow, the activities of things are forever changing, and the causes work in countless ways; there is hardly anything that stands still. And consider this thing close at hand, the boundless abyss of past and future, in which all things disappear. How then is the man not a fool who is puffed up over such things, or tormented by them, and makes himself miserable? For they trouble him only for a time, and a short time at that.

Think of the universal substance, of which you are a very small part; and of universal time, of which a brief, indivisible moment has been allotted to you; and of destiny, and what a tiny part of it you are.

Does another do me wrong? Let him see to it. He has his own disposition and his own way of acting. For my part, I now have what the universal nature now wills me to have, and I do what my own nature now wills me to do.

Let the part of your soul that leads and governs stay undisturbed by the movements of the flesh, whether of pleasure or of pain. Let it not mingle with them, but keep itself separate and confine those feelings to their own parts of the body. But when these feelings rise up to the mind through the natural sympathy that exists in a body that is all one, then do not try to resist the sensation, for it is natural; only do not let the ruling part add to the sensation the opinion that it is either good or bad.

Live with the gods. And he lives with the gods who constantly shows them that his soul is satisfied with what is assigned to him, and that it does all that the divinity within him wishes, the divinity that Zeus has given to each man as his guardian and guide, a portion of himself. And this is each man's understanding and reason.

Are you angry with the man whose armpits stink? Are you angry with the man whose breath smells foul? What good will your anger do you? He has such a mouth, he has such armpits; from such things such an odor must come. But the man has reason, you say, and could, if he took the trouble, work out where he is offending. Good luck to you with that discovery. Well then, you too have reason: use your rational faculty to rouse his; show him his error, set him straight. If he listens, you will cure him, and there is no need of anger. Neither tragic actor nor prostitute.

As you intend to live once you have departed, so it is in your power to live here. But if men will not allow it, then leave life, yet as one who suffers no harm by it. The room is smoky, and I walk out. Why think this any great trouble? But so long as nothing of the kind drives me out, I stay, I am free, and no one shall stop me from doing what I choose; and I choose to act according to the nature of a rational and social creature.

The intelligence of the universe is social. Accordingly it has made the inferior things for the sake of the superior, and it has fitted the superior to one another. You see how it has subordinated and coordinated them, assigning to each its proper portion, and bringing the best things into harmony with one another.

How have you behaved up to now toward the gods, toward your parents, brothers, children, teachers, toward those who cared for you in infancy, toward your friends, your relatives, your slaves? Consider whether up to now you have behaved toward them all in such a way that this could be said of you:

"Never has he wronged a man in deed or word."

And call to mind how many things you have lived through, and how much you have been able to endure; that the story of your life is now complete and your service ended; how many beautiful things you have seen; how many pleasures and pains you have looked past; how many so-called honors you have spurned; and to how many ill-natured people you have shown a kind disposition.

Why do unskilled and ignorant souls disturb the one who has skill and knowledge? And what soul has skill and knowledge? The one that knows the beginning and the end, and knows the reason that runs through all substance and, throughout all time, administers the universe in fixed cycles.

Soon, very soon, you will be ashes or a skeleton, and either a name or not even a name; and a name is mere sound and echo. The things much prized in life are empty and rotten and trivial, like little dogs snapping at one another, or little children quarreling, laughing, and the next moment in tears.

"Loyalty and modesty and justice and truth have fled up to Olympus from the wide-spread earth."

What, then, is left to keep you here, if the objects of sense are forever shifting and never stand still, if the organs of perception are dull and easily take in false impressions, and the poor soul itself is but a vapor risen from blood? To have a good reputation in such a world as this is an empty thing. Why then do you not wait calmly for your end, whether it be extinction or a passing into another state? And until that time comes, what is enough? Why, what else than to honor the gods and bless them, to do good to others, to practice tolerance and self-restraint; and as for everything beyond the bounds of this poor flesh and breath, to remember that it is neither yours nor in your power.

You can spend your life in an even flow of happiness, if you can go by the right road and think and act in the right way. These two things are common to the soul of God, to the soul of man, and to the soul of every rational being: not to be hindered by another, and to find the good in a disposition toward justice and the practice of it, and there to let your desire come to rest.

If this is not my own badness, nor the effect of my own badness, and the common good is not harmed by it, why am I troubled about it, and what is the harm to the common good?

Do not be carried away thoughtlessly by the appearance of things, but give help to all as far as you can and as they deserve; and if they have suffered loss in matters that are indifferent, do not imagine this to be real damage, for that is a bad habit. But just as the old man, when he was leaving, asked back his foster-child's top, remembering all the while that it was only a top, so do you act in this case too.

When you find yourself crying out on the Rostra, have you forgotten, man, what these things really are? "Yes, but they matter greatly to these people." Will you then let yourself be made a fool of along with them? "I was once a fortunate man, but I lost it, I don't know how." But to be fortunate means to have given yourself a good fortune, and a good fortune is a good disposition of the soul, good impulses, good actions.
