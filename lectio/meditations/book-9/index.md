# Meditations, Book IX

Whoever acts unjustly acts irreverently toward the divine. Since universal nature made rational beings for one another's sake, to help each other as each deserves and never to harm one another, anyone who breaks her will is plainly guilty of irreverence toward the highest divinity. The liar, too, is guilty of irreverence toward that same divinity, for universal nature is the nature of all things that exist, and existing things are related to everything that comes into being. Furthermore, this universal nature is named truth, and is the first cause of all things that are true. So the man who lies on purpose is guilty of irreverence, since by deceiving he acts unjustly; and the man who lies without meaning to is also guilty, since he is out of step with universal nature and disturbs the order of things by fighting against the world's nature. For whoever is carried, in spite of himself, toward what is contrary to truth is fighting against it, since he had received from nature the powers which, by neglecting them, he can no longer use to tell falsehood from truth. And the man who chases pleasure as good and runs from pain as evil is likewise guilty of irreverence. For such a man must constantly find fault with universal nature, complaining that it hands out things to the bad and the good unfairly, because the bad so often enjoy pleasure and own the things that produce it, while the good get pain as their lot and the things that cause it. Again, the man who is afraid of pain will sometimes also be afraid of things that are bound to happen in the world, and that itself is irreverence. And the man who chases pleasure will not hold back from injustice, and that is plainly irreverence. Now toward those things to which universal nature is equally disposed—for it would not have made both unless it were equally disposed toward both—those who wish to follow nature should be of the same mind and equally disposed. So with regard to pain and pleasure, death and life, honor and dishonor, all of which universal nature uses equally, whoever is not equally disposed is clearly acting irreverently. When I say universal nature uses them equally, I mean that they happen alike, in due sequence, to all that comes into being and all that follows after. This comes from a first movement of Providence, by which, from some beginning, it set out to make this ordered world: it gathered up the patterns of all that was to be, and marked out the powers that bring forth beings, their changes, and their successions.

It would be a man's happiest fate to leave the human race without ever having tasted lying and hypocrisy and excess and pride. But to breathe out one's life when one has had quite enough of these things is, as the saying goes, the next best course. Have you decided to stay on with vice? Has experience not yet persuaded you to flee this plague? For the ruin of the understanding is a plague—far more so, in fact, than any corruption or change in the air around us. That corruption is a plague to animals insofar as they are animals; but this other one is a plague to human beings insofar as they are human.

Do not look down on death, but welcome it gladly, since it too is one of the things nature wills. For just as it is natural to be young and to grow old, to develop and reach maturity, to grow teeth and a beard and gray hair, to father children, to conceive, to give birth, and to go through all the other natural processes the seasons of your life bring, so too is dying. This, then, befits a thoughtful person: to be neither careless nor impatient nor scornful about death, but to wait for it as one of nature's operations. Just as you now wait for the time when the child will come out of your wife's womb, so be ready for the time when your soul will drop out of this shell. But if you also want a more ordinary kind of comfort, something to touch your heart, you will be best reconciled to death by looking at the things you are about to leave behind and the characters of the people your soul will no longer be mixed up with. For it is in no way right to be angry with people; instead, it is your duty to care for them and bear with them gently. And yet remember that you will not be departing from people who share your principles. For this is the only thing, if anything, that could pull us the other way and keep us attached to life—the chance to live among people who share our principles. But as it is, you see how much trouble comes from the discord of those who live together, so that you may well say: Come quickly, death, lest I too forget myself.

Whoever does wrong does wrong to himself. Whoever acts unjustly acts unjustly to himself, because he makes himself bad.

A man often acts unjustly by not doing something, not only by doing something.

Your present judgment, grounded in understanding; your present conduct, directed toward the common good; your present attitude, content with everything that happens—that is enough.

Wipe out your impressions; rein in your desires; put out your appetites; keep the ruling faculty under its own control.

Among animals that have no reason, a single life is distributed; but among rational animals, a single intelligent soul is distributed—just as there is a single earth for all things of an earthly nature, and we all see by one light and breathe one air, all of us who have the power of sight and all who have life.

All things that share in something common to them move toward what is of their own kind. Everything earthy turns toward the earth; everything liquid flows together; everything of an airy kind does the same—so that they have to be kept apart by something, by the application of force. Fire, it is true, moves upward because of the elemental fire, yet it is so ready to catch with any fire here that even any fairly dry substance is easily set alight, because there is less mixed into it of what resists burning. In the same way, then, everything that shares in the common intelligent nature moves toward what is of its own kind—indeed moves even more strongly. For the more it surpasses all other things, the more ready it is to mingle with and fuse into what is akin to it. So among animals without reason we find swarms of bees, herds of cattle, the rearing of young birds, and, in a way, loves—for even in animals there are souls, and the power that draws them together is seen to assert itself to a higher degree than is ever observed in plants or stones or trees. But in rational animals there are political communities, friendships, families, and gatherings; and in wars, treaties and truces. And in things still higher, even though they are separated from one another, a kind of unity exists, as among the stars. Thus the ascent to a higher degree can produce sympathy even in things that are separated. See, then, what is happening now: only intelligent animals have forgotten this mutual longing and attraction, and in them alone the tendency to flow together is not seen. But still, though people try to avoid this union, they are caught and held by it, for their nature is too strong for them; and you will see what I mean if you simply observe. You will sooner find something earthy that touches no other earthy thing than a person wholly cut off from other people.

Both man and God and the universe bear fruit; each does so in its proper season. And if common usage has fixed the word "fruit" especially to the vine and such things, that is nothing. Reason bears fruit both for all and for itself, and from it come other things of the same kind as reason itself.

If you can, correct those who do wrong by teaching them; but if you cannot, remember that forbearance was given to you for this very purpose. The gods, too, are forbearing toward such people; for certain ends they even help them obtain health, wealth, and reputation—that is how kind they are. And this is in your power as well; or tell me, who is stopping you?

Do your work not like someone miserable, nor like someone who wants to be pitied or admired; aim your will at one thing only—to set yourself in motion and to check yourself, as social reason requires.

Today I have escaped all trouble—or rather, I have thrown out all trouble, for it was not outside me but within, in my own judgments.

All things are the same: familiar from experience, short-lived in time, and worthless in substance. Everything now is exactly as it was in the days of those we have buried.

Things stand outside of us, by themselves and on their own, knowing nothing of themselves and passing no judgment. What is it, then, that judges about them? The ruling faculty.

For the rational social animal, evil and good lie not in being acted upon but in acting, just as his virtue and his vice lie not in being acted upon but in acting.

For the stone that has been thrown up, it is no evil to come down, nor was it any good to have been carried up.

Look inside men's guiding principles, and you will see what kind of judges you are afraid of, and what kind of judges they are of themselves.

All things are changing, and you yourself are in constant change and, in a way, in constant destruction—and so is the whole universe.

It is your duty to leave another man's wrongdoing right where it is.

The ending of an activity, the cessation of a movement or an opinion—their death, in a sense—is no evil. Turn your thoughts now to your own life: your life as a child, as a youth, as a grown man, in old age; for in each of these every change was a death. Was this anything to fear? Turn your thoughts now to your life under your grandfather, then your life under your mother, then your life under your father; and as you find many other differences and changes and endings, ask yourself: Is this anything to fear? In the same way, then, neither is the ending and cessation and change of your whole life anything to be afraid of.

Be quick to examine your own ruling faculty, and that of the universe, and that of your neighbor: your own, so that you may make it just; that of the universe, so that you may remember what you are a part of; and that of your neighbor, so that you may know whether he acted in ignorance or with knowledge, and may also consider that his ruling faculty is akin to your own.

Just as you yourself are a part of a social system, so let every act of yours be a part of social life. Any act of yours, then, that has no reference, directly or indirectly, to a social end tears your life apart, does not let it be one, and is of the nature of mutiny—just as, in a public assembly, a man who acts on his own stands apart from the general agreement.

The squabbles and games of little children, and "little souls carrying corpses about"—such is everything; so that what is shown in the picture of the underworld strikes the eye more sharply.

Examine the quality of an object's form, separate it entirely from its material part, and then contemplate it; then work out the longest time that a thing of this particular form is naturally made to last.

You have endured endless troubles by not being content with your ruling faculty when it does the things it was constituted by nature to do. But enough of this.

When someone blames you or hates you, or when people say anything hurtful about you, go to their poor souls, get inside, and see what kind of men they are. You will discover that there is no reason to trouble yourself about what opinion they have of you. Even so, you must be well disposed toward them, for by nature they are friends. And the gods, too, help them in every way—by dreams, by signs—toward attaining the things they value.

The periodic movements of the universe are the same, up and down, from age to age. Either the universal intelligence sets itself in motion for each separate effect—and if so, be content with what results from its activity; or it set itself in motion once, and everything else follows as a kind of consequence; or indivisible elements are the origin of all things. In short, if there is a god, all is well; and if chance rules, do not act at random yourself.

Soon the earth will cover us all; then the earth itself will change, and the things that result from that change will go on changing forever, and these again forever. For if a man reflects on these changes and transformations, following one another like wave after wave, and on how fast they come, he will look down on everything that perishes.

The universal cause is like a winter torrent: it sweeps everything along with it. But how worthless are all these poor people busy with political affairs and, as they imagine, playing the philosopher—all of them drivelers. Well then, man: do what nature now requires. Set yourself in motion, if it is in your power, and do not look around to see whether anyone notices; and do not wait for Plato's Republic, but be content if the smallest thing goes well, and consider such an outcome no small matter. For who can change men's opinions? And without a change of opinions, what is there but the slavery of men who groan while pretending to obey? Come now, tell me of Alexander and Philip and Demetrius of Phalerum. They themselves shall judge whether they found out what the common nature required and trained themselves accordingly. But if they acted like heroes in a tragedy, no one has condemned me to imitate them. The work of philosophy is simple and modest. Do not pull me aside into insolence and pride.

Look down from above on the countless herds of men, their countless ceremonies, their endlessly varied voyages through storms and calms, and the differences among those who are born, live together, and die. Consider, too, the lives lived by others long ago, the lives of those who will live after you, and the lives now lived among foreign nations; how many do not even know your name, and how many will soon forget it; how those who perhaps praise you now will very soon blame you; and that neither a name after death, nor reputation, nor anything else is of any value.

Let there be freedom from disturbance regarding the things that come from external causes; and let there be justice in the things done by your own internal cause—that is, let your movement and action end in this, in social acts, for this is in keeping with your nature.

You can clear away many of the useless things that disturb you, for they lie entirely in your own opinion; and you will then gain ample room for yourself by taking in the whole universe with your mind, contemplating the eternity of time, and observing how rapidly each separate thing changes—how short the span is from birth to dissolution, and how limitless the time before birth, and equally boundless the time after dissolution.

All that you see will quickly perish, and those who watched it dissolve will very soon perish too. And the man who dies at the most extreme old age will be brought to the same condition as the one who died too soon.

What are these men's guiding principles? With what kinds of things do they busy themselves? For what kinds of reasons do they love and honor? Imagine you can see their poor souls laid bare. When they suppose they do harm by blaming you or good by praising you, what a delusion!

Loss is nothing other than change. But universal nature delights in change, and in obedience to her all things are now done well, have been done so from eternity in the same form, and will be so for time without end. What, then, are you saying—that all things have been and always will be bad, and that no power was ever found among so many gods to set these things right, but that the world has been condemned to be bound in unceasing evil?

The rottenness of the matter that underlies everything! Water, dust, bones, filth; or again, marble—the calluses of the earth; gold and silver—mere sediment; clothing—just bits of hair; purple dye—just blood; and everything else is of the same kind. Even what is of the nature of breath is another thing of the same kind, changing from this to that.

Enough of this wretched life, and grumbling, and aping tricks. Why are you disturbed? What is there new in this? What unsettles you? Is it the form of the thing? Look at it. Or is it the matter? Look at it. Beyond these there is nothing. So, toward the gods, become at last simpler and better. It makes no difference whether you examine these things for a hundred years or for three.

If a man has done wrong, the harm is his own. But perhaps he has not done wrong.

Either all things come from one intelligent source and fit together as in a single body—and then the part ought not to find fault with what is done for the good of the whole; or there are only atoms, and nothing but mixture and dispersion. Why, then, are you disturbed? Say to your ruling faculty: Are you dead? Are you corrupted? Are you playing the hypocrite? Have you become a beast? Do you herd and feed with the rest?

Either the gods have no power, or they have power. If they have no power, why do you pray to them? But if they have power, why not pray that they grant you the ability not to fear any of the things you fear, not to desire any of the things you desire, and not to grieve over anything—rather than praying that some of these things should or should not happen? For surely, if they can cooperate with men at all, they can cooperate toward these ends. But perhaps you will say: the gods have put these things in my own power. Well, then, is it not better to use what is in your power like a free man than to crave, in a slavish and groveling way, what is not in your power? And who told you that the gods do not help us even in the things that are in our power? Just start praying for such things, and you will see. One man prays: How may I manage to sleep with that woman? You pray: How may I not desire to sleep with her? Another prays: How may I be rid of this? You pray: How may I not desire to be rid of it? Another: How may I not lose my little son? You: How may I not be afraid to lose him? In short, turn your prayers this way, and see what comes of it.

Epicurus says: "In my illness my conversation was not about my bodily sufferings, nor," he says, "did I talk of such things to those who visited me; instead I went on discussing the nature of things as before, keeping to this main point: how the mind, while sharing in such disturbances as go on in the poor flesh, can stay free of upheaval and keep hold of its own proper good. Nor," he says, "did I give the doctors the chance to put on solemn looks, as if they were doing something great; my life went on well and happily." Do the same, then, that he did, both in sickness, if you fall sick, and in any other circumstance. For never to abandon philosophy whatever happens to us, and not to chatter idly with an ignorant man or one who knows nothing of nature, is a principle of all schools of philosophy; and so is being intent only on what you are doing now and on the instrument with which you do it.

When you are offended by someone's shameless behavior, ask yourself at once: Is it possible, then, for shameless men not to exist in the world? It is not possible. So do not demand the impossible. This man is simply one of those shameless men who must necessarily exist in the world. Keep the same considerations in mind in the case of the cheat, the faithless man, and everyone who does wrong in any way. For the moment you remind yourself that it is impossible for such men not to exist, you will be more kindly disposed toward each of them as an individual. It is useful, too, to notice right away, when the occasion arises, what virtue nature has given man to set against each kind of wrongdoing. As an antidote to the obtuse man she has given mildness, and against another kind of man some other power. And in every case it is possible for you to correct, by teaching, the man who has gone astray; for every man who errs misses his aim and has gone astray. Besides, how have you been harmed? You will find that none of those you are irritated with has done anything to make your mind worse; and what is bad and harmful to you has its foundation only in your mind. And what harm is done, or what is strange about it, if a man who has not been taught does the things of an untaught man? Consider whether you should not rather blame yourself for not expecting such a man to err in such a way. Your reason gave you the means to suppose he would likely make this mistake, and yet you forgot, and now you are amazed that he has erred. But most of all, when you blame a man as faithless or ungrateful, turn to yourself. For the fault is plainly your own—whether you trusted that a man with such a disposition would keep his promise, or whether, in doing him a kindness, you did not do it unconditionally, nor in such a way as to have received the whole profit from the very act. For what more do you want, when you have done a man a service? Is it not enough that you have done something in keeping with your nature—must you seek to be paid for it? That would be as if the eye demanded a reward for seeing, or the feet for walking. For just as these members were made for a particular purpose, and by working according to their own constitution get what is their own, so too man, formed by nature for acts of kindness, when he has done something kind or otherwise serving the common interest, has acted in keeping with his constitution and gets what is his own.
