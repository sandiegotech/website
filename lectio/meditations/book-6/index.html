Meditations, Book VI

Book VI6 of 12

The substance of the universe is obedient and yielding, and the reason that governs it has in itself no cause to do evil, for it holds no malice, does evil to nothing, and harms nothing. All things are made and brought to completion according to this reason.

Let it make no difference to you whether you are cold or warm, as long as you are doing your duty; whether you are drowsy or fully rested; whether spoken ill of or praised; whether dying or doing something else. For dying is one of the acts of life, and in this act too it is enough to do well what we have in hand.

Look beneath the surface. Do not let the particular quality of anything, or its worth, escape you.

All existing things change soon, and they will either be reduced to vapor, if indeed all substance is one, or else they will be scattered.

The reason that governs knows its own disposition, what it does, and the material on which it works.

The best way to avenge yourself is not to become like the wrongdoer.

Take pleasure and rest in one thing: in passing from one act done for others to another act done for others, with God in your thoughts.

The ruling part of you is what rouses and turns itself; it makes itself such as it is and such as it wills to be, and it also makes everything that happens appear to itself as it wills.

Every single thing is brought to completion in accordance with the nature of the universe; for it cannot be in accordance with any other nature that each thing is accomplished, neither a nature that contains this one from outside, nor a nature contained within it, nor a nature external to it and independent.

The universe is either confusion, things tangled together and scattered apart, or else it is unity, order, and providence. If it is the former, why do I wish to linger in a random jumble of things, in such disorder? Why should I care about anything except how I shall at last become earth? And why am I disturbed, since my elements will be scattered whatever I do? But if the other view is true, then I revere, I stand firm, and I trust in the one who governs.

When circumstances have forced you into some disturbance, return quickly to yourself, and do not stay out of tune any longer than the compulsion lasts; for you will gain greater mastery over the harmony by continually coming back to it.

If you had a stepmother and a mother at the same time, you would do your duty by the stepmother, yet you would constantly return to your mother. Let the court and philosophy now be your stepmother and your mother: return to philosophy often and find rest in her, for through her what you meet with at court becomes bearable to you, and you become bearable at court.

When we have meat and such food before us, we get the impression: this is the dead body of a fish, this the dead body of a bird or a pig; and again, this fine Falernian wine is only a little grape juice, this purple robe just sheep's wool dyed with the blood of a shellfish. Such impressions reach the things themselves and penetrate them, so that we see what kind of things they really are. We should act this way all through life: where things seem most worthy of our approval, lay them bare, look at how worthless they are, and strip away the words that exalt them. For outward show is a wonderful corrupter of reason, and it cheats you most when you are most sure you are busy with things worth your trouble. Consider, then, what Crates says of Xenocrates himself.

Most of the things the crowd admires belong to the most general categories, things held together by mere cohesion or natural growth, such as stones, wood, fig trees, vines, olives. The things admired by men a little more reasonable are held together by a living principle, like flocks and herds, or else they simply admire owning a crowd of slaves. The things admired by men still better instructed are held together by a rational soul, though valued not as rational but as skilled in some craft or clever in some other way. But the man who values a rational soul, one fitted for life in society, regards nothing else; above all he keeps his own soul in a state and activity in keeping with reason and social life, and he works toward this end together with others of his own kind.

Some things are hurrying into existence, others hurrying out of it; and of what is coming into being, part is already gone. Motions and changes are continually renewing the world, just as the unbroken passage of time is always renewing the infinite stretch of ages. In this flowing stream, where there is no firm footing, what is there among the things rushing by that anyone should prize highly? It would be like falling in love with one of the sparrows flying past, when it has already passed out of sight. Something like this is the very life of each of us, like the rising of vapor from the blood and the breathing of air. For as we draw in a breath and give it back every moment, so it is with the whole power of breathing that you received at birth, only yesterday or the day before: you will give it back to the element you first drew it from.

The breathing of plants is nothing to be valued, nor the breathing of tame animals and wild beasts; nor receiving impressions through the appearances of things; nor being pulled about by desires like a puppet by strings; nor herding together; nor being nourished by food, which is just like getting rid of the useless part of what we eat. What, then, is worth valuing? To be applauded? No. Nor should we value the applause of tongues, for the praise of the many is just a clapping of tongues. So suppose you have given up this worthless thing called fame; what remains worth valuing? This, in my opinion: to move yourself and to hold yourself back in keeping with your own constitution, the end to which all employments and arts lead. For every art aims at this, that the thing made should be fit for the work it was made for; and the vine grower tending his vine, the horse breaker, and the dog trainer all seek this end. And the education and teaching of the young aim at something too. Here, then, lies the value of education and teaching. And if this is sound, you will not seek anything else. Will you not stop valuing so many other things? If you do not, you will never be free, or sufficient for your own happiness, or free from passion. For you must of necessity be envious, jealous, and suspicious of those who can take those things away, and you will plot against those who have what you value. Anyone who wants any of these things must necessarily be in a state of inner turmoil, and besides, he must often find fault with the gods. But to reverence and honor your own mind will make you content with yourself, in harmony with society, and in agreement with the gods, that is, praising all that they give and have ordained.

Above, below, all around are the movements of the elements. But the motion of virtue is in none of these: it is something more divine, and advancing by a path scarcely noticed, it goes happily on its way.

How strangely people act! They will not praise those living at the same time and alongside them; yet to be praised themselves by future generations, by people they have never seen and never will see, they set great store. But this is much the same as being grieved that those who lived before you did not praise you.

If something is hard for you to accomplish, do not conclude that it is impossible for any human being; but if anything is possible for a human being and in keeping with his nature, consider that it is within your reach too.

Suppose in the wrestling ground a man has scratched you with his nails or struck your head and given you a bruise. We show no sign of being upset, take no offense, and do not afterward suspect him of foul play; yet we keep an eye on him, not as an enemy, nor with suspicion, but we quietly steer clear of him. Let your conduct be like this in all the other parts of life: let us overlook many things in those who are like our opponents in the ring. For it is in our power, as I said, to get out of the way and to hold no suspicion or hatred.

If anyone can convince me and show me that I am not thinking or acting rightly, I will gladly change; for I seek the truth, by which no one was ever harmed. But the one who is harmed is the one who persists in his own error and ignorance.

I do my duty: other things do not trouble me, for they are either things without life, or things without reason, or things that have wandered off and lost their way.

As for the animals that have no reason, and indeed all things and objects, since you have reason and they do not, make use of them in a generous and openhanded spirit. But toward human beings, since they have reason, behave in a spirit of fellowship. And on every occasion call on the gods; and do not worry about how long you will go on doing this, for even three hours so spent are enough.

Alexander of Macedon and his stable boy were brought by death to the same state; for either they were taken up into the same generative principles of the universe, or they were alike scattered among the atoms.

Consider how many things take place in each of us in a single indivisible moment, things concerning the body and things concerning the soul; then you will not be surprised that far more, indeed all things that come into being, exist at the same time in that single whole which we call the Cosmos.

If someone put to you the question of how the name Antoninus is spelled, would you strain your voice as you uttered each letter? And if they grew angry, would you grow angry too? Would you not calmly go on and count out every letter? Just so in this life, remember that every duty is made up of certain parts. It is your duty to observe these, and without being disturbed or showing anger toward those who are angry with you, to go on your way and finish what is set before you.

How cruel it is not to let people pursue the things that seem to them suited to their nature and to their advantage! And yet, in a way, you do not allow them this when you are vexed because they do wrong. For they are surely drawn toward such things because they suppose them suited to their nature and to their advantage. But it is not so. Then teach them, and show them, without being angry.

Death is a cessation of impressions through the senses, of the tugging of the strings that move our appetites, of the ranging movements of our thoughts, and of our service to the flesh.

It is a disgrace for your soul to give out first in this life, while your body still holds out.

Take care that you are not turned into a Caesar, that you are not stained with that dye; for such things happen. Keep yourself simple, good, pure, serious, free of pretense, a friend of justice, a worshiper of the gods, kind, affectionate, and vigorous in all proper acts. Strive to remain such as philosophy wished to make you. Reverence the gods and help your fellow human beings. Life is short. The one fruit of this earthly life is a devout disposition and acts done for others. Do everything as a disciple of Antoninus. Remember his steadiness in every act done in keeping with reason, his evenness in all things, his piety, the serenity of his face, his sweetness, his disregard for empty fame, and his efforts to understand things; how he would never let anything pass without first examining it most carefully and understanding it clearly; how he bore with those who blamed him unjustly without blaming them back; how he did nothing in a hurry; how he refused to listen to slanders; how careful an examiner he was of characters and actions; how he was not given to reproaching people, nor timid, nor suspicious, nor a hairsplitter; how little contented him in lodging, bed, dress, food, and servants; how hardworking and patient he was; how, thanks to his sparing diet, he could hold out till evening, not even needing to relieve himself except at the usual hour; his firmness and constancy in his friendships; how he tolerated frank opposition to his opinions; the pleasure he took when anyone showed him something better; and how religious he was, without superstition. Imitate all this, so that when your last hour comes you may have as good a conscience as he had.

Return to your sober senses and call yourself back; and when you have roused yourself from sleep and realized that it was only dreams that troubled you, then, awake again, look at the things around you as you looked at those dreams.

I am made up of a little body and a soul. To this little body all things are indifferent, for it cannot perceive differences. But to the understanding, only those things are indifferent that are not the work of its own activity; whatever is the work of its own activity is all within its power. And even of these, only those done with reference to the present, for as to the mind's future and past activities, even these are for the present indifferent.

Neither the labor done by the hand nor that done by the foot is contrary to nature, so long as the foot does the foot's work and the hand the hand's. So too a man's labor is not contrary to nature, as long as it does the things proper to a man. And if the labor is not contrary to his nature, neither is it an evil to him.

How many pleasures have been enjoyed by robbers, murderers of their own fathers, and tyrants.

Do you not see how craftsmen accommodate themselves up to a point to those unskilled in their trade, and yet hold fast to the principles of their art and will not bear to depart from them? Is it not strange, then, that the architect and the physician should have more respect for the principles of their own arts than a man has for his own reason, which he shares with the gods?

Asia and Europe are corners of the universe; the whole sea is a drop in the universe; Mount Athos a little clod of it; all present time a single point in eternity. All things are small, changeable, and perishable. All things come from that one universal ruling power, either directly or as a consequence. And so the lion's gaping jaws, and poison, and every harmful thing, like a thorn or like mud, are by-products of what is grand and beautiful. Do not imagine, then, that they are of a different kind from what you revere, but form a just opinion of the source of all things.

Whoever has seen present things has seen everything, both all that has happened from eternity and all that will be through endless time; for all things are of one kind and one form.

Frequently consider how all things in the universe are connected and related to one another. For in a way all things are interwoven, and so all are friendly to one another; for one thing follows another in order, and this is by virtue of their active movement, their working together, and the unity of the substance.

Adapt yourself to the things your lot has cast you among; and the people among whom your portion has fallen, love them, but truly and sincerely.

Every instrument, tool, or vessel does well if it does what it was made for, and yet the one who made it is not present in it. But in things held together by nature, the power that made them is within and abides in them; and for this reason it is all the more fitting to reverence that power, and to consider that if you live and act according to its will, everything in you is in keeping with intelligence. And so too in the universe, the things that belong to it are in keeping with intelligence.

Whatever of the things not in your power you suppose to be good or evil for you, then necessarily, if such an evil befalls you or you lose such a good, you will blame the gods and hate other people too, those who caused the misfortune or the loss, or those suspected of being likely to cause it; and indeed we do much wrong because we care so much how these things turn out. But if we judge only what is in our power to be good or bad, no reason remains either for finding fault with God or for taking a hostile stance toward any human being.

We are all working together toward one end, some knowingly and by design, others without knowing what they do, just as people do even while asleep, of whom it is Heraclitus, I think, who says that they too are workers and fellow contributors to what happens in the universe. But people cooperate in different ways: even those cooperate abundantly who find fault with what happens and try to oppose and hinder it, for the universe had need even of such men. It remains, then, for you to understand which kind of worker you place yourself among; for the one who governs all things will certainly make right use of you, and will receive you among some group of cooperators whose labors lead to one end. Only do not be such a part as the mean and ridiculous line in the play that Chrysippus speaks of.

Does the Sun take on the work of the Rain-Giver, or Asclepius the work of the Fruit-Bearer? And what of each of the stars: are they not different, and yet do they not work together toward the same end?

If the gods have made decisions about me and about the things that must happen to me, they have decided well, for it is not easy even to imagine a deity without foresight; and as for harming me, why would they have any wish to do so? What advantage would come to them from this, or to the whole, which is the special object of their providence? But if they have made no decision about me individually, they have certainly decided about the whole at least, and the things that happen as a consequence of this general arrangement I ought to accept gladly and be content with. But if they decide about nothing, which it is wicked to believe, and if we do believe it, then let us neither sacrifice, nor pray, nor swear by them, nor do anything else we do as though the gods were present and lived among us; but even if the gods decide about none of the things that concern us, I am able to decide about myself, and I can inquire into what is useful; and what is useful to each person is what is in keeping with his own constitution and nature. Now my nature is rational and social; and my city and country, insofar as I am Antoninus, is Rome, but insofar as I am a man, it is the world. The things that are useful to these cities, then, are the only things useful to me.

Whatever happens to anyone is for the good of the whole: this alone might be enough. But further you will observe this as a general truth, if you observe carefully, that whatever is profitable to one person is profitable to others too. But let the word profitable be taken here in the ordinary sense, as said of things of the middle kind, neither good nor bad.

Just as in the amphitheater and such places the constant sight of the same things and their sameness make the show tiresome, so it is with the whole of life; for all things, above and below, are the same and come from the same source. How long, then?

Think continually that all kinds of people, all kinds of pursuits, all sorts of nations are dead, so that your thoughts go right down even to Philistion, Phoebus, and Origanion. Now turn your thoughts to the other kinds of men. To that place we too must move, where there are so many great orators, so many noble philosophers, Heraclitus, Pythagoras, Socrates; so many heroes of former days, and so many generals after them, and tyrants; and besides these, Eudoxus, Hipparchus, Archimedes, and other men of sharp natural talent, great minds, lovers of work, versatile, bold, even mockers of the perishable and fleeting life of man, like Menippus and those like him. Consider that all of these have long been in the dust. What harm is this to them, then; and what to those whose names are utterly unknown? One thing here is worth a great deal: to pass your life in truth and justice, with goodwill even toward liars and unjust men.

When you wish to give yourself joy, think of the virtues of those who live with you, for instance the energy of one, the modesty of another, the generosity of a third, and some other good quality of a fourth. For nothing gives such joy as the examples of the virtues, when they are displayed in the characters of those who live with us and present themselves to us as fully as possible. So we should keep them before our eyes.

You are not dissatisfied, I suppose, because you weigh only so many pounds and not three hundred. Then do not be dissatisfied that you must live only so many years and not more; for just as you are content with the amount of matter assigned to you, so be content with the amount of time.

Let us try to persuade people. But act even against their will when the principles of justice point that way. If, however, anyone uses force to stand in your way, turn to contentment and tranquillity, and at the same time use the obstacle to exercise some other virtue; and remember that your attempt was made with a reservation, that you did not desire to do the impossible. What, then, did you desire? Just such an effort as this. And in that you succeed, even if the things you were aiming at are not accomplished.

The one who loves fame thinks another man's activity is his own good; the one who loves pleasure thinks his own sensations are; but the one who has understanding thinks his own acts are his own good.

It is in our power to hold no opinion about a thing and not to be disturbed in our soul; for things in themselves have no natural power to shape our judgments.

Train yourself to attend carefully to what another person says, and as far as possible to enter into the speaker's mind.

What is not good for the swarm is not good for the bee either.

If sailors abused the helmsman, or the sick abused the doctor, would they then listen to anyone else? Or how could the helmsman secure the safety of those in the ship, or the doctor the health of those he attends?

How many of those with whom I came into the world have already gone out of it.

To the jaundiced, honey tastes bitter; to those bitten by mad dogs, water brings terror; and to little children, a ball seems a fine thing. Why, then, am I angry? Do you think a false opinion has less power than the bile in the jaundiced man or the poison in the one bitten by a mad dog?

No one will keep you from living according to the reason of your own nature; nothing will happen to you contrary to the reason of universal nature.

What sort of people are those whom men wish to please, and for what ends, and by what kind of acts? How soon will time cover all things, and how many it has covered already.
