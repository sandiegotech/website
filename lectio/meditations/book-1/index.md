# Meditations, Book I

What I Learned, and From Whom

From my grandfather Verus, I learned to be good-natured and to keep my temper under control.

From everything I heard and remembered about my father, I learned modesty and a strong, manly character.

From my mother, I learned reverence and generosity; to hold back not only from doing wrong but even from thinking it; and to live simply, far from the habits of the rich.

From my great-grandfather, I learned the value of not relying on public schools but having good teachers at home—and that this is something worth spending freely on.

From my tutor, I learned not to back the Greens or the Blues at the races, or the small-shield or the big-shield fighters in the arena; to endure hard work, to want little, to do things with my own hands, to mind my own business, and to refuse to listen to gossip.

From Diognetus, I learned not to waste myself on trivial things; not to believe the claims of magicians and quacks about spells and exorcisms; and not to keep quails for fighting, or get excited about such things. He taught me to put up with plain speaking and to take philosophy seriously; he led me to hear the philosophers Bacchius, Tandasis, and Marcianus, to write dialogues as a boy, and to choose the simple camp-bed and animal skins of the Greek discipline.

From Rusticus, I came to see that my character needed work and discipline. He kept me from chasing clever showmanship, from writing on abstract theories, from delivering little sermons, and from showing off as a model of self-discipline or charity. He taught me to give up ornate rhetoric, poetry, and fine writing; not to walk around the house in formal dress, or do anything of that kind; to write plainly, like the simple letter he sent my mother from Sinuessa; to make peace readily with anyone who had wronged me, the moment they were willing; to read closely rather than settle for a superficial grasp; and not to nod along with people who only talk a lot. It was Rusticus who introduced me to Epictetus' teachings, lending me his own copy.

From Apollonius, I learned independence of mind and steady purpose—relying on reason and nothing else, even for a moment—and to stay the same person in sharp pain, in the loss of a child, and in long illness. In him I saw a living example of a man who could be both utterly firm and gentle, never irritable in teaching, who treated his real gift—his skill in explaining philosophy—as the least of his merits. From him I also learned how to accept favors from friends without feeling diminished or letting them pass unacknowledged.

From Sextus, I learned kindness, and the example of a household run the way a good father runs his family; the idea of living in agreement with nature; dignity without pretense; and genuine care for my friends. He bore patiently with ignorant people and with those who hold opinions without thinking them through. He could get along with anyone—his company was more pleasant than any flattery, and yet people held him in the deepest respect. He had a gift for working out and arranging, clearly and methodically, the principles a life needs. He never showed anger or any other passion; he was at once completely calm and deeply affectionate. He gave praise quietly and carried great learning without ever parading it.

From Alexander the grammarian, I learned not to pounce on mistakes—not to embarrass someone for a slip in grammar or pronunciation, but to slip in the right expression myself, by way of an answer, or of agreeing with the point, or of discussing the matter itself rather than the wording, or by some other tactful reminder.

From Fronto, I learned how much envy, cunning, and hypocrisy come with absolute power, and that the people we call aristocrats tend to be somewhat lacking in natural affection.

From Alexander the Platonist, I learned not to say or write to people, often or without real need, that I am "too busy," and not to keep dodging my obligations to those around me by always pleading urgent business.

From Catulus, I learned not to brush off a friend's complaint, even an unfair one, but to try to set things right; to speak warmly of my teachers, as people say Domitius and Athenodotus did; and to love my children truly.

From my brother Severus, I learned to love my family, the truth, and justice. Through him I came to know Thrasea, Helvidius, Cato, Dion, and Brutus; and from him I got the idea of a society governed by one law for all, administered with equal rights and freedom of speech, and of a kingship that values above all the liberty of those it governs. He taught me a steady, undistracted devotion to philosophy; a readiness to do good and to give generously; optimism; and trust that my friends loved me. He never hid what he thought of those he disapproved of, so his friends never had to guess his wishes—it was simply plain.

From Maximus, I learned self-mastery and not to be thrown off course by anything; good cheer in every circumstance, including illness; a character that mixed sweetness with dignity; and doing whatever lay before me without grumbling. Everyone trusted that he meant what he said and acted with good intentions. He was never astonished, never in a hurry, never put things off; he was never at a loss, never downcast, never forced a laugh to cover irritation, and never flew into anger or suspicion. He was generous, forgiving, and honest, and gave the impression of a man who had always been upright rather than one who had had to be corrected. No one could ever feel looked down on by him, or imagine himself the better man—and he had a gift for being agreeably funny.

In my adoptive father, Antoninus, I saw gentleness and an unshakable commitment to decisions made after careful thought. He was indifferent to the so-called honors people crave; he loved hard work and persistence, and was open to anyone with a proposal for the common good. He gave every man exactly what he deserved, and he knew from experience when to press hard and when to ease off. He put a stop to love affairs with boys. He thought of himself as no more than an ordinary citizen, and he freed his friends from any obligation to dine with him or attend him on his travels; those kept away by other duties always found him unchanged. In council he examined matters carefully and patiently, never breaking off his inquiry early because first impressions seemed good enough. He kept his friends without tiring of them and without being extravagantly attached. He was content in every situation, cheerful, and farsighted, attending to the smallest details without fuss. He put a stop to flattery and applause aimed at himself. He watched constantly over what the empire needed, managed its spending well, and patiently bore the criticism this drew. He was not superstitious toward the gods, and he never courted the people with favors, flattery, or playing to the crowd; he was sober and steady in everything, never vulgar, never chasing novelty. The comforts that fortune supplied in abundance he used without arrogance and without apology—enjoying them naturally when they were there, and not missing them when they were gone. No one could call him a sophist, a glib servant, or a pedant; everyone recognized him as a mature, complete man, beyond flattery, able to govern himself and others. He honored genuine philosophers and did not reproach the false ones, though he was never taken in by them. He was easy in conversation and charming without being tiresome. He cared for his health sensibly—not clinging to life or fussing over his looks, yet not neglecting himself—so that he rarely needed a doctor. He was quick to make way for those with real talent, in eloquence, law, ethics, or anything else, and helped each win the recognition he deserved. He followed his country's traditions without making a show of it. He disliked change and restlessness, preferring the same places and pursuits, and after his bouts of headache he came back fresh and vigorous to his work. He kept few secrets, and only about public affairs. He was prudent and economical in staging public shows, putting up buildings, and making gifts to the people—a man who looked to what ought to be done, not to the reputation it would earn. He did not bathe at odd hours, did not build for the sake of building, and was indifferent to his food, to the cut and color of his clothes, and to the good looks of his slaves. His clothes came up from his house at Lorium on the coast, and most of his things from Lanuvium. There was the way he treated the tax collector at Tusculum who begged his pardon; and that was his manner in everything. There was nothing harsh in him, nothing relentless or violent, nothing "driven to the sweating point"; he examined everything calmly, as if he had all the time in the world—orderly, steady, and consistent. What was said of Socrates fits him: he could go without the things most people cannot do without, and enjoy in moderation the things most people cannot enjoy without overdoing it. To be strong enough for both—to do without, and to enjoy with restraint—is the mark of a man whose soul is complete and unconquerable, exactly as he showed during Maximus' illness.

To the gods I owe good grandfathers, good parents, a good sister, good teachers, good companions, good relatives and friends—almost everything good. I owe it to them, too, that I never slipped into wronging any of them, though my nature was such that, given the chance, I might have; by their grace, the circumstances that would have tested me never came together. I am grateful that I was not brought up any longer than I was by my grandfather's mistress; that I kept my innocence and did not become a man before my time, but if anything waited longer than I needed to. I lived under a ruler and father who stripped away my pride and showed me that one can live in a palace without bodyguards, fine clothes, torches, statues, and such display, and can bring oneself nearly to the level of a private citizen without becoming, for that reason, any smaller in spirit or any slacker in the public duties a ruler must perform. The gods gave me a brother whose character spurred me to watch over myself, and whose respect and affection gladdened me; children who were not born dull or deformed. I did not get any further in rhetoric and poetry and the other studies that might well have absorbed me, had I felt I was making good progress in them. I was quick to give those who raised me the honors they seemed to want, instead of putting them off with promises because they were still young. I came to know Apollonius, Rusticus, and Maximus. I formed clear and frequent pictures of what it means to live according to nature, so that, as far as the gods and their gifts and help and inspiration were concerned, nothing kept me from living that way at once—though I still fall short through my own fault, by not heeding their reminders and almost explicit instructions. My body has held out this long in such a life. I never laid a hand on Benedicta or Theodotus, and when I later fell into infatuations, I recovered. Though I was often annoyed with Rusticus, I never went so far as to do something I would regret. My mother, though fated to die young, spent her last years with me. Whenever I wanted to help someone in need, I was never told there was no money for it—and I myself never had to ask such help from anyone. I have a wife so obedient, affectionate, and unaffected; and I had no shortage of good teachers for my children. Remedies were shown to me in dreams, including ones for spitting blood and for dizziness, and the one at Caieta, given as if by an oracle. And when I was drawn to philosophy, I did not fall into the hands of some sophist, or sit down to analyzing treatises or untangling logical puzzles, or busy myself with speculating about the heavens. For all of these, I needed the help of the gods and of fortune.
