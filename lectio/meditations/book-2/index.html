Marcus Aurelius · Lectio Edition · 2026

Meditations, Book II

Book II2 of 12

Begin each morning by telling yourself: today I will meet people who are meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, envious, and unsociable. They are like this because they cannot tell good from evil. But I have seen that the nature of the good is beautiful and the nature of the bad is ugly, and that the one who does wrong is related to me—not by blood or birth, but because we share the same mind and the same fragment of the divine. So none of them can harm me, since no one can entangle me in what is ugly, and I cannot be angry with a relative or hate him. We were made to work together, like the feet, the hands, the eyelids, the rows of upper and lower teeth. To work against one another, then, is against nature—and resentment and turning away are exactly that.

Whatever I am, I am just a little flesh and breath and the part that governs. Put your books aside; stop letting them distract you—you are not allowed that anymore. Instead, as though you were already dying, look down on the flesh: it is just blood and bones, a mesh of nerves, veins, and arteries. Look at the breath, too, and see what it is—air, never the same, breathed out and sucked back in every moment. The third thing is the governing part. Think of it this way: you are an old man now. Stop letting it be a slave. Stop letting it be jerked around like a puppet by unsociable impulses. Stop being dissatisfied with what the present holds, or flinching from what the future brings.

Everything that comes from the gods is full of providence. Even what comes from chance is not cut off from nature, but woven and tangled together with the things providence arranges. Everything flows from that source. And there is also necessity, and what serves the good of the whole universe, of which you are a part. Whatever the nature of the whole brings is good for each of its parts, and whatever keeps that nature going is good too. The universe is kept alive by the changes of the elements, and also by the changes of the things made from those elements. Let these principles be enough for you; hold them always as settled convictions. And throw away your thirst for books, so that you do not die complaining, but cheerfully, sincerely, and truly grateful to the gods from your heart.

Remember how long you have been putting these things off, and how many chances the gods have given you that you still do not use. It is time, at last, to grasp what universe you are part of, and that your existence flows from the one who administers that universe, and that a limit has been set on your time. If you do not use that time to clear the clouds from your mind, it will pass—and you will pass with it—and it will never come back.

Every moment, set yourself firmly, as a Roman and a man, to do the task in front of you with perfect and simple dignity, with affection, freedom, and justice, and to free yourself from every other thought. You will free yourself if you do each act of your life as if it were your last—setting aside all carelessness, all passionate resistance to what reason commands, all hypocrisy, all self-love, and all discontent with the lot you have been given. See how few things a man needs to master in order to live a smoothly flowing life, like the life of the gods; for the gods themselves will ask nothing more from anyone who keeps to these things.

You wrong yourself, you wrong yourself, my soul—and soon you will no longer have the chance to honor yourself. Each man's life is brief, and yours is nearly over, yet you still do not respect yourself; instead you stake your happiness on the souls of other people.

Do the things happening around you pull you in every direction? Then give yourself time to learn something new and good, and stop spinning. But beware of being swept off in the other direction too; for those who wear themselves out with constant activity are just as foolish if they have no goal to aim every action—and indeed every thought—toward.

A man is rarely seen to be unhappy because he failed to notice what was in someone else's mind; but those who fail to watch the movements of their own minds are bound to be unhappy.

Always keep this in mind: what the nature of the whole is, what my own nature is, how mine relates to the whole, what kind of part it is and of what kind of whole—and that no one can stop you from always acting and speaking in keeping with the nature you are part of.

When Theophrastus compares wrongdoings—the kind of comparison one would make according to ordinary human ideas—he says, like a true philosopher, that offenses committed out of desire are more blameworthy than those committed out of anger. For the man stirred by anger seems to turn away from reason with a kind of pain and a flinch he hardly notices; but the man who offends out of desire is overcome by pleasure and seems somehow more lacking in self-control and more unmanly in his wrongdoing. Rightly, then, and in a way worthy of philosophy, he said that the offense committed with pleasure deserves more blame than the one committed with pain. On the whole, the angry man is like someone who was first wronged and is driven by pain into anger, while the other moves on his own impulse to do wrong, carried into action by desire.

Since it is possible that you may leave this life at any moment, govern every action, word, and thought accordingly. As for departing from among the living, if there are gods, there is nothing to fear—they will not let you fall into evil. And if there are no gods, or if they take no interest in human affairs, then why would I want to live in a universe with no gods and no providence? But in truth they do exist, and they do care about human things, and they have placed everything within a man's power so that he need not fall into real evils. As for anything else, if it were truly evil, they would have arranged that too, so that it would be entirely in a man's power to avoid it. And anything that does not make a man worse—how could it make his life worse? It is not possible that the nature of the universe overlooked these things out of ignorance, nor out of knowing them while lacking the power to guard against or correct them. Nor is it possible that it made so great a mistake, through weakness or lack of skill, that good and evil should fall on the good and the bad alike. But death and life, honor and dishonor, pain and pleasure, wealth and poverty—all of these come equally to good men and bad, and they are neither noble nor shameful. So they are neither good nor evil.

How quickly everything vanishes—the bodies themselves into the universe, and the memory of them into time. Consider the nature of all the things we sense, especially those that lure us with the bait of pleasure, or terrify us with pain, or are trumpeted about by empty fame: how worthless, contemptible, shabby, perishable, and dead they are. It is the work of the mind to observe all this—to observe, too, who these people really are whose opinions and voices grant reputation, and what death is. If a man looks at death by itself, and uses reflection to strip away everything the imagination piles onto it, he will see it as nothing more than a process of nature; and to be afraid of a process of nature is childish. And death is not only a process of nature, but something that serves nature's purposes. Observe, too, how man draws near to the divine, by what part of himself, and when that part is rightly disposed.

Nothing is more pitiable than a man who circles around everything, prying into "the things beneath the earth," as the poet says, and trying to guess by conjecture what is in his neighbors' minds, never realizing that it is enough to attend to the divinity within himself and to revere it sincerely. To revere it means keeping it free from passion and thoughtlessness, and from discontent with what comes from gods and men. What comes from the gods deserves reverence for its excellence; what comes from men should be dear to us because they are our kin—and sometimes, in a way, they even move us to pity because they do not know good from bad, a defect no smaller than the one that robs a person of the power to tell white from black.

Even if you were going to live for three thousand years, or ten thousand times that long, still remember this: no one loses any life but the one he is living now, and no one lives any life but the one he is losing now. So the longest life and the shortest come to the same thing. The present moment is the same for everyone, even though what is passing away is not the same for each; and so what is lost turns out to be only a moment. For a man cannot lose either the past or the future—how can anyone take from him what he does not have? Keep two things in mind, then. First, that all things from eternity have the same forms and come round again in a circle, so it makes no difference whether a man sees the same things for a hundred years, or two hundred, or forever. Second, that the longest-lived man and the one who dies soonest lose exactly the same. For the present is the only thing a man can be deprived of, since it is the only thing he has—and a man cannot lose what he does not have.

Remember that everything is a matter of opinion. The saying of Monimus the Cynic makes this plain, and the use of it is plain too, if a man takes from it as much as is true.

The soul of a man does violence to itself, first of all, when it becomes, as far as it can, an abscess or a kind of tumor on the universe. For to be vexed at anything that happens is to cut yourself off from nature, which contains the natures of all other things in its various parts. Second, the soul does violence to itself when it turns away from another person, or moves toward him meaning to do harm, as the souls of angry people do. Third, when it is overpowered by pleasure or pain. Fourth, when it puts on an act, doing or saying anything insincerely or untruthfully. Fifth, when it lets any of its own acts or impulses have no aim, doing things thoughtlessly without considering what they are—when even the smallest things ought to be done with an end in view; and the end of rational beings is to follow the reason and the law of the most ancient city and commonwealth.

In human life, time is a mere point, the substance is in constant flux, perception is dim, the whole body is matter bound to rot, the soul is a whirl, fortune is hard to predict, and fame is mindless. In a word, everything of the body is a flowing stream, everything of the soul is dream and vapor, life is a war and a stranger's brief stay, and lasting fame is oblivion. What, then, can guide a man? One thing, and one thing only: philosophy. And this means keeping the divinity within him free from violence and unharmed, above pleasures and pains, doing nothing without a purpose and nothing falsely or with pretense, not depending on whether another man does or does not do something; and besides this, accepting everything that happens and everything allotted to him as coming from wherever he himself came; and, finally, awaiting death with a cheerful mind, since it is nothing but the breaking up of the elements that make up every living being. But if it does no harm to the elements themselves to be continually changing one into another, why should a man dread the change and dissolution of them all? It is in keeping with nature, and nothing that is in keeping with nature is evil.
