Meditations, Book XII

Book XII12 of 12

Everything you hope to reach by some roundabout path, you can have right now, if you stop denying it to yourself. And this means: take no notice of all the past, trust the future to providence, and direct only the present in line with piety and justice. In line with piety, so that you are content with the lot assigned to you, for nature designed it for you and you for it. In line with justice, so that you always speak the truth freely and without disguise, and do what accords with the law and with the worth of each person. Don't let yourself be held back by another's wickedness, nor by opinion, nor by anyone's words, nor even by the sensations of the poor flesh that has grown around you, for that passive part can look after itself. If then, whenever the time comes that you are near your departure, you set everything else aside and respect only your ruling faculty and the divinity within you, and if you are afraid not because you must someday stop living, but rather that you may never have begun to live according to nature, then you will be a person worthy of the universe that produced you. You will no longer be a stranger in your own homeland, no longer astonished at things that happen daily as if they were unexpected, and no longer dependent on this or that.

God sees the minds of all people stripped bare of their material clothing, their husk, their impurities. For with his intellectual part alone he touches only the intelligence that has flowed and been derived from himself into these bodies. And if you train yourself to do the same, you will rid yourself of much trouble. For someone who pays no attention to the poor flesh that wraps him will surely not bother himself chasing after clothing, housing, fame, and other such externals and show.

There are three things you are made of: a little body, a little breath, and intelligence. The first two are yours only in the sense that it is your duty to take care of them, but the third alone is truly yours. So separate from yourself, that is, from your understanding, whatever others do or say, and whatever you yourself have done or said, and whatever future things trouble you because they might happen, and whatever in the body that wraps you or in the breath naturally joined to it is attached to you independent of your will, and whatever the external swirling current whirls around you, so that your intellectual power, exempt from the things of fate, can live pure and free by itself, doing what is just, accepting what happens, and speaking the truth. Separate from this ruling faculty the things that cling to it through the impressions of the senses, and the things of time to come and time past, and make yourself like Empedocles' sphere, "all round, and resting in its own joyous repose." If you do this, and strive to live only what is really your life, that is, the present, then you will be able to spend whatever portion of life remains to you, right up to your death, free from disturbance, kindly, and at peace with the divinity within you.

I have often wondered how it is that every person loves himself more than everyone else, yet sets less value on his own opinion of himself than on the opinions of others. If a god or a wise teacher were to appear and order a man to think nothing and plan nothing that he would not say aloud the moment he conceived it, he could not bear it even for a single day. So much more do we respect what our neighbors will think of us than what we think of ourselves.

How can it be that the gods, having arranged everything well and kindly for humankind, overlooked this one thing: that some people, and very good ones, people who have had the closest communion with the divine and through pious acts and religious observance have been most intimate with it, once they have died should never exist again but be completely snuffed out? But if this is so, rest assured that if it ought to have been otherwise, the gods would have made it so. For if it were just, it would also be possible; and if it were according to nature, nature would have arranged it that way. But because it is not so, if in fact it is not so, then be convinced that it ought not to have been so. For you can see for yourself that in raising this question you are arguing with the Deity, and we would not argue with the gods this way unless they were utterly excellent and just; and if they are, they would not have allowed anything in the ordering of the universe to be unjustly or irrationally neglected.

Practice yourself even in the things you despair of accomplishing. For even the left hand, useless at everything else for lack of practice, grips the bridle more firmly than the right, because it has been practiced at this.

Consider what condition a person should be in, in both body and soul, when death overtakes him; and consider the shortness of life, the boundless abyss of time past and to come, and the feebleness of all matter.

Contemplate the formative principles of things stripped of their coverings; the purposes behind actions; consider what pain is, what pleasure is, and death, and fame; consider that each person is the cause of his own unease, that no one is hindered by another, and that everything is a matter of opinion.

In applying your principles, be like the bare-handed fighter, not the gladiator; for the gladiator puts down the sword he uses and is killed, but the fighter always has his hand and needs only to make a fist.

See things as they are in themselves, dividing them into matter, form, and purpose.

What power a person has to do nothing except what God will approve, and to accept everything God may give him.

As for what happens according to nature, we should blame neither the gods, since they do nothing wrong either willingly or unwillingly, nor other people, since they do nothing wrong except unwillingly. So we should blame no one.

How ridiculous, and what a stranger to life, is the person who is surprised at anything that happens in it.

Either there is a fated necessity and an unbreakable order, or a kind providence, or a confusion without purpose and without anyone directing it. If there is an unbreakable necessity, why do you resist? But if there is a providence that allows itself to be won over, make yourself worthy of the divinity's help. And if there is a confusion with no one governing it, be content that in such a storm you have within yourself a certain ruling intelligence. And even if the storm sweeps you away, let it sweep away the poor flesh, the poor breath, and everything else; for the intelligence, at least, it will not sweep away.

Does the light of the lamp shine without losing its brightness until it is put out? And shall the truth, the justice, and the self-control within you be put out before your death?

When someone appears to have done wrong, ask: how do I really know this was a wrongful act? And even if he did wrong, how do I know he has not already condemned himself, so that this is like clawing his own face? Consider that wanting a bad man not to do wrong is like wanting the fig tree not to yield juice in its figs, or babies not to cry, or the horse not to neigh, or anything else that must happen of necessity. For what else can a man do who has such a character? If this irritates you, then cure his disposition.

If it is not right, do not do it; if it is not true, do not say it.

In everything, always observe what the thing is that produces an impression on you, and break it down by dividing it into its form, its matter, its purpose, and the span of time within which it must end.

Realize at last that you have within you something better and more divine than the things that stir your passions and pull you about like a puppet on strings. What is in my mind right now? Is it fear, suspicion, desire, or something of the kind?

First, do nothing aimlessly or without a purpose. Second, let your actions aim at nothing but the good of the community.

Consider that before long you will be no one and nowhere, and none of the things you now see will exist, nor any of the people now living. For all things are made by nature to change, to be transformed, and to perish, so that other things may come into being in continuous succession.

Consider that everything is opinion, and opinion is in your power. So take away your opinion whenever you choose, and like a sailor who has rounded the headland, you will find calm, everything steady, a waveless bay.

Any activity, whatever it is, when it ends at its proper time suffers no harm by ending; nor does the one who performed it suffer any harm for the reason that the act has ended. In the same way, the whole made up of all our acts, which is our life, if it ends at its proper time suffers no harm by ending; nor is the one who brings this series to a close at the proper time badly treated. The proper time and the limit are fixed by nature, sometimes by a person's own nature, as in old age, but always by the universal nature, by the changing of whose parts the whole universe stays ever young and perfect. And whatever is useful to the whole is always good and timely. So the end of life is no evil for anyone, since it is not shameful, being beyond our will and not opposed to the common good; rather it is good, since it is timely and useful and fitting to the whole. For in this way too a person is moved by the Deity, when he is moved as the Deity is moved, and moved in his mind toward the same end.

Keep these three principles ready. First, in what you do, do nothing aimlessly or otherwise than justice herself would act; and as for what may come to you from outside, consider that it happens either by chance or by providence, and you must neither blame chance nor accuse providence. Second, consider what each being is from its seed until it receives a soul, and from receiving a soul until it gives that soul back, and what each is composed of and into what it dissolves. Third, that if you were suddenly lifted high above the earth and looked down on human affairs and saw how varied they are, and at the same time saw at a glance the great number of beings who dwell all around in the air and the heavens, you would see, however often you were lifted up, the very same things: the same forms, the same brief duration. Are these things to be proud of?

Throw away opinion, and you are saved. Who, then, stops you from throwing it away?

When you are troubled about anything, you have forgotten this: that all things happen according to the universal nature. And you have forgotten that another person's wrongful act is nothing to you. And you have forgotten that everything that happens always happened this way, will happen this way, and is happening this way everywhere right now. And you have forgotten how close the kinship is between one person and the whole human race, for it is a community not of a little blood or seed but of intelligence. And you have forgotten that each person's intelligence is a god and an outflow of the Deity; and that nothing is truly a person's own, but that his child, his body, and his very soul came from the Deity; and that everything is opinion; and, lastly, that every person lives only the present moment, and loses only this.

Constantly call to mind those who have complained loudly about something, those who were most conspicuous for the greatest fame, misfortunes, feuds, or fortunes of any kind, and then ask: where are they all now? Smoke and ash and a tale, or not even a tale. Bring to mind too every example of this sort: how Fabius Catullinus lived on his country estate, Lucius Lupus in his gardens, Stertinius at Baiae, Tiberius at Capri, and Velius Rufus, and in short the eager pursuit of anything joined with pride, and how worthless everything is that people violently strain after, and how much more philosophical it is for a person, in the opportunities given to him, to show himself just, self-controlled, and obedient to the gods, and to do this in all simplicity; for the pride that prides itself on having no pride is the most unbearable pride of all.

To those who ask, Where have you seen the gods, or how do you grasp that they exist, that you worship them? I answer, first, that they can be seen even with the eyes; and second, that I have never seen even my own soul, and yet I honor it. So with the gods too: from what I constantly experience of their power, I grasp that they exist, and I revere them.

The safety of life lies in this: to examine everything thoroughly, what it is in itself, what its matter is, what its form; and with all your soul to do justice and to speak the truth. What is left, except to enjoy life by joining one good thing to another so closely as to leave not even the smallest gap between them?

There is one light of the sun, though it is broken up by walls, mountains, and countless other things. There is one common substance, though it is distributed among countless bodies, each with its own qualities. There is one soul, though it is distributed among countless natures and individual forms. There is one intelligent soul, though it seems to be divided. Now of the things just mentioned, the other parts, such as air and matter, are without sensation and have no fellowship with one another, and yet even these the intelligent principle holds together, along with their tendency toward the same center. But intellect in a special way tends toward what is akin to it, joins with it, and its feeling for community is not broken off.

What do you wish for? To go on existing? Well then, do you wish to have sensation, movement, growth, and then to stop growing, to use your speech, to think? Which of all these seems to you worth desiring? But if it is easy to set little value on all of them, then turn to what remains, which is to follow reason and God. Yet it is inconsistent with honoring reason and God to be troubled that death will deprive you of those other things.

How small a part of boundless, unfathomable time is allotted to each of us, for it is soon swallowed up in eternity. And how small a part of the whole of substance, and how small a part of the universal soul; and on what a tiny clod of the whole earth you creep. Reflecting on all this, consider nothing great except to act as your nature leads you and to endure what the common nature brings.

How does your ruling faculty make use of itself? Everything depends on this. Everything else, whether it lies within your will or not, is only lifeless ashes and smoke.

This reflection is best suited to move us to scorn death: that even those who think pleasure a good and pain an evil have still despised it.

For the person to whom only what comes in due season is good, to whom it makes no difference whether he has done more acts or fewer in accord with right reason, to whom it makes no difference whether he contemplates the world for a longer or a shorter time, for this person death too is not a terrible thing.

Man, you have been a citizen in this great city; what does it matter to you whether for five years or fifty? For what accords with its laws is just for all alike. Where is the hardship, then, if you are sent out of the city not by a tyrant or an unjust judge, but by nature, who brought you into it, just as a director who hired an actor dismisses him from the stage? "But I have not finished the five acts, only three." You are right, but in life three acts are the whole play; for the one who once caused its composition and now causes its dissolution decides what counts as a complete drama, and you are the cause of neither. So depart contented, for the one who releases you is also contented.
