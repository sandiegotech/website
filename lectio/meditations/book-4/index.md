# Meditations, Book IV

The part of us that rules from within, when it is in accord with nature, takes whatever happens so readily that it always adapts itself to whatever is possible and is set before it. It needs no particular material to work with; it moves toward its purpose, though under certain conditions, and it turns whatever opposes it into material for itself, the way a fire seizes what falls into it. A small flame would be smothered, but a strong fire quickly takes hold of whatever is piled onto it, consumes it, and rises higher because of that very fuel.

Let nothing be done without a purpose, and nothing except according to the perfect principles of the craft.

People look for places to get away to: country houses, beaches, mountains. And you too are in the habit of longing for such places. But this is the mark of the most ordinary kind of person, since it is in your power, whenever you choose, to retreat into yourself. Nowhere can you find a more peaceful or untroubled retreat than in your own soul, especially when you have within you the kind of thoughts that, by simply looking into them, put you at once into complete tranquillity. And by tranquillity I mean nothing other than a well-ordered mind. So give yourself this retreat constantly, and renew yourself. Keep your principles brief and basic, so that the moment you turn to them they are enough to wash the soul clean and send you back free of all discontent with the things you are returning to. For what is it you are discontented with? With people's wickedness? Then call to mind the conclusion that rational beings exist for one another, that patience is a part of justice, and that people do wrong without meaning to. Consider how many, after feuding, suspecting, hating, and spearing one another, have been laid out dead and burned to ashes; and be at peace at last. Or perhaps you are dissatisfied with the portion the universe has assigned you. Then recall the alternative: either there is providence, or there are atoms; or remember the arguments that have proved the world to be a kind of political community. Or perhaps bodily things still take hold of you. Then consider further that the mind does not mingle with the breath, whether it moves gently or violently, once it has drawn itself apart and discovered its own power; and remember all you have heard and agreed to about pain and pleasure. Or perhaps the desire for what is called fame will torment you. Then see how quickly everything is forgotten; look at the chaos of infinite time on either side of the present, at the emptiness of applause, at the fickleness and poor judgment of those who pretend to give praise, and at the narrowness of the space within which praise is confined. For the whole earth is a mere point, and how tiny a corner of it is this home of yours, and how few people are in it, and what kind of people are those who will praise you.

This, then, is what remains: remember to retreat into this little domain of your own, and above all do not strain or distract yourself, but stay free, and look at things as a man, as a human being, as a citizen, as a mortal. Among the things readiest at hand, to which you should turn, let there be these two. The first is that things do not touch the soul; they stand outside it, unmoving, and our disturbances come only from the opinion within us. The second is that all the things you see are changing this very moment and will soon be no more; keep constantly in mind how many such changes you have already witnessed. The universe is transformation; life is opinion.

If the thinking part of us is shared, then so is the reason that makes us rational beings. If that is so, then the reason which commands us what to do and what not to do is also shared. If that is so, there is a common law as well. If that is so, we are fellow citizens. If that is so, we are members of some political community. If that is so, the world is in a way a state. For what other common community could anyone claim that the whole human race belongs to? And it is from this common community that our very power of thought, our reasoning, and our capacity for law come; or where else would they come from? Just as my earthly part is a portion given to me from a certain earth, my watery part from another element, my airy part from some source, and my hot, fiery part from some particular source (for nothing comes out of nothing, just as nothing returns to non-existence), so too the thinking part comes from some source.

Death is like birth, a mystery of nature: a combining of the same elements and a dissolving back into the same. It is nothing anyone should be ashamed of, for it is not contrary to the nature of a reasoning creature, nor contrary to the design of our constitution.

It is natural that such things should be done by such people; it is inevitable. If a man refuses to allow it, he might as well refuse to let the fig tree have its juice. By all means keep this in mind: within a very short time both you and he will be dead, and soon not even your names will remain.

Take away your opinion, and you take away the complaint, "I have been harmed." Take away the complaint, "I have been harmed," and the harm itself is gone.

Whatever does not make a man worse than he was also does not make his life worse, and does not harm him, either from outside or from within.

The nature of what is universally useful has been compelled to act this way.

Consider that everything which happens, happens justly, and if you observe carefully you will find this to be so. I do not mean merely with respect to the orderly sequence of events, but with respect to justice, as though done by someone who assigns to each thing its due. So keep observing as you have begun, and whatever you do, do it together with this aim: being good, in the sense in which a man is properly understood to be good. Hold to this in every action.

Do not take the same view of things that the person who wrongs you holds, or the view he wishes you to hold; but look at them as they truly are.

A man should always keep these two rules ready at hand. The first is to do only what the reason of the ruling and law-giving faculty suggests for the benefit of people. The second is to change your opinion if someone is at hand to set you right and move you from a mistaken view. But this change of mind must come only from a genuine conviction, of what is just or for the common good or the like, not because it seems pleasant or brings you a good reputation.

Do you have reason? I do. Then why not use it? For if reason does its own work, what more could you want?

You have existed as a part of the whole. You will disappear back into what produced you; or rather, you will be taken back, through transformation, into its generative principle.

Many grains of incense fall on the same altar; one falls sooner, another later, but it makes no difference.

Within ten days you will seem a god to the very people who now regard you as a beast and an ape, if you will return to your principles and to the worship of reason.

Do not act as though you were going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over you. While you are alive, while it is still in your power, be good.

How much trouble a man avoids who does not look to see what his neighbor says or does or thinks, but only to what he himself does, that it may be just and holy, as befits a good man. Do not glance around at the dark characters of others, but run straight along the line without swerving from it.

The man who has a fierce desire for fame after death does not consider that every one of those who remember him will himself soon die, and then those who succeed them, until the whole memory of him is snuffed out as it passes through people who admire foolishly and then perish. But suppose that those who remember you were even immortal, and the memory itself immortal: what is that to you? And I do not even ask what it is to the dead, but what it is to the living. What is praise, except so far as it has some practical use? For now you are wrongly neglecting nature's gift, which does not hang on what anyone else says.

Everything that is in any way beautiful is beautiful in itself and complete in itself; praise is no part of it. Being praised makes a thing neither worse nor better. I say this even of the things commonly called beautiful, such as material objects and works of art. What is truly beautiful needs nothing beyond itself, no more than law does, or truth, or kindness, or modesty. Which of these is made beautiful by being praised, or spoiled by being blamed? Is an emerald made worse than it was if no one praises it? Or gold, ivory, purple, a lyre, a little knife, a flower, a shrub?

If souls continue to exist, how does the air contain them from all eternity? But then how does the earth contain the bodies of those buried so long ago? For just as here the change of these bodies, after lasting however long, and their dissolution, make room for other dead bodies, so the souls that pass into the air, after lasting some time, are transformed and dispersed, and take on a fiery nature as they are received back into the generative intelligence of the universe, and in this way make room for fresh souls coming to dwell there. This is the answer a man might give on the assumption that souls continue to exist. But we must think not only of the number of bodies that are buried, but also of the number of animals eaten each day by us and by other animals. What a multitude is consumed, and in a way buried in the bodies of those who feed on them! And yet the earth still receives them, through their changes into blood and their transformations into the airy or the fiery element.

What is the way to investigate the truth here? To divide a thing into what is material and what is the cause of its form.

Do not be spun about, but in every impulse have regard for justice, and at every impression preserve the power of clear understanding.

Everything that is in harmony with you, O Universe, is in harmony with me. Nothing is too early or too late for me that is in due time for you. Everything is fruit to me that your seasons bring, O Nature. From you are all things, in you are all things, to you all things return. The poet says, "Dear city of Cecrops"; will you not say, "Dear city of Zeus"?

Keep yourself occupied with few things, the philosopher says, if you would be tranquil. But consider whether it might not be better to say: do what is necessary, and whatever the reason of a creature naturally social requires, and as it requires. For this brings not only the tranquillity that comes from acting well, but also the tranquillity that comes from doing few things. Since the greater part of what we say and do is unnecessary, a man who strips that away will have more leisure and less disquiet. So on every occasion a man should ask himself: is this one of the unnecessary things? And he should strip away not only unnecessary actions but also unnecessary thoughts, so that superfluous actions will not follow from them.

Try out how the life of a good man suits you, the man who is satisfied with the portion assigned to him out of the whole, and satisfied with his own just acts and kindly disposition.

Have you seen those things? Now look at these as well. Do not disturb yourself. Make yourself entirely simple. Does someone do wrong? It is to himself that he does the wrong. Has something happened to you? Very well; out of the universe, from the beginning, everything that happens has been apportioned and spun out for you. In short, your life is brief. You must turn the present to good account with the help of reason and justice. Be sober even in your relaxation.

Either this is a well-ordered universe, or it is a chaos thrown together; but a universe even so. Can there be order within you, yet disorder in the whole? And this when all things are so distinct, so spread out, and yet bound together in sympathy?

A black character, an unmanly one, a stubborn one, brutish, childish, animal, stupid, fraudulent, vulgar, deceitful, tyrannical.

If a man is a stranger to the universe because he does not know what is in it, he is no less a stranger because he does not know what is going on in it. He is a runaway who flees from the social reason. He is blind who shuts the eyes of his understanding. He is poor who has need of another and does not have within himself all that is useful for life. He is an abscess on the universe who withdraws and cuts himself off from the reason of our common nature out of displeasure with what happens, for the same nature that produces these things also produced you. He is a fragment torn from the state who tears his own soul away from the soul of reasoning beings, which is one.

One philosopher is without a tunic, another without a book; here is yet another half naked. "I have no bread," he says, "and still I hold to reason." And I, who have the nourishment my studies give, do not hold to it.

Love the craft you have learned, humble as it may be, and be content with it; and live out the rest of your life as one who has entrusted all that he has to the gods with his whole soul, making yourself neither the tyrant nor the slave of any man.

Consider, for instance, the times of Vespasian. You will see all the same things: people marrying, raising children, falling sick, dying, making war, feasting, trading, farming, flattering, putting on airs, suspecting, scheming, wishing some people dead, grumbling about the present, in love, hoarding treasure, craving a consulship, craving royal power. Well, that life of theirs no longer exists at all. Move on to the times of Trajan. Again, everything is the same; their life too is gone. In the same way look at the other ages of time and whole nations, and see how many, after great efforts, soon fell and dissolved into the elements. Above all you should think of those you have known yourself who wore themselves out over trivial things and neglected to do what their own nature required, to hold firmly to that and be content with it. And here you must remember that the attention given to each thing has its proper value and proportion. For thus you will not be dissatisfied, if you give to lesser matters no more attention than is fitting.

The words that were once familiar are now obsolete; and so too the names of those famous in old times are now in a way obsolete: Camillus, Caeso, Volesus, Dentatus, and a little later Scipio and Cato, then Augustus, then Hadrian and Antoninus. For all things soon pass away and become a mere story, and complete oblivion soon buries them. And I say this of those who shone in some remarkable way. As for the rest, the moment they have breathed their last they are "unseen, unheard of." And after all, what is everlasting remembrance? Utterly empty. What, then, is it that we ought to spend our serious effort on? This one thing: just thoughts, actions that serve the common good, words that never lie, and a disposition that gladly welcomes everything that happens, as necessary, as familiar, as flowing from a source and principle of the same kind.

Willingly give yourself up to Clotho, and let her spin your thread into whatever she pleases.

Everything lasts only for a day, both the one who remembers and the thing remembered.

Observe constantly that all things come to pass through change, and train yourself to consider that the nature of the universe loves nothing so much as to change what exists and to make new things like them. For everything that exists is in a way the seed of what will come from it. But you think only of seeds cast into the earth or the womb; that is a very crude notion.

You will soon die, and you are still not simple, still not free of disturbances, still not without the suspicion that external things can harm you, still not kindly toward everyone, nor do you yet locate wisdom solely in acting justly.

Examine the ruling principles of people, even of the wise: what kinds of things they avoid, and what kinds they pursue.

What is evil for you does not reside in the ruling principle of another, nor in any change or alteration of your bodily covering. Where, then, is it? It is in that part of you which has the power to form opinions about evils. So let this power form no such opinions, and all is well. Even if what lies nearest to it, the poor body, is cut, burned, festering with pus and rot, still let the part that forms opinions about these things stay quiet; that is, let it judge that nothing is either bad or good which can happen equally to a bad man and a good one. For whatever happens equally to the man who lives contrary to nature and to the man who lives according to nature is neither according to nature nor contrary to it.

Constantly regard the universe as one living being, with one substance and one soul. Observe how all things refer back to its single awareness; how all things act with one impulse; how all things are co-operating causes of everything that exists; and observe too the continuous spinning of the thread and the weaving of the web.

You are a little soul carrying around a corpse, as Epictetus used to say.

It is no evil for things to undergo change, and no good for things to come into being as a result of change.

Time is like a river of all that happens, a violent current; for no sooner has a thing come into view than it is swept away, and another takes its place, and that too will be swept away.

Everything that happens is as familiar and well known as the rose in spring and the fruit in summer; for such are disease and death and slander and treachery and everything else that delights or vexes fools.

In the sequence of things, what comes after always fits aptly with what has gone before; for this sequence is not like a mere list of disconnected items joined only by necessity, but a rational connection. And just as all existing things are arranged together in harmony, so the things that come into being show no mere succession but a certain wonderful kinship.

Always remember the saying of Heraclitus, that the death of earth is to become water, the death of water to become air, the death of air to become fire, and so on in reverse. Think too of the man who forgets where the road leads, and how people quarrel with the very thing they are in most constant communion with, the reason that governs the universe; and how the things they meet with every day seem strange to them. Consider that we ought not to act and speak as though we were asleep, for even in sleep we seem to act and speak; and that we ought not to act and speak merely as we have been taught, like children repeating what they learned from their parents.

If some god told you that you would die tomorrow, or certainly the day after, you would not care much whether it was the third day or tomorrow, unless you were utterly small-minded, for how slight the difference is. So think it no great matter to die after as many years as you can name rather than tomorrow.

Think continually how many physicians are dead, after so often knitting their brows over the sick; how many astrologers, after predicting other people's deaths with great pretension; how many philosophers, after endless discourses on death and immortality; how many warriors, after killing thousands; how many tyrants, who wielded their power over men's lives with terrible arrogance, as though they were immortal. Think how many whole cities have died, so to speak, Helice, Pompeii, Herculaneum, and countless others. Add to the count all whom you yourself have known, one after another. One man, after burying another, is laid out dead, and another buries him; and all this in a short time. In a word, always observe how fleeting and worthless human things are: what was a little mucus yesterday will be a mummy or ashes tomorrow. So pass through this small span of time in agreement with nature, and end your journey content, as an olive falls when it is ripe, blessing the nature that produced it and giving thanks to the tree on which it grew.

Be like the headland on which the waves break again and again, while it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it.

"How unlucky I am, that this has happened to me." Not so; rather, "How lucky I am that, though this has happened to me, I remain free of pain, neither crushed by the present nor afraid of the future." For such a thing might have happened to anyone, but not everyone would have remained free of pain through it. So why call that a misfortune rather than calling this a good fortune? And do you in general call something a man's misfortune when it is not a departure from man's nature? And does a thing seem to you a departure from man's nature when it is not contrary to the will of that nature? Well, you know the will of nature. Will this thing that has happened keep you from being just, great-souled, temperate, prudent, free from rash opinions and falsehood? Will it keep you from being modest, free, and from having everything else by whose presence a man's nature obtains all that is its own? Remember, then, on every occasion that leads you to distress, to apply this principle: this is not a misfortune, but to bear it nobly is good fortune.

It is a common but still a useful aid toward despising death to review those who have clung tenaciously to life. What more have they gained than those who died early? At any rate they lie in their tombs somewhere at last, Cadicianus, Fabius, Julianus, Lepidus, or anyone else like them, men who carried out many others to be buried and were then carried out themselves. The interval between birth and death is small in any case; and consider with how much trouble, in what company, and in what a feeble body it is laboriously passed. So do not regard life as a thing of any great value. Look at the vast stretch of time behind you, and at the time before you, another boundless expanse. In this infinity, what difference is there between the man who lives three days and the man who lives three generations?

Always take the short road, and the short road is the natural one. So say and do everything in keeping with the soundest reason. For such a purpose frees a man from troubles, from struggle, and from all scheming and showy display.
