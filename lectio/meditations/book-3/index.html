Meditations, Book III

Book III3 of 12

We should keep in mind not only that our life is wearing away day by day, with less and less of it left, but also this: even if we live longer, there is no guarantee that the mind will stay sharp enough to understand things and keep the power of reflection that strives to grasp what is divine and what is human. For once a person slips into senility, the breathing and digestion and imagination and appetite and all the rest will keep working, but the power to make full use of ourselves, to fulfill our duty completely, to sort out every impression clearly, to judge whether the time has come to leave life, and everything else that requires a trained reason, all of that is already extinguished. So we must hurry, not only because we draw closer to death every day, but because the grasp and understanding of things gives out first.

We should also notice that even the byproducts of natural processes have a certain charm and attractiveness. When bread is baked, for instance, some parts crack open at the surface, and these cracks, though they happen contrary to the baker's intention, are beautiful in their own way and somehow whet the appetite. Likewise figs split open when fully ripe; and in ripe olives the very nearness to rotting lends the fruit a peculiar beauty. The drooping ears of grain, the lion's brow, the foam dripping from a wild boar's mouth, and many other things, though far from beautiful if you examine them on their own, still add to the charm of the natural things they accompany and please the mind. So anyone with real feeling and deeper insight into what is produced in the universe will find that scarcely one of these secondary effects fails to seem pleasingly arranged. He will look at the actual gaping jaws of wild beasts with no less pleasure than at those painters and sculptors imitate; he will see in an old woman and an old man a kind of ripeness and grace; and he will be able to look on the appealing loveliness of the young with chaste eyes. Many such things will present themselves, pleasing not to everyone, but only to the person who has become truly familiar with Nature and her works.

Hippocrates cured many diseases, then fell sick and died himself. The Chaldean astrologers foretold the deaths of many, and then fate caught up with them too. Alexander, Pompey, and Gaius Caesar, after wiping out whole cities again and again and cutting to pieces tens of thousands of cavalry and infantry in battle, themselves at last departed from life. Heraclitus, after all his theories about the universe going up in flames, filled up with water inside and died smeared all over with mud. Lice killed Democritus, and other vermin killed Socrates. What does all this mean? You boarded the ship, you made the voyage, you have reached shore: get off. If it is to another life, there is no lack of gods even there; and if it is to a state without sensation, you will no longer be held by pains and pleasures, no longer a slave to this vessel, the body, a master as far beneath its servant as the servant is above it. For the servant is intelligence and divinity; the master is earth and gore.

Do not waste what remains of your life in thoughts about other people, unless you are relating those thoughts to some common good. For you lose the chance to do something else when you let your mind run on things like this: what is so-and-so doing, and why, and what is he saying, and what is he thinking, and what is he scheming, and everything of that kind that pulls you away from watching your own ruling power. So in the run of your thoughts you should cut out everything aimless and useless, and above all the over-curious and the spiteful. Train yourself to think only of things such that, if someone suddenly asked you, "What are you thinking about right now?", you could answer at once and openly, "This" or "That", and it would be plain from your words that everything in you is straightforward and kind, the thoughts of a social creature, one with no concern for pleasure or sensual indulgence at all, no rivalry, no envy, no suspicion, nothing you would blush to admit was on your mind. A man like this, who no longer puts off being among the best, is a kind of priest and servant of the gods, and makes use as well of the divinity seated within him, which keeps a man uncontaminated by pleasure, unhurt by any pain, untouched by any insult, immune to wrong, a fighter in the noblest of fights, one who cannot be overpowered by any passion, steeped deep in justice, welcoming with his whole soul everything that happens and is assigned to him as his share. He rarely, and only out of great necessity and for the common good, lets himself imagine what someone else is saying or doing or thinking. He takes only what is his own as the material for his action; he keeps in mind what has been allotted to him out of the sum of things; he makes his own conduct fair; and he is convinced that his own portion is good. For the lot assigned to each person is carried along with him and carries him along with it. He also remembers that every rational being is his kin, that caring for all people is in keeping with human nature, and that he should hold on to the opinions not of everyone, but only of those who admittedly live according to nature. As for those who do not live so, he keeps in mind what sort of people they are at home and out of doors, by night and by day, and what sort of company they keep in their impure lives. So he sets no value at all on the praise of such men, since they are not even satisfied with themselves.

Do not work unwillingly, nor without regard for the common good, nor without careful thought, nor in a distracted way. Do not dress up your thoughts with studied ornament. Do not be a man of many words or one who busies himself with too many things. And further, let the divinity within you be the guardian of a being who is manly and mature, engaged in public affairs, a Roman, and a ruler, one who has taken up his post like a man waiting for the signal that calls him out of life, ready to go, needing no oath and no one else's testimony. Be cheerful, too, and do not look outside yourself for help or for the peace of mind that others give. A man should stand upright on his own, not be propped up by others.

If you find in human life anything better than justice, truth, self-control, and courage; in short, anything better than your own mind's contentment in the things it lets you do according to right reason, and in your contentment with the lot assigned to you without your own choosing; if, I say, you see anything better than this, then turn to it with all your soul and enjoy what you have found to be best. But if nothing appears better than the divinity planted within you, which has brought all your appetites under its control, which scrutinizes every impression, which has, as Socrates said, detached itself from the persuasions of the senses, submitted itself to the gods, and made the welfare of mankind its care; if you find everything else smaller and of less worth than this, then give way to nothing else. For once you swerve and lean toward something else, you will no longer be able, without distraction, to give first place to that good thing that is properly and uniquely your own. It is not right that anything of a different kind, such as the praise of the crowd, or power, or wealth, or the enjoyment of pleasure, should compete with what is good in the rational and social sense. All these things may seem to sit comfortably beside the good for a little while; then suddenly they take over and sweep us away. So you, I say, must simply and freely choose the better and hold to it. "But the useful is the better," you say. Well then, if it is useful to you as a rational being, keep to it; but if it is useful only to you as an animal, say so, and hold to your judgment without arrogance, only taking care to make the inquiry by a sound method.

Never value as good for yourself anything that would force you to break a promise, to lose your self-respect, to hate anyone, to grow suspicious, to curse, to play the hypocrite, or to crave anything that needs walls and curtains to hide it. The man who has put his own intelligence and inner divinity, and the honoring of its excellence, above everything else plays no tragic role, does not groan, and will need neither solitude nor a crowd. Best of all, he will live without either chasing death or running from it. Whether his soul is to be enclosed in the body for a longer or a shorter time makes no difference to him at all; even if he must depart at once, he will go as readily as if turning to any other task that can be done decently and in order, caring all through life about only this: that his mind never turns away from anything that belongs to a thinking creature and a member of a community.

In the mind of a man who has been disciplined and purified you will find nothing rotten, no foulness, no sore merely scabbed over. Fate does not catch his life unfinished, as one might say of an actor who leaves the stage before the play is over and done. And there is nothing in him servile, nothing affected, nothing too tightly bound to other things nor yet cut off from them, nothing that deserves blame, nothing that looks for a place to hide.

Revere the faculty that forms opinions. Everything depends on it, whether or not there will arise in your ruling part any opinion out of keeping with nature and the constitution of a rational being. This faculty promises freedom from hasty judgment, friendship toward others, and obedience to the gods.

So throw everything else away and hold on to these few things only. And remember as well that each of us lives only in the present moment, this indivisible point of time, and that all the rest of our life is either past or uncertain. Short, then, is the time anyone lives, and small the corner of the earth where he lives, and short too the longest fame after death, kept alive only by a succession of poor mortals who will die very soon themselves, and who do not even know their own selves, much less someone who died long ago.

To the aids already mentioned let this one be added: make for yourself a definition or description of whatever is presented to you, so that you can see clearly what kind of thing it is in its essence, stripped bare, in its full reality, and can name it properly, and name the parts of which it is composed and into which it will dissolve. Nothing lifts the mind so much as being able to examine, methodically and truthfully, every object that comes up in life, and always to look at things in such a way as to see at the same time what kind of universe this is, what purpose each thing serves in it, what value it has in relation to the whole and what value in relation to man, who is a citizen of the highest city, of which all other cities are like households. Of whatever is now making an impression on me I must ask: what is it, what is it made of, how long is it its nature to last, and what virtue do I need to meet it, such as gentleness, courage, truthfulness, loyalty, simplicity, self-sufficiency, and the rest. So on every occasion a man should say: this comes from god; and this comes by the apportioning and spinning of the thread of destiny, by such coincidence and chance; and this comes from one of my own kind, a kinsman and partner, who does not know, however, what accords with his nature. But I know; and for that reason I treat him, according to the natural law of fellowship, with kindness and justice. At the same time, in things that are neither good nor bad, I try to gauge the worth of each.

If you work at the task before you, following right reason in earnest, with vigor and calm, letting nothing else distract you, but keeping the divinity within you pure as though you might have to give it back at any moment; if you hold to this, expecting nothing, fearing nothing, but content with your present activity in accordance with nature, and with heroic truthfulness in every word and sound you utter, then you will live happily. And there is no one who can stop you.

Just as doctors always keep their instruments and scalpels ready for cases that suddenly call for their skill, so you should keep your principles ready for understanding things divine and human, and for doing everything, even the smallest thing, mindful of the bond that links the divine and the human to each other. For you will do nothing well in human affairs without at the same time keeping the divine in view, and nothing well in divine affairs without keeping the human in view.

Do not drift aimlessly any longer. You are not going to read your own notebooks now, nor the deeds of the ancient Romans and Greeks, nor the selections from books you were saving up for old age. So hurry to the goal before you, throw away idle hopes, and come to your own rescue, if you care about yourself at all, while it is still in your power.

People do not realize how many things are meant by the words stealing, sowing, buying, keeping quiet, seeing what ought to be done; for this last is not done with the eyes but by another kind of sight.

Body, soul, intelligence: to the body belong sensations, to the soul appetites, to the intelligence principles. To receive impressions of forms through appearances belongs even to animals. To be jerked about by the strings of desire belongs both to wild beasts and to men who have turned themselves into women, and to a Phalaris and a Nero. And to have the intelligence that points the way to what seems fitting belongs even to those who do not believe in the gods, who betray their country, and who do their foul deeds behind shut doors. If, then, everything else is shared by all those I have mentioned, what remains as peculiar to the good man is this: to be pleased and content with whatever happens, and with the thread spun for him; not to defile the divinity seated in his breast nor disturb it with a crowd of images, but to keep it serene, following it obediently as a god, never saying anything contrary to the truth nor doing anything contrary to justice. And if everyone refuses to believe that he lives a simple, modest, and contented life, he is not angry with any of them, nor does he stray from the road that leads to the end of life, to which a man ought to come pure, tranquil, ready to depart, and without compulsion perfectly reconciled to his lot.
