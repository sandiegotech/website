Meditations, Book VIII

Book VIII8 of 12

This reflection also helps remove the craving for empty fame: it is no longer in your power to have lived your whole life, or even your life from youth onward, as a philosopher. It is plain, both to many others and to yourself, that you are far from being a philosopher. You have fallen into a confused way of living, so it is no longer easy for you to gain the reputation of a philosopher, and your manner of life works against it too. So if you have truly seen how matters stand, let go of the question of how you will appear to others, and be content to live the rest of your life as your nature wills. Notice, then, what it wills, and let nothing else distract you. You have wandered far and wide and found happiness nowhere: not in logical arguments, not in wealth, not in reputation, not in pleasure, nowhere. Where is it, then? In doing what human nature requires. And how does a man do this? By holding to principles from which his impulses and actions flow. What principles? Those about good and bad: the belief that nothing is good for a man unless it makes him just, self-controlled, courageous, and free, and that nothing is bad unless it produces the opposite of these.

With every action you take, ask yourself: How does this affect me? Will I regret it? In a little while I will be dead and all of this will be gone. What more do I want, as long as what I am doing now is the work of an intelligent being, a social being, one who lives under the same law as God?

Alexander, Caesar, and Pompey, what are they compared to Diogenes, Heraclitus, and Socrates? Those philosophers understood the realities of things, their causes, their substance, and their ruling minds were their own. But as for the others, how many things they had to worry about, and to how many things they were enslaved!

Bear in mind that people will go on doing the same things regardless, even if you burst with frustration.

This is the main thing: do not be disturbed, for all things happen according to the nature of the universe, and in a little while you will be no one and nowhere, just like Hadrian and Augustus. Next, fix your eyes steadily on the task before you, look at it, and at the same time remember that it is your duty to be a good person and to do what human nature demands, doing it without wavering; and say what seems to you most just, but say it with good will, with modesty, and without pretense.

The nature of the universe has this work to do: to move the things that are here to elsewhere, to change them, to take them away from here, and to carry them there. All things are change, yet we have no reason to fear anything new. All things are familiar to us, and their distribution always stays the same.

Every nature is content with itself when it follows its proper course, and a rational nature follows its proper course when it gives its assent to nothing false or uncertain in its thoughts, when it directs its impulses only toward social action, when it limits its desires and aversions to things within its power, and when it is satisfied with everything assigned to it by the universal nature. For of this universal nature every particular nature is a part, just as the nature of a leaf is part of the nature of a plant; except that the leaf's nature is part of a nature that has no perception or reason and can be obstructed, while a human's nature is part of a nature that cannot be obstructed, that is intelligent and just, since it allots to everything its fair share according to its worth: its time, its substance, its cause, its activity, and its circumstances. But do not look to find any single thing equal in all respects to another single thing; rather, compare the whole of one thing, taken together, with the whole of another.

You do not have the leisure to read. But you do have the leisure to curb arrogance; you have the leisure to rise above pleasure and pain; you have the leisure to rise above the love of fame, and not to be annoyed by foolish and ungrateful people, but even to care for them.

Let no one ever again hear you complaining about life at court, or about your own life.

Regret is a kind of self-reproach for having neglected something useful; but what is good must also be useful, and the truly good person should attend to it. Yet no such person would ever regret refusing a sensual pleasure. Therefore pleasure is neither good nor useful.

This thing, what is it in itself, in its own makeup? What is its substance and material? What is its causal nature? What is it doing in the world? And how long does it last?

When you rise from sleep reluctantly, remember that it is according to your makeup and to human nature to perform social acts, while sleeping is something you share even with irrational animals. And what is according to each individual's nature is more truly its own, more suited to it, and indeed more agreeable.

Constantly, and if possible at every impression that strikes your soul, apply to it the principles of physics, of ethics, and of logic.

Whatever person you meet, immediately say to yourself: What are this person's opinions about good and bad? For if they hold such and such views about pleasure and pain and their causes, about fame and disgrace, about death and life, then it will seem nothing surprising or strange to me when they act accordingly, and I will keep in mind that they are compelled to act this way.

Remember that just as it would be absurd to be surprised that a fig tree produces figs, so it is absurd to be surprised that the world produces the things it is bound to produce; and for a doctor and a ship's captain it would be a disgrace to be surprised that someone has a fever or that the wind is unfavorable.

Remember that changing your opinion and following someone who corrects your mistake is just as consistent with freedom as stubbornly persisting in your error. For the act is your own, carried out according to your own impulse and judgment, and indeed according to your own understanding.

If something is within your own power, why do you do it? But if it is in another's power, whom do you blame, the atoms or the gods? Both are foolish. You must blame no one. For if you can, correct the cause; if you cannot do that, at least correct the thing itself; and if you cannot do even that, what good does it do you to find fault? Nothing should be done without purpose.

What has died does not fall out of the universe. If it remains here, it also changes here, and is dissolved into its proper parts, which are elements of the universe and of you. And these parts change too, and they do not complain.

Everything exists for some purpose: a horse, a vine. Why are you surprised? Even the sun will say, I exist for a purpose, and the rest of the gods will say the same. So what purpose do you exist for, to enjoy pleasure? See whether common sense allows this.

Nature has regard, in everything, no less for the ending than for the beginning and the continuance, just like a man throwing a ball into the air. What good is it for the ball to be thrown up, or what harm for it to come down, or even to have fallen? And what good is it to a bubble while it holds together, or what harm when it bursts? The same can be said of a flame.

Turn the body inside out and see what kind of thing it is; and what kind of thing it becomes when it has grown old, and when it is diseased.

Short-lived are both the one who praises and the one praised, the one who remembers and the one remembered, and all of this in a small corner of this part of the world; and even here people do not all agree, no, not even one man with himself; and the whole earth too is but a point.

Attend to the matter before you, whether it is an opinion, an action, or a word.

You suffer this justly, because you would rather become good tomorrow than be good today.

Am I doing something? I do it for the good of mankind. Does something happen to me? I accept it and refer it to the gods and to the source of all things, from which everything that happens flows.

Just as bathing appears to you as oil, sweat, dirt, filthy water, all of it disgusting, so is every part of life and everything in it.

Lucilla saw Verus die, and then Lucilla died. Secunda saw Maximus die, and then Secunda died. Epitynchanus saw Diotimus die, and then Epitynchanus died. Antoninus saw Faustina die, and then Antoninus died. So it is with everything. Celer saw Hadrian die, and then Celer died. And those sharp-witted people, whether prophets or men puffed up with pride, where are they now? For instance the sharp-witted Charax, Demetrius the Platonist, Eudaemon, and anyone else like them? All short-lived, dead long ago. Some were not remembered even briefly; others have become the heroes of legends; and still others have vanished even from the legends. Remember, then, that this little composite thing, yourself, must either be dissolved, or your poor breath must be snuffed out, or be moved and placed elsewhere.

It is a satisfaction to a person to do the proper work of a human being. Now the proper work of a human being is to be kind to one's own kind, to disdain the urges of the senses, to form a sound judgment of plausible appearances, and to take a survey of the nature of the universe and of the things that happen in it.

There are three relationships: the first to the body that surrounds you; the second to the divine cause from which all things come to all; and the third to the people who live alongside you.

Pain is either an evil to the body, in which case let the body say what it thinks of it, or an evil to the soul; but it is in the soul's power to keep its own serenity and calm, and not to think pain an evil. For every judgment, impulse, desire, and aversion is within, and no evil rises that high.

Wipe out your imaginings by often saying to yourself: Right now it is in my power to let no wickedness be in this soul, nor desire, nor any disturbance at all; but looking at all things I see what their nature is, and I use each according to its value. Remember this power that nature has given you.

Speak in the senate and to anyone at all, appropriately and without affectation; use plain speech.

Augustus' court, his wife, daughter, descendants, ancestors, sister, Agrippa, his relatives, his close companions, his friends; Areius, Maecenas, his physicians, his sacrificing priests, the whole court is dead. Then turn to the rest, considering not the death of a single person but of a whole line, like the Pompeys; and that inscription on the tombs, The last of his line. Then consider what trouble those before them went through so as to leave a successor; and then that, of necessity, someone must be the last. Here again, consider the death of an entire line.

It is your duty to order your life well in every single act; and if every act does its duty as far as possible, be content; and no one can prevent each act from doing its duty. "But some external thing will get in the way." Nothing will get in the way of your acting justly, soberly, and thoughtfully. "But perhaps some other capacity for action will be hindered." Well then, by accepting the hindrance and being content to redirect your efforts to what is permitted, another opportunity for action is immediately set before you in place of the one that was blocked, one that fits the very ordering of life we are speaking of.

Receive without arrogance; let go without reluctance.

If you ever saw a hand cut off, or a foot, or a head, lying somewhere apart from the rest of the body, that is what a man makes of himself, as far as he can, who is not content with what happens, who cuts himself off from others, or who does anything unsocial. Suppose you have detached yourself from the natural unity, for by nature you were made a part, but now you have cut yourself off; yet here is a beautiful provision: it is in your power to unite yourself again. God has granted this to no other part, that after being severed and cut off it may come together again. But consider the kindness with which he has honored humanity, for he has given man the power not to be severed from the whole at all, and, once severed, to return, to be reunited, and to resume his place as a part.

Just as the nature of the universe has given every rational being all the other powers it has, so we have received this power from it too. For just as the universal nature turns and fixes in its appointed place everything that stands in its way and opposes it, making such things a part of itself, so too the rational being is able to turn every obstacle into material of its own and use it for whatever purposes it has in mind.

Do not disturb yourself by thinking of your whole life. Do not let your thoughts take in all at once the various troubles you may expect to befall you; instead, on each occasion ask yourself: What is there in this present thing that is unbearable and beyond enduring? You will be ashamed to admit there is anything. Next, remember that neither the future nor the past weighs on you, but only the present. And this shrinks to very little if you isolate it by itself, and rebuke your mind if it cannot hold out against even this.

Does Panthea or Pergamus now sit by the tomb of Verus? Does Chabrias or Diotimus sit by the tomb of Hadrian? That would be ridiculous. And even if they did sit there, would the dead be aware of it? And if the dead were aware, would they be pleased? And if they were pleased, would that make them immortal? Was it not in the order of destiny that these people too should first become old women and old men, and then die? And what would they do once these mourners were dead? All of this is foul-smelling stuff and blood in a bag.

If you can see clearly, look and judge wisely, says the philosopher.

In the makeup of the rational being I see no virtue that opposes justice; but I do see a virtue that opposes the love of pleasure, and that is self-control.

If you take away your opinion about what seems to give you pain, you yourself stand in perfect safety. "Who is this self?" The reason. "But I am not just reason." Very well. Then let the reason not trouble itself. And if any other part of you suffers, let it hold its own opinion about itself.

Obstruction to the perceptions of the senses is an evil to the animal nature. Obstruction to its impulses is equally an evil to the animal nature. And something else likewise is an obstruction and an evil to the makeup of plants. So in the same way, whatever obstructs the intelligence is an evil to the intelligent nature. Now apply all of this to yourself. Does pain or sensual pleasure affect you? The senses will deal with that. Has some obstacle blocked you in your efforts toward a goal? If you were pursuing that goal without reservation, then yes, this obstacle is an evil to you considered as a rational being. But if you take into account the usual course of things, you have not yet been harmed, or even hindered. As for the things that properly belong to the understanding, no other person is able to hinder them, for neither fire, nor iron, nor a tyrant, nor abuse can touch it in any way, once it has become a sphere, "all round, and resting in its own joyous repose."

It is not right that I should give myself pain, for I have never intentionally given pain even to another.

Different things delight different people; but my delight is to keep my ruling faculty sound, turning away from no person and from none of the things that happen to people, but looking at all of them and receiving them with welcoming eyes, and using everything according to its value.

See that you secure this present time for yourself, for those who chase fame after death do not consider that the people of later times will be exactly like these whom they cannot stand now, and both are mortal. And what is it to you, in any case, if these people of later times utter this or that sound, or hold this or that opinion about you?

Take me and cast me wherever you wish; for there too I will keep my divine part tranquil, that is, content, as long as it can feel and act in keeping with its proper makeup. Is this change of place a good enough reason for my soul to become unhappy and worse than it was, downcast, overstretched, shrinking, terrified? What could you find that would be a good enough reason for that?

Nothing can happen to a person that is not a human experience, nor to an ox that is not in keeping with the nature of an ox, nor to a vine that is not in keeping with the nature of a vine, nor to a stone that is not proper to a stone. So if what happens to each thing is both usual and natural, why should you complain? For the common nature brings nothing you cannot bear.

If you are pained by some external thing, it is not that thing that disturbs you, but your own judgment about it. And it is in your power to wipe out this judgment right now. But if something in your own disposition gives you pain, who prevents you from correcting your view? And even if you are pained because you are not doing something that seems right to you, why not act instead of complaining? "But something stands in the way that I cannot get past." Then do not be distressed, for the cause of its not being done lies not with you. "But life is not worth living if I cannot do this." Then depart from life contentedly, just as one dies who is in full activity, and at peace even with the things that obstruct him.

Remember that the ruling faculty is invincible when, gathered within itself, it is satisfied with itself, doing nothing it does not choose to do, even if its resistance comes from mere stubbornness. What, then, will it be like when it forms a judgment about something with the aid of reason and deliberation? For this reason a mind free from passions is a citadel, for a man has nothing more secure to flee to for refuge and to be forever unassailable. Whoever has not seen this is ignorant; but whoever has seen it and does not flee to this refuge is wretched.

Say nothing more to yourself than what the first appearances report. Suppose it is reported to you that a certain person speaks ill of you. That has been reported; but that you have been harmed, that has not been reported. I see that my child is sick. I do see that; but that he is in danger, I do not see. So always stay with the first appearances, and add nothing of your own from within, and then nothing happens to you. Or rather, add something, like a person who knows everything that happens in the world.

A cucumber is bitter? Throw it away. There are briars in the road? Turn aside from them. That is enough. Do not add, And why were such things made in the world? For you would be laughed at by someone who understands nature, just as you would be laughed at by a carpenter or a shoemaker if you found fault with the shavings and offcuts you see in their workshop. And yet they have places to throw their shavings and offcuts, while the universal nature has no space outside herself; but the marvel of her art is that, though she has enclosed herself within limits, she changes into herself everything within her that seems to decay, grow old, and become useless, and from these very same things she makes other new things, so that she needs no material from outside and requires no place to dump what decays. She is content, then, with her own space, her own matter, and her own art.

Do not be sluggish in your actions, nor disorderly in your conversation, nor scattered in your thoughts; let there be no inner conflict in your soul, nor any outward gushing of it, and do not be so busy in life that you have no leisure.

Suppose people kill you, cut you to pieces, curse you. What can these things do to keep your mind from remaining pure, wise, sober, and just? For instance, if someone stood beside a clear, pure spring and cursed it, the spring would never stop sending up drinkable water; and if he threw clay or filth into it, it would quickly scatter and wash them away, and would not be polluted at all. How, then, will you possess a perpetual fountain, and not a mere well? By shaping yourself, hour by hour, toward freedom joined with contentment, simplicity, and modesty.

Whoever does not know what the world is does not know where he is. And whoever does not know the purpose for which the world exists does not know who he is, nor what the world is. But whoever has failed to grasp any one of these could not even say for what purpose he himself exists. What, then, do you make of someone who chases the praise of those who applaud, of people who know neither where they are nor who they are?

Do you wish to be praised by someone who curses himself three times an hour? Would you wish to please someone who does not please himself? Does a man please himself who regrets nearly everything he does?

No longer let your breathing alone be in concert with the air that surrounds you; let your intelligence too now be in harmony with the intelligence that embraces all things. For the intelligent power is no less diffused through all parts and pervading all things, for the one who is willing to draw it in, than the power of the air is for the one who is able to breathe it.

In general, wickedness does no harm at all to the universe; and in particular, one man's wickedness does no harm to another. It is only harmful to the one who possesses it, who has it in his own power to be free of it the moment he chooses.

To my own free will, my neighbor's free will is just as indifferent as their poor breath and flesh. For though we are made above all for one another's sake, still the ruling power of each of us has its own province; otherwise my neighbor's wickedness would be my harm, which God has not willed, so that my unhappiness need not depend on another.

The sun seems to be poured down and is indeed diffused in all directions, yet it is not poured away. For this diffusion is an extension; that is why its rays are called aktines, from ekteinesthai, "to be extended." You can judge what kind of thing a ray is by looking at the sun's light passing through a narrow opening into a darkened room, for it is extended in a straight line, and is, as it were, divided when it meets any solid body that stands in its way and blocks the air beyond; yet there the light remains fixed and does not slide off or fall away. Such, then, should be the outpouring and diffusion of the understanding: in no way a pouring away, but an extension, making no violent or forceful collision with the obstacles in its path, and not falling down, but staying fixed and illuminating whatever receives it. For a body deprives itself of the light if it does not let it in.

Whoever fears death fears either the loss of sensation or a different kind of sensation. But if you will have no sensation, then you will feel no harm; and if you will acquire a different kind of sensation, you will be a different kind of living being and will not cease to live.

People exist for the sake of one another. So either teach them, or put up with them.

An arrow moves in one way, the mind in another. The mind, even when it exercises caution and when it is engaged in inquiry, moves no less straight ahead, toward its goal.

Enter into every person's ruling faculty, and also let every other person enter into yours.
