# Meditations, Book XI

These are the properties of the rational soul: it sees itself, analyzes itself, and makes itself whatever it chooses. The fruit it bears, it enjoys itself—whereas the fruit of plants, and what corresponds to fruit in animals, others enjoy. It reaches its own end wherever the limit of life happens to fall. It is not like a dance or a play or anything of that kind, where the whole performance is left incomplete if something cuts it short; rather, in every part, and wherever it may be stopped, it makes whatever was set before it full and complete, so that it can say, "I have what is my own." What is more, it ranges over the whole universe and the empty space surrounding it, surveys its shape, reaches out into the infinity of time, and embraces and grasps the periodic renewal of all things. It understands that those who come after us will see nothing new, and that those before us saw nothing more; in a sense, anyone forty years old who has any understanding at all has already seen, through the uniformity that prevails, everything that has been and everything that will be. This too is a property of the rational soul: love of your neighbor, and truth, and modesty, and valuing nothing more than itself—which is also a property of Law. So right reason and the reason of justice are no different at all.

You will set little value on a pleasing song, or on dancing, or on the wrestling match, if you break the melody of the voice down into its separate sounds and ask yourself, about each one, whether this is what has mastered you—for shame will keep you from admitting it. Do the same with dancing, taking each movement and posture separately, and the same with the wrestling match. In all things, then—except virtue and the acts of virtue—remember to break them down into their parts, and by this dividing learn to value them little. Apply this rule to your whole life as well.

What a soul it is that is ready, the moment it must be separated from the body, to be extinguished, or scattered, or to go on existing—but ready in a way that comes from its own considered judgment, not from sheer stubbornness, as with the Christians, but with deliberation and dignity, and in a way that could persuade another, without any theatrical display.

Have I done something for the common good? Then I have had my reward. Keep this always before your mind, and never stop doing such good.

What is your craft? To be good. And how is that done well, except through general principles—some about the nature of the universe, others about the proper makeup of a human being?

Tragedies were first brought onto the stage to remind people of the things that happen to them, and that it is according to nature for such things to happen, and that if you enjoy watching them on the stage you should not be distressed when they take place on the larger stage of life. For you see that these things must turn out this way, and that even those who cry out "O Cithaeron!" still have to bear them. And indeed the dramatists do say some things well, especially lines like this one: "If the gods care nothing for me and my children, there is a reason for this too." And again: "We must not be angry at things." And: "Life must be reaped like a ripe ear of grain." And other things of the same kind. After tragedy came the old comedy, with its blunt freedom of speech, useful by its very plainness in reminding people to beware of arrogance—which is also why Diogenes drew on these writers. As for the middle comedy that came next, look at what it was; and consider, too, what the new comedy was introduced for, which gradually sank into mere mimicry and stage trickery. Everyone knows that even these writers say some good things—but the whole aim of such poetry and drama, what does it ultimately come to?

How plainly it appears that there is no condition of life so well suited to philosophy as this one you happen to be in right now.

A branch cut off from the branch next to it is necessarily cut off from the whole tree as well. In the same way, a person cut off from another person has fallen away from the whole community. Now a branch is cut off by someone else, but a person cuts himself off from his neighbor by his own act, when he hates him and turns away from him—not realizing that at the same time he has cut himself off from the whole social system. Yet he still has this gift from Zeus, who framed society: it is in our power to grow back onto what is near us and to become once more a part that helps make up the whole. But if this kind of separation happens often, it becomes hard for the part that detaches itself to be brought back into unity and restored to its former state. In the end, the branch that grew with the tree from the start and shared one life with it is not the same as one that is cut off and then grafted back on. As the gardeners put it, such a branch grows together with the rest of the tree, but it does not share the same mind with it.

Just as those who try to block your way when you are acting according to right reason cannot turn you aside from your proper course, do not let them drive you from your goodwill toward them either. Stand guard equally on both fronts: not only in steadiness of judgment and action, but also in gentleness toward those who try to hinder you or otherwise give you trouble. For it is a weakness to be irritated at them, just as it is a weakness to be turned from your action or to give way through fear. Both are deserters from their post—the one who acts from fear, and the one who becomes estranged from a man who is by nature his kinsman and friend.

No nature is inferior to art, for the arts imitate the natures of things. And if that is so, then the most perfect and all-embracing of natures cannot fall short of the skill of art. Now all the arts make lesser things for the sake of greater ones; so the universal nature does the same. And from this, in fact, comes the origin of justice, and on justice the other virtues are founded—for justice will not be honored if we care about indifferent things, or if we are easily deceived, careless, and changeable.

If the things whose pursuit and avoidance disturb you do not come to you, still, in a way, you go to them. Let your judgment about them be at rest, then, and they will stay quiet, and you will not be seen either chasing or fleeing anything.

The soul keeps its spherical form when it is neither stretched out toward any object, nor drawn inward, nor scattered, nor collapsed inward, but is lit by a light through which it sees the truth—the truth of all things and the truth that is in itself.

Suppose someone despises me. That is his concern. Mine is to make sure I am not caught doing or saying anything that deserves contempt. Suppose someone hates me. That too is his concern. I will be mild and kindly toward everyone, ready even to show that man his mistake—not in reproach, nor as a show of my own patience, but honestly and nobly, like the great Phocion (unless even he was only pretending). For the inner self ought to be like this, and a man ought to be seen by the gods neither dissatisfied with anything nor complaining. What harm can it be to you, if you are now doing what suits your own nature and are content with what at this moment suits the nature of the universe—you, a human being stationed at your post so that the common good may somehow be served?

People look down on one another and flatter one another; they want to rise above one another, and yet they grovel before one another.

How false and insincere is the man who says, "I have made up my mind to deal honestly with you!" What are you doing, man? There is no need to announce it. It will show itself soon enough in your actions. The truth ought to be plainly written on your forehead. A person's character shows at once in his eyes, just as someone who is loved reads everything in the eyes of those who love him. A man who is honest and good ought to be like a man with a strong smell, so that anyone who comes near him cannot help noticing it whether he wants to or not. But a put-on simplicity is a hidden knife. Nothing is more disgraceful than a wolfish, false friendship. Avoid that above all. The good, simple, kindly man shows all this in his eyes; there is no mistaking it.

As for living in the best way, this power lies in the soul, provided it stays indifferent to things that are indifferent. And it will stay indifferent if it looks at each of these things separately and all together, and remembers that none of them forces an opinion of itself on us or comes to us; they stay still and unmoving, and it is we ourselves who form the judgments about them and, so to speak, write them into ourselves—when it is in our power not to write them, and in our power, if such judgments have slipped in unnoticed, to wipe them out. Remember too that such attention is needed only for a short while, and then life will be over. Besides, what is so hard about doing this? If these things are according to nature, take joy in them and they will come easily; but if they are contrary to nature, look for what agrees with your own nature and strive toward that, even if it brings no reputation—for every man is allowed to seek his own good.

Consider where each thing comes from, what it is made of, what it changes into, and what kind of thing it will be once it has changed—and that it will take no harm at all.

First: what is my relation to other people? We are made for one another; and, in another sense, I was made to be set over them, as a ram over the flock or a bull over the herd. But examine the matter from first principles, this way: if all things are not mere atoms, then it is nature that orders everything; and if that is so, the lesser things exist for the sake of the greater, and these for the sake of one another.

Second, consider what kind of men they are at the table, in bed, and so on; and especially what compulsions of opinion they are under; and as for their acts, with what pride they do what they do.

Third, that if people do rightly what they do, we have no reason to be displeased; and if they do wrong, it is plainly against their will and out of ignorance. For just as every soul is robbed of the truth against its will, so too it is robbed against its will of the power to treat each person as he deserves. That is why people are stung when they are called unjust, ungrateful, greedy—in a word, wrongdoers toward their neighbors.

Fourth, consider that you too do many things wrong, and that you are a man like the rest. And even if you do hold back from certain faults, you still have the disposition to commit them, and if you refrain it is only through cowardice, or concern for your reputation, or some such mean motive.

Fifth, consider that you do not even really know whether people are doing wrong or not, for many things are done with some reference to circumstances. In short, a man must learn a great deal before he can judge another man's actions correctly.

Sixth, consider, when you are very vexed or grieved, that a man's life is only a moment, and that in a short time we are all laid out dead.

Seventh, that it is not people's acts that disturb us, for those acts rest on their own ruling principles; it is our own opinions that disturb us. Take away these opinions, then—resolve to drop your judgment that the act is something dreadful—and your anger is gone. And how do I take away these opinions? By reflecting that no wrong done by another brings shame on me. For unless what is shameful were the only bad thing, I too would necessarily do many wrongs and become a robber and everything else.

Eighth, consider how much more pain is caused us by the anger and vexation over such acts than by the acts themselves that we are angry and vexed about.

Ninth, consider that a good disposition is unconquerable, provided it is genuine and not an affected smile and play-acting. For what can even the most violent man do to you if you go on being kind toward him, and, as the chance arises, gently advise him and calmly correct his errors at the very moment he is trying to harm you—saying, "Not so, my child; we were made by nature for something else. You will certainly not injure me, but you are injuring yourself, my child." And show him, with gentle tact and by general principles, that this is so—that not even bees behave as he does, nor any animals that nature has made to live in groups. And you must do this without any double meaning and without reproach, but affectionately and with no bitterness in your soul—not as if you were lecturing him, nor so that any bystander might admire you, but either when he is alone, or, if others are present, gently take him aside.

Remember these nine rules as if you had received them as a gift from the Muses, and begin at last to be a man while you are alive. But you must equally avoid flattering people and being vexed at them, for both are unsocial and lead to harm. And keep this truth before you when anger flares up: that to be moved by passion is not manly, whereas mildness and gentleness, as they are more in keeping with human nature, are also more manly. The man who has these qualities has strength, sinew, and courage—not the man given to fits of passion and discontent. For the nearer a man's mind comes to freedom from passion, the nearer it comes to strength; and just as pain is a mark of weakness, so is anger. The man who gives way to pain and the man who gives way to anger are both wounded, and both have surrendered.

And if you will, take a tenth gift as well, from the leader of the Muses: that to expect bad men not to do wrong is madness, for that is to want the impossible. But to allow them to behave so toward others while expecting them not to wrong you is irrational and tyrannical.

There are four chief errors of the ruling faculty to be constantly on guard against; when you have caught them, wipe them out, saying in each case: "This thought is not necessary." "This one tends to destroy the bonds of society." "What you are about to say does not come from your real thoughts"—and you should count it among the most absurd of things for a man not to speak from his real thoughts. And the fourth is when you reproach yourself, for that is proof that the diviner part within you has been overpowered and is yielding to the lower, perishable part—the body—and to its coarse pleasures.

The airy part in you and all the fiery parts mixed into you, though by nature they tend upward, still obey the arrangement of the universe and are held down here in the compound mass of the body. And the earthy and watery parts in you, though their tendency is downward, are still raised up and kept in a position that is not their natural one. In this way the elements obey the whole: once fixed in some place, they stay there by necessity until the universe again sounds the signal for their dissolution. Is it not strange, then, that only your intelligent part should be disobedient and discontented with its place? And yet nothing is forced on it but what agrees with its own nature; still it does not submit, but is carried the opposite way. For the movement toward injustice, intemperance, anger, grief, and fear is nothing but the act of one who departs from nature. And when the ruling faculty is discontented with anything that happens, it too is deserting its post, for it was made for piety and reverence toward the gods no less than for justice. These qualities, too, are included under the general heading of contentment with the way things are constituted—indeed, they come before acts of justice.

A man who does not have one and the same aim throughout his life cannot be one and the same person throughout his life. But what I have said is not enough unless this is added too: what that aim ought to be. For just as people do not hold the same view about all the things commonly thought to be good, but only about certain ones—those that concern the common interest—so too the aim we set before ourselves should be of a common, social, and civic kind. The man who directs all his efforts toward this aim will make all his actions of one piece, and so he will always be the same.

Think of the country mouse and the town mouse, and of the alarm and panic of the town mouse.

Socrates used to call the opinions of the crowd "Lamiae"—bogeymen to frighten children.

At their public spectacles the Spartans used to set out seats in the shade for strangers, while they themselves sat down wherever they could.

Socrates made his excuses to Perdiccas for not visiting him, saying it was because he did not want to perish by the worst of all deaths—that is, to receive a favor and then be unable to return it.

In the writings of the Epicureans there was this precept: to keep thinking of someone from earlier times who practiced virtue.

The Pythagoreans tell us to look up at the heavens in the morning, so that we may be reminded of those bodies that forever do the same things in the same way as they go about their work, and reminded too of their purity and their nakedness—for there is no veil over a star.

Consider what a man Socrates was, when he wrapped himself in a skin after Xanthippe had taken his cloak and gone out, and what he said to the friends who were ashamed of him and shrank back when they saw him dressed like that.

Neither in writing nor in reading will you be able to set down rules for others before you have first learned to obey rules yourself. And this is far more true in life.

You are a slave: free speech is not for you.

"And my heart laughed within me."

"And they will curse virtue, speaking harsh words."

To look for a fig in winter is madness; so is looking for your child when that is no longer granted.

When a man kisses his child, said Epictetus, he should whisper to himself, "Perhaps tomorrow you will die." "But those are words of bad omen." "No word is of bad omen," said Epictetus, "that names a work of nature; or, if it is, then it is also of bad omen to speak of the ears of corn being reaped."

The unripe grape, the ripe cluster, the dried raisin—all are changes, not into nothing, but into something that does not yet exist.

No one can rob us of our free will.

Epictetus also said that a man must work out an art, or set of rules, for giving his assent; and in his impulses to act he must take care that they suit the circumstances, serve the common interest, and match the worth of their object; and as for desire, he should keep away from it altogether; and as for aversion, he should not direct it at anything that is not in our power.

The dispute, then, he said, is not about some trivial matter, but about whether we are sane or mad.

Socrates used to ask: "What do you want—the souls of rational beings, or of irrational ones?" "Of rational ones." "Rational beings of what kind—sound or unsound?" "Sound." "Then why do you not look for them?" "Because we already have them." "Then why do you fight and quarrel?"
