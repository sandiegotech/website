The Bhagavad Gita, Chapter VI

The Discipline of Meditation

Chapter VI6 of 18

Krishna said: The one who does the work he ought to do, without leaning on its fruit, is the true renouncer, the true disciplined soul — not the one who merely lights no sacrificial fire and lifts a hand to no task. What the world calls renunciation, Arjuna, you should know to be the very discipline of action, the Yoga of work; for no one becomes a disciplined soul who has not first let go of every grasping intention. For the sage who is climbing toward this discipline, work is said to be the means; for the one who has already reached it, stillness is the means. When a person is no longer bound to the objects of sense, no longer bound to his own deeds, when he has let go of every grasping intention, then he is said to have arrived.

Let a man lift himself up by his own self, and never let himself sink down; for the self is the friend of the self, and the self is also its enemy. To the man who has mastered himself, his own self is a friend; but to the man who has not, his own self stands against him like an enemy. The self of one who has conquered himself and found his peace is gathered and steady, alike in cold and heat, in pleasure and pain, in honor and disgrace. He is the disciplined soul, full and content with the wisdom he has realized, unshaken, his senses mastered, to whom a clod of earth, a stone, and a lump of gold are all one and the same. And he stands highest of all who holds the same even regard for friend and companion and enemy alike, for the indifferent stranger and the one who hates him, for the good and the wicked together.

Let the seeker of this discipline withdraw to a solitary place and keep at his practice, alone, his thoughts and his whole self held in check, wanting nothing, owning nothing. Let him set up a firm seat for himself in a clean spot, neither too high nor too low, with a cloth and a deerskin and the kusha grass laid one upon another. There, seated, holding his mind to a single point, his thoughts and the working of his senses reined in, let him take up the practice of meditation to make his heart pure. Let him hold his body, his head, and his neck straight and unmoving, his gaze steady, looking toward the tip of his own nose and not glancing about in any direction. Serene and unafraid, firm in his vow of chastity, his mind held in check, let him sit absorbed, his thought fixed on me, intent on me alone. So practicing always, his mind held in, the disciplined soul comes at last to the peace whose end is nirvana, the peace that abides in me.

But this discipline is not for the one who eats too much, nor for the one who eats nothing at all; it is not for the one who sleeps too much, nor for the one who keeps himself always awake, Arjuna. For the one whose food and recreation are measured, whose efforts in all his work are measured, whose sleeping and waking are measured — for that man the discipline becomes the very thing that puts an end to his sorrow. When the mind, held under control, comes to rest in the self alone, free of all craving for any object whatsoever, then a person is called a disciplined soul.

As a lamp in a windless place does not flicker — that is the image given for the disciplined yogi, his mind held in check, deep in the practice of communion with the self. When the mind comes to rest, stilled by the practice of this discipline; when, looking on the self by the self, a person finds his contentment in the self alone; when he knows that boundless joy which lies beyond the senses and is grasped by the awakened reason, and, knowing it, never again strays from the truth of it; and when, having gained it, he counts no other gain greater than this, and, settled in it, is not shaken even by the heaviest grief — that, you should know, is what is meant by Yoga, the loosing of every bond with sorrow. This discipline must be practiced with resolve and with a heart that does not lose heart.

Letting go, without exception, of every desire born of his own imagining, holding in his senses from every side by the power of his mind, let him little by little come to rest, his reason held firm, his mind settled in the self and turned toward nothing else. Wherever the restless and unsteady mind wanders off, let him rein it back from there and bring it under the control of the self alone. For the deepest happiness comes to the yogi whose mind is at peace, whose passion has been stilled, who is without fault and has become Brahman. So keeping himself always in this discipline, freed from every stain, the yogi reaches with ease the boundless happiness of contact with Brahman. The one whose self is joined in this discipline, who looks on all things with an even eye, sees the self present in every creature, and every creature present in the self. The one who sees me everywhere, and sees all things in me — I am never lost to him, and he is never lost to me. The yogi who, settled in oneness, worships me as dwelling in all creatures, lives in me, whatever his outward way of life. And the one who, taking his own self as the measure, looks on all things as he looks on himself, Arjuna — whether in pleasure or in pain — that one is held to be the highest yogi of all.

Arjuna said: This discipline you have spoken of, Krishna, this equal-mindedness — I cannot see how it can hold steady, for the mind is so restless. The mind is restless, Krishna, turbulent, willful, unyielding; to hold it down seems to me as hard as holding down the wind.

Krishna said: Beyond doubt, strong-armed one, the mind is restless and hard to hold; and yet, Arjuna, it can be held — by steady practice and by dispassion. For the man who has no command of himself, this discipline is hard to reach, I agree; but the man who strives, and who keeps a hold on himself, can reach it by the right means.

Arjuna said: But what of the one who has faith, yet cannot master himself, whose mind wanders away from the discipline before he reaches its goal — what becomes of him, Krishna? Failing of both worlds, does he not simply break apart like a torn cloud and come to nothing, with no firm ground beneath him, lost on the road to Brahman? Clear away this doubt of mine entirely, Krishna; there is no one but you who can clear it.

Krishna said: Son of Pritha, no such man is lost, neither in this world nor in the world to come. For no one who does what is good, my friend, ever comes to a bad end. The one who falls short in this discipline reaches the worlds that belong to those who have done right, and dwells there for unmeasured years; and then he is born again into the house of the pure and the prosperous. Or he may even be born into a family of yogis who are wise — though a birth like that is very hard to come by in this world. There he recovers the understanding he had built up in his former body, and from that point he strives once more toward perfection, Arjuna. For the very practice of his earlier life carries him onward, even against his will. Even the one who only longs to know this discipline rises beyond the letter of the Vedas. And the yogi who strives with all his strength, washed clean of every fault, perfected through many births, comes at last to the highest goal. The yogi is greater than those who punish their bodies in penance, greater than the learned, greater than those who pour out works of ritual; so be a yogi, Arjuna. And of all yogis, the one I hold to be the most deeply joined to me is the one who worships me with faith, his inmost self resting in me.
