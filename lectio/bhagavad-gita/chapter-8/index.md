# The Bhagavad Gita, Chapter VIII

The Hour of Death and the Imperishable

Arjuna said: Tell me, best of all, what is that Brahman? What is the Self, the adhyatma? What is action, karma? What do you call the realm of beings, the adhibhuta, and what the realm of the gods, the adhidaiva? Who, here in this body, is the lord of sacrifice, the adhiyajna — and how is he the lord of it? And at the hour of death, Krishna, how are you to be known by those who keep themselves in check?

Krishna said: Brahman is the supreme, the imperishable; the Self is what it is called as it dwells in each person. Action is the creative force that sends all beings forth and holds them in being. The realm of beings is my perishable nature; the realm of the gods is the spirit, the Purusha, who lies behind them; and the lord of sacrifice is I myself, here within this body, scourge of your enemies.

And at the hour of death, the one who lets go of the body remembering me alone, and goes out so, comes into my being; of this there is no doubt. But whatever state a person dwells on as he leaves the body at the end, to that state he goes, Arjuna, having been shaped by it all his life long. So at all times remember me, and fight. With your mind and your understanding fixed on me, you will come to me; do not doubt it. The one whose thought is disciplined by constant practice, never straying after anything else, reaches the supreme spirit, the divine Purusha — for to meditate on him is to go to him.

Whoever holds in mind the ancient Seer, the ruler of all, smaller than the smallest, the sustainer of everything, whose form is past imagining, radiant as the sun and beyond all darkness — whoever holds him so at the hour of death, with a still mind and an unwavering devotion, the breath drawn up between the brows by the power of his discipline, that person reaches the supreme spirit, the divine Purusha.

I will tell you, in brief, of that imperishable goal which those who know the Vedas call the changeless, which the seekers enter once they are free of all craving, the goal for which they take up the discipline of chastity. The one who closes all the gates of the senses, who pens the mind up within the heart, who draws his life-breath up into the head and holds himself firm in the practice of his discipline, and who, uttering OM, the single syllable that is Brahman, keeps me in mind as he goes out from the body: that person walks the highest road. To the one who thinks of me always, whose mind never turns aside to anything else, to that disciplined soul, ever steadfast, I am easy to reach. Having reached me, these great souls are not born again into this fleeting house of sorrow; they have come to the highest perfection.

All the worlds, Arjuna, up to the very world of Brahma the creator, are places of return: a soul that reaches them comes back again. But the one who reaches me is born no more. Those who understand that a day of Brahma lasts a thousand ages, and that his night also lasts a thousand ages — they are the ones who truly know what day and night are. When that long day dawns, all things come forth into the visible world out of the unmanifest; when that long night falls, they dissolve back again into the same unmanifest. This whole host of beings, the very same, comes into existence again and again, and is dissolved, helpless, at the fall of night, and rises up again, helpless, at the break of day. But beyond this unmanifest there is another, a higher unmanifest still, everlasting, which does not perish when all things perish. This unmanifest, this imperishable, is called the supreme goal; and those who reach it never return. That is my highest dwelling place. That supreme spirit, Arjuna, in whom all beings rest and by whom this whole universe is spread out, can be reached by a devotion that turns to nothing else.

Now I will tell you of the times at which those who are disciplined go out from the body never to return, and the times at which they go out only to come back. Fire, light, the day, the bright fortnight of the waxing moon, the six months of the sun's northern journey — those who know Brahman and go out by this path go on to Brahman. Smoke, the night, the dark fortnight of the waning moon, the six months of the sun's southern journey — by this path the disciplined one reaches only the light of the moon, and returns. These two ways of the world, the bright and the dark, are held to be everlasting. By the one a soul goes and does not come back; by the other it goes and returns again. Knowing these two paths, Arjuna, no disciplined soul is ever deluded; so at all times hold fast to this discipline.

Whatever fruit of merit is promised in the Vedas, in the sacrifices, in penance, in the giving of gifts — the disciplined soul who has understood this teaching passes beyond all of it, and comes at last to the supreme, the first and highest dwelling place.
