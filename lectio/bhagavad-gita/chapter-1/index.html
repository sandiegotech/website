The Bhagavad Gita, Chapter I

The Distress of Arjuna

Chapter I1 of 18

Dhritarashtra said: On the sacred plain of Kurukshetra my people and the sons of Pandu have gathered, hungry for battle. Tell me, Sanjaya: what did they do?

Sanjaya said: When he saw the army of the Pandavas arrayed for war, King Duryodhana went to his teacher Drona and said: "Master, look at this great host of Pandu's sons, marshalled by the son of Drupada, your own pupil in the art of war. Here stand heroes, great archers, the equals of Bhima and Arjuna in battle — Yuyudhana, Virata, and Drupada the mighty charioteer; Dhrishtaketu, Chekitana, and the valiant king of Kashi; Purujit, Kuntibhoja, and Saivya, that bull among men; bold Yudhamanyu and brave Uttamaujas; the son of Subhadra, and the sons of Draupadi — every one of them a great chariot-warrior.

"Know, too, best of brahmins, the finest of our own side — the captains of my army. Let me name them, so you may recognize them: yourself first of all, then Bhishma and Karna, and Kripa ever victorious in the fight; Ashvatthaman, Vikarna, and the son of Somadatta as well — and many another hero besides, each armed with his chosen weapon, all skilled in war, every one of them ready this day to lay down his life for me. Yet our forces, guarded by Bhishma, seem to me too few, while theirs, guarded by Bhima, seem strong enough. So let all of you, standing each in his place along the line, give support to Bhishma above all."

Then, to lift Duryodhana's heart, Bhishma, the eldest of the Kurus and their grandsire, raised his conch and sounded a blast like a lion's roar. At once conches and kettledrums, cymbals, drums, and trumpets broke out all together, and the sound was a tumult. Then, standing in their great chariot yoked with white horses, Krishna and Arjuna blew their divine conches. Krishna sounded the conch called Panchajanya, and Arjuna the one called Devadatta; Bhima of the terrible deeds, the wolf-bellied, blew his great conch Paundra. King Yudhishthira, the son of Kunti, sounded Anantavijaya, "Endless Victory"; Nakula and Sahadeva blew Sughosha, the "Sweet-toned," and Manipushpaka, the "Jewel-flowered." The great archer who is king of Kashi, and Shikhandi the mighty charioteer, Dhrishtadyumna and Virata and Satyaki the unconquered, Drupada and the sons of Draupadi, and the strong-armed son of Subhadra — all of them, lord of the earth, blew their conches, each his own. And that din tore through the hearts of Dhritarashtra's sons, until heaven and earth themselves seemed to shake with the roar.

Then, seeing Dhritarashtra's men drawn up and the clash of weapons about to begin, Arjuna — whose banner bore the figure of Hanuman the monkey — lifted his bow and said to Krishna: "Unshaken One, drive my chariot out into the open ground between the two armies, that I may look closely at these men gathered here, eager for battle, and see with whom I must contend in this coming fight. Let me see them, those who have come together here ready in this war to please the wicked-minded son of Dhritarashtra."

So spoken to by Arjuna, Krishna drove that splendid chariot out into the space between the armies, and halted it before Bhishma and Drona and all the kings of the earth. "Look, Arjuna," he said, "at the Kurus gathered here." And there Arjuna saw them standing — fathers and grandfathers, teachers, uncles, brothers, sons and grandsons, comrades, fathers-in-law, and dear friends, in both armies alike. Seeing all these kinsmen arrayed against him, Arjuna was overcome with deep pity, and in his sorrow he said:

Arjuna said: Krishna, when I look on these, my own people, gathered here and longing to fight, my limbs give way and my mouth goes dry; my body shakes and my hair stands on end. Gandiva, my great bow, slips from my hand, and my very skin is on fire. I cannot stand; my mind is reeling. And I see nothing but evil omens, Krishna. No good can come of killing my own kin in battle. I have no wish for victory, Krishna, nor for kingship, nor for pleasures. What is a kingdom to us, Govinda, what are delights, or life itself, when the very men for whose sake we would want kingdom and delight and pleasure are standing here in the line of battle, ready to give up their lives and their wealth — teachers, fathers, sons, and grandfathers too; uncles, fathers-in-law, grandsons, brothers-in-law, and all our kin? Even if they kill me, Krishna, I would not want to kill them — not for the lordship of all three worlds, much less for this earth.

What joy could be ours, Krishna, in killing the sons of Dhritarashtra? Strike these men down, and only sin would cling to us — even though they have become aggressors. So we have no right to slay our own kinsmen, the sons of Dhritarashtra; for how could we ever be happy, Krishna, having killed our own people? Even if they, with minds blinded by greed, see no wrong in the ruin of a family or the betrayal of friends, why should we not have the wisdom to turn back from this crime — we who can see plainly the evil that comes of destroying a family? For when a family is destroyed, its ancient ways perish with it; and when the old ways are gone, lawlessness overtakes the whole house. With lawlessness, Krishna, the women of the family are corrupted; and when the women are corrupted, the order of castes breaks down. And that confusion drags the destroyers of the family, and the family itself, down into hell; for their ancestors fall from their place, robbed of the offerings of rice and water that are owed to the dead. By these crimes of the family-destroyers, which throw the castes into confusion, the eternal ways of caste and of the household are wiped out. And we have heard, Krishna, that for those whose household ways are wiped out, a dwelling in hell is certain. Alas, that we should be ready to commit so great a sin — that, out of greed for the joys of kingship, we are prepared to kill our own people! Better by far that the sons of Dhritarashtra, weapons in hand, should kill me in the battle, unresisting and unarmed.

Having spoken thus on the field of battle, Arjuna sank down upon the seat of his chariot, letting his bow and arrows fall, his heart overwhelmed with grief.
