# The Bhagavad Gita, Chapter XIII

The Field and the Knower of the Field

Arjuna said: I would like to understand, Kesava, what is meant by nature and by spirit, by the field and by the one who knows the field, and what it is to have knowledge and what it is that is worth knowing.

Krishna said: This body, son of Kunti, is called the field; and the one who knows it is called the knower of the field. Know that I am that knower of the field, in every field there is. To know both the field and the knower of the field: that, I hold, is true knowledge. Now hear from me, in brief, what this field is, what it is like, how it changes and out of what it arises; and who the knower is, and what powers belong to him.

Sages have sung of this in many ways, in the various hymns and in the well-reasoned, decisive verses about Brahman, full of certainty. The great elements, the sense of "I," the intellect, and the unmanifest ground of all; the ten senses and the one mind, and the five fields the senses range over; desire and hatred, pleasure and pain, the body as a whole, awareness, and steadfastness — this, in brief, is the field, with all its changes set out.

And this is what knowledge is: humility, sincerity, doing no harm, patience, uprightness; service of the teacher, purity, steadiness, mastery of oneself; turning away from the things the senses crave, having no pride; keeping clear sight of the suffering and the flaws that run through birth and death, old age and disease; standing free and unentangled, not bound up in son or wife or home; an even mind, unshaken whether what comes is welcome or unwelcome; unwavering devotion to me with a love that turns to no one else; a love of quiet, out-of-the-way places, and no taste for the press of the crowd; a constant dwelling in the knowledge of the self, and a clear eye for the goal that this knowledge serves. All this is knowledge; whatever is otherwise is ignorance.

Now I will tell you what is worth knowing — the knowing of which a person tastes the deathless. It is the supreme Brahman, without beginning, which can be called neither being nor non-being. With hands and feet everywhere, with eyes and heads and faces on every side, with hearing everywhere, it stands in the world enfolding all things. It seems to have the qualities of all the senses, yet is free of every sense; unattached, and yet it sustains everything; free of the three qualities of nature, and yet it experiences them. It is outside all beings and within them; unmoving, and yet it moves; too subtle to be made out; far off, and at the same time near. Undivided, it seems as if divided up among all beings. It is to be known as the sustainer of beings, the one who draws them in and brings them forth again. It is the light even of all lights, said to lie beyond the darkness; it is knowledge, the thing worth knowing, and the goal that knowledge reaches — seated in the heart of every creature.

So I have told you, in brief, what the field is, what knowledge is, and what is worth knowing. The one who is devoted to me, grasping this, comes to share in my own being.

Know that nature and spirit are both without beginning; and know that all the changing forms, and the three qualities, are born of nature. Nature is said to be the cause when there is anything to do, or made, or done; spirit is said to be the cause in the experiencing of pleasure and pain. For spirit, seated in nature, undergoes the qualities that nature brings forth; and its clinging to these qualities is the reason it is born again and again, in good wombs and in evil ones.

Yet within this body there is also the supreme spirit — the one who looks on, who consents, who upholds and experiences, the great Lord, the highest self. Whoever knows this — knows the spirit, and knows nature with its three qualities — however he may now be living, will not be born again. Some come to see the self within themselves, by the self, in stillness of meditation; others reach it by the discipline of Sankhya, and others by the discipline of work. And there are still others who, not knowing this of themselves, take it up because they have heard it from others; and they too, holding fast to what they have heard, cross over death.

Whatever comes into being, Arjuna — anything that moves, anything that stands still — know that it arises from the union of the field and the knower of the field. The one who truly sees is the one who sees the supreme Lord dwelling alike in all beings, undying within the dying. For seeing the same Lord seated everywhere, he does no violence to himself by himself, and so he walks the highest road. And the one who truly sees is the one who sees that all action is done by nature alone, and that the self does nothing at all. When he sees that the whole variety of beings rests in the One, and spreads out from that One alone, then he comes to Brahman.

This supreme self, without beginning and free of the three qualities, does not act and is not stained, Arjuna, even though it dwells within the body. As all-pervading space, for its very subtlety, is never tainted by anything it holds, so the self, seated everywhere in the body, is never tainted. As the one sun lights up this whole world, so the knower of the field lights up the whole field. Those who see, with the eye of wisdom, this difference between the field and the knower of the field, and the way of release from the nature that binds all beings — they go to the highest.
