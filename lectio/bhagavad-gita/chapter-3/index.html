The Bhagavad Gita, Chapter III

Action as Sacrifice

Chapter III3 of 18

Arjuna said: If you hold that the way of understanding is higher than the way of action, Janardana, then why, Kesava, do you urge me into this terrible deed? Your words seem to point two ways at once, and they leave me confused. Tell me plainly, settle it for me: which single road leads to what is best?

Krishna said: I told you, blameless one, that in this world there are two paths, two disciplines of wisdom. There is the way of knowledge, which the followers of Sankhya walk through clear reflection; and there is the way of action, the discipline of work. No one escapes action by refusing to act, and no one reaches perfection by renunciation alone.

In fact no one can stay still even for a moment without acting. Everyone is driven to act, helpless under the forces of their own nature. The man who sits rigidly holding his body still while his mind keeps circling the very things he has given up is deluded, and he is rightly called a hypocrite. But the man who keeps his senses in hand and turns his powers of action to the discipline of work, without grasping at any reward — that man, Arjuna, is the one to honor. So do the work that is yours to do. Action is better than inaction; without it you could not even keep your body alive.

There is, however, a kind of work that binds the soul, and a kind that sets it free. Only work done as an offering escapes the chain; all other work shackles the world to it. So, Arjuna, do your work as an offering, free of attachment. At the beginning, when the Lord of creatures made humankind, he made sacrifice along with them, and said: By this you shall flourish; let this be the cow that grants every wish. By it nourish the gods, and let the gods nourish you; sustaining one another, you will reach the highest good. Fed by your offerings, the gods will give you the food you need; but whoever takes their gifts and gives nothing back is no better than a thief.

The good eat what is left after the offering is made, and they are freed of fault; but those who cook only for themselves feed on their own wrongdoing. All living things are sustained by food, food comes from the rain, rain comes from sacrifice, and sacrifice arises from action. Know that action arises from Brahman, and Brahman from the Imperishable; so Brahman, pervading all things, is forever present in sacrifice. Whoever does not help to turn this wheel as it was set turning, but lives only to gratify his senses, lives a wasted and shameful life.

But the one who finds his joy in the Self, who is content and satisfied within the Self, has nothing left that he needs to do. He has no stake in what is done or left undone here, and depends on no other creature for anything. So do the work that falls to you, but always without attachment; for the one who works unattached reaches the highest. It was through action alone that Janaka and others of old attained perfection. And you should act, if only for the holding-together of the world. Whatever a great man does, ordinary people imitate; the standard he sets, the whole world follows.

Look at me, son of Pritha. In all three worlds there is nothing I am bound to do, nothing left for me to gain or reach — and yet I keep acting. For if I did not go on working, tireless and watchful, people everywhere would follow my example; the human race would take me as its excuse and fall into ruin, and I would be the cause of confusion and the undoing of these creatures. So, just as the ignorant act because they are bound to their work, the wise should act too — but unattached, working to hold the world together. Let the wise not unsettle the minds of the ignorant who are still bound to their work; rather, doing all things with discipline, let the wise lead them to take joy in all their work.

Everything that happens is the work of nature's own forces; only a man fooled by his pride imagines, "I am the one who acts." But the one who sees the truth of how these forces and their work move among the senses keeps himself apart, knowing it is the forces acting upon the forces, not the Self. Those deceived by nature's forces cling to their work; let the one who knows the whole not unsettle these others who know only a part. So surrender all your actions to me, fix your mind on the Self, and fight — with no craving, no sense of "mine," and the fever gone out of you.

Those who keep faith with this teaching of mine and act on it without complaint are freed from the bondage of their actions. But those who carp at my teaching and refuse to act on it — know them for fools, blind to all real understanding, and lost. Even the wise act according to their own nature; every creature follows its nature, and what good does it do to fight it? The senses are bound to be drawn toward what they like and pushed from what they dislike; do not fall under the power of these two, for they are the ambush set across your path.

It is better to do your own duty imperfectly than to do another's duty well. Better to die doing what is yours to do; to take up another's work is dangerous.

Arjuna said: But then what is it, Krishna, that drives a man to do wrong even against his own will, as though some force were pushing him into it?

Krishna said: It is desire, it is anger, born of the force of passion in us — devouring, never satisfied, deeply corrupting. Know this for the enemy. As smoke hides fire, as rust films over a mirror, as the womb wraps the unborn child, so is wisdom veiled by this. Knowledge itself is veiled, Arjuna, by this steady enemy of the wise — desire, that burns on like a fire and is never filled. The senses, the mind, and the reason are said to be its seat; through these it veils wisdom and deludes the embodied soul. So first take hold of your senses, son of Bharata, and strike this thing down, this destroyer of wisdom and understanding. The senses are strong, they say; stronger than the senses is the mind; stronger than the mind is the reason; but stronger than the reason is the Self. So know the Self, which is higher than reason; steady yourself by your own deeper self, and kill this enemy, Arjuna — desire, so hard to corner and overcome.
