The Bhagavad Gita, Chapter XVIII

Freedom Through Surrender and Letting Go

Chapter XVIII18 of 18

Arjuna said: I want to understand truly, Krishna, the real nature of renunciation, and of letting go as well — and what it is that sets these two apart.

Krishna said: The wise call it renunciation when a man gives up all actions done out of desire; and they call it letting go when he surrenders the fruit of every action he performs. Some thinkers hold that all action is tainted and ought to be abandoned; others say that acts of sacrifice, of giving, and of self-discipline must never be abandoned. Hear my own judgment on this, Arjuna, best of the Bharatas, for letting go is itself of three kinds.

Acts of sacrifice, of giving, and of self-discipline are not to be abandoned at all; they are to be performed. For sacrifice, giving, and discipline are what purify the thoughtful. Yet even these acts should be done with attachment laid aside, and every claim on their fruit given up. This is my settled and final judgment, Arjuna.

To renounce an action that one is duty-bound to do is never right; to abandon such a work out of confusion is the way of darkness. And whoever gives up a work merely because it is hard, or because it troubles the body, has let it go in the wrong spirit — that is the renunciation of passion, and from it he gains nothing. But when a person does the work that is set before him simply because it ought to be done, and lays aside both his attachment to it and any thought of reward — that is the renunciation of light. The man who has true insight, whose doubts are cut away, does not shrink from work that is unwelcome and does not cling to work that is pleasant. No one who wears a body can give up action entirely; but the one who gives up the fruit of his actions — he is the true renouncer.

For those who have not let go, the fruit of action is waiting after death, in three forms: the welcome, the unwelcome, and the mixture of the two. But for those who have truly renounced, there is no such fruit at all, ever.

Learn from me, Arjuna, the five things that must come together for any action to be accomplished — as the Sankhya teaching lays them out, where all action comes to rest. There is the field, the body itself; there is the doer; there are the various instruments; there are the many separate efforts; and fifth, there is fate, the divine. Whatever action a person sets in motion with body, speech, or mind, whether it is right or wrong, these same five are always at work in it. This being so, the man who, through poverty of understanding, sees only himself as the doer sees nothing as it really is. But the one whose mind is free of the sense of "I," whose understanding is unstained — even if he kills these armies, he does not truly kill, and he is not bound by it.

Knowledge, the thing known, and the knower — these three set action in motion. The instrument, the act, and the doer — these three make up the sum of the deed. And knowledge, action, and the doer are each said to be of three kinds, according to the three qualities of nature. Hear how this is so.

Know that the knowledge of light is this: to see in all things one undying being, undivided in the divided, whole within the many separate lives. The knowledge born of passion is the knowledge that sees only the many separate beings, each cut off from the rest, and takes that separateness for the whole truth. And the knowledge of darkness clings to one single thing as if it were everything, without reason, without grasp of the real cause — a small, blind, and shabby thing.

The action of light is the work that ought to be done, done without attachment, without craving or hatred, by one who asks nothing for himself in return. The action of passion is the work a man drives himself to out of his desires, or out of his sense of self, straining and laboring after what he wants. And the action of darkness is the work undertaken in sheer confusion, with no thought for its consequences, for the harm or the loss it brings to others, or for one's own ruin.

The doer of light goes about his work free of attachment and free of all talk of "I," steady and resolute, unmoved whether the work succeeds or fails. The doer of passion is greedy for the fruit of what he does, grasping, given to violence, never clean within, swept this way and that by joy and grief. And the doer of darkness is undisciplined and coarse, stubborn, a cheat, malicious, lazy, forever putting things off, sunk in gloom.

Now hear, Arjuna, how understanding and resolve also fall into three kinds, set apart by the three qualities — and let me lay them out one by one.

The understanding of light knows when to act and when to hold back, what must be done and what must be left undone, what is to be feared and what is not, what binds the soul and what sets it free. The understanding of passion grasps right and wrong, what should be done and what should not, but never clearly, never as things truly are. And the understanding of darkness, wrapped in its own night, takes wrong for right and sees everything turned upside down.

The resolve of light is the steadiness with which a man holds his mind, his breath, and his senses in hand through a discipline that never wavers. The resolve of passion is the steadiness with which a man clings to duty, pleasure, and wealth, hungry at every step for the reward they will bring. And the resolve of darkness is the dull persistence with which a fool will not let go of his sleep, his fear, his grief, his vanity, and his despair.

And now, Arjuna, hear from me the three kinds of happiness. There is the happiness a man comes to enjoy through long practice, in which his sorrows finally end — the happiness that is bitter as poison at the first taste and sweet as nectar at the last, born of the clear serenity of his own mind. That is the happiness of light. There is the happiness that springs from the senses meeting what they crave — sweet as nectar at first and bitter as poison in the end. That is the happiness of passion. And there is the happiness that deludes the soul from beginning to end, the happiness that comes of sleep, of idleness, and of heedlessness. That is the happiness of darkness. There is no creature on earth, nor any god in heaven, that is free of these three qualities, for they are woven into nature itself.

The work of the brahmins, of the warriors, of the merchants and farmers, and of those who serve, Arjuna, is parceled out according to the qualities that arise from each one's own nature. Serenity, self-control, discipline, purity, patience, honesty, learning, judgment, and religious faith — this is the work of a brahmin, born of his nature. Boldness, vigor, firmness, skill, courage that does not flee the battle, generosity, and the bearing of one who is born to lead — this is the work of a warrior, born of his nature. Tilling the land, tending cattle, and trade is the work of the merchant and farmer, born of his nature; and labor in the service of others is the natural work of those who serve.

Each man reaches perfection by devoting himself, content, to the work that is his own. Hear how a person finds that perfection by giving himself to the work that is his. By laying his own labor as worship before the One from whom all beings flow and by whom this whole world was stretched out, a man comes to perfection.

It is better to do your own duty imperfectly than to do another's duty well. No one falls into sin who does the work that his own nature lays upon him. So do not abandon the work you were born to, Arjuna, even when it is flawed; for every undertaking is clouded by some fault, as every fire is wrapped in smoke. The man whose mind clings to nothing, who has mastered himself and let his cravings die away, reaches through renunciation the highest perfection of all: the freedom that lies beyond all action.

Learn from me, Arjuna, how the one who has reached that perfection then attains to Brahman, the highest reach of all wisdom. Joined to a purified understanding, holding himself firmly in check, turning away from the lures of sound and the other senses, casting off both craving and hatred; living apart, eating sparingly, his speech, his body, and his mind held in hand, given over always to the discipline of meditation, taking refuge in dispassion; freed of the sense of "I," of force and pride, of desire and anger and possession, no longer calling anything his own, at peace — such a man is ready to become one with Brahman. And having become Brahman, serene in himself, he no longer grieves and no longer craves; looking on all beings alike, he comes into the highest love for me. By that love he comes to know me as I truly am, how vast I am and who; and knowing me in truth, he enters into me at once. Even while he goes on doing all his work, the man who takes refuge in me reaches by my grace the place that is eternal and unchanging. So in your heart surrender all your actions to me, hold me as the highest, and keep your mind always fixed on me through the discipline of understanding. With your thoughts on me, you will pass by my grace over every hard place; but if out of pride you will not listen, you will be lost. If, leaning on your own self-will, you tell yourself "I will not fight," your resolve is empty — your own nature will drive you to it. Arjuna, the very work you now refuse out of confusion you will do in spite of yourself, bound by the nature that is born in you. The Lord dwells in the heart of every creature, and by his power he turns them all, as though they were mounted on a wheel and spun around. With your whole being take refuge in him, Arjuna; by his grace you will come to the highest peace, the everlasting home.

So I have unfolded for you this wisdom, the most secret of all secrets. Weigh it fully in your heart, and then do as you choose.

But hear once more my final word, the deepest secret of them all. Because you are dearly loved by me, I will tell you what is for your good. Fix your mind on me, give me your love, offer your worship to me, bow down before me; so you shall come to me. This I promise you truly, for you are dear to me.

Abandon all duties and come to me alone for refuge. I will set you free from every sin; do not grieve.

This teaching is never to be spoken to one who has no discipline in him, no love, no wish to hear, who mocks at me. But whoever passes on this highest secret to those who love me, offering me that supreme devotion, comes to me beyond all doubt; and no one among men does anything dearer to me than that, nor will any on earth ever be dearer to me than he. And whoever studies this sacred conversation of ours, I count it as though he had worshipped me with the offering of wisdom. And even the one who only listens to it, with faith and without scorn — he too is set free, and reaches the bright worlds of those whose deeds are pure.

Have you heard all this, Arjuna, with your mind held steady upon it? Has the confusion that came of your ignorance been driven out?

Arjuna said: My confusion is gone. By your grace, Krishna, I have come back to myself. I stand here now with my doubts dispelled, firm at last. I will do as you say.

Sanjaya said: So I have heard this conversation between Krishna and the great-souled Arjuna — a thing so wondrous that the hair stands up on my body. By the grace of Vyasa I have heard this highest secret, this Yoga, told by Krishna, the lord of Yoga, with my own ears, from his own mouth. And as I call to mind, O King, again and again, this marvelous and holy conversation between Krishna and Arjuna, I rejoice over and over. And as I remember, again and again, that most marvelous form of Krishna, my wonder is great, O King, and I rejoice again and again.

Wherever Krishna is, the lord of Yoga, and wherever Arjuna stands, the bearer of the bow — there, I am certain, will be fortune, victory, prosperity, and the steadfast right.
