The Iliad, Book XIII

Poseidon at the Ships

Book XIII13 of 24

Now that Zeus had brought Hector and the Trojans up to the ships, he left them there to their endless toil and trouble and turned his bright eyes away, gazing off into the distance — toward the horse-breeding Thracians, the Mysians who fight hand to hand, the proud Hippemolgi, who live on milk, and the Abians, the justest men alive. He no longer turned so much as a glance toward Troy, for it never crossed his mind that any of the immortals would go down to help the Trojans or the Greeks.

But lord Poseidon had not been keeping a blind watch. He sat marveling at the battle from the highest wooded crest of Samothrace, and from there he could see the whole of Ida, and Priam’s city, and the Greek ships. He had come up out of the sea and taken his seat there, for he pitied the Greeks as the Trojans beat them down, and his anger at Zeus burned in him.

Down he came at once from the mountaintop, striding hard, and the high hills and the forest shook under the tread of his immortal feet. Three strides he took, and with the fourth he reached his goal — Aegae, where his glittering golden palace stands, deep in the sea, imperishable. There he yoked his swift bronze-hoofed horses, their golden manes streaming, and clothed himself in gold, and took up his golden whip, well made, and mounted his chariot. He drove out over the waves, and the sea-creatures came gamboling up around him from every hollow of the deep, for they knew their lord; and the sea parted in gladness to open a path before him. The horses flew so lightly that the bronze axle below was never wetted, and his bounding team carried him on to the ships of the Greeks.

There is a great cave in the depths of the sea, midway between Tenedos and rocky Imbros. There Poseidon, shaker of the earth, halted his horses and unyoked them, and set ambrosial fodder before them. He hobbled their feet with golden hobbles, which none could loose or break, so they would wait for their lord until he came back. Then he went his way to the Greek host.

Meanwhile the Trojans came on after Hector, son of Priam, in a packed mass, like a storm-cloud or a sheet of flame, raising the war cry, fighting with all their might — sure that they would take the Greek ships now and kill the best of the men there beside them. But Poseidon, who holds the earth and shakes it, came up out of the sea to rouse the Greeks, taking on the form and the voice of Calchas.

He spoke first to the two Ajaxes, who were already straining at the work: “You two, you Ajaxes, you can be the saving of the Greeks if you put your whole strength into it and don’t give way to fear. Anywhere else along the wall I’m not afraid the Trojans will break us, even though they’ve come over it in force — the Greeks will hold all the rest of them. But here is where I dread some ruin — here, where furious Hector leads them on like a pillar of fire, Hector who boasts he is the son of mighty Zeus. So may some god put it in your hearts to stand firm here yourselves and to steady the others. If you do, you can drive him back from the fast ships, however hard Zeus himself urges him on.”

As he spoke, the lord who shakes the earth touched both of them with his staff and filled them with daring strength, and made their limbs light — their feet, their hands above them. Then, like a swift hawk that lifts from a sheer high rock and stoops to chase a bird across the plain, the earth-shaker rose into the air and flew from them. Swift Ajax, son of Oileus, was the first of the two to know him, and he said to Ajax, son of Telamon: “Ajax, that was one of the gods who hold Olympus, taking the shape of the prophet and telling us to fight by our ships. That was not Calchas, the seer who reads the birds — I knew him as he turned to go, by the look of his feet and his legs from behind. The gods are easy to recognize. And now my own heart in me burns harder for battle, and my hands and feet below are itching for the fight.”

Ajax, son of Telamon, answered: “My hands too — they grip the spear-shaft tighter now, my strength is rising in me, and my feet feel quick beneath me. I long to meet furious Hector, son of Priam, even alone, one against one.”

So the two of them talked, glad of the lust for battle the god had put in them. But the earth-shaker, behind them, was rousing the rest of the Greeks, who were standing back by the fast ships, resting, worn out at once with the hard fighting and with grief at the sight of the Trojans over the wall in force. They watched, and the tears welled in their eyes, for they did not think they would come out of it alive. But the shaker of the earth went lightly among them and set their ranks in motion toward the front.

First he came to Teucer and Leitus, to the hero Peneleos, to Thoas and Deipyrus, and to Meriones and Antilochus, brave men in the war cry — to all of these he called sharp words: “Shame on you, young men of Greece! It was on your fighting that I counted to save our ships. If you flinch from the hard work of war, the day has come that sees us beaten by the Trojans. This is a sight I never thought to see — a great and terrible thing — the Trojans at our very ships. Until now they were like frightened does, drifting through the woods with no fight in them, prey for jackals and leopards and wolves, good for nothing but running. Until now the Trojans never once dared to stand and face the Greeks. But now they have come far from their city and are fighting among our ships, all through our leader’s failure and the slackness of the men, who in their grudge against him will not fight to save the ships, but are cut down beside them. Yes, the fault may lie with the hero Agamemnon, son of Atreus, who dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus — but that is no excuse for us to drop out of the fighting. Heal it, and quickly; the hearts of good men mend fast. You do wrong to let your courage go cold, you, the best soldiers in the whole army. I would not pick a quarrel with a man who hung back from the war because he was weak — but with you I am furious. Friends, this slackness will soon make a worse thing of it. Let each of you take to heart his own honor, his own good name; the struggle now is at its sharpest. Great Hector is fighting at the ships, full-voiced; he has burst the gates and the long bolt that held them.”

So the earth-encircler called to the Greeks and drove them on. Around the two Ajaxes the ranks closed up, strong companies that not even Ares could have faulted, going among them, nor Athena who marshals armies — for these were the picked men, waiting for Hector and the Trojans. They made a living wall, spear braced to spear, shield to shield, edge to overlapping edge, helmet to helmet, man to man. The horsehair crests on the gleaming helmet-ridges brushed together as the heads bent forward, so close they stood; the spears, brandished in bold hands, crossed and locked; and their hearts were set straight ahead, on battle.

The Trojans came on in a dense body, with Hector at their head, driving straight in — like a boulder that crashes down a mountainside, torn from the brow of a cliff by a winter flood that has eaten away the rock that held it: it leaps and bounds, and the forest roars beneath it, and on it goes, swerving for nothing, until it hits the level ground, and there for all its fury it rolls no farther. So Hector, for a while, threatened to cut his way clear through the Greek huts and ships to the sea, killing as he came — until he ran up against the close-packed ranks, and there he stopped short. The sons of the Greeks stood and met him, stabbing with swords and double-pointed spears, and thrust him back so that he reeled and gave ground. Then he raised his voice and shouted to the Trojans: “Trojans! Lycians! Dardanians who fight hand to hand! Hold your ground! The Greeks won’t keep me off for long, packed against me like a wall though they are. They’ll fall back before my spear, if the greatest of the gods has truly set me on — Hera’s loud-thundering husband.”

With that he put fresh heart and fire into every man. And Deiphobus, son of Priam, strode out among them, high in his own daring, his round shield held before him, moving light and quick behind its cover. Meriones took aim at him and let his bright spear fly, and did not miss — he struck the broad orb of bull’s hide — but the bronze did not break through; the long shaft snapped clean in two before it could, for Deiphobus had seen it coming and held the shield well out from his body. Furious — at losing both the kill and his spear — Meriones drew back under cover of his own men, and went off toward the huts and the ships to fetch another spear, one he had left behind.

The rest fought on, and the war cry rose to the sky. Teucer, son of Telamon, was the first to kill his man — the spearman Imbrius, son of Mentor, rich in horses. He had lived in Pedaeum before the Greeks came, and had married Medesicaste, a daughter of Priam’s, born out of wedlock; but when the curved ships of the Greeks arrived, he came back to Ilios, and he was a great man among the Trojans, and lived close to Priam, who honored him like one of his own sons. The son of Telamon struck him below the ear with the long spear, then wrenched it free, and Imbrius fell — like an ash tree on the crown of a high hill, seen far off, cut down by the bronze, that brings its soft green leaves to the ground. So he fell, and his armor of bright bronze rang harshly around him. Teucer sprang forward to strip it, but as he came on, Hector cast his bright spear at him. Teucer saw it and just managed to dodge aside, and the spear caught Amphimachus instead — son of Cteatus, the son of Actor — full in the chest as he came into the fighting, and he crashed down, and his armor clattered around him. Hector rushed in to tear the close-fitting helmet from the temples of great-hearted Amphimachus, but Ajax lunged with his shining spear at Hector — though he could not reach the flesh, sheathed as it was in fearsome bronze; he struck the boss of the shield and shoved him back by main force from the two bodies, and the Greeks dragged them out. Stichius and Menestheus, captains of the Athenians, carried Amphimachus back to the Greek host; and the two Ajaxes, hot for the fight, took up Imbrius. As two lions snatch a goat from sharp-toothed hounds and carry it off through the thick brush, holding it high in their jaws above the ground, so the two armed Ajaxes held Imbrius high and stripped his armor. And the son of Oileus, in anger for the death of Amphimachus, hacked the head from the soft neck and sent it spinning through the crowd like a ball, and it rolled in the dust to fall at Hector’s feet.

Then Poseidon’s heart blazed with anger that his grandson had fallen in the cruel fighting, and he went off among the huts and ships of the Greeks to spur the men on still more, and to set trouble brewing for the Trojans. Idomeneus met him, coming away from a comrade who had just left the fighting, struck behind the knee by the sharp bronze; his men had carried him in, and Idomeneus, after giving orders to the healers, had gone on toward his own hut, for he was still hungry for war. The mighty shaker of the earth spoke to him, taking on the voice of Thoas, son of Andraemon — who ruled the Aetolians in all Pleuron and steep Calydon, and was honored among his people like a god: “Idomeneus, lawgiver of the Cretans — what has become now of the threats the sons of the Greeks once made against the Trojans?”

Idomeneus, lord of the Cretans, answered him: “Thoas, no one is at fault, so far as I can see — we all know how to fight. No man is gripped by helpless fear, and no man hangs back out of slackness. It must be the will of almighty Zeus himself, that the Greeks should die here without glory, far from Argos. But you, Thoas, were always one to stand firm, and to rally any man you saw giving way. Don’t let up now. Call on every man to do his utmost.”

Poseidon, the shaker of the earth, answered: “Idomeneus — may the man never come home from Troy, but stay here to feast the dogs, who willingly holds back from the fight this day. Come, take up your armor and go. We must hurry, the two of us together, in case we can do some good, even though we are only two. Courage comes even to cowards when they stand together, and you and I know how to fight beside the best.” With that the god went back into the toil of men. And Idomeneus, when he reached his well-built hut, pulled on his fine armor, took up two spears, and went out — like the lightning the son of Cronus catches up from gleaming Olympus to show a sign to mortals, and the flash leaps far and bright. So the bronze blazed about his chest as he ran. And Meriones, his sturdy squire, met him still close to the hut — he was going for the bronze spear he meant to fetch — and mighty Idomeneus said to him:

“Meriones, swift son of Molus, dearest of my companions — why have you come away from the war and the fighting? Are you hurt? Is a spear-point’s pain bearing down on you? Or have you come with some message for me? I have no wish to sit in my hut; I would far rather fight.”

Meriones, the cool-headed, answered: “Idomeneus, lawgiver of the bronze-armored Cretans — I am coming for a spear, if there is one left in your hut to take. I broke the one I had, throwing it against the shield of arrogant Deiphobus.”

Idomeneus, lord of the Cretans, answered: “You will find spears, if you want them — one, or twenty — standing against the bright wall inside my hut. I took them from Trojans I have killed; I am not a man to fight my enemies from a distance. So I have spears, and bossed shields, and helmets, and breastplates polished to a shine.”

Then Meriones, the cool-headed, said: “In my own hut and by my ship I too have plenty of Trojan spoils, but they are not close to hand. I tell you, I am not one to forget my courage. I stand in the front ranks, where men win glory, whenever the fighting of war breaks out. Some other Greek may not know how I fight — but you, you know it well enough.”

Idomeneus, lord of the Cretans, answered: “I know what you are worth. You needn’t say it. If the best of us were being picked now, beside the ships, for an ambush — and there is no better test of what a man is made of, the place where the brave man and the coward both stand revealed — the coward’s color keeps changing, this way and that; his heart will not let him sit still, but he keeps shifting his weight from one foot to the other; his heart pounds in his chest as he broods on death, and his teeth chatter in his head. But the brave man’s color does not change, and once he has settled into the ambush, he is not afraid; he only prays for the moment to plunge into the bitter work. In a place like that, no one could find fault with your courage or your hands. And if you were hit, in the toil of war, by a thrown spear or a thrust at close quarters, it would not strike you from behind, in the neck or the back — it would catch you full in the chest or the belly, as you pressed in among the foremost fighters. But come, let’s not stand here talking like children — someone might take it the wrong way. Go to the hut and get yourself a strong spear.”

At that, Meriones, the equal of swift Ares, went and quickly took a bronze spear from the hut, and went after Idomeneus, his whole mind set on war — like Ares himself, the bane of men, going out to battle, with Terror at his side, his own dear son, strong and fearless, who frightens even the steadfast warrior: the two of them arm and stride out of Thrace to the Ephyrians, or to the great-hearted Phlegyans, and they will not hear the prayers of both sides at once, but give the glory to one. So Meriones and Idomeneus, leaders of men, went out to the fighting in their gleaming bronze. And Meriones spoke first: “Son of Deucalion, where would you have us go into the fighting? On the right of the whole host? In the center? Or to the left? — for nowhere, I think, are the long-haired Greeks so hard pressed as there.”

Idomeneus, lord of the Cretans, answered: “There are others to hold the center — the two Ajaxes, and Teucer, who is the finest archer of all the Greeks, and good as well in close fighting. They will give Hector enough of a fight, hungry for it as he is. He’ll find it hard work, however he strains for the killing, to break their unbeatable hands and their fury and set the ships afire — unless the son of Cronus himself drops a blazing brand on the fast ships with his own hand. Great Ajax, son of Telamon, will give way to no man who is mortal and eats the grain of Demeter, no man bronze and great stones can break. He would not even step back before Achilles, who shatters the ranks — not in standing fight; in speed of foot there’s no matching Achilles. So steer us to the left of the army, and let’s find out, and soon, whether we win glory from another, or another from us.”

So Meriones, the equal of swift Ares, led the way to the part of the host that Idomeneus had named. And when the Trojans saw Idomeneus coming on like a flame, him and his squire in their finely worked armor, they raised the cry and made for him in one body, and a furious fight broke out at the ships’ sterns, all together. As gusting winds shriek across the land on a day when the dust lies thick on the roads, and they whip it up into a great cloud — so the battle joined and clashed together, and every man’s heart was set on killing his enemy in the press. The field of slaughter bristled with the long flesh-tearing spears they held, and the eyes ached with the glare of bronze from the bright helmets, the new-burnished breastplates, the shining shields, as the men closed in. Hard-hearted indeed would the man be who could look on that struggle and feel joy and no dread.

So the two mighty sons of Cronus, set against each other, were forging hard fortune for the heroes of men. Zeus willed victory for the Trojans and for Hector, to glorify swift-footed Achilles — yet he did not mean to destroy the Greek host utterly before Troy; he only wanted to lift up Thetis and her strong-hearted son. Poseidon, for his part, slipped up secretly out of the gray sea and went among the Greeks to drive them on, grieved that the Trojans were beating them, and his anger at Zeus burning. The two of them were of one stock and one fathering, but Zeus was the elder-born and knew the more. So Poseidon held back from helping the Greeks in the open, and instead, always in the likeness of a man, kept stirring them through the host. So the two of them stretched taut over both armies the rope of fierce strife and the leveling war — a knot none could break or loose, that buckled the knees of many men.

And now Idomeneus, though his hair was already shot with gray, called to the Greeks and leapt in among the Trojans and sent them scattering in fear. He killed Othryoneus, a man from Cabesus who had been living among them, who had only lately come for the war’s sake. He had asked for the loveliest of Priam’s daughters, Cassandra — and offered no gifts for her, but a great promise: he would drive the sons of the Greeks out of Troy whether they liked it or not. Old Priam had agreed and pledged her to him, and so the man fought on, trusting that promise. Idomeneus took aim with his bright spear and struck him as he came striding on, and the bronze corselet he wore did not hold; the spear stood fixed in his belly, and he crashed down. And Idomeneus boasted over him: “Othryoneus — I’ll honor you above all men in the world, if you really mean to do everything you promised Priam, of the line of Dardanus, who pledged you his daughter. Come, we’ll make you a promise too, and keep it: we’ll give you the loveliest of the daughters of the son of Atreus, and bring her here from Argos for you to wed — if only you’ll join us in sacking the strong city of Ilios. Come along, then, to the seafaring ships, and let’s settle the marriage. You’ll find us no hard bargainers over the bride-gifts.” And with that the hero Idomeneus began dragging him by the foot through the thick of the fighting.

But Asius came up to defend the body, on foot, in front of his horses, which his charioteer drove so close behind that their breath was forever on his shoulders. His heart was set on striking Idomeneus down — but Idomeneus was quicker, and stabbed him with the spear in the throat, under the chin, and drove the bronze straight through. He fell as an oak falls, or a poplar, or a tall pine that carpenters fell on the mountains with their whetted axes to make ship-timber: so he lay there, full length, before his horses and his chariot, groaning, clutching at the bloody dust. The charioteer lost his wits and had not the nerve to wheel the horses round and escape, and steadfast Antilochus caught him full in the body with a spear; the bronze corselet he wore did not hold, and the spear stood fixed in his belly. He fell gasping from the well-built chariot, and Antilochus, son of great-hearted Nestor, drove the horses off, out of the Trojans, and in among the Greeks.

Then Deiphobus came in close on Idomeneus, grieving for Asius, and let his bright spear fly. But Idomeneus saw it and dodged the bronze, ducking under his round shield — the one he always carried, made of bull’s hide and gleaming bronze, fitted with two arm-rods on the inside. He crouched low behind it all, and the spear flew over him, but the shield rang dryly as it grazed past; and still it did not leave the heavy hand of Deiphobus for nothing — it struck Hypsenor, son of Hippasus, shepherd of his people, in the liver below the midriff, and at once his knees gave under him. And Deiphobus boasted terribly over him, in a great voice: “There — Asius does not lie unavenged after all. Even on his way down to the strong gatekeeper, Hades, his heart will be glad: I have given him an escort.” So he boasted, and the grief of it stung the Greeks — and most of all it stirred the heart of warlike Antilochus. Yet for all his grief he did not forget his comrade, but ran and stood astride him and covered him with his shield, while two trusted friends, Mecisteus, son of Echius, and noble Alastor, stooped under and bore him away, groaning heavily, to the hollow ships.

But Idomeneus did not slack his fury. He kept straining, always, either to shroud some Trojan in the dark of death or to go down himself, holding ruin off from the Greeks. And next there fell Alcathous, the son of lordly Aesyetes — he was son-in-law to Anchises, having married his eldest daughter, Hippodameia, whom her father and her honored mother loved beyond all the children in the house, for she surpassed everyone her age in beauty, in skill, and in good sense; and so the best man in all wide Troy had taken her to wife. Now Poseidon brought him low under the hand of Idomeneus, casting a fog over his bright eyes and binding fast his shining limbs, so that he could not run back, nor swerve aside, but stood stock-still as a pillar or a high-crowned tree — and Idomeneus drove the spear straight into the middle of his chest, and split the bronze plate of the corselet that until then had warded death from his body. It rang harshly now, torn through by the spear. He fell with a crash, and the spear stood fixed in his heart, which still beat on, so that the butt of the spear quivered with it — until at last mighty Ares stilled its force. And Idomeneus boasted over him, terribly, in a great voice: “Deiphobus — are we even now, would you say, three men killed for your one? You did your share of boasting. Madman, come stand and face me yourself, and learn what kind of son of Zeus I am, come here against you. Zeus first fathered Minos, the warden of Crete; and Minos fathered a son, the blameless Deucalion; and Deucalion fathered me, to be lord over many men in broad Crete; and now my ships have carried me here, to be a curse to you, and to your father, and to all the Trojans.”

So he spoke, and Deiphobus was torn two ways — whether to draw back and bring up some other great-hearted Trojan as a partner, or to try it alone. As he weighed it, this seemed the better course: to go for Aeneas. He found him standing at the very rear of the fighting, for Aeneas was forever angry with noble Priam, who gave him no honor among the men, for all his courage. Deiphobus stood close and spoke winged words: “Aeneas, counselor of the Trojans — now is the time, if any tie of kin can touch you, to come defend your sister’s husband. Follow me, and let us protect Alcathous, who was your sister’s husband, and who raised you when you were small in his halls. Now Idomeneus, the famous spearman, has killed him.”

So he spoke, and stirred the heart in Aeneas’s chest. He went for Idomeneus, his whole mind set on war. But Idomeneus did not take to his heels like a frightened boy; he stood his ground, like a boar in the mountains, sure of his own strength, that waits for a great crowd of men coming on against him in a lonely place: the bristles rise along his back, his eyes blaze with fire, and he whets his tusks, raging to fight off the dogs and the men. So Idomeneus, the famous spearman, stood and did not give way as Aeneas came on. But he called to his companions — to Ascalaphus, Aphareus, Deipyrus, Meriones, and Antilochus, all of them masters of the war cry — and urged them on with winged words: “Here, friends! Stand by me — I’m alone, and badly afraid of swift-footed Aeneas, bearing down on me, who is a great killer of men in battle, and in the very flower of his youth, when a man’s strength is at its height. If we were the same age, with the spirit I have now, soon he or I would carry off the great prize.”

So he spoke, and they all, as one, came and stood near him, shields tilted to their shoulders. And on the other side Aeneas called to his own — to Deiphobus, to Paris, and to noble Agenor, who were leaders of the Trojans with him; and the people followed them, as sheep follow the ram out of the pasture down to their drinking, and the shepherd’s heart is glad. So the heart of Aeneas was glad in his chest when he saw the people streaming after him.

Then they fell on one another in close fight around Alcathous, with their long spears, and the bronze rang fearfully on their bodies as they aimed at each other in the press. And two warriors beyond all the rest, Aeneas and Idomeneus, both the equals of Ares, strained to tear each other’s flesh with the pitiless bronze. Aeneas threw first at Idomeneus, but Idomeneus saw it coming and dodged the bronze, and the spear of Aeneas drove on past and stood quivering in the ground, flung for nothing from that strong hand. But Idomeneus struck Oenomaus full in the belly, and broke the plate of the corselet, and the bronze let the guts spill out, and he fell in the dust and clawed the earth in his hands. Idomeneus pulled the long spear from the body, but could not strip the fine armor from his shoulders — the missiles were raining too thick. And his legs no longer had the spring in them, after the strain, to dart after his own cast spear or to swerve from another’s. So while he still fought off the pitiless day at close quarters, his feet would no longer carry him swiftly out of the fighting. As he drew slowly back, Deiphobus cast at him again — for he kept his hard hatred for the man — and missed him again, but hit Ascalaphus, the son of Ares; the heavy spear went through his shoulder, and he fell in the dust and clawed the earth in his hands.

And mighty loud-voiced Ares did not yet know that his son had fallen in the hard fighting; he was sitting up on the heights of Olympus, under the golden clouds, held back by the will of Zeus, where the other immortal gods were also held from the war. Around the body the men fought on, hand to hand. Deiphobus tore the bright helmet off Ascalaphus, but Meriones, the equal of swift Ares, sprang in and struck his arm with a spear, so that the crested helmet dropped ringing from his grip to the ground. Meriones swooped on him again like a vulture, dragged the heavy spear out of the top of his arm, and fell back among his own men. Then Polites, Deiphobus’s own brother, threw his arms around his waist and brought him out of the wailing war until he reached his swift horses, which were standing behind the toil of the fight with their charioteer and the gleaming chariot. These carried him toward the city, groaning hard, racked with pain, and the blood ran from his fresh-wounded arm.

The others fought on, and the war cry rose without end. Aeneas lunged at Aphareus, son of Caletor, and struck his throat as he turned toward him with the sharp spear; his head dropped to one side, and his shield came down with him, and his helmet, and death that breaks the spirit poured around him. Antilochus watched Thoon turn, and rushed him, and slashed him as he wheeled, cutting clean through the vein that runs all the way up the back to the neck. He severed it entire, and Thoon went down on his back in the dust, stretching both hands out to his dear companions. Antilochus sprang on him and stripped the armor from his shoulders, glancing about him as he did — and the Trojans came at him from every side and struck his broad bright shield, but could not lay open his tender flesh with the pitiless bronze, for Poseidon, the shaker of the earth, guarded Nestor’s son even in the storm of spears. He was never clear of the enemy but kept turning among them; his spear was never still, but always shaking, always poised, his mind set on hurling it at some man far off or rushing him hand to hand.

But as he aimed across the press, Adamas, the son of Asius, did not miss him; he closed in and struck the middle of his shield with the sharp bronze. But Poseidon, the dark-haired god, robbed the spear of its force, grudging him the life of Antilochus. Half the shaft stuck fast in Antilochus’s shield, like a fire-hardened stake, and half lay on the ground. Adamas drew back among his own men to escape his doom, but Meriones came after him and threw, and caught him with the spear midway between the navel and the groin, where wounding is most cruel for wretched mortals at war. There the spear went home, and Adamas convulsed about it as a bull twists when the herdsmen in the hills have bound it with cords against its will and drag it off. So he writhed when he was struck — but not for long, only until the hero Meriones came up and pulled the spear from his flesh, and the darkness closed over his eyes.

Then Helenus came in close and struck Deipyrus on the temple with a great Thracian sword, and tore the helmet off, and it fell to the ground, and one of the Greeks fighting there caught it up as it rolled between his feet. And the dark of death closed over the eyes of Deipyrus. Grief seized Menelaus, good at the war cry, and he made for the warrior Helenus, brandishing his sharp spear with a threat; and Helenus drew his bow. The two came on together — the one to hurl the long spear, the other to loose an arrow from the string. The son of Priam hit the breastplate of Menelaus on the chest, but the bitter arrow glanced off. As black beans or chickpeas leap from a broad winnowing-shovel on the great threshing floor, driven by the whistling wind and the heave of the winnower, so the bitter arrow glanced far off the breastplate of glorious Menelaus. But Menelaus, good at the war cry, struck the hand that held the polished bow, and the bronze spear drove on through the hand and into the bow itself. Helenus shrank back among his own men to escape his doom, his hand hanging at his side, dragging the ashwood spear along with it — until great-hearted Agenor drew the spear out of his hand and bound the hand up in a sling of well-spun wool that his squire carried for him, the shepherd of the people.

Then Pisander came straight at glorious Menelaus — his evil fate leading him on toward death’s end, to be beaten down by you, Menelaus, in the dread crash of war. When the two had come up close against each other, the son of Atreus missed his throw, the spear turning aside; and Pisander struck the shield of glorious Menelaus, but could not drive the bronze clean through, for the broad shield checked it, and the shaft snapped at the socket. Yet his heart leapt, and he hoped for the kill. But the son of Atreus drew his silver-studded sword and sprang on him. And Pisander, from behind his shield, took up a fine axe of good bronze on a long, smooth handle of olive wood, and the two came together. Pisander struck the ridge of the crest on Menelaus’s helmet, just under the plume itself; and Menelaus struck Pisander on the forehead as he came on, above the nose, and the bones cracked, and the two eyes, dripping blood, dropped in the dust at his feet. He doubled over and fell, and Menelaus set his heel on his chest, stripped the armor, and boasted over him: “This is how you will leave the ships of the fast-horsed Greeks at last — you arrogant Trojans, who can never get your fill of the dread war cry. Nor will you go without the rest of the shame and the outrage you heaped on me, you cowardly dogs, who felt no fear in your hearts of the hard anger of Zeus, who thunders on high, the lord of host and guest — Zeus, who will one day bring your steep city down in ruin. You carried off my wedded wife, and a heap of treasure with her, for nothing, when she had made you welcome. And now you are mad to fling deadly fire on the seafaring ships and kill the Greek heroes. But you will be stopped from war, however hungry you are for it. Father Zeus — they say you surpass all others, gods and men alike, in wisdom, and all this flows from you — how can you favor men like these, lawless men, the Trojans, whose fury never tires, who can never get their fill of the leveling war? A man can take his fill of all things — of sleep, of love, of sweet song and the faultless dance, things a man would sooner take his fill of than of war. But the Trojans are insatiable for battle.”

So saying, noble Menelaus stripped the bloody armor from the body and handed it to his men, and went back himself to mix among the foremost fighters. There Harpalion, the son of King Pylaemenes, came at him — he had followed his dear father to the war at Troy, and he never went home again. He struck the middle of Menelaus’s shield from close in with his spear, but could not drive the bronze through, and drew back among his own men to escape his doom, glancing about on every side in case the bronze should graze him. But Meriones loosed a bronze-tipped arrow after him as he went, and hit him in the right buttock; the arrow pierced clean through, under the bone, and on into the bladder. He sat down where he was, in the arms of his dear companions, breathing his life out, stretched on the earth like a worm; and the dark blood ran out and soaked the ground. The great-hearted Paphlagonians tended him, lifted him into the chariot, and bore him, grieving, to holy Ilios; and his father went with them, weeping — but there was no blood-price that could pay for his dead son.

Paris’s heart was bitter at the death of Harpalion, for he had been his host among the many Paphlagonians. In his anger for him he let an arrow fly. Now there was a man named Euchenor, son of Polyidus the seer, a rich man and a brave one, who had his home in Corinth. He had taken ship for Troy knowing well his own ruin, for his good old father Polyidus had told him again and again that he must either die in his own halls of a wretched sickness, or be killed by the Trojans among the ships of the Greeks. So he chose to avoid both the Greeks’ heavy fine for staying home and the hateful pain of disease. Paris struck him below the jaw, under the ear, and the life fled fast from his limbs, and the hateful dark took him.

So they fought on, like blazing fire. But Hector, beloved of Zeus, had not yet heard, and did not know, that off to the left of the ships his own men were being cut down by the Greeks — for the glory was very nearly turning to the Greeks, so hard did the earth-encircler urge them on, and lend them his own strength besides. No, Hector held on where he had first burst through the gates and the wall, breaking the close ranks of the Greek shield-bearers. There, where the ships of Ajax and of Protesilaus were drawn up on the gray sea’s shore, the wall was built lowest, and there the fighting of men and horses raged at its fiercest. The Boeotians and the long-robed Ionians, the Locrians, the men of Phthia, and the bright Epeians could barely hold Hector off from the ships, and could not drive him back, blazing as he was like fire. The picked men of the Athenians held the front, led by Menestheus, son of Peteos, and with him Pheidas, Stichius, and brave Bias; Meges, son of Phyleus, with Amphion and Dracius, led the Epeians; and Medon and steadfast Podarces led the men of Phthia. Medon was a bastard son of noble Oileus and a brother of Ajax, but he lived in Phylace, far from his own land, for he had killed a kinsman of his stepmother Eriopis, the wife of Oileus; and Podarces was the son of Iphiclus, son of Phylacus. These two stood armed in the front of the great-hearted men of Phthia, fighting beside the Boeotians to defend the ships.

And swift Ajax, son of Oileus, would not leave the side of Ajax, son of Telamon — not for a moment. As two dark-coated oxen strain with one heart to draw the jointed plow through a fallow field, and the sweat wells up at the roots of their horns, and only the polished yoke holds them apart as they break the furrow down to the field’s edge — so the two stood close, shoulder to shoulder. Many good companions followed the son of Telamon, ready to take his great shield from him whenever the toil and the sweat reached his knees. But the Locrians did not follow the great-hearted son of Oileus, for their hearts were no good for standing close in the fight: they had no bronze helmets crested with horsehair, no round shields, no ashwood spears. They had come to Ilios trusting in their bows and in slings of well-twisted wool, and with these they shot fast and broke the Trojan ranks. So while the men in front, in their heavy armor, bore the brunt against the Trojans and against bronze-helmeted Hector, the Locrians shot from behind, under cover, unseen; and the Trojans began to lose all heart for the fight, for the arrows threw them into confusion.

Then the Trojans would have been driven, in misery, back from the ships and the huts to windy Ilios, had not Polydamas come and stood by bold Hector and said: “Hector, you are an impossible man to win over with reason. Because a god has made you supreme in the work of war, you think you must therefore outdo everyone in counsel too. But you cannot take everything for yourself at once. To one man a god gives the work of war; to another, dancing; to another, the lyre and song; and in the heart of yet another, far-seeing Zeus sets a good mind, and many men reap the good of it, and many are saved by it, and the man himself knows it best of all. So I’ll say what seems best to me. The ring of war blazes around you everywhere. Our own great-hearted Trojans, now they are over the wall, some are standing apart in their armor, and the rest are fighting scattered, few against many, strung out among the ships. Draw back, then, and call all our best leaders here, and let us take counsel together on the whole matter — whether to fall on the many-benched ships, in the hope that a god will hand us the victory, or to pull back from the ships unharmed while we still can. For I am badly afraid the Greeks will pay back yesterday’s debt in full. There is a man waiting beside their ships, insatiable for war, and I think he will not hold off from the fighting much longer.”

So Polydamas spoke, and his safe counsel pleased Hector. At once he leapt to the ground from his chariot in his armor and spoke winged words: “Polydamas, keep all the best men here with you; I will go over there into the fighting, and come straight back once I have given them their orders.” So he spoke, and away he went, towering like a snowy mountain, shouting, and flew through the Trojans and their allies. And they all hurried toward kind Polydamas, son of Panthous, when they heard Hector’s voice. But Hector kept on through the foremost fighters, hunting for Deiphobus and for the strong prince Helenus, for Adamas, son of Asius, and Asius, son of Hyrtacus — to find them, if he could, alive and unhurt. But he could not. Some were lying dead at the sterns of the Greek ships, struck down by Greek hands; and others were back inside the wall, wounded, hit by arrow or by spear. But on the left of the wailing battle he found one — Alexandros, the husband of lovely-haired Helen — heartening his companions and rousing them to fight. He came up to him and reproached him with shaming words: “Cursed Paris, fine to look at, woman-mad seducer — where are Deiphobus and the strong prince Helenus, and Adamas, son of Asius, and Asius, son of Hyrtacus? Where is Othryoneus? Now all steep Ilios is falling from its height — now is your own headlong ruin sure.”

Godlike Alexandros answered him: “Hector, since your heart is set on blaming a man who deserves no blame — there may be other days when I hold back from the fighting, but not today: my mother bore me no coward. From the moment you set our companions on around the ships, we have stood here and never let up against the Greeks. The friends you ask after are dead. Only Deiphobus and the strong prince Helenus have left the field, both struck through the hand by the long spears — and the son of Cronus warded off their death. Now lead on, wherever your heart and spirit command, and we will follow you eagerly. I tell you, you will not find us short of courage, as far as our strength holds out — and no man can fight beyond his strength, however willing he is.”

So the hero spoke, and turned his brother’s heart. They went to where the war and the slaughter were thickest — to Cebriones and faultless Polydamas, to Phalces and Orthaeus and godlike Polyphetes, to Palmys and Ascanius and Morys, son of Hippotion, who had come the day before, in turn, from deep-soiled Ascania, as a relief; and now Zeus stirred them to the fight. They came on like a blast of furious winds that strikes the earth under the thunder of Father Zeus, and dives into the salt sea with a roar, and many are the seething waves all along the sounding water, arching, white with foam, one after another in front of the rest. So the Trojans drew up rank behind rank, gleaming with bronze, and followed their leaders. And Hector, son of Priam, led them, the equal of Ares, the bane of men, his round shield before him, packed thick with hides and beaten bronze, and the bright helmet shaking on his temples. He kept testing the ranks on every side, stepping out from behind his shield to see whether they would give way before him — but he could not break the steady heart in the Greeks’ chests. And Ajax, striding far ahead, was the first to challenge him: “You madman, come closer. Why try to frighten the Greeks for nothing? We are not unskilled in war — it is only the cruel lash of Zeus that has beaten us down. Your heart is set, I’m sure, on burning our ships. But we too have hands ready to defend them — and your own well-built city will fall, taken and sacked by our hands, far sooner than that. The day is near, I tell you, when you yourself will be running, and praying to Father Zeus and the rest of the immortals that your fine-maned horses be swifter than hawks, as they raise the dust and carry you back to the city.”

As he spoke, a bird flew past him on the right, an eagle, high up; and the host of the Greeks shouted, heartened by the omen. But glorious Hector answered: “Ajax, you blundering boaster — what nonsense you talk. I wish I were as sure of being, all my days, the son of Zeus who carries the storm-shield, born of lady Hera, and held in honor like Athena and Apollo, as I am that this day brings ruin to the Greeks — to all of them — and you among them, if you have the nerve to stand against my long spear. It will tear your lily-soft skin, and you will glut the dogs and birds of Troy with your fat and your flesh, struck down beside the Greek ships.”

So he spoke and led the way, and they followed him with an unearthly cry, and the whole host roared behind them. And the Greeks raised their own shout in answer; they did not forget their courage, but stood and waited for the best of the Trojans charging in. And the roar of the two went up together, into the high clear air and the brightness of Zeus.
