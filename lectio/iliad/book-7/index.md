# The Iliad, Book VII

The Duel of Hector and Ajax

So speaking, glorious Hector passed out through the gates, and his brother Paris went with him, both of them hungry for the fight. As when a god sends a fair wind to sailors who have prayed for it in vain, worn out from heaving at the oars over the gray sea — so welcome was the sight of these two to the Trojans, who longed for them.

At once Paris killed Menesthius, son of Areithous the Mace-man, born to him by Phylomedusa; the man had lived in Arne. And Hector hurled his spear at Eioneus and struck him dead, a wound in the neck under the bronze rim of his helmet, so that his limbs went slack. Glaucus too, son of Hippolochus, the Lycian captain, in the close press of the fighting drove his spear into the shoulder of Iphinous, son of Dexius, as he was springing up behind his fast mares; and he fell from the chariot to the ground, and the life went out of him.

When Athena saw these men cutting down the Greeks, she came darting from the peaks of Olympus to Troy. And Apollo, watching from Pergamus, went out to meet her, for he wanted the Trojans to win. The two of them met by the great oak tree, and lord Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke first: “Why now, daughter of mighty Zeus — what does that proud heart of yours want, that it has sent you down from Olympus? Have you no pity for the Trojans? You mean to tip the scales of victory toward the Greeks. Listen to me — it would be better so. Halt the fighting for today, and let them take it up again later, until they bring about the fall of Troy, since you goddesses are set on tearing the city down.”

And the goddess Athena answered: “So be it, far-shooter. That is the very thought I had, coming down from Olympus to the Trojans and the Greeks. But tell me — how do you mean to break off the fighting now?”

Apollo, son of Zeus, replied: “Let us stir up great Hector to challenge one of the Greeks to single combat, man against man. Then the Greeks will be shamed into putting forward someone to face him.”

Athena agreed, and Helenus, son of Priam, caught in his heart the plan the gods had made. He went up to Hector and said: “Hector, son of Priam, who matches the gods in counsel — I am your brother, so hear me. Make the rest of the Trojans and all the Greeks sit down, and call out the best man among the Greeks to face you alone, man to man. I have heard the voice of the immortal gods: the day of your death is not yet come.”

Hector was glad to hear it. He went out between the armies, gripping his spear by the middle to hold the Trojans back, and they all sat down. Agamemnon made the Greeks sit too. And Athena and Apollo, in the likeness of vultures, perched in the high oak tree of father Zeus, taking joy in their men. The ranks settled close together, bristling with shields and helmets and spears. As when the rising west wind ruffles the face of the sea and the water darkens beneath it, so the companies of Trojans and Greeks sat over the plain. And Hector stood up between them and spoke:

“Hear me, Trojans and Greeks, while I say what is in my heart. Zeus on his high throne has brought our oaths and our truce to nothing; he plans evil for both sides, until either you take the towers of Troy, or you are beaten down beside your own ships. The best of the Greeks are here among you. Let the man whose heart drives him to fight me come forward now as your champion against Hector. And I say this, and let Zeus be witness between us: if your man kills me, let him strip my armor and carry it to the hollow ships, but give my body back, so the Trojans and their wives may grant me my due of fire when I am dead. And if Apollo grants me the glory and I kill him, I will strip his armor and carry it to holy Troy and hang it in the temple of Apollo who strikes from afar — but his body I will give back, so the long-haired Greeks may bury him beside their ships and raise a mound for him over the broad water of the Hellespont. And one day a man sailing the wine-dark sea in his ship will say, ‘That is the grave-mound of a man who died long ago — a champion that glorious Hector killed.’ So he will say, and my glory will never die.”

So he spoke, and they all sat silent, ashamed to refuse the challenge, afraid to take it up. At last Menelaus rose and rebuked them, groaning in his anger: “Cowards — women of Greece, not men. This will be a foul disgrace, doubly foul, if not one of the Greeks will stand against Hector now. May every one of you turn to earth and water where you sit, gutless and inglorious. I will arm and go out against this man myself. The end of it lies above, in the hands of the immortal gods.”

With that he began to put on his armor. And then, Menelaus, your life would have come to its end at the hands of Hector, far the stronger man, had not the Greek leaders sprung up and held you back. Agamemnon himself, lord of men, caught his brother by the right hand and said: “This is madness, Menelaus. You don’t need such folly. Hold back, hard as it is, and don’t long to fight a man stronger than you — Hector, son of Priam, whom others dread as well. Even Achilles, far the better man, shrinks from meeting him in the fighting where men win glory. Go, sit down among your own people, and the Greeks will send out another champion against him. Fearless as Hector is, and greedy for battle, I think he will be glad enough to rest his knees, if he comes out of this fight alive, out of the brunt of it.”

So the warrior spoke, and with sound words he turned his brother’s heart; and Menelaus obeyed, and his attendants gladly took the armor from his shoulders. Then Nestor rose and spoke among them: “Now grief has truly fallen on the land of Greece. How the old horseman Peleus would groan to hear it — that fine speaker and counselor of the Myrmidons, who loved, when I was in his house, to ask me about the breeding and the line of all the Greeks. If he heard that they all cower now before Hector, he would lift his hands again and again to the immortal gods and pray that the life leave his body and go down to the house of Hades. By father Zeus, and Athena, and Apollo — if only I were young again, as I was when the men of Pylos and the Arcadians fought by the rushing river Celadon, under the walls of Pheia, around the streams of the Iardanus. Their champion was the godlike Ereuthalion, with the armor of King Areithous on his shoulders — Areithous whom everyone, men and women alike, called the Mace-man, because he fought with neither bow nor spear but broke the enemy’s ranks with an iron club. Lycurgus killed him, not by fair strength but by cunning, trapping him in a narrow pass where the club was no use to him; for Lycurgus got in first and ran him through the middle with a spear, and the man fell on his back to the ground. Then Lycurgus stripped the armor that Ares had given him and wore it himself in battle ever after; but when he grew old at home, he gave it to his faithful attendant Ereuthalion to wear. And Ereuthalion, in that armor, challenged the best of us. They all hung back, shaking with fear; not one would face him. But my proud heart drove me to take him on, hot as I was — and I was the youngest of them all. So I fought him, and Athena gave me the victory. He was the biggest, strongest man I ever killed, and he covered a great stretch of ground, sprawling there. If only I were young again, with that strength still in me — then Hector, son of Priam, would soon find his match. But you who are the best of the whole army, not one of you has the stomach to face Hector.”

So the old man shamed them, and nine men sprang to their feet. First of all rose Agamemnon, lord of men, and after him brave Diomedes, son of Tydeus; then the two named Ajax, clothed in furious courage; and after them Idomeneus and his comrade-in-arms Meriones; then Eurypylus, son of Euaemon, and Thoas, son of Andraemon, and Odysseus too. All of these were ready to fight Hector. Then Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, spoke again: “Cast lots, all of you, to see who is chosen. The man who wins it will do good service to the Greeks, and to his own heart too, if he comes out of this fight alive.”

So he spoke, and each man marked his lot and threw it into the helmet of Agamemnon, son of Atreus. And the soldiers lifted their hands in prayer to the gods, and one of them would say, looking up at the wide sky: “Father Zeus, let the lot fall to Ajax, or to the son of Tydeus, or to the king of rich Mycenae himself.”

So they prayed, and Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, shook the helmet, and out leapt the very lot they wanted — the lot of Ajax. The herald carried it through the crowd and showed it to all the chiefs of the Greeks, going left to right, but none of them owned it. Only when he came at last to the man who had marked it and dropped it in did brave Ajax hold out his hand, and the herald stopped and laid it in his palm. Ajax looked at the mark, knew it, and was glad. He threw it to the ground at his feet and said: “My friends — the lot is mine, and my heart is glad, for I think I will beat glorious Hector. Come now, while I put on my armor, pray to lord Zeus, the son of Cronus — quietly among yourselves, so the Trojans won’t hear, or aloud if you like, for we are afraid of no one. No man will drive me off against my will, by force or by skill, for I was not born and raised on Salamis to be a fool in war.”

So he spoke, and they prayed to lord Zeus, the son of Cronus, and one of them would say, looking up at the wide sky: “Father Zeus, who rule from Ida, most glorious, most great — grant the victory to Ajax and let him win bright glory. But if you love Hector too and care for him, then give the two of them equal strength and equal fame.”

So they prayed, and Ajax armed himself in gleaming bronze. When the armor was all on his body, he strode out like monstrous Ares, who goes among men when the son of Cronus sets them fighting in heart-eating strife. So huge Ajax sprang forward, the bulwark of the Greeks, with a grim smile on his face, taking long strides and shaking his long spear. The Greeks rejoiced to look at him, but a hard trembling came over the limbs of the Trojans, every man, and the heart of Hector himself beat fast in his chest. But there was no drawing back for him now, no slipping away into the ranks behind, for he was the one who had given the challenge. Ajax came on, holding his shield before him like a wall — bronze, with seven folds of ox-hide, the work of Tychius, far the best leather-worker in Hyle, who had made it from the hides of seven well-fed bulls and laid an eighth layer of bronze over them. Holding this shield before his chest, Ajax, son of Telamon, came close up to Hector and spoke his threat: “Hector, now you’ll learn, man to man, what kind of champions the Greeks still have, even apart from Achilles, the lion-hearted breaker of men. He lies by the curved ships now, nursing his anger at Agamemnon, shepherd of the people — but there are plenty of us able to face you. Begin the fight.”

And great Hector of the gleaming helmet answered him: “Ajax, son of Telamon, sprung from Zeus, leader of armies — don’t try me like some weak boy, or a woman who knows nothing of war. I know war and the killing of men. I know how to swing my ox-hide shield to the right and to the left — that, I think, is the art of standing one’s ground. I know how to charge into the rush of chariots and horses; I know, in the close fight, how to step the dance of murderous Ares. But I won’t strike a man like you on the sly, watching for a chance. I’ll hit you in the open, if I can.”

He spoke, drew back his long spear, and hurled it. It struck the dread sevenfold shield of Ajax on the outermost layer, the eighth, which was bronze, and tore through six of the folds, but the seventh hide stopped it. Then Ajax, sprung from Zeus, hurled in his turn and struck the round shield of the son of Priam. The heavy spear drove through the gleaming shield and pressed on into the finely-worked breastplate; it ripped the tunic against his flank, but Hector swerved aside and escaped death. Then both of them pulled the long spears free and fell on each other like ravening lions, or wild boars whose strength is not easily worn down. The son of Priam struck the center of Ajax’s shield, but the bronze did not break, and the point of his spear was bent back. Then Ajax sprang in and pierced Hector’s shield; the spear punched straight through and checked him in his charge, and it grazed his neck, and the dark blood welled up. But even so Hector of the gleaming helmet did not stop fighting. He gave ground and seized in his broad hand a stone lying on the plain, black and jagged and huge, and hurled it and hit the great seven-layered shield of Ajax on the boss in its middle, so that the bronze rang loud. But Ajax caught up a far bigger stone, whirled it around, and let it fly with all his strength behind it. This boulder, big as a millstone, smashed Hector’s shield inward and knocked his knees out from under him; he was thrown flat on his back, pinned beneath the shield — but Apollo lifted him up at once. And now the two of them would have closed and slashed each other with their swords, had not the heralds, the messengers of gods and men, come between them — one from the Trojans, one from the Greeks, Talthybius and Idaeus, both wise men. They held their staves out between the fighters, and the good herald Idaeus spoke: “Stop now, dear sons. Fight no more. Zeus who gathers the clouds loves you both, and you are both spearmen — that much we all know. But night is coming on, and it is good to give way to the night.”

Ajax, son of Telamon, answered: “Idaeus, tell Hector to say so himself — for he was the one who called our best men out to fight. Let him speak first, and whatever he says, I’ll agree to it.”

Then great Hector of the gleaming helmet said: “Ajax, since a god gave you size and strength and good sense, and you are the best of the Greeks with a spear, let us break off the fight and the killing for today. Another day we’ll fight again, until the gods decide between us and give the victory to one of us. Night is coming on, and it is good to give way to the night. Go now and gladden the hearts of the Greeks beside your ships, and most of all your own people and your kinsmen; and I will go through the great city of King Priam and gladden the Trojans and the Trojan women in their long robes, who will be praying for me in the temples. But come — let us trade gifts with each other, so that men may say, Greeks and Trojans both: ‘They fought each other in heart-eating strife, and then they parted, joined in friendship.’”

So speaking, he gave Ajax a sword studded with silver, with its scabbard and its well-cut belt; and Ajax gave him a war-belt bright with purple. So the two of them parted, the one going to the army of the Greeks, the other to the throng of the Trojans, who rejoiced to see him coming back to them alive and whole, safe from the fury and the unbeatable hands of Ajax; and they brought him into the city, hardly daring to believe he was saved. On the other side, the Greeks led Ajax, glad of his victory, to noble Agamemnon.

When they had come to the huts of the son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men, sacrificed an ox for them, a male of five years, to the almighty son of Cronus. They flayed it and made it ready, cut it all into joints, sliced the meat with care, ran it on the spits, roasted it through, and drew it off. When the work was done and the feast was ready, they ate, and no man’s hunger went unsatisfied at the shared meal; and Agamemnon, the warrior son of Atreus, gave Ajax the long cuts of the back to honor him. And when they had put away their desire for food and drink, the old man Nestor, whose counsel had always proved the best, was first to weave his thoughts before them; and with good will he spoke:

“Son of Atreus, and you other leaders of the Greeks — many of the long-haired Greeks are dead now, their dark blood spilled by Ares along the lovely streams of the Scamander, and their souls have gone down to the house of Hades. So when morning comes you should hold the fighting back. We will gather our dead and bring them in on wagons drawn by oxen and mules, and burn them a little way from the ships, so that each of us may carry home the bones of his fallen comrades to their children, when we sail back to our own land. And around the pyre let us heap one single mound, raised from the plain for all in common. Close by it let us build a high wall, a shelter for ourselves and for our ships, and set well-fitted gates in it so that there is a road through for our chariots. And just outside it let us dig a deep trench all the way around, to hold off their horses and their men, so the proud Trojans can never break in upon us.”

So he spoke, and all the leaders shouted their approval. Meanwhile the Trojans held an assembly up on the height of Troy, by the gates of Priam’s palace, an angry, turbulent assembly. And wise Antenor was the first to speak: “Hear me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies, while I say what is in my heart. Come — let us give Argive Helen and all her wealth back to the sons of Atreus, and let them take her away. We are fighting now as men who have broken their sworn truce, and I see no good coming of it for us, unless we do as I say.”

So he spoke, and sat down. Then Paris, the husband of lovely-haired Helen, rose among them and answered with winged words: “Antenor, what you say does not please me. You can think of a better word than this if you choose. But if you have really said it in earnest, then the gods themselves have destroyed your wits. I will speak out plainly among the horse-taming Trojans: I say it straight — I will not give up the woman. But the wealth I brought home with her from Argos, all of that I am willing to give back, and to add still more of my own.”

So he spoke and sat down, and Priam rose among them, of the line of Dardanus, who matched the gods in counsel; and with good will he spoke before them: “Hear me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies, while I say what is in my heart. Take your supper now through the city as you always have, and remember to keep the watch, every man awake. At first light let Idaeus go down to the hollow ships and tell Agamemnon and Menelaus, the sons of Atreus, the offer of Paris, through whom this quarrel began. And let him put this wise word to them as well: ask whether they will hold off from the bitter fighting until we have burned our dead. Afterward we will fight again, until the gods decide between us and hand the victory to one side or the other.”

So he spoke, and they heard him and obeyed. They took their supper through the army in their companies, and at first light Idaeus went down to the ships. He found the Greeks, the men of Ares, in assembly by the stern of Agamemnon’s ship, and the loud-voiced herald stood in their midst and spoke: “Son of Atreus, and you other leaders of the Greeks — Priam and the other noble Trojans have sent me to tell you, in hope you will find it acceptable, the offer of Paris, through whom this quarrel began. All the treasure that Paris carried in his hollow ships to Troy — would that he had died first — he is willing to give back, and to add still more of his own; but the wedded wife of glorious Menelaus he says he will not give up, though the Trojans urge him to it. And they told me to ask this too: whether you will hold off from the bitter fighting until we have burned our dead. Afterward we will fight again, until the gods decide between us and hand the victory to one side or the other.”

So he spoke, and they all sat silent. At last Diomedes of the great war cry spoke out: “Let no one take anything now from Paris — not the treasure, and not Helen either. Even a fool can see it: the noose of destruction is already around the Trojans’ necks.”

So he spoke, and all the sons of the Greeks shouted their approval, marveling at the words of Diomedes, breaker of horses. Then lord Agamemnon said to Idaeus: “Idaeus, you have heard for yourself the answer of the Greeks; it is theirs, and it is mine as well. But as for the dead — I do not begrudge you the burning of them. When men are dead there is no holding back the swift mercy of fire. Let Zeus be witness to it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera.”

So he spoke, and held up his scepter to all the gods, and Idaeus went back to holy Troy. The Trojans and Dardanians were sitting gathered in assembly, all of them waiting for him to return; and when he came he stood in their midst and gave them the message. Then they set quickly to their double task — some to gather the dead, others to bring in wood. And on their side the Greeks too hurried out from their benched ships, some to gather the dead, others to bring in wood.

The sun was just striking the fields, rising fresh into the sky out of the deep, slow-flowing stream of Ocean, when the two armies met on the plain. It was hard to know each man for who he had been; but they washed the clotted blood from the corpses, and weeping hot tears, lifted them onto the wagons. Priam had forbidden the Trojans to wail aloud, so they piled their dead on the pyre in silence, grieving in their hearts, and when they had burned them they went back to holy Troy. And in the same way the Greeks piled their dead on the pyre in silence, grieving in their hearts, and when they had burned them they went back to the hollow ships.

In the twilight, while it was still not day, chosen men of the Greeks gathered around the pyre and heaped a single mound, raised from the plain for all in common. Close beside it they built a high wall, a shelter for themselves and for their ships, and set strong gates in it so that there was a road through for the chariots. And just outside it they dug a deep, wide trench, and planted sharp stakes along it.

So the long-haired Greeks labored; and the gods, sitting beside Zeus the lord of lightning, looked on in wonder at the great work of the bronze-armored Greeks. But Poseidon, who shakes the earth, spoke among them: “Father Zeus — is there any mortal left on the wide earth who will tell the gods his mind, and trust them, after this? Don’t you see? The long-haired Greeks have built a wall around their ships and run a trench around it, and they offered no fine sacrifice to the gods. The fame of this wall will spread as far as the dawn itself spreads its light — and men will forget the wall that Phoebus Apollo and I toiled to raise for the hero Laomedon.”

Deeply troubled, Zeus who gathers the clouds answered him: “What a thing to say, you who shake the wide earth. Some other god, weaker than you, might be afraid of this scheme of theirs; but your fame spreads as far as the dawn itself spreads its light. Take heart. Once the long-haired Greeks have gone home in their ships to their own land, then break this wall down, every stone of it, and sweep it into the sea, and cover the long beach again with sand. So the great wall of the Greeks will be wiped away.”

So they spoke to each other; and as the sun went down the work of the Greeks was finished, and they slaughtered oxen by their huts and took their meal. Many ships had come in from Lemnos carrying wine, sent by Euneus, son of Jason, whom Hypsipyle bore to Jason, shepherd of the people. To the sons of Atreus alone, to Agamemnon and Menelaus, the son of Jason had given a thousand measures of wine to carry over. From these ships the long-haired Greeks bought their wine, some paying with bronze, some with gleaming iron, some with hides, some with whole cattle on the hoof, and some with slaves; and they spread a rich feast. All night long the long-haired Greeks feasted, and so did the Trojans and their allies in the city. And all that night Zeus, the lord of counsel, was plotting evil for them, and he thundered terribly. Pale fear took hold of them, and they poured the wine from their cups onto the ground, and no man dared drink until he had poured an offering to the almighty son of Cronus. Then at last they lay down and took the gift of sleep.
