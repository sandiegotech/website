# The Iliad, Book XXIII

The Funeral Games for Patroclus

So the Trojans mourned throughout their city. But the Greeks, when they reached the ships and the Hellespont, scattered each man to his own vessel — all but the Myrmidons. Achilles would not let them go. He spoke to his battle-hardened companions: “Myrmidons, my swift horsemen, my trusted friends — not yet. Don’t unyoke the horses. Drive close to the body with chariot and team, and mourn Patroclus; that is the due of the dead. When we have had our fill of grieving, then we’ll loose the horses and take our meal here together.”

At that they raised one cry of lament, and Achilles led them. Three times they drove their horses around the body, weeping, and Thetis stirred a deeper longing in them still. The sands were wet with their tears, and so was their armor — such a man was the one they had lost, who could turn the tide of fear. Above them all grieved the son of Peleus. He laid his man-killing hands on his friend’s breast and said: “Fare well, Patroclus, even in the house of the dead. I am keeping every promise I made you. I have dragged Hector here for the dogs to tear raw, and at your pyre I will cut the throats of twelve sons of the Trojans, in payment for your death.”

And as he spoke, he did a shameful thing to noble Hector: he stretched the body face-down in the dust beside the bier of Patroclus. Then the others stripped off their bronze armor, unyoked their high-whinnying horses, and sat down in their thousands beside the ship of swift-footed Achilles, who set out a great funeral feast for them. Many sleek oxen went under the knife, bellowing, and many sheep and bleating goats; many tusked boars rich with fat were singed and stretched out to roast in the fire of Hephaestus. And around the body the blood ran everywhere, deep enough to dip a cup in.

Then the leaders of the Greeks brought the son of Peleus to Agamemnon — and it was hard to bring him, so furious was he still over his dead companion. When they reached the king’s hut, they ordered the servants to set a great cauldron over the fire, hoping to persuade Achilles to wash the clotted gore from his body. But he refused them flatly, and swore an oath on it: “No — by Zeus, the highest and the greatest of the gods, it is not right that water touch my head until I have laid Patroclus on the fire, raised him a mound, and cut my hair. Never again, as long as I live, will grief like this come over me. So for now, let us submit to this bitter feast. But at first light, lord Agamemnon, have the men bring wood and lay in everything a dead man needs for his road down into the dark. Then the fire can take him quickly out of our sight, and the army can turn back to its work.”

So he spoke, and they listened and obeyed. They made the meal ready in haste, and ate, and no one’s hunger went unsatisfied at the shared feast. When they had put away their desire for food and drink, the rest went off to rest, each to his own hut. But the son of Peleus lay down among his Myrmidons by the shore of the sounding sea, groaning, in an open place where the surf came washing in. And there a deep sleep took hold of him and loosed the weight from his heart, for his fine limbs were worn out with the chase, running Hector down around windy Troy. Then the spirit of grieving Patroclus came to him — the same in height, the same beautiful eyes, the same voice, dressed as he had dressed in life. It hovered above his head and spoke:

“You sleep, Achilles, and you have forgotten me. You never neglected me alive — only now that I’m dead. Bury me, and quickly, so I can pass the gates of the house of the dead. The spirits keep me out, the phantoms of the worn-out dead; they won’t yet let me cross the river to join them, and I drift, lost, along the wide gates of that house. Give me your hand — I beg you — for I will never come back out of the dark once you have given me my share of the fire. Never again will the two of us sit apart from our friends and take counsel together, alive. The fate I was born to has opened its jaws and swallowed me. And you too, Achilles, godlike as you are — it is set down that you will die beneath the wall of the rich Trojans.

“And one thing more I ask, if you’ll grant it. Don’t let my bones lie apart from yours, Achilles — let them lie together, as we grew up together in your house, from the day Menoetius brought me, only a boy, out of Opoeis. I had killed the son of Amphidamas — by an unlucky accident, a quarrel over the dice, not meaning to — and your father Peleus took me into his home, raised me with kindness, and named me your squire. So let one urn hold us both — the golden two-handled jar your mother gave you.”

Swift-footed Achilles answered him: “Why have you come to me, dear heart, to lay all these charges on me? Of course I will do every one of them, just as you ask. But come closer — let us hold each other, just once more, and take what comfort we can in our cold grief.”

He reached out his arms — and held nothing. The spirit was gone, down into the earth like smoke, with a thin cry. Achilles started up, stunned, and struck his two hands together, and his words came out broken with grief: “There it is, then — even in the house of the dead something remains, a soul and a phantom, but no mind in it at all. All night long the spirit of poor Patroclus stood over me, weeping and wailing, telling me everything I must do — and it looked so exactly like himself.”

His words set them all weeping again over the pitiful dead, and they were still mourning when rosy-fingered Dawn appeared. Then King Agamemnon sent out men and mules from across the whole camp to bring the wood, and Meriones, the squire of Idomeneus, went in charge of them. They set out with axes for cutting timber and stout ropes in their hands, and the mules went ahead. Up the slopes and down, this way and that, they climbed, until they came to the spurs of Ida with its many springs. There they fell to work with the bronze, and the tall oaks came crashing down. They split the trunks and lashed them to the mules, which picked their way down through the thick brush to the plain. The men carried logs themselves, for Meriones had ordered it, and they laid them down in a line on the shore, at the place where Achilles meant to raise a great mound for Patroclus, and for himself.

When they had laid down the great logs all over the ground, they sat and waited. Then Achilles ordered his Myrmidons to gird on their armor and yoke their horses. They rose and armed and mounted their chariots, drivers and fighters both, the chariots leading, a cloud of foot soldiers behind them in their thousands. And in the middle his companions bore Patroclus, covering the body with locks of their hair that they cut off and cast upon it. Last of all came Achilles, cradling the head of his friend in his hands, grieving — for he was sending so true a comrade down into the dark.

When they reached the place Achilles had named, they set the body down and built up the wood. Then the son of Peleus thought of one more thing. He stood apart from the pyre and cut off the golden lock he had let grow long for the river Spercheius, and he looked out, full of sorrow, over the dark sea, and said: “Spercheius — it was for nothing that my father Peleus vowed to you that when I came home to my own dear country I would cut this lock for you and offer you a holy sacrifice, fifty rams at your springs, where your sacred grove and your altar stand. That was his vow, and you have not answered his prayer. So now, since I will never see my home again, let me give this lock instead to the warrior Patroclus, to carry with him.”

He placed the lock in the hands of his dead friend, and a fresh wave of longing and lament ran through them all. The sun would have gone down on their weeping, but Achilles turned to Agamemnon and said: “Son of Atreus — it is you the army listens to — men can have their fill even of mourning. Send the men away from the pyre now to see to their meal. We who were closest to the dead will take care of what remains; only let the other leaders stay here with me.”

When Agamemnon, lord of men, heard this, he sent the army off to the ships. But those who tended the dead stayed, and heaped up the wood, and built a pyre a hundred feet on every side, and laid the body, heavy with grief, on the top of it. They flayed and dressed many fat sheep and oxen before the pyre, and great-hearted Achilles took the fat from all of them and wrapped the dead from head to foot in it, and piled the flayed carcasses around. Against the bier he leaned two-handled jars of honey and oil. Four strong-necked horses he threw on the pyre, groaning aloud. Patroclus had kept nine house-dogs at his table; Achilles cut the throats of two of them and threw them on. And twelve brave sons of the great-hearted Trojans he put to the sword and laid among the rest — for his heart was set on cruelty. Then he let loose the iron strength of the fire to feed on it all. He cried out and called his dead friend by name: “Fare well, Patroclus, even in the house of the dead. I am keeping every promise I made you. Twelve brave sons of the Trojans the fire is eating along with you — but Hector, son of Priam, I will not give to the fire. The dogs will have him.”

So he threatened. But no dogs came near the body of Hector. Day and night Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, kept them off and anointed him with rose-sweet oil, divine, so his flesh would not be torn as Achilles dragged him. And Phoebus Apollo drew a dark cloud down from the sky over the whole place where the body lay, so the heat of the sun would not shrivel the skin on the limbs.

But the pyre of dead Patroclus would not catch. Then swift-footed Achilles thought of one more thing. He stood apart from the fire and prayed to the two winds, Boreas of the north and Zephyr of the west, promising them rich offerings. Pouring from a golden cup, he begged them again and again to come and make the wood burn fast, so the bodies would be consumed. Swift Iris heard his prayer and went racing off to fetch the winds. They were all together, feasting in the house of the blustering West, when Iris came running and halted at the stone threshold. The moment they saw her they leaped up, every one of them calling her to his side — but she would not sit. “No time,” she said. “I’m bound back to the streams of Ocean, to the land of the Ethiopians, where they are making their great sacrifices to the immortals, and I’ll have my share. But Achilles is praying that Boreas and the loud West Wind will come — and he promises you rich offerings — to blow on the pyre of Patroclus, for whom all the Greeks are mourning.”

With that she was gone, and the two winds rose with a great roar, driving the clouds before them. They swept out over the sea, and the waves climbed under their breath; and when they reached the rich land of Troy they fell on the pyre, and the huge flame leaped and roared. All night long together they beat upon the fire, blowing shrill, and all night long swift-footed Achilles, holding a double cup, drew wine from a golden bowl and poured it on the ground, soaking the earth, calling on the spirit of poor Patroclus. As a father weeps when he burns the bones of his own son — a young man, just married, whose death has broken his parents’ hearts — so Achilles wept as he burned the bones of his friend, dragging himself around the pyre, groaning without end.

At last, when the Morning Star rose to herald the light, and after it Dawn spread her saffron robe over the sea, the flames sank and the fire began to die down. The winds went home again, back over the Thracian sea, which heaved and boiled as they passed. And the son of Peleus turned away from the pyre and lay down, worn out, and sweet sleep came over him. Then the men around Agamemnon gathered and came near, and the noise and tramp of them woke him. He sat up and said: “Son of Atreus, and you other leaders of the Greeks — first put out the pyre with red wine, everywhere the fire reached. Then let us gather the bones of Patroclus, son of Menoetius, picking them out with care. They are easy to find: he lay in the middle of the pyre, while the rest, men and horses, burned at the edge, all thrown together. We’ll lay his bones in a golden urn, sealed in a double layer of fat, to wait until I myself go down into the dark. And for the mound — don’t trouble to build a great one now, only a fair one. Later, those of you who are left at the ships when I am gone can raise it broad and high.”

So he spoke, and they did as the son of Peleus asked. First they quenched with red wine all the ground the fire had touched, where the ash lay deep, and through their tears they gathered the white bones of their gentle friend into a golden urn, sealed in a double layer of fat. They wrapped the urn in soft linen and carried it into the hut, and laid it away. Then they marked out the circle of the mound, set its foundation around the pyre, and heaped up the earth. When they had raised it, they began to leave — but Achilles held the people back and sat them down in a wide ring, and brought out prizes from the ships: cauldrons and tripods, horses and mules and strong oxen, women with lovely belts, and gray iron.

For the chariot race he set out the prizes first. For the winner, a woman skilled in fine work, and a tripod with handle-ears that held twenty-two measures. For second, a six-year-old mare, unbroken, carrying a mule foal. For third, a cauldron never touched by fire, still bright from the maker, holding four measures. For fourth, two bars of gold; for fifth, a two-handled jar, untouched by smoke. Then he stood and spoke among the Greeks: “Son of Atreus, and you other Greeks — these are the prizes waiting for the charioteers. If we were holding these games for anyone else, I would take the first prize back to my own hut myself; you know how far my horses are the best, for they are immortal. Poseidon gave them to my father Peleus, and he gave them to me. But I will hold back, I and my team. They have lost the noble, gentle driver who so often poured clean water over their manes and rubbed them with soft oil. Look at them, standing there mourning him, their manes trailing in the dust, sick at heart. The rest of you, take your places, every man who trusts his horses and his strong-built chariot.”

So spoke the son of Peleus, and the drivers came forward. First of all rose Eumelus, son of Admetus, a fine horseman. After him came mighty Diomedes, son of Tydeus, yoking the Trojan horses he had once taken from Aeneas, the day Apollo carried Aeneas out of the fight. Then golden-haired Menelaus, son of Atreus, yoked his fast pair — Agamemnon’s mare Aethe and his own horse Podargus. Echepolus, son of Anchises, had given the mare to Agamemnon as a bribe to be left at home, in his comfort, in broad Sicyon, where Zeus had made him rich, rather than march to Troy. Her Menelaus put under the yoke, and she strained to run.

Fourth, Antilochus made his fine-maned horses ready — the son of noble Nestor, who was the son of Neleus. His horses had been bred in Pylos, and his father came up beside him with good advice, though he scarcely needed it: “Antilochus, you’re young, but Zeus and Poseidon have loved you and made you a fine horseman, so I don’t need to teach you much. You know how to wheel close around the post. But your horses are slow — that’s what I’m afraid will cost you. The other men have faster teams, but they themselves know less than you do. So put your mind to work, son, and find some skill to keep the prize from slipping away. A woodman does more by skill than by main strength; by skill the steersman holds his fast ship straight on the wine-dark sea in a gale; and by skill one driver beats another. The man who trusts only his horses and his chariot swings out wide at the turn, this way and that, and lets his team wander, and never holds them in hand. But the man who knows his craft, even with the slower horses, keeps his eye on the post and cuts it close, knows from the start how to play out the reins, and watches the man ahead of him. Let me give you a sure mark you can’t miss. There’s a dry stump standing about six feet up — oak, or pine — not yet rotted by the rain, with a white stone set on either side of it, at the fork of the track, and clear ground all around it. It may be the grave-marker of some man long dead, or a turning-post from races before our time; now Achilles has set it as the mark to round. Hug it close as you wheel. Lean a little to the left in the chariot, and call to your right-hand horse and lash him on and give him the rein, while you let the left-hand horse run so tight to the post that the wheel-hub almost grazes the stone — but only almost. Touch the stone, and you’ll wound your horses and smash the chariot to pieces, and you’ll be the others’ sport and your own ruin. Keep your wits about you, son. If you round the post first, no one will catch you after — not if they had Adrestus’s horse Arion behind them, that swift, half-divine animal, or the horses of Laomedon, the finest bred in this land.”

When Nestor had finished counseling his son, he sat down. Fifth, Meriones made his fine-maned horses ready. Then they all mounted their chariots and cast their lots. Achilles shook the helmet, and the lot of Antilochus, Nestor’s son, jumped out first; then King Eumelus; then Menelaus, son of Atreus; then Meriones; and last, best of them all, came the lot of Diomedes, son of Tydeus. They took their stations in a line. Achilles showed them the turning-post, far off on the level plain, and stationed Phoenix beside it, his father’s old companion, to watch the running and report the truth.

All at once, every man lifted his whip and cracked it down, shook the reins, and shouted the horses on. They flew out over the plain, away from the ships, and the dust rose under their chests like a cloud, like a whirlwind, and their manes streamed in the wind of their own speed. One moment the chariots seemed to skim the ground, the next they leaped clear into the air. The drivers stood tall, and the heart of each beat hard with the hunger to win. Every man called to his horses, and the horses tore across the plain in the storm of dust they raised.

It was on the last stretch, racing back toward the gray sea, that each team showed its full speed, and the field broke open. The mares of the son of Admetus took the lead, and right behind them came the Trojan stallions of Diomedes — so close they seemed about to climb into his chariot, their hot breath on Eumelus’s back and broad shoulders, their heads almost laid on him as they flew. And now Diomedes would have passed him, or at least drawn even — but Phoebus Apollo, who bore a grudge, struck the shining whip from his hand. Tears of rage filled his eyes as he watched the mares pulling away while his own horses lost ground with no lash to drive them. But Athena saw the trick Apollo had played on the son of Tydeus. She flew to him, gave him back the whip, and put fresh fire into his horses — and then, in her anger, she went after the son of Admetus and broke his yoke. The mares bolted apart, one to each side of the track, and the pole crashed and twisted to the ground. Eumelus was flung from the chariot beside the wheel, his elbows and mouth and nose torn open, his forehead gashed above the brows; his eyes filled with tears, and his strong voice failed him. But Diomedes swung his team wide and shot far out in front, for Athena had put strength into his horses and crowned him with the glory.

Behind him came golden-haired Menelaus, son of Atreus. But Antilochus called out to his father’s horses: “On with you, both of you — full speed now. I’m not telling you to beat the team of Tydeus’s son; Athena has put running into them and given Diomedes the glory. But catch the horses of Atreus’s son, and don’t be left behind — or Aethe, a mare, will shame you. Why are you falling back, you brave hearts? I’ll tell you, and I mean it: Nestor will not feed either of you another day; he’ll put you to the sword if we carry off a poorer prize through your slacking. After them, fast as you can. I’ll find the way to slip past them where the track narrows — it won’t fail me.”

They were afraid of their master’s voice, and for a short stretch they ran harder. And soon Antilochus saw the narrow place, where the track had sunk — a broken hollow, where winter rain had pooled and worn the road away. Menelaus was driving straight for it, holding the line to keep clear of a collision. But Antilochus turned his horses off the track and came up hard alongside, just to one side. Menelaus took fright and shouted: “Antilochus — you’re driving like a madman! Rein in! The road is too narrow here. It widens just ahead — pass me there. Foul my chariot, and you’ll wreck us both.”

But Antilochus only plied the whip and drove faster, as if he hadn’t heard. They ran neck and neck about as far as a young man can fling a discus testing his strength — and then Menelaus’s mares dropped back. He himself eased off, afraid the teams would tangle and the chariots go over, and both of them pitch headlong into the dust in their hunger to win. And he shouted after Antilochus in anger: “There is no greater cheat alive than you. Go to ruin — we Greeks were fools to call you a man of sense. Even so, you won’t carry off the prize without swearing an oath for it.”

Then he called to his own horses: “Don’t slacken, don’t stand there grieving. Their legs will tire before yours — neither of them is young anymore.” They feared their master’s voice and ran harder, and soon they were closing on the team ahead.

Meanwhile the Greeks sat watching from the rings, as the horses scoured the plain in their own storm of dust. Idomeneus, the captain of the Cretans, was the first to make out the running, for he was sitting apart from the crowd, high up on the best vantage. He heard the driver shouting, far off, and made out the leading horse clearly — all chestnut, with a round white blaze like the moon on its forehead. He stood and spoke among the Greeks: “Friends — leaders and counselors of the Greeks — am I the only one who can see the race, or can you see it too? It seems to me another team is in front now, with another driver. The mares that led off at the start must have come to grief out on the plain. I saw them first, rounding the post; now, search the plain of Troy as I will, I can’t find them anywhere. Maybe the reins slipped from the driver’s hands and he couldn’t hold them at the turn, and missed the wheel and was thrown, and broke his chariot, and his mares bolted off the course in a panic. Stand up and look for yourselves — I can’t be sure — but the man leading looks to me like an Aetolian by birth, a lord among the Greeks: brave Diomedes, the son of Tydeus.”

Ajax, the son of Oileus, jeered at him rudely: “Idomeneus — why this rush to talk? The mares are still way out on the plain. You’re not the youngest among us, and your eyes aren’t the sharpest, but you’re forever holding forth. You’ve no business doing it — there are better men than you here. Those are Eumelus’s mares in front, same as they’ve been the whole way, and there he stands on the chariot holding the reins.”

The captain of the Cretans rounded on him in anger: “Ajax — champion at insults, but short on sense, and the worst-tempered of the Greeks besides. Come, I’ll wager you a tripod, or a cauldron, and we’ll name Agamemnon, son of Atreus, to judge whose horses are first. Then you’ll learn, at your own cost.” Ajax, son of Oileus, was on his feet to answer in fury, and the quarrel would have gone further still, but Achilles himself rose and stepped between them: “Stop trading insults, the two of you — Ajax, Idomeneus. It isn’t seemly. You’d be the first to blame anyone else for it. Sit down and keep your eyes on the track. The horses are coming in fast, straining for the line; any moment now you’ll both see for yourselves whose team is first and whose comes after.”

Even as he spoke, the son of Tydeus came driving in, swinging the whip hard from the shoulder, his horses stepping high as they flew over the ground. The sand spattered up over the driver, and the chariot, inlaid with gold and tin, ran close behind the racing team; the wheels left scarcely a trace in the fine dust, so fast they came. He pulled up in the middle of the ring, sweat streaming from his horses’ necks and chests to the ground, and sprang down from the gleaming chariot and leaned the whip against the yoke. Sthenelus lost no time: he took up the first prize, gave the woman and the eared cauldron to his men to lead away, and unyoked the team.

Next in came Antilochus, of the line of Neleus — who had passed Menelaus by a trick, not by the speed of his horses. Even so, Menelaus drove in close behind him, no further off than the wheel is from the horse that draws the master and his chariot at full stretch: the tail-hairs brush the rim, and there’s no gap to speak of as it runs. That was how close Menelaus came in behind Antilochus, though at the start he’d been a full discus-throw back. He had caught up fast, for Agamemnon’s mare Aethe pulled harder with every stride; and had the course run a little longer, he’d have passed him clean, with no question of a tie. A spear-cast behind Menelaus came Meriones, the noble squire of Idomeneus, with the slowest horses of all and the least skill at driving. Last of them came the son of Admetus, dragging his fine chariot, driving his horses on foot before him. When Achilles saw him, he pitied him, and stood and spoke among the Greeks: “The best man comes in last. Come — let us give him a prize; it’s only right. The second prize; the first must go to the son of Tydeus.”

So he spoke, and they all agreed, and would have done it — but Antilochus, Nestor’s son, stood up and claimed his right against the son of Peleus: “Achilles, I’ll take it very ill if you do this. You mean to rob me of my prize, because you think Eumelus’s chariot and horses came to grief, and the good man with them. He should have prayed to the immortals — then he’d not have finished last. If you pity him so much, you have plenty of gold in your hut, and bronze, and sheep, and serving-women, and horses. Take from those later and give him a still greater prize, or give it now, and let the Greeks praise you for it. But the mare I will not give up. Let any man who wants her come and fight me for her.”

Swift-footed Achilles smiled, glad in Antilochus, who was one of his dearest friends, and answered: “Antilochus — if you want me to find Eumelus some other prize from my own store, I will. I’ll give him the breastplate I stripped from Asteropaeus, bronze, with a casting of bright tin running all around the rim. It will be worth a great deal to him.” He told his companion Automedon to bring the breastplate from the hut, and he brought it, and Achilles put it in Eumelus’s hands, and he took it gladly.

But Menelaus rose, sore at heart, his anger hot against Antilochus. A herald set the staff in his hand and called the Greeks to silence, and the great man spoke: “Antilochus — you, who had been so sound until now — what have you done? You’ve shamed my skill and fouled my horses, throwing your slower team across their path. So I ask you, leaders and counselors of the Greeks: judge between us, and show neither of us favor — or one of the Greeks may someday say, ‘Menelaus won the mare by browbeating Antilochus; his horses were the slower, but he had the rank and the weight.’ No — I’ll settle it myself, and no man will fault me; I’ll do what’s fair. Come here, Antilochus, as our custom is. Stand before your horses and your chariot, take in your hand the supple whip you drove with, lay your hand on the horses, and swear by Poseidon, who holds and shakes the earth, that you did not foul my chariot on purpose, by a trick.”

And Antilochus, with good sense, answered: “Forgive me. I’m much the younger, King Menelaus; you are the elder and the better man. You know how a young man oversteps — quick-tempered, short on judgment. Let your heart bear with me. I’ll give up the mare I won of my own free will; and if you asked anything more of mine, I would rather hand it to you here and now than fall, all my days after, out of your good favor, and stand in the wrong before the gods.” With that the son of Nestor led the mare over and put her in the hands of Menelaus, and his anger melted — as the dew softens the standing grain when the fields bristle with the ripening harvest. So your heart softened, Menelaus, within you. And he answered him warmly: “Antilochus, now it is I who give way to you, of my own will, for all my anger. You were never reckless or foolish before this; it was only your youth that got the better of your sense. Another time, think twice before you outwit your betters. No other man could have talked me round so easily — but you and your good father and your brother have suffered too much on my account. So I yield to your asking, and I give you the mare, though she is mine by right; and let the people see that my heart is neither hard nor unforgiving.” He gave the mare to Antilochus’s companion Noemon to lead away, and took the gleaming cauldron for himself.

Meriones, who came in fourth, carried off the two bars of gold. The fifth prize — the two-handled jar — went unclaimed, and Achilles carried it across the ring to Nestor and set it in his hands, and said: “Take this, old friend, and keep it as a treasure, a remembrance of the funeral of Patroclus — for you will see him no more among the Greeks. I give it to you for nothing won, for you can no longer box, or wrestle, or enter the javelin, or run the footrace. The weight of the years lies hard on you now.” And he put the jar in his hands. Nestor took it gladly and answered: “Yes, my son, everything you say is true. The strength is gone from my legs and my feet, and my arms no longer flash out quick from the shoulder. If only I were young again, and my strength still whole, as in the days when the Epeans buried lord Amarynceus at Buprasium, and his sons set out games in his honor! No man could match me there — not of the Epeans, nor the Pylians themselves, nor the brave Aetolians. In boxing I beat Clytomedes, son of Enops; in wrestling, Ancaeus of Pleuron, who stood up against me. I outran Iphiclus, fast as he was, and I threw the spear past both Phyleus and Polydorus. Only in the chariot race did the two sons of Actor get the better of me, crowding me with their numbers, sore that the greatest prizes were still to come. They were twins: one held the reins, steady, while the other plied the whip. That was the man I was. But these things are for younger men now; I must bow to bitter old age, though once I shone among heroes. Go on, then — honor your friend with the games. This gift I take with gladness, and my heart is warm that you remember me, and remember the honor that is due me among the Greeks. May the gods give you grace in return, in full measure.”

When the son of Peleus had heard out all of Nestor’s thanks, he went back through the gathered Greeks and set out the prizes for the hard art of boxing. He led out a sturdy mule and tied her in the middle of the ring — six years old, unbroken, and a mule is hardest of all to break at that age. That was for the winner; for the loser, a two-handled cup. He stood and spoke among the Greeks: “Son of Atreus, and you other Greeks — let our two best men stand up and box for these prizes, hands high. The man Apollo grants the longer wind, and the Greeks acknowledge the winner, takes the mule home to his own hut; the loser carries off the two-handled cup.”

At that a man rose — big, and brave, and a skilled boxer — Epeius, son of Panopeus. He laid his hand on the mule and said: “Let the man who’ll settle for the cup come forward — for the mule, I tell you, no one but me is leading away. I’m the best boxer here. Isn’t it enough that I fall short on the battlefield? No man can be good at everything. But this I’ll say plainly, and it will come true: I’ll smash whoever fights me, I’ll break his bones. Better have his people standing by in a crowd to carry him off when I’m done.”

They all sat silent. Only one man rose against him: Euryalus, son of Mecisteus, who was the son of Talaus — Mecisteus, who once went to Thebes for the funeral games after the fall of Oedipus, and beat every son of Cadmus there. Diomedes, the son of Tydeus, was Euryalus’s second, cheering him on, longing with all his heart to see him win. He buckled the belt around his waist and gave him well-cut thongs of ox-hide. The two men, girded, stepped into the middle of the ring, and squared off, and threw up their heavy fists at each other. There was a terrible grinding of jaws, and the sweat ran from every limb. Then Epeius came in and caught Euryalus on the cheek as he glanced aside, and his legs went out from under him on the spot. As a fish leaps clear of the water in the weed-strewn shallows when the North Wind ruffles the surface, and the dark wave takes it back — so Euryalus leaped at the blow. But great-hearted Epeius caught him in his arms and set him on his feet, and his companions gathered round and led him out of the ring, his legs dragging, his head lolling to one side, spitting out clots of blood. They sat him down among them, dazed, and went themselves to fetch the two-handled cup.

Then the son of Peleus set out the prizes for the third contest, the hard art of wrestling, and showed them to the Greeks. For the winner, a great tripod for the fire, which the Greeks valued among themselves at twelve oxen. For the loser, a woman skilled in fine work, valued at four oxen. He stood and spoke: “Stand up, the two who will try this contest.” And up rose great Ajax, the son of Telamon, and Odysseus rose too, full of cunning. The two girded themselves and stepped into the ring, and gripped each other in their strong arms like the crossed rafters a master builder pitches in a high house to hold out the wind. Their backbones cracked under the strain of those mighty arms, and the sweat poured down. Welts rose red along their ribs and shoulders, but they wrestled on, straining for victory and the tripod. Odysseus could not throw Ajax, nor Ajax Odysseus — Odysseus was too solid. But when the Greeks were tiring of the long contest, the great Ajax, son of Telamon, said: “Son of Laertes, resourceful Odysseus — lift me, or I’ll lift you, and let Zeus settle the rest.”

And he heaved him up. But Odysseus did not forget his cunning: he kicked Ajax behind the knee, buckled his legs, and dropped him on his back, and came down on his chest — and the watchers stared in wonder. Then Odysseus tried to lift Ajax in turn, and stirred him just clear of the ground, but could not heave him up; his own knee gave, and the two of them went down side by side and were caked with the dust. They sprang up to grapple a third time — but Achilles rose and held them back: “No more — don’t wear each other down with this hard punishment. Victory goes to you both. Take equal prizes, and go, and let the other Greeks have their turn.” They heard him gladly and obeyed, wiped the dust from their bodies, and put their tunics back on.

Then the son of Peleus set out the prizes for the footrace. First, a mixing-bowl of worked silver, holding six measures, the loveliest thing of its kind in all the world — the work of master craftsmen of Sidon, carried over the misty sea by Phoenician traders, who had given it to Thoas; Euneus, son of Jason, had passed it to Patroclus as the ransom for Priam’s son Lycaon; and now Achilles set it down as a prize in his friend’s honor, for the swiftest runner. For second, a great ox, fat and well-fed; for last, half a bar of gold. He stood and spoke: “Stand up, the men who will try this contest.” And up rose swift Ajax, son of Oileus, and resourceful Odysseus, and Antilochus, Nestor’s son, the fastest of all the young men of his day. They lined up side by side, and Achilles showed them the mark. From the start the son of Oileus shot to the front, and right on his heels came Odysseus — as close as the weaving-rod rides to a woman’s breast when she draws the thread across the warp and holds it tight against herself; that close Odysseus ran, his feet falling in the prints before the dust could rise in them, his breath on the back of Ajax’s head. All the Greeks shouted for him as he strained to win. But as they came into the last of the course, Odysseus prayed in his heart to bright-eyed Athena: “Hear me, goddess — be a good help to my feet.” So he prayed, and Pallas Athena heard him and made his limbs light, his legs and his arms. And just as they were about to lunge for the prize, Athena tripped Ajax in his stride, where the dung lay strewn from the bellowing oxen swift Achilles had slaughtered for Patroclus — and his mouth and his nostrils were filled with the filth. So resourceful Odysseus, getting in front, carried off the silver bowl, and Ajax took the ox and stood holding it by the horn, spitting out the dung, and said to the Greeks: “Ah, no — the goddess fouled my running. She stands by Odysseus like a mother, and always has.” And they all laughed at him, hard.

Then Antilochus carried off the last prize, smiling, and said to them all: “You see it again, friends — the gods still honor the older men. Ajax is a little older than I am, but Odysseus belongs to an earlier generation altogether — and yet they say his is a green old age. It’s no light thing for a Greek to race him; only Achilles.” So he spoke, and paid a compliment to the swift-footed son of Peleus, and Achilles answered: “Antilochus — that praise won’t go unpaid. I’ll add a half-bar of gold to your prize.” And he put it in his hands, and Antilochus took it gladly.

Then the son of Peleus brought out into the ring a long spear, a helmet, and a shield — the gear that had been Sarpedon’s, that Patroclus stripped from him. He stood and spoke: “Let two champions stand up — the best — and arm themselves, take up the cutting bronze, and try each other before the whole company. Whichever of the two first reaches the other’s flesh, through the armor, and draws dark blood, to him I’ll give this fine Thracian sword, silver-studded, that I took from Asteropaeus; the armor the two will hold in common, and I’ll set a good meal before them both in my hut.” At that great Ajax, son of Telamon, rose, and mighty Diomedes, son of Tydeus, rose too. When they had armed, each on his own side of the ring, they came together in the middle, hungry to fight, the glint of it terrible in their eyes, and the Greeks marveled to see them. Three times they closed and lunged, and three times they tried to strike. Ajax drove his spear through the round shield of Diomedes, but did not reach the flesh — the breastplate behind it held. Then the son of Tydeus, over the top of the great shield, kept reaching again and again for the throat of Ajax with the point of his spear. And the Greeks, afraid for Ajax, called out for them to stop and to share the prizes between them. But Achilles gave the great sword to the son of Tydeus, with its sheath and the well-cut sword-belt.

Next the son of Peleus brought out a lump of pig-iron, the unworked mass that mighty Eetion used to hurl, until Achilles killed him and carried it off among the rest of the spoils in his ships. He stood and spoke: “Stand up, the men who will try this contest. Win this, and a man will have iron enough to last him five whole years, however far his good fields lie from a town. His shepherd and his plowman won’t have to make the journey for it — he’ll have his own store at home.” At that rose the two strong men Polypoetes and Leonteus, and Ajax, son of Telamon, and noble Epeius. They took their stand in turn. Epeius gripped the mass, swung it, and let it fly — and set all the Greeks laughing, it fell so short. Then Leonteus, of the line of Ares, threw. Third, Ajax, son of Telamon, hurled it past every mark made yet. But when mighty Polypoetes took the iron, he sent it as far past the others as a herdsman flings his throwing-stick to wheel his cattle in. The whole crowd roared, and his companions carried the prize off for him to his ship.

Then for the archers he set out dark iron — ten double axes and ten single. He raised a ship’s mast far off in the sand and tethered a fluttering dove to it by the foot with a fine cord, and that was the mark. “Whoever hits the dove,” he said, “takes all ten double axes home; whoever misses the bird but hits the cord has shot worse, and takes the single axes.” Up rose lord Teucer, and Meriones, the strong squire of Idomeneus, rose too. They shook lots in a bronze helmet, and Teucer’s came out first. He let his arrow fly at once — but he had not promised Apollo, the lord of the bow, a glad sacrifice of first-born lambs, and so he missed the bird; the god begrudged him that. But he struck the cord that tethered her, close by the foot, and the sharp arrow cut it clean through, so the cord swung down toward the ground and the dove shot up into the sky, and the Greeks shouted. Then Meriones, who had his own arrow ready while Teucer aimed, snatched the bow from his hand, and at once vowed to Apollo, the lord of the bow, a glad sacrifice of first-born lambs. High up under the clouds he saw the dove still circling, and as she wheeled he struck her clean through the wing; the arrow passed through and came back down and stood in the ground at his feet, while the bird settled on the mast with her head drooping and her thick feathers slack, and the life went out of her, and she dropped, far off, from the mast. The people watched, and marveled. Meriones took all ten double axes, and Teucer carried the single ones away to his ships.

Last, the son of Peleus brought in a long spear and a cauldron, untouched by fire, chased with a flower-pattern, worth one ox. The spear-throwers stood up: Agamemnon, son of Atreus, lord of men, and Meriones, the strong squire of Idomeneus. But swift-footed Achilles spoke: “Son of Atreus — we know how far you surpass all others, and how far the strongest you are at the throw. So take this prize back to your ships, and let us give the spear to Meriones — if it pleases you. That, at least, is what I would wish.” And Agamemnon, lord of men, did not refuse him. Achilles gave the bronze spear to Meriones, and put the splendid cauldron, Agamemnon’s prize, into the hands of the herald Talthybius.
