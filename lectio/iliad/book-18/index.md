# The Iliad, Book XVIII

The Shield of Achilles

So they fought on like a blaze of fire. Meanwhile the fast runner Antilochus reached Achilles, where he sat by his tall ships, already sensing what had truly come to pass. Heavy at heart, he said to himself: “Why are the Greeks streaming back over the plain to the ships again, driven in panic? I’m afraid the gods are about to bring on me the grief my mother once warned of — that while I still lived, the bravest of the Myrmidons would fall to the Trojans and leave the light of the sun. It must be true: the brave son of Menoetius is dead. The fool — I told him to beat the fire off the ships and come straight back, and not to fight Hector head-on.”

While he was turning this over, the son of Nestor came up to him in tears and told him the bitter news: “Son of brave Peleus, I bring you terrible word — I wish it weren’t so. Patroclus is dead. They are fighting over his naked body. Hector has his armor.”

A black cloud of grief closed over Achilles. He scooped up the grimy dust in both hands and poured it over his head, fouling his handsome face, blackening his clean tunic with ash. He threw his whole great length down in the dirt and lay there, tearing at his hair with his hands. The captive women that he and Patroclus had taken cried out in their grief and ran from the tents to him, beating their breasts, their knees buckling under them. And Antilochus knelt over him, weeping, holding both his hands as he groaned — afraid he might cut his own throat with a blade.

Then Achilles let out a terrible cry, and his honored mother heard him, far down in the sea’s depths where she sat beside her aged father. She cried out in answer, and the goddesses gathered to her, all the daughters of Nereus who live at the bottom of the sea — Glauce and Thalia and Cymodoce, Nesaia, Speo, Thoe, and dark-eyed Halie; Cymothoe, Actaea, and Limnorea; Melite, Iaera, Amphithoe, and Agave; Doto and Proto, Pherusa and Dynamene; Dexamene, Amphinome, and Callianeira; Doris and Panope and the famous Galatea; Nemertes, Apseudes, and Callianassa; Clymene, Ianeira, and Ianassa; Maera, Oreithyia, and lovely-haired Amatheia, and all the other Nereids of the deep sea. The shining cave filled with them, and they all beat their breasts while Thetis led the lament.

“Listen, sisters, daughters of Nereus,” she cried, “so you may know the full weight of my sorrow. I am wretched, wretched, the mother of the best of men. I bore him strong and faultless, a hero among heroes, and he shot up like a young tree. I raised him like a plant in a rich garden and sent him out in his ships to Troy to fight the Trojans — and I will never welcome him home to the house of Peleus. As long as he lives and sees the light of the sun, he lives in pain, and I can’t help him, though I go to him. Still I will go, to see my dear child and learn what grief has reached him, even now, while he keeps out of the fighting.”

She left the cave, and her sisters followed, weeping, and the waves of the sea parted around them. When they came to the rich plain of Troy, they climbed up out of the water one after another onto the sand, where the Myrmidons’ ships were drawn up close around Achilles. His mother came to him as he lay groaning, cradled his head in her hands, and spoke through her tears: “My child, why are you weeping? What grief has reached your heart? Tell me — don’t hide it. Zeus has surely given you everything you prayed for, when you lifted your hands and begged that the Greeks be penned against their ships and feel your loss bitterly.”

With a deep groan swift-footed Achilles answered: “Mother, yes — the Olympian has done all that for me. But what good is it to me now, when my dear companion Patroclus is dead, the man I honored above all the rest, as much as my own life? I’ve lost him. And Hector, who killed him, has stripped that splendid armor, that wonder to look at, which the gods gave Peleus on the day they put you in a mortal man’s bed. I wish you had stayed among the immortal sea-nymphs, and Peleus had married a mortal woman. Now you will have endless grief in your heart for a dead son you can never welcome home — because I will not live, I will not walk among men, unless Hector is run through by my spear first and pays for stripping the life from Patroclus, son of Menoetius.”

Thetis answered him, weeping: “Then your own death is near, my child. For your doom comes hard on the heels of Hector’s.”

And swift-footed Achilles, in his great grief, said: “Then let me die at once — since I was not there to save my friend when he was killed. He fell far from home, and he needed me, and I was not there to fight for him. I will never go back to the land of my fathers. I was no light at all to Patroclus, or to all the others Hector has killed — I sat by my ships, a useless weight on the earth, though there is no Greek my equal in war, whatever better men there are in council. So let strife vanish from gods and from men — and anger, that drives even a level-headed man to fury, anger that wells up in a man’s chest sweeter than dripping honey and spreads through him like smoke. That is how Agamemnon angered me. But let it be — it’s done, it’s behind us; I’ll force my heart to bow to it, hard as it is. I’m going now, to find the man who killed the one I loved — to find Hector. And I will take my death whenever Zeus and the other gods choose to send it. Not even Heracles, the man Zeus loved most, escaped death; fate and the bitter anger of Hera brought him down. So I too will lie still, when I die, if the same doom is set for me. But until then, let me win glory — let me make some Trojan woman, some daughter of Dardanus, wipe the tears from her soft cheeks with both hands and sob out loud, so they all know I have stayed out of the war long enough. Don’t try to hold me back, mother, much as you love me. You will not move me.”

Silver-footed Thetis answered: “What you say is true, my child. It is a fine thing to keep your comrades from ruin. But your beautiful armor is in Trojan hands; Hector wears it himself and struts in it — though not for long, I tell you, for his own death is close. Only do not go into the press of battle until you see me come back. At dawn, with the rising sun, I will be here, and I will bring you fine new armor from Hephaestus the lord.”

With that she turned from her son and said to her sea-sisters: “Dive now into the wide lap of the sea and go to the house of our father, the old sea-god. Tell him everything. I am going up to high Olympus, to Hephaestus the master craftsman, to ask him for a suit of glorious armor for my son.” At once they slipped beneath the waves, and silver-footed Thetis went on her way to fetch the armor for her son.

While her feet carried her up to Olympus, the Greeks were running with great cries before man-killing Hector, all the way back to the ships and the Hellespont. They could not pull the body of Patroclus out of range of the weapons raining down on it, for Hector, son of Priam, had caught up with it again, men and horsemen both, like a sheet of roaring fire. Three times glorious Hector seized the body by the feet, straining to drag it off, shouting to the Trojans; and three times the two Ajaxes, clothed in fighting fury, beat him back from it. But he held his ground, sure of himself, now charging into the thick of the fight, now planting his feet and shouting — and never giving an inch. As shepherds in the hills cannot drive a starving lion off a carcass, so the two Ajaxes could not frighten Hector, son of Priam, away from the body.

And now he would have dragged it off and won undying glory, if Iris, swift as the wind, had not come racing from Olympus to the son of Peleus, telling him to arm. Hera had sent her, in secret, without the knowledge of Zeus or the other gods. She came close and said: “Up, son of Peleus, most terrifying of men. Save Patroclus — it is for him this savage fight rages now by the ships. They are killing one another, the Greeks defending the dead body, the Trojans straining to drag it off to windy Troy. Hector is wildest of them all, mad to hack the head from the shoulders and fix it on a stake of the wall. Up, and lie here no longer. The shame of it, if Patroclus should become a plaything for the dogs of Troy. The disgrace is yours, if his body is defiled.”

Swift-footed Achilles said: “Iris — which of the gods sent you to me?” And Iris answered: “It was Hera, the honored wife of Zeus. The son of Cronus does not know I came, nor do any of the immortals who live on snow-capped Olympus.”

Swift-footed Achilles said: “But how can I go into the fighting? They have my armor. And my mother told me not to arm until I saw her come back; she promised to bring me fine armor from Hephaestus. I know of no one whose gear I could put on — except the shield of Ajax, son of Telamon, and surely he is up in the front rank, working his spear over the body of dead Patroclus.”

Iris said: “We know well your armor was taken. But go as you are — just show yourself at the trench, so the Trojans see you and shrink back, and break off the fighting. That will give the worn-out Greeks a moment to breathe, brief as it may be in the heat of battle.”

Iris said this and was gone. Then Achilles, dear to Zeus, rose up, and Athena flung her tasseled storm-shield, the aegis, around his strong shoulders. The bright goddess wrapped his head in a golden cloud and kindled a blaze of fire from it. As when smoke rises into the high sky from a city far off on an island, ringed by enemies — all day long its men fight it out from the walls, and at sundown the beacon-fires flare up in a line, the glow shooting high for the neighbors to see, in hope that they will come by ship to fight off the ruin — so the light blazed from Achilles’ head into the sky. He went from the wall and stood at the trench, but he did not mix with the Greeks, for he obeyed his mother’s word.

There he stood, and shouted, and from far off Athena raised her voice with his, and panic past all telling broke over the Trojans. Sharp as a trumpet’s blast when a city is ringed by murderous foes, so sharp and brazen was the voice of the grandson of Aeacus. And when the Trojans heard that brazen cry, the heart went out of them; their long-maned horses wheeled the chariots around, sensing the ruin to come, and their drivers stood aghast at the steady fire blazing over the head of great-hearted Achilles — the fire the gray-eyed goddess had kindled.

Three times brilliant Achilles shouted across the trench, and three times the Trojans and their famous allies were thrown into confusion. There and then twelve of their best men died under their own chariots and on their own spears. And the Greeks, overjoyed, drew Patroclus out of range of the weapons and laid him on a litter. His comrades stood around him, mourning, and among them was swift-footed Achilles, weeping hot tears at the sight of his loyal friend lying torn on the bier. He had sent him out with horses and chariot into the fighting — and would never welcome him back.

Then Hera sent the tireless sun, unwilling, down into the streams of Ocean. So the sun set, and the Greeks had a rest from the grinding work of war.

On the Trojan side, when they had broken off the fight, they unyoked their horses and gathered in assembly before thinking of supper. They stayed on their feet — not a man dared sit down, for fear had hold of all of them, now that Achilles had shown himself after staying so long out of the bitter fighting. Polydamas, son of Panthous, was the first to speak. He alone of them could see ahead and behind, weigh past against future. He was Hector’s friend, born on the same night, but better with words than with the spear. With clear good will he spoke:

“Think it over carefully, my friends. My counsel is this: let us go back to the city now, and not wait here by the ships for dawn out on the open plain, far from our walls. As long as this man was at odds with Agamemnon, the Greeks were easier to face, and I was glad enough to camp by their ships, hoping to take them. But now I am badly afraid of the swift son of Peleus. The way his spirit drives him, he will not be content to stay on the plain, where Trojans and Greeks share the fighting between them; he will fight for the city itself, for our wives. Let us pull back. Hear me, for this is how it will go: night, for now, holds off the son of Peleus. But if he finds us still here in the morning, when he comes out in his armor, then we will know him for what he is — and the man who gets back to Troy alive will count himself lucky, for the dogs and vultures will feed on many a Trojan. May that word never reach my ears. If we do as I say, hard as it is, we will keep our strength through the night in counsel, and the great gates and their tall doors will guard the city. At dawn we will arm and man the walls — and it will go badly for him if he comes up from the ships to fight us there. He will go back when he has worn his horses out, driving them every which way under our walls. He will never have the heart to force his way inside, and he will never sack it. The dogs will eat him first.”

But Hector glared at him and said: “Polydamas, I don’t like what you’re saying — telling us to fall back and shut ourselves in the city. Haven’t you had enough of being penned behind walls? There was a time when the whole world spoke of Priam’s city for its gold and its bronze — but our treasures are gone from our houses now, sold off to Phrygia and lovely Maeonia, since Zeus turned against us. And now, when the son of crooked Cronus has granted me glory here, to pin the Greeks against the sea by their ships — now is no time to peddle that kind of talk to the people. Not one Trojan will follow you; I won’t allow it. Do as I say, all of you. Take your supper through the ranks, keep the watch, every man of you stay awake. Any Trojan who frets over his wealth — let him gather it and hand it out to the army; better our own men have the good of it than the Greeks. At first light we arm and take the fight to the ships. And if it’s true that brilliant Achilles has risen up to defend them, so much the worse for him. I will not run from him. I will stand and face him, to win or to fall. Ares fights for both sides alike — and the killer is often killed.”

So Hector spoke, and the Trojans — the fools — roared their approval, for Pallas Athena had stripped them of their wits. They cheered Hector and his ruinous plan, and not a man had a word for the wise counsel of Polydamas. They took their supper through the camp. And all night long the Greeks mourned Patroclus, with the son of Peleus leading the lament. He laid his man-killing hands on his friend’s breast and groaned over him again and again, like a bearded lion whose cubs a hunter has stolen from some thick wood; the lion comes back to find them gone and ranges through the glens, hunting the man’s track, mad with fury — so Achilles groaned, and spoke among his Myrmidons: “What an empty promise I made, that day I tried to comfort the old hero Menoetius in his own house. I told him I would bring his brave son back to Opoeis covered in glory, with his share of the spoils, once we had sacked Troy. But Zeus does not grant men everything they hope for. The same earth here at Troy is fated to drink the blood of us both — for I will never come home either, never be welcomed by the old horseman Peleus, or by my mother Thetis; this ground will cover me too. But now, Patroclus, since I go down into the earth after you, I will not bury you until I have brought back the armor and the head of Hector, who killed you. And I will cut the throats of twelve noble sons of Troy before your pyre, in my fury at your death. Until then you will lie here by my curved ships just as you are, and the women of Troy and Dardania, the ones we took with the spear when we sacked rich cities, will weep over you night and day.”

Then Achilles told his men to set a great cauldron on the fire and wash the clotted blood from Patroclus’ body. They set a tripod of bath-water over the blaze and heaped wood under it, and the flames licked the cauldron’s belly until the water was hot. When it boiled, they washed the body, anointed it with oil, and filled its wounds with a salve nine years old. Then they laid him on a bier, wrapped him in soft linen from head to foot, and spread a clean white cloth on top. So all night long the Myrmidons gathered around Achilles, mourning Patroclus.

Then Zeus said to Hera, his wife and his sister: “So you’ve had your way after all, ox-eyed lady Hera — you’ve roused swift-footed Achilles. Anyone would think the long-haired Greeks were your own children.” And Hera answered: “Dread son of Cronus, what a thing to say. Even a mortal, who knows so much less than we do, will do all he can for another man. Was I, then — the highest of the goddesses, both by my birth and as your wife, you who rule all the gods — not to plot ruin for the Trojans in my anger?”

So the two of them talked. Meanwhile silver-footed Thetis came to the house of Hephaestus — a house that never dies, set with stars, the finest of all the immortal halls, built of bronze by the lame god’s own hands. She found him sweating over his bellows, hard at work, finishing twenty tripods to stand around the wall of his hall. He had set golden wheels under each one, so they could roll off to the gatherings of the gods on their own and roll home again — a marvel to see. They were nearly done; only the finely worked handles were left to fit, and he was hammering out the rivets. As he worked at this, silver-footed Thetis came to him. Charis of the bright headdress, the lovely wife of the famous lame god, saw her and came forward, took her by the hand, and said: “Thetis, honored and always welcome — what brings you to our house? You come so seldom. Come inside, and let me set something before you.”

She led the way in and seated Thetis on a beautiful silver-studded chair, with a footstool beneath her feet, and called out: “Hephaestus, come here — Thetis needs you.” And the famous lame god answered: “Then it is a goddess I honor and revere who has come to my house — the one who saved me when I was in pain after my great fall, when my own shameless mother wanted me out of sight because I was lame. I would have suffered badly then, if Eurynome and Thetis had not taken me to their breasts — Eurynome, daughter of Ocean that flows in a ring around the world. Nine years I stayed with them in their cave, working all kinds of fine bronze — brooches, curved armlets, cups, and chains — with the great stream of Ocean roaring and foaming past us, and no one knew, god or man, except Thetis and Eurynome who saved me. Now Thetis has come to my house, and I must pay back everything I owe her for my life. Set out a fine welcome for her, while I put away my bellows and my tools.”

With that the huge, hulking god heaved himself up from the anvil, his thin legs working busily under him. He set the bellows back from the fire and gathered his tools into a silver chest, then sponged the sweat from his face and hands, his thick neck and hairy chest. He pulled on his tunic, took up a stout staff, and limped to the door. Golden serving-women bustled around to support their master — they looked like living girls, with minds and voices and strength, and all the skills the gods had taught them. They moved busily to hold their master up, and he limped near to where Thetis was, sat down on a shining chair, took her hand, and said: “Thetis, honored and always welcome — what brings you to our house? You come so seldom. Tell me what you want; my heart says do it, if it can be done, if it is the kind of thing that can be done at all.”

Thetis answered him through her tears: “Hephaestus, is there another goddess on Olympus who has had to carry such grief in her heart as Zeus has loaded on me? Of all the sea-goddesses, he forced me alone to submit to a mortal man, Peleus, son of Aeacus — and I endured a mortal’s bed, sorely against my will, and now he lies at home, worn out with age. And that is not the end of it. He gave me a son to bear and raise, a hero among heroes, and he shot up like a young tree. I tended him like a plant in a rich garden and sent him out in his ships to Troy to fight the Trojans — and I will never welcome him home to the house of Peleus. As long as he lives and sees the light of the sun, he lives in pain, and I cannot help him, though I go to him. The girl the Greeks chose out for him as his prize, King Agamemnon tore from his hands — and he was wasting his heart out with grief for her, when the Trojans penned the Greeks against the sterns of their ships and would not let them out. The elders of the Greeks pleaded with him and named him splendid gifts. He would not go to their rescue himself — but he put his own armor on Patroclus and sent him into battle with a great force behind him. All day they fought at the Scaean gates, and would have sacked the city that very day, if Apollo had not given Hector the glory of killing the brave son of Menoetius, who had done the Trojans so much harm in the front line. That is why I have come to your knees — to beg you, if you will, to give my son, whose death is near, a helmet and a shield, fine greaves with ankle-clasps, and a breastplate. For he lost his own when his loyal friend went down to the Trojans — and now he lies in the dirt, his heart broken.”

And Hephaestus answered: “Take heart, and let none of this trouble you. I wish I could hide him from cruel death when his hour comes, as surely as I can give him armor so splendid that everyone who sees it will be amazed.”

So he said, and left her there and went back to his bellows, turning them to the fire and ordering them to work. Twenty bellows blew on the crucibles, breathing every kind of blast — fierce gusts to drive the work when he needed them, gentler ones as he willed it, whatever served the labor in hand. He threw tough bronze into the fire, and tin, and precious gold and silver. He set his great anvil on its block, took the heavy hammer in one hand and the tongs in the other.

First he made the shield — huge and strong, worked all over, and bound around the rim with a bright triple band; and the strap was silver. The shield itself was five layers thick, and across its face he worked his cunning into one wonder after another.

On it he made the earth, and the sky, and the sea; the tireless sun and the moon at the full; and all the stars that crown the heavens — the Pleiades, the Hyades, the great strength of Orion, and the Bear, which men also call the Wagon: it wheels in one place, watching Orion, and alone has no share in the baths of Ocean.

On it he made two cities of mortal men, both beautiful to see. In one there were weddings and feasts: by the light of torches they were leading the brides from their chambers through the city, and the wedding-song rose loud, and young men whirled in the dance to the cry of flutes and lyres, while the women stood in their doorways, each marveling at the sight. But the people had crowded into the marketplace, where a quarrel had broken out: two men were contending over the blood-price for a man who had been killed. One swore before the people that he had paid it in full; the other denied he had taken anything. Both were pressing to have the matter settled by a judge. The crowd shouted for both sides, taking their parts, and the heralds held them back. The elders sat on polished stones in a sacred circle, holding in their hands the staves of the clear-voiced heralds; with these they rose, each in turn, and gave their judgment. And in the middle lay two talents of gold, to be given to the one among them who spoke the judgment most justly of all.

Around the other city sat two armies, their armor flashing. They were divided between two plans — to storm the place and sack it, or to take half of all the wealth it held. But the townsmen would not give in, and were arming for an ambush. Their wives and little children stood guard on the walls, and with them the old men, past fighting; the rest went out, with Ares and Pallas Athena at their head — both worked in gold, golden in their robes, great and beautiful in their armor as gods should be, and standing out above the smaller mortals who followed them. When they reached the place chosen for the ambush, a riverbed where the herds came down to drink, they settled there, hidden, sheathed in bright bronze. Two scouts kept watch some way off for the coming of sheep and cattle. Soon the animals came, with two herdsmen behind them playing their pipes, never dreaming of the trap. The men in ambush saw them, rushed out, and quickly cut off the herds of cattle and the fine flocks of white sheep, and killed the herdsmen too. When the besiegers, sitting in council before their walls, heard the great uproar among the cattle, they sprang up behind their horses and came at a gallop, and soon reached the place. They drew up for battle along the banks of the river and hurled their bronze-tipped spears at one another. And among them moved Strife and Uproar, and deadly Fate — she had one man freshly wounded in her grip, and another not yet hit, and a third already dead, and she was dragging him by the feet through the press; the cloak across her shoulders was soaked red with the blood of men. They closed and fought like living men, and hauled away each other’s dead.

On it he set a soft fallow field, broad and rich, three times plowed; and many plowmen were driving their teams up and down across it, turning at each end. Every time they came back to the headland, a man would meet them and put a cup of honey-sweet wine in their hands, and they would turn down the furrows again, working toward the far edge of the deep field. The ground turned black behind them, the look of plowed earth — though it was made of gold. That was the wonder of his craft.

On it he set a king’s estate, where reapers were cutting the harvest with sharp sickles in their hands. Armful after armful of grain fell along the swath; others bound it with twisted straw. There were three binders standing ready, and behind them boys gathered up the cut grain in their arms and kept bringing it to be bound. And among them the king stood in silence by the swath, leaning on his staff, glad at heart. Off to one side, under an oak, heralds were making a feast: they had killed a great ox and were dressing it, while the women sprinkled white barley in handfuls for the workers’ meal.

On it he set a vineyard heavy with grapes, beautiful, all of gold, with the clusters hanging dark and the vines staked up on poles of silver. He ran a ditch of dark enamel around it and a fence of tin, and left one path leading in, the one the pickers took at the vintage. Young men and girls, light-hearted, were carrying off the sweet fruit in woven baskets, and in their midst a boy played a clear-toned lyre and sang the lovely Linus-song in his thin sweet voice; and they followed, beating the ground in time, singing and shouting as they skipped along.

On it he set a herd of straight-horned cattle. The cows were worked in gold and tin, and they came lowing from the farmyard to pasture along a murmuring river, by the swaying reeds. Four golden herdsmen walked beside the cattle, and nine quick dogs ran with them. But two terrible lions had brought down a bellowing bull among the lead cows, and were dragging him off as he roared, while the dogs and the young men gave chase. The lions had torn through the bull’s tough hide and were gulping his blood and his inner parts, and the herdsmen kept urging the fast dogs on, in vain; the dogs would not close their jaws on the lions but stood near, barking, then shrank away.

On it the famous lame god set a meadow, broad and lovely, in a beautiful mountain glen, where a great flock of white sheep grazed, with their pens and roofed huts and folds.

And on it the famous lame god worked a dancing-floor, like the one Daedalus once made for lovely-haired Ariadne in broad Cnossus. There young men and girls worth many cattle were dancing, holding one another by the wrist. The girls wore fine linen, and the young men wore well-woven tunics with a soft sheen of oil; the girls had lovely garlands, and the young men carried golden daggers on silver belts. Now they would run in a ring on knowing feet, light and easy, the way a potter sits and tries his wheel to see if it will spin; and now they would run in lines toward one another. A great crowd stood around the lovely dance, enjoying it, and among them a singer sang to his lyre, while two tumblers spun and led the dance through the middle of them.

And around the very rim of the strong shield he set the great River Ocean.

When he had finished the shield, huge and strong, he made a breastplate brighter than the blaze of fire, and a heavy helmet, fitted close to the temples, beautifully worked, with a golden crest on top; and he made greaves of beaten tin. And when the famous lame god had finished all the armor, he gathered it up and laid it before the mother of Achilles. And she swooped down like a hawk from snowy Olympus, carrying the gleaming armor from the house of Hephaestus.
