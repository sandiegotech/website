The Iliad, Book XI

The Day of Agamemnon

Book XI11 of 24

Dawn rose from her bed beside Tithonus to bring her light to gods and men, and Zeus sent Strife down to the fast Greek ships — grim Strife, with the war-sign of battle in her hands. She took her stand at the great black hull of Odysseus’s ship, the one in the middle of the line, so her cry would carry both ways at once — to the huts of Telamonian Ajax at one end, and to those of Achilles at the other, for those two had drawn up their ships at the far edges, trusting their own strength. There she stood and let loose a cry, loud and shrill, and it poured courage into the heart of every Greek, the will to fight on without stopping. War was suddenly sweeter to them than the voyage home in the hollow ships.

The son of Atreus shouted to the Greeks to gird themselves, and he put on his own gleaming bronze. First he fastened the fine greaves around his shins, clasping them at the ankle with silver. Then he buckled on the breastplate Cinyras had once given him as a guest-gift, far off in Cyprus, when word came even there that the Greeks were about to sail for Troy. It had ten bands of dark blue enamel, twelve of gold, and twenty of tin; three blue serpents reared toward the neck on either side, like the rainbows the son of Cronus sets in the clouds as a sign to mortal men. Over his shoulders he slung the sword, studded with gold, its scabbard silver, hung from a chain of gold. He took up the great shield that covered his whole body, splendid to see, ringed with ten circles of bronze, twenty bosses of white tin on its face and one of dark blue at the center — and on that center a Gorgon’s head, glaring and terrible, with Rout and Terror flanking it. On his head he set a helmet, two-ridged, four-plumed, the horsehair nodding above it. He gripped two heavy bronze-shod spears, and the light off his armor leapt up to the sky like fire, while Hera and Athena thundered overhead in honor of the king of golden Mycenae.

Each man left his horses with his charioteer to wait in line at the trench, and went forward on foot in full armor, and a great roar climbed into the breaking day. The fighters reached the trench ahead of the horses, and the chariots came up behind. The son of Cronus stirred an evil din over them, and sent down dew thick with blood, for he was about to hurl many strong heads down to the house of the dead.

Across from them, on the rising ground of the plain, the Trojans gathered around great Hector and noble Polydamas, and Aeneas, whom the Trojans honored like a god, and the three sons of Antenor — Polybus, godlike Agenor, and young Acamas, beautiful as the immortals. Hector showed his round shield in the front rank, and like a baleful star that flashes for a moment through a tear in the clouds and is gone again under them, so he flickered — now blazing among the foremost, now among the last — and his bronze flashed like the lightning of Zeus who carries the storm-shield.

Like reapers driving their swathes toward each other across a rich man’s field of wheat or barley, the sheaves dropping thick under them, so the Trojans and the Greeks fell on one another and cut each other down. Neither side gave a thought to ruinous flight; they held even, fighting like wolves. And Strife rejoiced to watch them — she alone of the gods was there in the killing. The others kept apart, quiet in their own houses among the folds and valleys of Olympus, and all of them blamed the son of Cronus for meaning to hand the Trojans victory. But Father Zeus paid them no mind. He sat apart from the rest, alone in his glory, gazing down at the city of Troy and the Greek ships, at the glint of bronze, at the killers and the killed.

All morning, while the light grew, the spears bit on both sides and the men kept falling. But at the hour when a woodcutter in a mountain forest sets out his midday meal — his arms worn out from felling the tall trees, weariness on him, hunger for food in his heart — at that hour the Greeks broke the enemy line with a cry that ran down their whole front. Agamemnon led the way. First he killed Bienor, a leader of his people, and then his comrade and driver Oileus, who had jumped from the chariot to come straight at him; Agamemnon caught him full in the forehead with his spear, and the bronze visor of his helmet did not hold — the point drove through bronze and bone, and his brains were spattered inside, and he went down.

Agamemnon left them there, their chests bared to the sky, and went on to kill Isus and Antiphus, two sons of Priam — one a bastard, one trueborn — riding together in one chariot, the bastard at the reins, noble Antiphus fighting beside him. Achilles had once caught the pair of them on the slopes of Ida, tied them with fresh-cut willow shoots while they herded their flocks, and let them go for a ransom. Now the son of Atreus struck Isus in the chest above the nipple, and ran Antiphus through by the ear and pitched him from the chariot. He stripped their fine armor fast — and knew their faces, for he had seen them by the ships when Achilles brought them down from Ida. As a lion takes the helpless fawns of a running deer, snapping them up in his strong jaws and crushing the soft life out of them, and the doe, though she is near, can do nothing — terror runs through her, and she bolts through the thick brush, sweating, straining, away from the beast — so not one of the Trojans could help those two, for they themselves were running before the Greeks.

Then Agamemnon came on the two sons of Antimachus, Pisander and brave Hippolochus. Their father was the man who, more than any, had argued against giving Helen back to Menelaus — heavily bribed by Paris to say it. Now Agamemnon took his two sons, both in the one chariot, fighting to rein in their bolting team; the reins had slipped from their hands and the horses had gone wild. The son of Atreus sprang at them like a lion, and the two of them reached out from the chariot and begged: “Take us alive, son of Atreus, and you’ll have a rich ransom. Our father Antimachus has gold and bronze and worked iron stored up — he’ll pay you handsomely if he hears we’re alive at the Greek ships.”

So they pleaded, with tears, in soft words — and got back no soft answer. “If you are the sons of Antimachus,” Agamemnon said, “the man who once, in the Trojan council, urged that Menelaus and Odysseus, who had come to you as envoys, be killed on the spot and never let go home — then you’ll pay now for your father’s filthy crime.”

With that he drove Pisander from the chariot to the ground, the spear in his chest, and he fell flat on his back. Hippolochus leapt down to run, but Agamemnon cut him too — lopped off his hands, struck off his head, and sent it rolling through the press like a log. He left them there and went where the ranks were thickest, and the rest of the Greeks came after. Foot soldiers killed the foot soldiers driven before them, horsemen killed horsemen, and the trampling hooves beat a cloud of dust off the plain. King Agamemnon kept on, killing, shouting his men forward. As a fire falls on a dry forest and the wind whirls it everywhere, and the thickets shrivel and go down under the rush of the flame — so the heads of the running Trojans went down before Agamemnon, son of Atreus, and many a fine team dragged an empty chariot rattling down the lanes of war, missing the drivers who lay on the ground, dearer now to the vultures than to their wives.

Zeus drew Hector out of the spears and the dust, out of the carnage and the din. But the son of Atreus pressed on, roaring to the Greeks. Past the tomb of old Ilus, of the line of Dardanus, in the middle of the plain, past the wild fig tree they swept, always making for the city — Agamemnon still shouting, his hands fouled with gore. But at the Scaean gates and the oak tree they halted, and waited for the rest to come up. Out on the open plain the Trojans were still running like a herd of cattle scattered by a lion that has fallen on them in the dead of night — he springs at one, breaks her neck in his jaws, then laps the blood and tears out the guts — so Agamemnon harried them, always killing the last man as they fled headlong, and many were flung from their chariots face-first into the dust under his hand, for his spear raged on ahead of him.

But just as he was about to reach the high wall and the town, the father of gods and men came down from the sky and took his seat, thunderbolt in hand, on the peak of Ida of the many springs. He called golden-winged Iris and sent her with a word: “Go, swift Iris, and tell Hector this. As long as he sees Agamemnon out in front, raging, mowing down the ranks, let him hold back and order the others to bear the brunt. But the moment Agamemnon is wounded — by spear or by arrow — and climbs into his chariot, then I will hand Hector the strength to kill, all the way to the ships, until the sun goes down and the dark comes on.”

Iris obeyed. Down she came from the crests of Ida to sacred Troy, and found Hector, son of Priam, standing by his chariot and horses. She came near and said: “Hector, son of Priam, equal to Zeus in counsel — Father Zeus has sent me to you with this. As long as you see Agamemnon out in front, raging, cutting down the ranks, hold off from the fighting and send the rest in to take the worst of it. But the moment he’s wounded — by spear or arrow — and takes to his chariot, Zeus will give you the strength to kill, all the way to the ships, until the sun goes down and the dark comes on.”

Iris said it and was gone, and Hector leapt from his chariot to the ground in full armor, brandishing his two spears, ranging everywhere through the army, rousing the men to fight, waking the terrible work of war. They wheeled and stood and faced the Greeks, and the Greeks closed their own ranks to meet them. The line was set; they stood front to front — and Agamemnon, hungry to be first of all, pressed forward ahead of them.

Tell me now, you Muses who hold the halls of Olympus — which of the Trojans, or of their famous allies, was first to come against Agamemnon? It was Iphidamas, son of Antenor, a big man and a brave one, raised in fertile Thrace, the mother of flocks. His mother’s father Cisses brought him up from a child in his own house — Cisses, father of lovely Theano. When he came to the glory of manhood, Cisses tried to keep him there and gave him his daughter in marriage; but no sooner had he wed than word of the Greeks’ coming pulled him away, and he sailed with twelve ships of his own. He left the ships at Percote and came on to Troy by land. This was the man who now met the son of Atreus. When they were close, Agamemnon’s cast went wide. Iphidamas struck him on the belt below the breastplate and threw his whole weight behind it, trusting his strong arm — but the belt held; the point never got near, turned aside on the silver like lead. Agamemnon caught the spear in his hand, dragged it toward him with a lion’s fury, wrenched it from his grip, then drew his sword and struck him across the neck and dropped him. So he fell there and slept the sleep of bronze, poor man, killed defending his own people, far from the bride he had wed and never had joy of, though he had paid so much for her — a hundred cattle down, a thousand sheep and goats promised after, out of the countless flocks he owned. Agamemnon, son of Atreus, stripped him and carried the fine armor back into the Greek host.

When Coon, Antenor’s eldest son, saw it, grief darkened his eyes at the sight of his fallen brother. He came at Agamemnon from the side, unseen, spear in hand, and stabbed him in the middle of the arm, below the elbow, the bright point driving clean through. Agamemnon shuddered with the pain — but even then he did not quit the fight. He went for Coon with his wind-swift spear as the man was hauling his brother off by the foot — his own father’s son — and crying out to the best of his comrades for help; and Agamemnon caught him under his shield with the bronze and killed him, standing over the body of Iphidamas, and cut off his head there. So the sons of Antenor, at the hands of the son of Atreus, filled out their fate and went down to the house of Hades.

As long as the blood still ran warm from the wound, Agamemnon ranged the enemy ranks with spear and sword and great heaved stones. But once the blood stopped and the gash dried, sharp pains broke over him — like the piercing pangs the Eilithyiae send on a woman in labor, the goddesses of childbirth, daughters of Hera, keepers of bitter pain — so sharp were the pangs that broke over the son of Atreus. He climbed into his chariot and told his driver to make for the hollow ships, for the agony was on him; and with a great clear shout he called to the Greeks: “Friends, captains and counselors of the army — it falls to you now to fight off the ships, since Zeus will not let me battle the Trojans all day long.”

His driver lashed the horses toward the ships, and they flew on willingly, their chests white with foam, their bellies gray with dust, carrying the wounded king out of the war.

When Hector saw Agamemnon leave the field, he shouted to the Trojans and Lycians: “Trojans, Lycians, Dardanian fighters — be men, friends, remember your courage! Their best man is gone. Zeus has handed me a great prize. Drive your horses straight at the Greeks and win yourselves still greater glory.”

With those words he put fire into every man. As a hunter sets his white-toothed hounds on a wild boar or a lion, so Hector, the equal of Ares, set the proud Trojans on the Greeks; and full of fight he plunged in among the foremost and fell on the battle like a screaming squall that swoops down and lashes the wine-dark sea to fury.

Who, then, were the first and the last that Hector killed, in the hour Zeus gave him the glory? Asaeus first, then Autonous and Opites; Dolops, son of Clytius, and Opheltius, and Agelaus; Aesymnus, and Orus, and Hipponous steady in the fight. These were the captains he cut down, and then he fell on the ranks behind them. As when the west wind drives the white clouds of the south and beats them down in its blasting rage, and the swells roll big and the spray is flung high under the wandering wind — so thick were the heads that fell under Hector’s hand.

Now ruin would have come, with no way out, and the Greeks would have been driven in a rout back to their ships — if Odysseus had not called to Diomedes: “Son of Tydeus, what’s wrong with us, that we forget our fighting strength? Come here, friend, stand at my side. We’ll be shamed forever if Hector takes the ships.”

And strong Diomedes answered: “I’ll stand, and I’ll hold. But there’ll be little joy in it — Zeus means to give the Trojans the day, and not us.”

With that he struck Thymbraeus from his chariot to the ground, the spear in his left breast, while Odysseus killed Molion, the man’s squire. They left those two, done fighting now, and the pair of them tore through the Trojans like two wild boars that round on the hounds that hunt them — and the Greeks, fleeing before Hector, were glad of the breath it bought them.

Next they took two men with their chariot, the two sons of Merops of Percote, the best of all men at reading what is to come. He had forbidden his sons to go to the war, but they would not listen — their own death drew them on. Diomedes, son of Tydeus, killed them both and stripped them, and Odysseus killed Hippodamus and Hypeirochus.

And the son of Cronus, looking down from Ida, held the scales level between them, and they went on killing one another. The son of Tydeus speared Agastrophus, son of Paeon, through the hip — his chariot was not at hand to carry him off, so blind had his confidence made him; his squire had it back at a distance while he himself fought on foot among the front rank, until he lost his life. Hector was quick to see the havoc Diomedes and Odysseus were making, and he bore down on them with a shout, the Trojan ranks behind him. Brave Diomedes felt a chill at the sight, and said to Odysseus beside him: “Here comes great Hector — disaster rolling down on us. Stand fast, and we’ll take his charge where we are.”

He balanced his spear and let it fly, and he did not miss: he hit Hector full on the head, high on the helmet — but bronze turned bronze, and Hector was not touched, for the point was stopped by the visored helmet of three plates that Phoebus Apollo had given him. Hector sprang far back into the cover of his men, dropped to one knee, and braced himself with a heavy hand against the ground, and the dark came down over his eyes. While the son of Tydeus chased his own spear far through the front ranks to where it had stuck in the earth, Hector got his breath, swung back into his chariot, and drove into the crowd, and so escaped death. But Diomedes came after him with his spear and called: “Dog — you’ve slipped death again, close as it came. Phoebus Apollo saved you once more, the god you must pray to before you walk into the spears. But I’ll finish you yet, the next time we meet — if there’s any god who’ll stand by me too. For now I’ll go after whoever else I can reach.”

And he bent to strip the son of Paeon — but Paris, the husband of lovely Helen, was drawing on him with his bow, leaning against a pillar on the barrow men had raised long ago for Ilus, of the line of Dardanus, a lord of the old days. Diomedes had just lifted the breastplate from Agastrophus’s chest, the heavy helmet, the shield from his shoulders, when Paris pulled the string and let the arrow go — and it did not leave his hand for nothing. It pierced the flat of Diomedes’ right foot, drove clean through, and stuck in the ground. With a happy laugh Paris jumped up from his cover and crowed: “You’re hit! My shot was not wasted. I only wish I’d caught you in the gut and killed you — then the Trojans, who fear you the way goats fear a lion, might have had a rest from their troubles.”

But Diomedes, not shaken at all, answered him: “Archer — you with your bow, your foul tongue, your pretty hair, you ogler of girls — if you ever faced me man to man in armor, your bow and your arrows would do you no good at all. You scratched the flat of my foot, and you boast of it. I no more feel it than if a woman or a foolish child had struck me — a worthless coward’s dart raises a feeble wound. When I hit a man, it’s another matter: even a graze lays him out dead. His wife claws her cheeks, his children are left fatherless, and he rots there reddening the ground, with more vultures around him than women.”

He spoke, and Odysseus the spearman came up and stood over him as cover, and Diomedes sat down behind him and drew the arrow from his foot, and a sharp pain shot through his flesh as it came. Then he climbed into his chariot and told his driver to make for the hollow ships, for his heart was heavy.

Now Odysseus was alone. Not one of the Greeks stood with him; fear had taken them all. Troubled, he spoke to his own brave heart: “What is happening to me? It’s a bad thing to run from these numbers — but a worse thing to be caught alone, now that Zeus has scattered the rest in panic. Yet why do I argue it out with myself? I know well enough: the coward leaves the field, but the man of worth must hold his ground hard, whether he wounds or is wounded.”

While he turned it over, the Trojan ranks came on and closed around him — and they hemmed in their own grief. As hounds and strong young men set on a boar that breaks from his lair, gnashing his white tusks, and they press in from every side, and the gnashing rings out, and fierce as he is they hold their ground — so the Trojans crowded around Odysseus. First he lunged with his spear and wounded faultless Deiopites in the shoulder from above; then he killed Thoon and Ennomus; then he caught Chersidamas in the belly below the shield as the man dropped from his chariot, and he fell in the dust and clutched the earth in his hand. He left them and ran Charops, son of Hippasus, the brother of noble Socus, through with the spear. And Socus came to help his brother — a hero — and stood close and said: “Far-famed Odysseus, who can never get enough of tricks and toil — today you’ll either boast you killed both sons of Hippasus and stripped their armor, or you’ll fall on the end of my spear.”

With that he struck the shield of Odysseus. The spear punched through the shield and on through the worked breastplate and tore the flesh off his side — but Pallas Athena would not let it reach his guts. Odysseus knew the wound was not his death, and he gave ground and said to Socus: “Poor wretch, your end has come. You’ve stopped me fighting the Trojans for a moment — but now you’ll go down on my spear, and hand the glory to me and your soul to Hades of the fine horses.”

Socus had turned to run; and as he turned, the spear caught him in the back between the shoulders and drove on through his chest. He fell with a crash, and Odysseus stood over him and cried: “Socus, son of Hippasus the horse-tamer — death came too fast for you; you didn’t outrun it. Poor man — your father and mother won’t be there to close your eyes; the carrion birds will wrap you in the beating of their dark wings and tear you. But if I die, the Greeks will give me my burial.”

He drew Socus’s heavy spear out of his flesh and out of the shield, and the blood spurted when it came free, and his heart sank at the sight of it. When the Trojans saw Odysseus bleeding, they raised a great shout and rushed at him together, and he gave ground and called his comrades to him. Three times he shouted, as loud as a man’s head can hold, and three times Menelaus heard him; and at once he turned to Ajax beside him and said: “Ajax, son of Telamon, born to lead — the cry of Odysseus is in my ears, and it sounds as if the Trojans have cut him off and are wearing him down, alone, in the thick of them. Let’s drive through the crowd. Better to stand by him. I’m afraid for him, brave as he is, alone among that many — and the Greeks would feel the loss of him hard.”

He led the way, and great Ajax went with him. They found Odysseus ringed by Trojans, like tawny jackals in the hills around a wounded stag that a hunter has hit with an arrow — the stag ran on while the blood was warm and his legs would hold him, but when the shaft has worn him down the jackals fall to tearing him in a shaded glen — until some power sends a lion their way, and the jackals scatter in terror and the lion has the meat. So the Trojans, many and brave, crowded around cunning Odysseus, and the hero stood at bay and held them off with his spear. Then Ajax came up with his shield like a wall before him and stood beside him, and the Trojans broke and ran in all directions. Menelaus took Odysseus by the hand and led him out of the press while his squire brought up the chariot; and Ajax fell on the Trojans and killed Doryclus, a bastard son of Priam, and then wounded Pandocus, and Lysandrus, and Pyrasus, and Pylartes. As a swollen river comes roaring down from the mountains onto the plain, fat with the rain of the sky, sweeping dry oaks and pines along with it, and flinging the mud out into the sea — so glorious Ajax stormed across the plain, killing men and horses both.

Hector did not yet know any of this, for he was fighting away on the far left of the line, by the banks of the Scamander, where the dying was thickest and the war-cry rose loudest around Nestor and brave Idomeneus. There Hector worked among them with his spear and his driving, wrecking the ranks set against him; and even so the Greeks would not have given ground — if Paris, the husband of lovely Helen, had not stopped Machaon, shepherd of his people, in his stride, hitting him in the right shoulder with a three-barbed arrow. The Greeks, with the fight already turning, were afraid the Trojans might take him; and Idomeneus said to Nestor: “Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Greeks — get up into your chariot, take Machaon, and drive your horses to the ships as fast as they’ll go. A healer is worth many other men, for he can cut out arrows and lay on the soothing herbs.”

Nestor, the Gerenian horseman, did as Idomeneus said. He climbed into his chariot at once, and Machaon, son of the great healer Asclepius, climbed up beside him. Nestor laid on the whip, and the horses flew toward the ships, glad enough to go.

Then Cebriones, riding beside Hector, saw the Trojans thrown into confusion, and said: “Hector, here we are fighting on the very edge of the battle, out at the rim — while the rest of the Trojans are routed, men and horses tangled together. Ajax, son of Telamon, is driving them; I know him by the breadth of his shield. Let’s turn the chariot and horses there, where the fighting is worst, horse and foot, and the war-cry is loudest.”

With that he lashed the fine team, and at the stroke they tore the chariot in among the Greeks and Trojans, over bodies and over shields, and the axle below was sprayed with blood, and the rail around the chariot splashed with the gore the hooves and the wheel-rims threw up. Hector cut his way in and flung himself into the thick of it, scattering the Greeks, his spear never long idle. He ranged the ranks with sword and spear and great stones — but he kept clear of Ajax, son of Telamon, for Zeus would have been angry to see him take on a better man than himself.

Then Father Zeus, on his high seat, put fear into the heart of Ajax, so that he stood stunned, slung his great shield behind him, and gave ground, glancing about at the crowd of his enemies like a cornered beast, turning this way and that, drawing slowly back. As farmers and their dogs drive a lion off from the cattle-yard, keeping the night-long watch so he won’t carry off the best of the herd — he lunges, hungry, and gets nothing, for the darts come thick from the bold hands against him, and the burning brands that scare him for all his rage, and at dawn he slinks away sullen and balked — so Ajax drew back from the Trojans, against all his will, in dread for the ships of the Greeks. Or as a stubborn donkey gets the better of the boys in a field — one who has had many a stick broken across his back — and pushes into the standing grain and eats his fill, and they beat him with their sticks but their strength is a child’s strength, and only when he’s had enough do they at last drive him out — so the Trojans and their allies pressed on great Ajax, jabbing always at the center of his shield. Now and then he would round on them and turn the whole Trojan front back, and then again he gave ground — but he kept any of them from breaking through to the ships. He stood alone between the two armies, and the spears flung from their hands stuck in his great shield, while many, thirsting for his flesh, fell short in the dirt before they could taste it.

Now when Eurypylus, the brave son of Euaemon, saw Ajax being worn down under the storm of arrows, he came and stood beside him and let his spear fly. He hit Apisaon, son of Phausius, in the liver below the midriff and dropped him on the spot, and sprang to strip the armor off his shoulders. But Paris saw him and drew on him, and the arrow caught Eurypylus in the right thigh; the shaft broke off, and the point dragged in the wound. He fell back into the cover of his comrades to save his life, shouting to the Greeks as he went: “Friends, captains and counselors of the army — turn back, stand, defend Ajax. He’s overwhelmed, and I doubt he’ll come out of this alive. Here — to the rescue of great Ajax, son of Telamon!”

So the wounded man cried, and the others came up and gathered around him, bracing their shields against their shoulders to give him cover; and Ajax made his way toward them, and turned to stand at bay once he had reached his own men.

So they fought on, like a blaze of fire. Meanwhile the mares of Neleus, lathered with sweat, were carrying Nestor out of the battle, and Machaon, shepherd of his people, with him. Achilles saw it and took note, for he was standing on the high stern of his ship, watching the bitter labor of the rout. He called from the ship to his comrade Patroclus, and Patroclus heard him in the hut and came out looking like Ares himself — and here was the beginning of the evil that would soon come on him. “Why do you call me, Achilles?” he said. “What do you want of me?” And swift-footed Achilles answered: “Noble son of Menoetius, dear to my heart — now, I think, the Greeks will be at my knees, begging, for they’re in real straits. Go, Patroclus, dear to Zeus, and ask Nestor who it is he’s carrying wounded from the field. From behind I’d say it’s Machaon, son of Asclepius, but I couldn’t see the man’s face — the horses went past me too fast.”

Patroclus did as his dear comrade told him, and set off running along the line of huts and ships.

When Nestor and Machaon reached the huts of the son of Neleus, they got down, and a squire, Eurymedon, unhitched the horses. The two of them stood in the breeze by the shore to dry the sweat from their shirts, then came inside and took their seats. Lovely Hecamede mixed them a draught — the woman Nestor had been given out of Tenedos when Achilles sacked it; she was the daughter of great-hearted Arsinous, and the Greeks had picked her out for Nestor because he was the best of them in counsel. First she drew up a fine table with feet of blue enamel, and set on it a bronze dish with an onion to give an edge to the drink, and pale honey, and the sacred barley-meal, and beside them a magnificent cup the old man had brought from home, studded with gold, with four handles, and two golden doves feeding at each, and two supports beneath. Anyone else would have strained to lift it from the table when it was full, but old Nestor raised it easily. In it the woman, lovely as a goddess, mixed them the draught with Pramnian wine — she grated goat cheese into it with a bronze grater, scattered white barley over it — and when it was ready she told them to drink. When they had drunk and put off their parching thirst, they fell to talking with one another, and just then Patroclus appeared in the doorway.

When the old man saw him, he sprang up from his shining seat, took him by the hand, led him in, and pressed him to sit. But Patroclus held back and said: “No seat for me, old sir — you won’t talk me in. The man who sent me is not one to take lightly, and he sent me to ask who it is you’ve carried wounded from the field. But I can see for myself now — it’s Machaon, shepherd of his people. I’ll go back and tell Achilles. You know yourself, old sir, what a hard man he is — quick to find fault even where no fault lies.”

And Nestor, the Gerenian horseman, answered: “Why should Achilles care now which of the Greeks have been hit? He has no notion of the grief that’s loose in this camp. Our best men are down, struck and wounded. Brave Diomedes, son of Tydeus, is wounded; Odysseus is wounded, and Agamemnon too; Eurypylus has an arrow in the thigh; and I have just brought this man here out of the fighting, hit by an arrow as well. And still Achilles, for all his courage, feels nothing for us, has no pity. Will he wait until the ships are burning at the water’s edge, do what we can, and we are killed one on top of another? I have no strength left in my limbs as I once had. If only I were young again, and my power were what it was in the days of the cattle-raid, when there was a war between us and the men of Elis. I killed Itymoneus then, the brave son of Hypeirochus, who lived in Elis, as I was driving off the spoil — he was hit by a dart from my hand as he stood in the front rank defending his herds, and he fell, and the country people around him scattered in fear. We drove a vast plunder off that plain — fifty herds of cattle, as many flocks of sheep, as many droves of pigs, as many wide-ranging flocks of goats, and a hundred and fifty horses, all of them mares, many with foals running beside them. We drove the whole of it into Pylos, the city of Neleus, by night, and the heart of Neleus was glad that I had taken so much my first time out. At daybreak the heralds went round crying that anyone owed a debt in Elis should come; and the leading men of Pylos gathered to divide it — for the Epeians owed many of us, since we of Pylos were few and had been wronged. In years past Heracles had come and laid us low, and our best men had died. Neleus had twelve sons, and I alone was left; all the others were gone. The Epeians grew arrogant on that and worked us much harm. So my father chose out a herd and a great flock — three hundred head, with their herdsmen — for a heavy debt was owed him in Elis: a team of four prize horses, with their chariot, that had gone to the games to run for a tripod, and King Augeas had kept them, and sent the driver home grieving for his horses. Angered by all this, in word and in deed, Neleus took great recompense, and divided out the rest, so that no man should go without his fair share.

“So we set everything in order and made sacrifice to the gods throughout the city. But on the third day the Epeians came against us in force, men and chariots together, and with them the two Molione in their armor, still only boys then, untried in war. There is a city, Thryoessa, set on a steep hill far off on the Alpheus, on the border of sandy Pylos. This they meant to destroy, and they ringed it about. But when they had crossed the whole plain, Athena came down to us in the night from Olympus to call us to arms — and she found the men of Pylos willing, hungry for the fight. Neleus would not let me arm; he hid my horses, saying I knew nothing of war yet. Even so, on foot among our own horsemen, I made my mark, for Athena ordered the fight that way. There is a river, the Minyeius, that runs into the sea near Arene, and there we who had horses waited for the holy Dawn, while the companies of foot came up behind us. From there, in full armor, we came at midday to the sacred stream of the Alpheus. We made fine sacrifices to almighty Zeus there, a bull to the Alpheus, a bull to Poseidon, and an unbroken heifer to Athena. Then we took our supper by companies, and lay down to sleep, each in his armor, along the river.

“The Epeians were closing on the city, set on taking it — but a great fight stood between them and that. When the sun’s first light struck the earth, we joined battle, praying to Zeus and Athena. And when the two armies met, I was the first to kill my man and take his horses — the spearman Mulius, son-in-law of Augeas, who had married the eldest daughter, golden-haired Agamede, who knew every healing herb the wide earth grows. I speared him as he came at me, and when he fell in the dust I leapt up into his chariot and took my place in the front rank. The Epeians broke and ran in every direction when they saw the captain of their horsemen, their best man, go down. And I came down on them like a black whirlwind. I took fifty chariots, and at each of them two men bit the earth on my spear. I would have killed the two young Molione, sons of Actor, if their true father, Poseidon who shakes the earth, had not hidden them in a thick mist and carried them out of the war. Then Zeus handed the men of Pylos a great victory, and we chased the enemy far across the plain, killing them and gathering their armor, until we drove our horses to Buprasium rich in wheat, and the Olenian rock, and the hill they call Alesium — and there Athena turned the army back. There I killed the last man, and left him; and the Greeks drove their horses back from Buprasium to Pylos, and gave thanks to Zeus among the gods, and to Nestor among men.

“That is the man I was among my people, when I was a man. But Achilles will keep all his courage for himself alone — and bitterly he’ll weep for it later, when the army has been cut to pieces. My friend, didn’t Menoetius give you this very charge the day he sent you out of Phthia to Agamemnon? Odysseus and I were inside the house, and we heard every word of it. We had come to the fine house of Peleus, gathering men through the length of Achaea, and there we found Menoetius, and you, and Achilles beside you. Old Peleus was in the courtyard, burning the fat thighs of an ox to Zeus the thunderer, holding a golden cup and pouring bright wine over the burning offering, and the two of you were busy carving the ox. We stood at the gate, and Achilles sprang up startled, took us by the hand and led us in, sat us down, and set before us all that guests are owed. When we’d had our fill of food and drink, I made my case and urged you both to come with us. You were eager enough — and the two old men charged you hard. Old Peleus told his son Achilles always to be the best, to outdo the rest; and Menoetius, son of Actor, said this to you: ‘My son, Achilles is the higher born, but you are the elder, though he is far the stronger in his hands. Counsel him well, guide him the right way, advise him — and he’ll follow you to his own good.’ That is what your father charged you, and you have forgotten it. Even now you might say all this to Achilles, and he might listen. Who knows but you could win him over — a friend’s word has its own power. And if some prophecy holds him back, or his mother has told him something from Zeus, then let him send you out, and the rest of the Myrmidons with you, in case you might be the light and the saving of the Greeks. And let him send you into the fighting in his own armor, so the Trojans take you for him and pull back, and the Greeks, hard pressed and barely able to breathe, get a moment’s rest. You, fresh, could easily drive worn-out men back from the ships and the huts, back to their own walls.”

So he spoke, and stirred the heart in Patroclus, and he set off running back along the line of ships toward Achilles, grandson of Aeacus. But when he came level with the ships of Odysseus, where the men held assembly and gave judgment and where the altars of the gods were set, there Eurypylus met him — Euaemon’s son, wounded in the thigh with an arrow, limping out of the fight. The sweat poured down his head and shoulders, and the dark blood welled from his cruel wound, but his mind was still clear. The son of Menoetius saw him and pitied him, and said in grief: “You poor men — captains and counselors of the Greeks — was this your fate, far from your friends and your own land, to feed the dogs of Troy with your white fat? But tell me, Eurypylus, can the Greeks still hold great Hector off, or will they go down now under his spear?”

And the wounded Eurypylus answered: “No hope left for the Greeks now, noble Patroclus. They’ll be driven back against the black ships. All the men who were our best are lying there, struck and wounded at the Trojans’ hands, and the Trojans grow stronger and stronger. But save me — get me to my ship, cut the arrow out of my thigh, wash the dark blood off it with warm water, and lay on those soothing herbs they say you learned from Achilles, who learned them from Chiron, the most honest of the Centaurs. For of our two healers, I hear Machaon is lying wounded in the huts, in need of a healer himself, and Podalirius is still out on the plain, facing the Trojans.”

And the brave son of Menoetius answered: “How can this be, Eurypylus? What am I to do? I’m on my way to carry a message to Achilles from Nestor of Gerene, bulwark of the Greeks. But even so — I won’t leave you in your pain.” With that he put his arm around him and led him into the hut, and a servant, seeing them, spread ox-hides on the ground. Patroclus laid him out full length, and with a knife cut the sharp arrow from his thigh, and washed the dark blood from the wound with warm water; then he crushed a bitter root between his hands and rubbed it on — a root that takes away all pain — and the wound dried, and the bleeding stopped.
