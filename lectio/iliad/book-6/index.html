The Iliad, Book VI

Hector and Andromache

Book VI6 of 24

Now the gods had left the field, and the battle of Trojans and Greeks was left to itself, the tide of war surging this way and that across the plain as they leveled their bronze-tipped spears at one another between the streams of the Simois and the Xanthus.

Ajax, son of Telamon, the bulwark of the Greeks, was first to break a Trojan line. He brought hope to his men by killing Acamas, son of Eussorus, the best of the Thracians, a big, brave man. The spear caught the ridge of his helmet, and the bronze point drove on through his forehead into the brain, and darkness closed over his eyes.

Then Diomedes killed Axylus, son of Teuthranus, a rich man from the strong city of Arisbe, and a man everyone loved — his house stood by the road, and he took in all who passed. But not one of the men he had welcomed stood now between him and death. Diomedes killed him and his driver Calesius both, and the two of them went down under the earth together.

Euryalus killed Dresus and Opheltius, then went after Aesepus and Pedasus, twin sons the water-nymph Abarbarea had borne to noble Bucolion — Bucolion, the eldest son of Laomedon, though born out of wedlock, who had lain with the nymph while tending his sheep. The son of Mecisteus killed them both and stripped the armor from their shoulders. Polypoetes killed Astyalus; Odysseus killed Pidytes of Percote with his spear; Teucer brought down Aretaon; Antilochus, Nestor’s son, killed Ablerus; and Agamemnon, lord of men, killed Elatus, who had lived in Pedasus by the banks of the Satnioeis. Leitus cut down Phylacus as he ran, and Eurypylus killed Melanthus.

Then Menelaus, good at the war cry, took Adrestus alive. His horses had bolted in panic across the plain, run into a tamarisk bush, and snapped the chariot pole; they galloped on toward the city with the rest of the rout, while Adrestus was thrown out by the wheel and pitched face-down in the dust. Menelaus stood over him with his spear, and Adrestus grabbed his knees and begged: “Take me alive, son of Atreus, and you’ll have a worthy ransom. My father is rich; he has bronze and gold and worked iron stored in his house. He would gladly pay you out of all of it, if he heard I was alive at the ships of the Greeks.”

So he pleaded, and Menelaus was on the point of yielding — he had nearly handed him to a squire to take back to the ships — when Agamemnon came running up and stopped him: “Soft-hearted Menelaus — why such tenderness for these men? Has your house been treated so kindly by the Trojans? Not one of them escapes a hard death at our hands — not even the boy his mother still carries in her womb. Let every soul in Ilios die, unwept and unremembered.”

And his brother gave way, for the words were just. He shoved Adrestus off, and lord Agamemnon ran him through the flank. The man went down, and the son of Atreus set his heel on his chest to pull the spear free.

Then Nestor shouted to the Greeks: “My friends, fighting men of Greece — let no one hang back now to strip a corpse and carry the most loot to the ships. Kill while you can. The bodies will lie on the plain, and you can plunder the dead at your leisure afterward.”

His words put heart into them all. And now the Trojans would have been driven back inside Ilios, beaten down by their own faintness, if Helenus, son of Priam, the best of the seers, had not come to Hector and Aeneas and said: “Hector, Aeneas — you two carry the Trojans and the Lycians, for you are first in everything, in the fighting and in the council. Stand your ground here, and go through the army and hold the men together at the gates, before they break and fling themselves into their wives’ arms, and hand our enemies the laugh. When you have steadied the whole line, we will hold here and fight the Greeks, hard as they press us; there’s nothing else to be done. But you, Hector, go up to the city, and tell our mother to gather the older women at the temple of Athena on the height. Have her open the doors of the sacred house with her key, and take the largest, most beautiful robe in the hall — the one she prizes most — and lay it on the knees of Athena. Let her promise to sacrifice twelve yearling heifers, never yet under the goad, if the goddess will pity the city and the wives and little children of the Trojans, and hold the son of Tydeus back from holy Ilios — that wild fighter, who fills men with terror. I tell you, I think he is the strongest of them all. We never feared even Achilles like this, and he is born of a goddess. This man’s rage is past all bounds; no one can match him.”

Hector did as his brother said. He leapt down from his chariot in his armor, and went everywhere through the army, shaking his two spears, urging the men to fight, raising the terrible cry of battle. They wheeled and faced the Greeks again, and the Greeks gave ground and broke off their killing — they thought one of the immortals had come down from the starry sky to fight for Troy, so suddenly had the line turned. And Hector called out to them: “Trojans, and allies from far off — be men, my friends. Hold hard while I go up to the city and tell the elders and our wives to pray to the gods and promise them sacrifice.”

With that he turned away, and the dark rim of leather around the edge of his shield knocked against his ankles and his neck as he went.

Now Glaucus, son of Hippolochus, and the son of Tydeus came together in the open ground between the armies, hungry to fight. When the two had closed the distance, Diomedes, good at the war cry, spoke first: “Who are you, brave man, among mortals? I’ve never seen you before in the fighting where men win glory, and now here you stand out ahead of everyone, with the nerve to face my spear. The fathers whose sons stand against me are unlucky men. But if you’re one of the immortals come down from heaven, I won’t fight you — I’ll have nothing to do with the gods. Even powerful Lycurgus, son of Dryas, did not live long once he raised his hand against them. He drove the nurses of raving Dionysus down the slopes of holy Nysa, and they flung their wands to the ground while murderous Lycurgus beat them with his ox-goad; and Dionysus himself dove in terror beneath the sea, and Thetis took him to her breast, trembling, for the man’s threats had broken his nerve. Then the gods who live at ease turned against Lycurgus, and the son of Cronus struck him blind, and he did not live long after, hated as he was by all the immortals. No — I will not fight the blessed gods. But if you are a man who eats the fruit of the earth, then come closer, and meet your death the sooner.”

And the splendid son of Hippolochus answered: “Great son of Tydeus, why ask about my birth? The generations of men are like the leaves of the trees. The wind scatters one year’s leaves to the ground, but the tree is alive, and when spring comes round it puts out new ones. So it is with men: one generation grows even as another dies away. Still, if you want to know my line — and many men already know it — I’ll tell you. There is a city, Ephyra, deep in the horse-pasturing heart of Argos, and there lived Sisyphus, the most cunning man alive — Sisyphus, son of Aeolus. His son was Glaucus, and Glaucus fathered faultless Bellerophon, to whom the gods gave beauty and a courage all men loved. But Proetus plotted his ruin and drove him out of Argos, the land Proetus ruled, for Zeus had put the people under his hand. The trouble was Anteia, the wife of Proetus, who burned to lie with Bellerophon in secret; but he was an honorable man and would not, so she went to her husband with a lie: ‘Die yourself, Proetus, or kill Bellerophon — he wanted to force himself on me.’ The king’s blood rose to hear it, but he shrank from killing the man outright. Instead he sent him off to Lycia, and put in his hand a folded tablet, scratched over with deadly signs, and told him to show it to his father-in-law — meaning him to die by it.

“So Bellerophon went to Lycia, and the gods saw him safely there. When he reached the river Xanthus, the king of wide Lycia received him with full honor — feasted him nine days and killed nine oxen for him. But when the tenth dawn came up rosy-fingered, the king at last questioned him and asked to see the token he carried from his son-in-law Proetus. And when he had read the deadly message, the first thing he ordered was that Bellerophon should kill the raging Chimaera — no mortal creature, but a thing of the gods, with the head of a lion, the tail of a serpent, and a goat in the middle, breathing out fierce blasts of fire. But Bellerophon killed her, trusting the signs of heaven. Next he fought the famous Solymi — the hardest battle, he said, he ever fought against men. Third, he cut down the Amazons, the women who fight like men. And when he was on his way back, the king laid one more trap for him: he chose the best men in all Lycia and set them in ambush — and not one of them came home, for Bellerophon killed them all. Then the king knew he was a god’s true son, and kept him there, and gave him his daughter in marriage and half of all his royal honor; and the Lycians marked out for him the finest tract of land in the country, rich with orchards and plowland, to hold for his own.

“The king’s daughter bore him three children — Isander, Hippolochus, and Laodameia. Zeus, the lord of counsel, lay with Laodameia, and she bore him godlike Sarpedon, the bronze-armored. But in the end even Bellerophon came to be hated by all the gods, and he wandered alone across the Aleian plain, eating out his own heart, keeping clear of the paths of men. Ares, who never has his fill of war, killed his son Isander fighting the Solymi; and Artemis of the golden reins killed his daughter in her anger. But Hippolochus fathered me — and I am proud to say it. He sent me to Troy, and charged me again and again to be always among the best and bravest, and never to shame the line of my fathers, who were the noblest in Ephyra and in all wide Lycia. That is the blood and the line I claim to be from.”

So he spoke, and Diomedes, good at the war cry, was glad. He planted his spear in the earth that feeds us all and answered the captain warmly: “Then you are an old guest-friend of my father’s house. Long ago noble Oeneus hosted faultless Bellerophon under his roof for twenty days, and the two of them gave each other fine parting gifts. Oeneus gave a belt bright with crimson, and Bellerophon a golden two-handled cup, which I left at home when I came here. I never knew Tydeus — he was taken from me while I was still a child, when the Greek army died before the walls of Thebes. So now I am your host in the heart of Argos, and you are mine in Lycia, if I ever come to that country. Let us keep clear of each other’s spears, even in the thick of the fighting. There are plenty of Trojans and famous allies for me to kill, whomever a god sends into my hands and my feet can run down — and plenty of Greeks for you to kill, if you can. And let us trade armor, so everyone here can see that we are proud to be guest-friends from our fathers’ time.”

With that the two of them sprang down from their chariots, clasped hands, and pledged their faith. But Zeus, the son of Cronus, took away Glaucus’s wits, for he traded his armor with Diomedes, son of Tydeus — gold for bronze, the worth of a hundred oxen for the worth of nine.

When Hector reached the Scaean gates and the oak tree, the wives and daughters of Troy came running to him, asking after their sons, their brothers, their kinsmen, their husbands. He told them, each in turn, to pray to the gods; and grief was coming for many of them.

Then he came to Priam’s splendid palace, built with smooth colonnades of stone. Inside it were fifty bedchambers of polished stone, set side by side, where the sons of Priam slept beside their wedded wives; and across the courtyard, facing them, were twelve more roofed chambers of polished stone, side by side, where the sons-in-law of Priam slept beside his daughters. There his generous mother met him, on her way to Laodice, the loveliest of her daughters. She took his hand in hers and said: “My son — why have you left the hard fighting and come here? The cursed Greeks must be wearing you down at the city walls, if you’ve come to lift your hands to Zeus from the height. Wait — let me bring you honeyed wine, so you can pour an offering to Zeus and the other immortals, and then drink some yourself and be refreshed. Wine builds a man’s strength back up when he’s worn out, the way you are now, fighting for your own people.”

And great Hector of the gleaming helmet answered: “Bring me no wine, honored mother — you’ll only sap my strength and make me forget my courage. And I dare not pour a bright offering to Zeus with unwashed hands; a man spattered with blood and filth has no business praying to the son of Cronus. No — you go to the temple of Athena, who drives the spoil, and take the older women with you and your offerings. Take the largest, most beautiful robe in the hall, the one you prize most, and lay it on the knees of Athena. And promise to sacrifice twelve yearling heifers, never yet under the goad, in her temple, if she will pity the city and the wives and little children of Troy, and hold the son of Tydeus back from holy Ilios — that wild fighter, who fills men with terror. Go, then, to the temple of Athena, while I go to find Paris and call him out, if he’ll hear me. If only the earth would open and swallow him where he stands. Zeus raised him up to be a curse on Troy, on great-hearted Priam, and on Priam’s sons. If I could only see him go down into the house of Hades, I think my heart might forget its grief.”

She went into the house and called her women, who gathered the older women from all through the city. She herself went down into the fragrant storeroom where her embroidered robes were kept, the work of Sidonian women, whom Paris had brought back from Sidon on the same long voyage when he carried off Helen. From these Hecuba lifted out the largest robe, the loveliest in its embroidery — it shone like a star, and lay at the very bottom of the chest. With this she set out, and a crowd of older women went with her.

When they reached the temple of Athena on the height, the doors were opened to them by lovely Theano, daughter of Cisseus and wife of Antenor, whom the Trojans had made the priestess of Athena. With a great cry the women lifted their hands to the goddess, and Theano took the robe and laid it on the knees of Athena, praying to the daughter of great Zeus: “Lady Athena, who guards our city, brightest of goddesses — break the spear of Diomedes, and let him fall headlong before the Scaean gates. Do this, and we will sacrifice to you, here in your temple, twelve yearling heifers never yet under the goad, if you will pity the city and the wives and little children of Troy.” So she prayed. But Pallas Athena refused her.

While they were praying to the daughter of great Zeus, Hector came to the fine house of Paris, which he had built for himself with the best builders in the rich land of Troy — house, hall, and courtyard, close to the homes of Priam and Hector on the height. Hector entered, his spear in his hand, eleven cubits long, the bronze point gleaming before him, ringed at the socket with gold. He found Paris in the bedchamber, busy with his beautiful gear — handling his shield and breastplate, fingering his curved bow. And Helen of Argos sat there among her women, directing their fine handiwork. Hector took one look at him and let the scorn fall: “You strange man — this is no time to be nursing your grudge. The men are dying out there, fighting around the city and its steep walls; it’s for you that the war cry and the battle blaze up around this town. You’d be the first to round on another man you caught hanging back from the fighting. Get up — before the city goes up in fire.”

And Paris answered him: “Hector, that’s a fair charge — you have it right, and I won’t argue. But hear me out. I wasn’t sitting here so much out of any grudge or ill will toward the Trojans, but because I wanted to give myself over to my grief. Just now, though, my wife was urging me with gentle words to go back into the fighting; and I think myself it’s the better thing — victory shifts from one man to another. So wait while I put on my armor, or go ahead, and I’ll catch up; I’m sure I’ll overtake you.”

Hector said nothing to that. But Helen spoke to him in a low voice: “Brother — brother-in-law to me, a hateful bitch of a woman, who brings on this horror. I wish that on the day my mother first bore me, some foul gust of wind had caught me up and carried me off to a mountain, or into the surf of the roaring sea, and the wave had swept me away before all this could happen. But since the gods have decreed these evils, I wish at least I had been the wife of a better man — one who could feel the anger of his people and the shame they heap on him. This one has no steadiness in him, now or ever, and he’ll reap what he has sown, I think. But come in, brother — sit here and rest, for it’s you the work falls hardest on, because of me, bitch that I am, and because of the blindness of Paris. Zeus has laid a hard fate on us, so that even in the years to come we’ll be a song in the mouths of people not yet born.”

And great Hector of the gleaming helmet answered: “Don’t ask me to sit, Helen, kind as you are to me. You won’t talk me into it. My heart is already set on helping the Trojans, who feel my absence keenly when I’m gone. Stir this man instead — and let him hurry himself, too, to catch me before I’m out of the city. I’m going home to see my household, my wife and my little son. For I do not know whether I’ll ever come back to them again, or whether the gods will bring me down at the hands of the Greeks.”

With that great Hector of the gleaming helmet turned away, and quickly came to his own well-built house. But he did not find white-armed Andromache there. She had gone with the child and a serving woman to stand on the wall, weeping and grieving. When Hector did not find his faultless wife inside, he stopped on the threshold and called to the serving women: “Tell me the truth, women — where did Andromache go when she left the house? To one of my sisters, or one of my brothers’ wives? Or to the temple of Athena, where the other women of Troy are trying to soften the dread goddess?”

And the busy housekeeper answered him: “Hector, since you ask me for the truth — she did not go to your sisters or your brothers’ wives, or to the temple of Athena where the other women are. She went to the great tower of Ilios, because she heard the Trojans were hard pressed and the Greeks were winning. She rushed to the wall like a woman out of her mind, and the nurse went with her, carrying the child.”

So the housekeeper spoke, and Hector hurried back out of the house, down through the streets the way he had come. When he had gone all through the great city and reached the Scaean gates, where he meant to go out onto the plain, his wife came running to meet him — Andromache, generous daughter of great Eetion, who had lived under wooded Mount Placus, in Thebe under Placus, and ruled the Cilician men. His daughter had married Hector of the bronze helmet, and now she came to him, and with her the nurse, holding the small boy against her breast, a baby still, Hector’s beloved son, beautiful as a star. Hector called him Scamandrius, but everyone else called him Astyanax — “lord of the city” — for Hector alone was the shield of Ilios. Hector smiled when he saw the boy, and said nothing. But Andromache came and stood close to him, weeping, and put her hand in his and spoke:

“You strange, fierce man — this courage of yours will be your death. You have no pity for your baby son, or for me, who will soon be your widow. The Greeks will fall on you all together and kill you. And if I lose you, it would be better for me to go down into the earth. There’ll be no comfort left for me when you have met your fate — only grief. I have no father and no honored mother now. Brilliant Achilles killed my father when he sacked the high city of the Cilicians, Thebe of the high gates. He killed Eetion, but he had too much respect to strip him; he burned him in his fine armor and heaped a barrow over him, and the mountain nymphs, daughters of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, planted elm trees all around. And the seven brothers I had in my father’s house all went down to the house of Hades on a single day; swift-footed brilliant Achilles killed them all, out among their cattle and white sheep. My mother, who had been queen under wooded Placus, he brought here with the rest of the spoils, then set her free for a great ransom — but Artemis the archer struck her down in her father’s house. So, Hector — you are father to me now, and honored mother, and brother; and you are my strong young husband. Pity me, then. Stay here on the wall. Don’t make your child an orphan and your wife a widow. Set the army by the fig tree, where the city is easiest to climb and the wall is weakest. Three times their best men have come and tried it there — the two named Ajax, and famous Idomeneus, and the sons of Atreus, and the brave son of Tydeus — whether some prophet told them, or their own hearts drove them on.”

And great Hector of the gleaming helmet answered her: “All of this is on my mind too, wife. But I would be deeply ashamed before the men of Troy, and the women in their trailing robes, if I hung back from the fighting like a coward. And my own heart will not let me. I have learned to be brave, always, and to fight in the front rank of the Trojans, winning great glory for my father and for myself. For I know this well, in my mind and in my heart: a day will come when holy Ilios will fall, and Priam, and the people of Priam of the strong ash spear. Yet it is not the coming grief of the Trojans that wrings me most — not even Hecuba’s, or King Priam’s, or my brothers’, many and brave, who will go down in the dust at the hands of their enemies. It is yours, when one of the bronze-armored Greeks leads you off in tears and takes your freedom away. In Argos you would work another woman’s loom, and carry water from the spring Messeis or Hypereia, hating every step of it, with hard necessity bearing down on you. And someday a man who sees you weeping will say, ‘There goes the wife of Hector, who was the best of the horse-taming Trojans at fighting, in the war for Troy.’ He will say it, and the grief will break over you again, for the want of a husband like me to keep the day of slavery from you. But may I be dead, and the earth heaped over me, before I hear you cry out as they drag you away.”

So speaking, glorious Hector reached out for his son. But the boy shrank back, screaming, against the breast of his well-girded nurse, terrified at the sight of his own father — frightened by the bronze and the horsehair crest he saw nodding grimly from the top of the helmet. His father laughed, and his honored mother laughed. At once glorious Hector lifted the helmet from his head and laid it on the ground, where it shone. Then he kissed his dear son and rocked him in his arms, and prayed aloud to Zeus and the other gods: “Zeus, and you other gods — grant that this boy of mine may be, like me, foremost among the Trojans, as strong as I am, and rule Ilios in his strength. And may they say of him one day, ‘He is far greater than his father,’ as he comes home from the war, carrying the blood-stained armor of an enemy he has killed — and may his mother’s heart be glad.”

With that he placed his son in the arms of his wife, and she took him to her sweet-smelling breast, smiling through her tears. Her husband saw it and pitied her; he stroked her with his hand and said: “My dearest — don’t grieve too much for me in your heart. No man is going to send me down to Hades before my time. And no one, I tell you, ever escapes his fate — not the coward, not the brave man — once he has been born. Go home now, and see to your own work, the loom and the distaff, and tell your women to get on with theirs. The war will be the men’s concern — every man’s who was born in Ilios, and mine above all.”

So speaking, glorious Hector picked up his helmet with its horsehair crest, and his dear wife started home, turning again and again to look back, the tears falling fast. Quickly she came to the well-built house of man-killing Hector, and found her women inside, and set them all to weeping. So they mourned for Hector in his own house while he still lived, for they did not believe he would come back from the fighting, out of the fury and the hands of the Greeks.

Paris, for his part, did not linger long in his high house. Once he had pulled on his fine armor worked with bronze, he came tearing through the city, trusting his quick feet. He was like a stalled horse, well fed at the manger, that breaks his halter and gallops thundering across the plain to bathe in a clear-running river — head high, mane streaming back over his shoulders, glorying in his own strength, his legs carrying him to the haunts and pastures of the mares. So Paris, son of Priam, came down from the height of Pergamus, his armor flashing like the sun, laughing aloud, and his quick feet carried him on. He caught up with his brother Hector just as he was turning away from the place where he had talked with his wife. Paris spoke first: “Brother — here I’ve kept you waiting in your hurry, dragging my feet, and didn’t come on time, the way you told me.”

And Hector of the gleaming helmet answered him: “You strange man — no fair-minded person could make light of your work in a fight, for you are brave enough. But you give way on purpose, and you don’t want to fight; and it cuts me to the heart to hear the Trojans speak ill of you, when they suffer so much on your account. But come — we’ll make all this right another time, if Zeus ever grants us to set the bowl of freedom before the deathless gods of heaven in our halls, once we have driven the bronze-greaved Greeks out of Troy.”
