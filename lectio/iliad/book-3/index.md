# The Iliad, Book III

The Duel for Helen

When the companies had formed up, each behind its own captain, the Trojans came on with a din and a clamor, like birds — like the cranes that scream overhead when winter and the rains drive them south over the streams of Ocean, carrying death and ruin to the Pygmy men, and they fall on them out of the dawn with a murderous quarrel. But the Greeks came on in silence, breathing fury, their hearts set on standing by one another.

As the south wind drapes the mountaintops in mist — no friend to the shepherd, but better than night to the thief, for a man can see no farther than the cast of a stone — so the dust rose in clouds under their feet as they crossed the plain at speed.

When the two armies had closed to within reach of each other, Alexandros stepped out ahead of the Trojan line to fight as their champion. A leopard’s skin hung from his shoulders, and his curved bow, and his sword; and he shook two bronze-tipped spears and dared the best of the Greeks to come and face him man to man. Menelaus saw him striding out before the ranks and his heart leaped — the way a lion’s does, starved, when it lights on the carcass of a great goat or an antlered stag and tears into it on the spot, though hounds and quick young hunters press in on it. So Menelaus rejoiced when he set eyes on Alexandros, for he thought: now I pay back the man who wronged me. Down from his chariot he sprang in his armor.

But Alexandros, husband of lovely-haired Helen, saw Menelaus come forward, and his courage broke. He shrank back into the ranks of his own men to save his life. As a man jumps back when he comes on a snake in a mountain hollow — a shudder takes him, he goes pale, he gives ground — so Alexandros, struck with terror at the sight of the son of Atreus, ducked back into the press of the proud Trojans.

Hector saw it, and turned on him with scorn: “Paris — you cursed, pretty Paris, you woman-crazed seducer, all looks and lies — better you had never been born, or had died unmarried. I’d take that, far rather than have you stand here a disgrace, a thing men look at sideways. You can hear the Greeks laughing now — they thought our champion would be a great fighter, because he’s a fine-looking man, and they’ve found out he has no nerve and no fight in him at all. Was it this same weakness in you when you gathered your loyal crews and sailed across the sea, mixed with foreigners, and carried off a beautiful woman from a far country, a bride married into a nation of spearmen — and brought down ruin on your father, on your city, on your whole people, joy to your enemies, and shame on your own head? And now you won’t stand and face Menelaus? You’d learn fast enough what kind of man it is whose wife you took. Your lyre would do you no good then, and neither would Aphrodite’s gifts — your good looks, your fine hair — when you lay in the dust. The Trojans are too soft, or you’d have been stoned in a coat of rock long ago for the harm you’ve done them.”

And Alexandros answered him: “Hector, that’s a fair charge — I won’t pretend it isn’t. Your heart is always unbending, like an axe a shipwright drives through a beam, the man’s own strength giving the stroke its weight — that’s how fearless the heart in your chest is. But don’t throw golden Aphrodite’s gifts in my face. The gods’ own gifts are not to be thrown away; no man takes them by wanting them — the gods give them where they please. Still, if you want me to fight, then sit the Trojans and the Greeks down, and put me in the middle with Menelaus, the two of us, to fight it out for Helen and all her wealth. Whichever of us wins, whoever proves the better man, let him take the woman and everything she owns and carry it home — and let the rest of you swear a binding peace, so that you Trojans go on living here in the rich land of Troy, and the Greeks sail home to Argos, where the horses graze, and to Achaea of the lovely women.”

Hector was glad to hear it. He went out into the no-man’s-land between the armies, gripping his spear by the middle to hold his own men back, and they all sat down at his signal. But the Greeks kept aiming at him, loosing arrows and slinging stones, until Agamemnon, lord of men, shouted them down: “Hold — Greeks, hold! Don’t shoot. Hector wants to speak.”

They stopped, and held their fire, and fell quiet, and Hector spoke between the two armies: “Hear me, Trojans, and you Greeks. I bring the offer of Alexandros, the man this whole war began over. He asks both sides to lay their arms down on the ground, and he and Menelaus will fight, the two of them alone in the middle, for Helen and all her wealth. Whichever wins, whoever proves the better man, let him take the woman and everything she owns, and let the rest of us swear a binding peace.”

He finished, and they all went still. Then Menelaus, who roars in battle, spoke up: “Now hear me too — for the wound is mine most of all. It’s my belief the Greeks and the Trojans can part at last, after all the suffering you have borne for my quarrel, and for what Alexandros did to me. Whichever of us is fated to die, let him die — and let the rest of you be done with fighting. Bring two lambs — a white ram and a black ewe, one for the Sun and one for the Earth — and we’ll bring a third for Zeus. And send for Priam, so the old king swears the oath himself; his sons are reckless men, not to be trusted, and the oaths of Zeus must not be made and broken. A young man’s mind blows whichever way the wind goes, but where an old man takes part, he looks before and after, and weighs what will turn out best for both sides.”

The Trojans and the Greeks were glad to hear it, hoping the war was finally over. They reined their chariots back into the lines, climbed down, and stripped off their armor, laying it on the ground; and the two armies came close, with only a little stretch of bare earth between them. Hector sent two runners to the city for the lambs, and to fetch Priam, and Agamemnon sent Talthybius back to the ships for the third lamb; and he did as the king commanded.

Meanwhile Iris came to white-armed Helen with the news, taking the shape of her sister-in-law Laodice, the loveliest of Priam’s daughters, wife of Antenor’s son, lord Helicaon. She found Helen in her chamber, weaving at a great purple web, working into the cloth the many battles that the Trojans and the Greeks had fought for her sake at the hands of Ares. Iris came close and said: “Come and see, dear girl — there’s a strange thing happening out there. The Trojans and the Greeks were on the plain a moment ago, mad for the fight; now they’ve stopped. The war has gone silent. They’re leaning on their shields with their spears stuck upright in the ground beside them. And Alexandros and Menelaus are going to fight for you, the two of them with the long spears — and you’ll be called the wife of whichever man wins.”

So the goddess spoke, and she set in Helen’s heart a sweet longing for the husband she had left, and her city, and her parents. She threw a white veil over her head and hurried out of the chamber, the tears already coming, and not alone — two of her women went with her, Aithra, daughter of Pittheus, and wide-eyed Clymene. And quickly they came to the Scaean gates.

There the elders of the people sat by the Scaean gates — Priam, and Panthous, and Thymoetes, Lampus and Clytius and Hicetaon of the line of Ares, and the two wise ones, Ucalegon and Antenor. Too old now to fight, but fine speakers still, they sat on the tower like cicadas that perch in a tree and pour out their thin, delicate song through the wood. When they saw Helen coming toward the tower, they spoke low to one another, in winged words: “No shame, then, that the Trojans and the Greeks have borne so much, so long, for a woman like that. She is terribly like the deathless goddesses to look on. And yet — beautiful as she is, let her go home in the ships, and not be left here to bring grief on us, and on our children after us.”

But Priam called her over to him: “Come here, dear child, and sit in front of me — you’ll see your first husband, and your kinsmen, and your friends. I lay no blame on you. To my mind it is the gods who are to blame; it is they who brought this miserable war of the Greeks down on us. Now tell me: who is that huge man over there, that great and lordly Greek? There are taller men in that army by a head, but I have never laid eyes on one so handsome, so much a king to look at. Surely he is a king.”

And Helen, radiant among women, answered him: “You are dear to me, and I hold you in awe, father of my husband. I wish a hard death had been my choice instead, when I followed your son here and left behind my marriage bed, my kinsmen, my one beloved daughter, and the dear friends of my girlhood. But that was not to be, and so I waste away in tears. Still, I’ll tell you what you ask. That man is Agamemnon, son of Atreus, wide-ruling — a good king and a strong spearman both. And he was my own brother-in-law, shameless as I am — if that life was ever real.”

The old man gazed at him in wonder and said: “Fortunate son of Atreus — born lucky, blessed by the gods. So many sons of the Greeks are under your command. I went once to Phrygia, the land of vines, and saw the great host of horsemen there, the people of Otreus and of godlike Mygdon, camped along the banks of the Sangarius. I was their ally, drawn up among them, the day the Amazons came against us, those women who fight like men. But even they were not so many as these bright-eyed Greeks.”

Then the old man looked at Odysseus and asked: “Now tell me about that one, dear child. Who is he? He’s shorter than Agamemnon by a head, but broader in the chest and across the shoulders. His armor lies on the ground, and he ranges along the front of the ranks like a ram — like a great thick-fleeced ram going up and down through a flock of white ewes.”

And Helen, child of Zeus, answered: “That is Laertes’ son, Odysseus, a man of many turns. He was raised in Ithaca, that rugged island, and there is no trick or scheme he does not know.”

Wise Antenor took up her words: “Lady, what you say is true. Odysseus came here once, on an embassy for your sake, and Menelaus with him; I was their host and gave them welcome under my own roof, so I came to know both men, their bodies and their minds. When they stood up among the assembled Trojans, Menelaus was the broader-shouldered of the two; but when both sat, Odysseus had the more lordly presence. When they rose to make their case before us all, Menelaus spoke fluently, only a few words, but very clear and to the point — no man of many words, though he was the younger. But when Odysseus, the man of many turns, got up, he would stand there with his eyes fixed on the ground, holding the speaker’s staff stiff and still, moving it neither forward nor back, like a man who had never spoken in public — you would have taken him for some sullen fool. But once he let the great voice out of his chest, and the words came driving down like winter snow, then no living man could stand against Odysseus; and we no longer marveled at the look of him.”

Then Priam saw Ajax and asked a third time: “And who is that other Greek, great and tall, head and shoulders above the rest of the Argives?”

Helen answered, the long robe trailing, radiant among women: “That is huge Ajax, the wall of the Greeks. And over on the other side, among the Cretans, stands Idomeneus, like a god, with the captains of Crete gathered around him. Often Menelaus, dear to Ares, welcomed him into our house when he came across from Crete. And I can see all the other Greeks now, the bright-eyed men I could name for you one by one. Only two I cannot find anywhere — Castor, breaker of horses, and Polydeuces, the great boxer — my own brothers, the two my mother bore. Either they never left lovely Lacedaemon, or they sailed here in their ships but will not come out into the fighting, in shame at the disgrace and the dishonor I have brought on them.”

So she spoke. But the life-giving earth already held them fast, away in Lacedaemon, in their own dear country.

Meanwhile the heralds were bringing the offerings for the oath through the city — two lambs, and wine, the cheer of the earth, in a goatskin; and Idaeus carried the gleaming mixing-bowl and the golden cups. He came up to the old king and roused him: “Up, son of Laomedon. The leading men of the Trojans and the Greeks are calling for you to come down onto the plain and swear a binding oath. Alexandros and Menelaus are to fight with the long spears for the woman; she and all her wealth go to the winner. The rest of us are to swear a peace, so that we Trojans live on here in the rich land of Troy, and the Greeks sail home to Argos, where the horses graze, and to Achaea of the lovely women.”

The old man shuddered to hear it, but he gave the word to yoke the horses, and his men were quick to obey. Priam mounted and drew back the reins, and Antenor climbed up beside him in the splendid chariot, and they drove the fast horses out through the Scaean gates onto the plain. When they reached the Trojans and the Greeks, they stepped down from the chariot onto the teeming earth and walked out into the open ground between the two armies.

At once Agamemnon, lord of men, rose to meet them, and Odysseus, the man of many turns. The proud heralds brought the offerings together for the oath, mixed wine in the bowl, and poured water over the hands of the kings. Then the son of Atreus drew the knife that always hung beside the great scabbard of his sword and cut some hairs from the heads of the lambs, and the heralds passed them around among the leading men of both sides. And Agamemnon lifted his hands and prayed aloud for them all: “Father Zeus, lord of Ida, greatest and most glorious — and you, Sun, who see all things and hear all things — and you Rivers, and you Earth, and you powers below the ground who punish the dead that swore falsely: be our witnesses, and keep faith with these oaths. If Alexandros kills Menelaus, then let him keep Helen and all her wealth, and we will sail home in our seagoing ships. But if fair-haired Menelaus kills Alexandros, then the Trojans give Helen back and all her wealth, and pay the Greeks a fitting penalty besides, one that men yet unborn will remember. And if, once Paris has fallen, Priam and Priam’s sons refuse to pay it, then I will stay and fight on for that payment until I see this war to its end.”

With that he drew the pitiless bronze across the lambs’ throats and laid them on the ground, gasping out their lives, for the knife had taken their strength. Then they dipped wine from the bowl into the cups, and poured it out, and prayed to the gods who live forever — Trojans and Greeks, each man among them: “Zeus, greatest and most glorious, and all you other deathless gods: whichever side is first to break this oath, let their brains run out on the ground the way this wine runs out — theirs and their children’s — and let their wives be carried off by other men.” So they prayed. But the son of Cronus would not yet grant it.

Then Priam of the line of Dardanus spoke among them: “Hear me, Trojans and Greeks. I am going back now to windswept Ilios. I cannot bring myself to watch with my own eyes my dear son fight Menelaus. Zeus knows, and the other immortal gods know, which of the two is fated to die.” So the godlike old man laid the lambs in his chariot, mounted, and drew back the reins, and Antenor climbed up beside him in the splendid chariot, and the two of them turned and drove home to Ilios.

Then Hector, Priam’s son, and brilliant Odysseus first measured out the ground, and then they took two lots and shook them in a bronze helmet, to see which of the two would throw his spear first. And the armies lifted their hands to the gods and prayed — Trojans and Greeks both: “Father Zeus, lord of Ida, greatest and most glorious: whichever of the two set this trouble between us, let him die and go down into the house of the dead, and let the rest of us hold to our oaths in peace.”

Great Hector of the flashing helmet shook the lots, looking away — and Paris’ lot leaped out first. Then the armies sat down, each man by his horses where his gleaming gear was laid, and Alexandros, husband of lovely-haired Helen, put on his splendid armor. First the greaves, fitted to his shins with silver clasps at the ankle. Next the breastplate of his brother Lycaon, settled to his own body. Over his shoulders he slung the silver-studded bronze sword, and then the great heavy shield. On his strong head he set the crafted helmet, its horsehair crest nodding above him with menace, and he took up a heavy spear that sat well in his grip. And in the same way Menelaus, dear to Ares, armed himself.

When the two had armed, each among his own people, they strode out into the space between the armies with murder in their eyes, and a kind of awe took hold of everyone watching, Trojans and Greeks alike. They came to a stop close together on the measured ground, shaking their spears, each one raging at the other. Alexandros threw first, and his long spear struck the round shield of the son of Atreus — but it did not break through; the point bent back against the strong shield. Then Menelaus drove up with his own spear, and as he did, he prayed to Father Zeus: “Lord Zeus, let me pay back the man who wronged me first — Alexandros — and beat him down under my hands, so that men still unborn will think twice before doing harm to the host who befriends them.”

He balanced the long spear and let it fly, and struck the round shield of Alexandros. Straight through the bright shield the heavy spear drove, and on through the worked breastplate, and tore the shirt along his flank — but Alexandros swerved aside, and dodged the black end of death. Then the son of Atreus drew his silver-studded sword, reared back, and brought it down on the ridge of Paris’ helmet — and the sword shattered against it, into three, four pieces, and fell from his hand. Menelaus groaned and looked up at the wide sky: “Father Zeus — no god is crueler than you. I was sure I had paid Alexandros back for his wrong, and now the sword breaks in my hand, and the spear has flown from my grip for nothing, and I have not killed him.”

And with that he sprang at Alexandros, seized him by the horsehair crest of his helmet, spun him around, and started hauling him toward the Greeks — and the embroidered strap, drawn tight under the soft of his throat, was choking him. Menelaus would have dragged him off and won unending glory, but Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, was watching, and she was quick: she snapped the strap of slaughtered ox-hide, and the empty helmet came away in Menelaus’ fist. He swung it around his head and sent it flying to his comrades among the Greeks, who gathered it up. Then he charged in again, hungry to run Paris through with the bronze — but Aphrodite snatched him away, easily, the way a god does, and hid him in a thick mist, and set him down in his own fragrant, vaulted bedchamber.

Then she went herself to call Helen. She found her up on the high tower, the Trojan women thick around her, and she took the shape of an old woman, a wool-carder who had once worked the lovely wool for Helen back in Lacedaemon, and whom Helen loved. In that disguise the goddess plucked at Helen’s sweet-scented robe and said: “Come — Alexandros is calling you home. He’s there in the bedroom, on the carved bed, glowing with beauty in his fine clothes. You’d never think he had just come from fighting a man — more like he was on his way to a dance, or had just sat down from one.”

So she spoke, and stirred Helen’s heart in her breast. But when Helen saw the goddess’s lovely throat, and her breasts that wake desire, and her glittering eyes, she was struck with wonder, and then with anger, and she said: “You strange, uncanny power — why this longing to deceive me again? Where will you carry me off to next, to some city farther still, somewhere in Phrygia or in lovely Maeonia, if there’s another mortal man down there you favor? Is it because Menelaus has beaten Alexandros now, and means to take his hated wife back home — is that why you’ve come here to me with your treachery? Go sit beside Alexandros yourself, then. Step off the gods’ high road. Never set foot on Olympus again. Fret over him, guard him, until he makes you his wife — or makes you his slave. I will not go to him. It would be shameful. I will not warm that man’s bed again. Every woman in Troy would have it to throw at me — and I have grief enough in my heart already.”

Aphrodite, who loves laughter, rounded on her in fury: “Don’t push me, you reckless girl — or I’ll let you go, in anger, and hate you as fiercely as I have loved you. I’ll set a vicious hatred between the Trojans and the Greeks, and you — you’ll come to a wretched end.”

So she spoke, and Helen, child of Zeus, was afraid. She drew the white shining veil close about her and went in silence, slipping past the Trojan women unseen, with the goddess leading the way.

When they came to the beautiful house of Alexandros, the serving women turned at once to their work, and Helen, radiant among women, climbed to the high bedchamber. There Aphrodite, who loves laughter, took up a chair with her own hands and carried it over and set it down facing Alexandros. And Helen, daughter of Zeus who bears the storm-shield, sat — but she turned her eyes away from him, and her words came down on her husband hard: “So. Back from the fighting. I wish you had died out there, beaten by the strong man who used to be my husband. You were always boasting how you were the better man, your hands and your spear stronger than Menelaus. Go on, then — challenge him again, fight Menelaus face to face. No — don’t. I tell you, stay where you are; don’t be fool enough to trade blows with fair-haired Menelaus, or his spear will have you down in a moment.”

And Paris answered her: “Don’t cut at my heart with these hard words, wife. This time Menelaus won, with Athena’s help — but next time it will be me; I have gods on my side as well. Now come, let’s lie down together and take our pleasure. Never has desire so wrapped itself around my heart — not even that first day I carried you off from lovely Lacedaemon, sailing away in the seagoing ships, and held you on the island of Cranae, and lay with you in love. Not even then did I want you the way I want you now, with this sweet ache running through me.” And he led the way to the bed, and his wife went with him.

So the two of them lay together on the carved bed. But the son of Atreus stalked up and down through the crowd like a wild beast, searching everywhere for Alexandros. And no one — not one of the Trojans, not one of their famous allies — could point him out to Menelaus. They would not have hidden him out of love, if any of them had seen him: they hated him, every one, the way they hated black death. Then Agamemnon, lord of men, spoke out among them: “Hear me, Trojans, and Dardanians, and allies. The victory plainly belongs to Menelaus, dear to Ares. So give Helen back, and all the wealth with her, and pay the penalty besides — a fitting one, that men still to be born will remember.”

So spoke the son of Atreus, and all the Greeks roared their approval.
