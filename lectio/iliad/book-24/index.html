The Iliad, Book XXIV

The Ransom of Hector

Book XXIV24 of 24

The assembly broke up, and the men scattered, each to his own ship. They made their supper and turned to the blessing of sleep. But Achilles wept, remembering his dear friend, and sleep, that masters all things, could get no hold on him. He turned this way and that, aching for Patroclus — his strength, his manhood, all they had done and suffered together, the battles fought side by side, the hard miles of the cruel sea. The memory broke him, and he wept, lying now on his side, now on his back, now face down — until at last he rose and wandered out along the shore like a man out of his mind. And when he saw the dawn breaking over the beach and the water, he yoked his horses to the chariot, lashed Hector’s body behind it, and dragged it three times around the tomb of the son of Menoetius. Then he went back inside, and left the body stretched in the dust, face down. But Apollo would not let it be disfigured. He pitied the man, dead though he was, and sheltered him with his golden aegis, so the body took no hurt as Achilles dragged it.

So in his fury Achilles shamed noble Hector. But the blessed gods looked down and pitied him, and pressed Hermes, the keen-eyed slayer of Argus, to steal the body away. All of them wanted it — all but Hera and Poseidon and the bright-eyed daughter of Zeus, who held to the hatred they had carried for sacred Troy from the start, for Priam and his people, ever since Paris insulted the goddesses who came to his sheepfold, and chose the one who offered him the wages of lust and his own ruin.

When the twelfth morning came, Phoebus Apollo spoke out among the immortals: “You gods are cruel, and hard to the bone. Did Hector never burn for you the thigh-bones of bulls and unblemished goats? And now you cannot bring yourselves to save even his dead body — for his wife to look on, and his mother, and his child, and his father Priam, and his people, who would give him to the fire and bury him with honor? No, it’s murderous Achilles you would help — a man with no decency, no pity, nothing to bend him. His heart is iron. He is like a lion that gives in to its own great strength and savage pride and falls on a flock of sheep to make its feast. So Achilles has thrown away pity, and that sense of shame that does men great harm, and great good. A man may lose someone dearer than Achilles has lost — a brother born of the same mother, or a son. He weeps, he mourns, and then he lets him go, for the Fates have given mortals an enduring heart. But this one — he has killed noble Hector, and now he ties him behind his chariot and drags him round the tomb of his friend. There’s no honor in it, no good for him. Let him take care: brave as he is, he may stir our anger, for it is dead, dumb earth he rages against.”

Then white-armed Hera rounded on him in anger: “What you say might stand, lord of the silver bow, if you gods meant to give Hector and Achilles equal honor. But Hector is a mortal, and a mortal woman’s breast suckled him; Achilles is the child of a goddess — and I myself raised her and brought her up and gave her in marriage to Peleus, the man the immortals loved above all others. Every one of you came to the wedding; you sat at the feast among us, lyre in hand, you who keep low company and break faith.”

Then Zeus who gathers the clouds answered her: “Hera, don’t turn on the gods like this. The two men will not have equal honor. But of all the mortals in Troy, Hector was dearest to the gods, and dearest to me — for he never failed in his offerings. My altar never went without its share, the libation and the savor of sacrifice, the honor that is ours by right. Still, we will not let his body be stolen; there is no doing it behind Achilles’ back, for his mother is always at his side, night and day. Let one of you call Thetis to me. I have something to say to her — that Achilles is to take a ransom from Priam, and give the body up.”

At that, Iris, swift as the storm, went to carry his word. Down she plunged into the dark sea, midway between Samos and rocky Imbros, and the water groaned as it closed over her. She sank like the lead weight on a fishing line, set in a tube of wild-ox horn, that drops down carrying death to the fish. She found Thetis in a hollow cave, the sea-goddesses gathered around her, and Thetis there in the middle of them, weeping for her flawless son, who was fated to die far from home on the rich earth of Troy. Swift Iris came up to her and said: “Rise, Thetis. Zeus, whose counsels never fail, calls you to him.” And the silver-footed goddess answered: “Why does the great god summon me? I shrink from going among the immortals; my heart is heavy with endless grief. Still, I will go, and whatever he says will not be said in vain.”

Then the lovely goddess took her veil, and there is no darker robe than that, and went out, swift Iris leading the way before her, and the swell of the sea parted around them. They climbed up onto the shore, then up into the sky, and found the wide-seeing son of Cronus, with the other blessed immortals seated around him. Athena gave up her place, and Thetis sat down beside Father Zeus. Hera put a fair golden cup into her hand and spoke a word of comfort, and Thetis drank, and gave it back. Then the father of gods and men was the first to speak.

“You have come up to Olympus, goddess, for all your sorrow — and I know the grief that never leaves your heart. Even so, I’ll tell you why I called you. For nine days the immortals have quarreled over Hector’s body and Achilles, sacker of cities. They would have keen-eyed Hermes steal the corpse away. But I mean to give your son this honor instead, and keep your love and goodwill in the days to come. Go quickly to the camp, and lay this on him: tell him the gods are angry, and that I am angrier than them all, that in his fury he keeps Hector at the ships and will not give him up. Let him fear me, then, and release the body. Meanwhile I will send Iris to great-hearted Priam, to bid him go to the ships of the Greeks and ransom his dear son, carrying gifts to soften Achilles’ heart.”

Silver-footed Thetis did not refuse. Down she darted from the peaks of Olympus and came to her son’s shelter. She found him there, groaning without end, while his companions moved about him, busy, making the morning meal — a great fleeced ram lay slaughtered in the hut. His honored mother sat close beside him, stroked him with her hand, and spoke his name: “My child, how long will you eat your own heart out with grief and mourning? You think nothing of food, nothing of a woman’s bed. It would do you good — for you have little time left; already death and the hard hand of fate are standing close. Now listen to me. I come to you from Zeus. He says the gods are angry with you, and that he is angrier than them all, that in your fury you keep Hector at the ships and will not give him up. Let the body go, then, and take a ransom for the dead.”

And swift-footed Achilles answered her: “So be it. Let the man who brings the ransom take the body away, if the Olympian himself, of his own will, commands it.” So at the ships, mother and son spoke on together at length.

Meanwhile the son of Cronus sent Iris to holy Troy: “Go, swift Iris, down from the heights of Olympus, and tell great-hearted Priam in the city to go to the ships of the Greeks and ransom his dear son, bearing gifts to soften Achilles’ heart. He is to go alone — no other Trojan with him, only an old herald to drive the mules and the smooth-running wagon, and to carry home the body of the man noble Achilles killed. Let him put away the thought of death, and any fear; I will give him keen-eyed Hermes as escort, to lead him all the way to Achilles. And once Hermes has brought him inside, Achilles will not kill him — he will hold the others back as well. He is no fool, nor blind, nor hardened against the gods; he will spare a suppliant, and treat him with the honor that is owed.”

So Iris, swift as the wind, sped off with the message. She came to Priam’s house and found it full of weeping and wailing. The king’s sons sat in the courtyard around their father, their clothes soaked with tears, and the old man in their midst, wrapped close in his cloak, his head and neck fouled with the dung he had clawed up and ground into himself, rolling on the ground. His daughters and his sons’ wives moved through the house lamenting, remembering all the brave men who lay dead, cut down by the Greeks. The messenger of Zeus came up to Priam and spoke softly, and the trembling took him as she did. “Take heart, Priam, of the line of Dardanus,” she said. “Take heart, and have no fear. I am not here to bring you harm, but good. I come from Zeus, who, far off as he is, takes thought for you and pities you. The Olympian commands you to go and ransom noble Hector, carrying gifts to soften Achilles’ heart. You are to go alone, no other Trojan with you, only an old herald to drive the mules and the smooth-running wagon, and to bring back to the city the body of the man noble Achilles killed. Put away the thought of death, and any fear. Hermes will go as your escort and guide, and lead you all the way to Achilles. And when he has brought you inside the tent, Achilles will not kill you — he will hold the others back as well. He is no fool, nor blind, nor hardened against the gods; he will spare a suppliant, and treat him with the honor that is owed.”

With that, swift Iris was gone. Priam told his sons to make ready a strong-wheeled mule-cart and to lash the wicker body fast on top of it. Then he went down to his fragrant storeroom, high-roofed and built of cedar, where his many treasures lay, and called his wife Hecuba to him. “Wife,” he said, “a messenger has come to me from Olympus and told me to go to the ships of the Greeks and ransom our dear son, carrying gifts to soften Achilles’ heart. Tell me — what do you make of it? My own heart and spirit drive me hard to go down there, to the ships, to the broad camp of the Greeks.”

His wife cried out and answered him: “No — where has your judgment gone, the judgment that once made you famous among strangers and among your own? How can you bring yourself to go alone to the ships of the Greeks, to look in the face of the man who has killed so many of your brave sons? Your heart is iron. If that cruel, faithless man lays eyes on you and gets you in his hands, he will show you no respect, no pity at all. No — let us mourn Hector here, in our own house, from far off. This is the doom that fate spun for him at his birth, when I bore him: that he should glut the swift dogs far from his parents, in the house of that violent man. I wish I could fasten on his liver and eat it. That would be some payment for my son — who was no coward when Achilles killed him; he stood his ground for the men of Troy and the deep-girdled women of Troy, with no thought of flight or hiding.”

Then the old man, godlike Priam, answered: “I am set on going. Do not hold me back, and do not be a bird of ill omen in my own house — you will not turn me. If some mortal had told me this — a prophet, a reader of sacrifice, a priest — I would have called it a lie and turned my back on it. But I heard the goddess myself; I saw her face to face. So I will go, and her word will not be in vain. And if it is my fate to die at the ships of the Greeks, then let it be. Let Achilles cut me down — once I have taken my son in my arms, once I have let my grief have its way.”

So he lifted the lids of his fine chests and chose out twelve splendid robes, twelve single cloaks, twelve blankets, twelve white mantles, and as many tunics with them. He weighed out ten full talents of gold, and brought two gleaming tripods, four cauldrons, and a cup of great beauty that the men of Thrace had given him when he went to them as an envoy — a treasured thing, but the old man spared not even this, so fiercely did he long to ransom his dear son. Then he drove all the Trojans from his porch, scolding them in his anger: “Out — you good-for-nothings, you disgraces. Have you no grief of your own at home, that you come to torment me here? Is it nothing to you that Zeus has sent me this sorrow — to lose the best of my sons? You’ll learn what it is soon enough; now that he’s dead, you’ll be that much easier for the Greeks to kill. But as for me, let me go down into the house of Hades before my eyes see the city sacked and laid waste.”

He drove them off with his staff, and they fled before the old man’s fury. Then he called angrily to his own sons — Helenus and Paris and noble Agathon, Pammon and Antiphonus and Polites of the war cry, Deiphobus and Hippothous and brave Dius. To these nine the old man shouted his commands: “Hurry, you worthless sons, my shame. I wish all of you had been killed at the ships instead of Hector. What a wretched man I am. I fathered the best sons in all wide Troy, and not one of them is left — not Mestor like a god, nor Troilus who fought from his chariot, nor Hector, who walked among men like a god, who seemed the son of no mortal but of a god. Ares has destroyed them all, and only the shameful ones are left me — the liars, the dancers, fine heroes at beating the floor, thieves of lambs and kids from your own people. Will you not get the wagon ready for me, now, this moment, and load all this on it, so I can be on my way?”

They cringed under their father’s rebuke and brought out the strong, new mule-cart, and lashed the wicker body fast on top of it. They lifted down the boxwood mule-yoke from its peg, knobbed and fitted with rings for the reins, and brought the yoke-strap with it, eleven cubits long. They settled the yoke on the polished pole at the upturned end, slipped the ring over the peg, lashed it with three turns each way around the knob, then bound the loose end fast and tucked it under. Then they carried out from the storeroom the boundless ransom for Hector’s head and stacked it on the cart, and yoked the strong-hooved mules the Mysians had once given Priam as a fine gift. And for Priam they led under the yoke the horses the old king himself had bred and reared at his own stall.

So the two of them, the king and his herald, both with care on their minds, were having the chariot yoked in the high palace, when Hecuba came near them, grieving. She carried sweet wine in a golden cup in her right hand, for them to pour an offering before they went. She stood in front of the horses and spoke: “Here — pour to Father Zeus, and pray for a safe return from the hands of your enemies, since your heart is set on going to the ships against my will. Pray to the son of Cronus, lord of the storm-cloud, who watches over all of Troy from Ida, and ask him to send his swift bird of omen on your right — the bird he loves best, the strongest in the sky — so you may see it with your own eyes and trust it as you go down to the ships of the Greeks. But if Zeus the wide-seeing will not send you his messenger, then I would not have you go, however set on it you are.”

And godlike Priam answered: “Wife, I will not refuse you in this. It is good to lift the hands to Zeus, in hope that he will pity us.” So the old man told the housekeeper to pour clean water over his hands, and she came, holding the basin and the pitcher. He washed, and took the cup from his wife, and standing in the middle of the courtyard he prayed, looking up to the sky, and poured the wine: “Father Zeus, who rule from Ida, greatest and most glorious — grant that I find welcome and mercy when I come to Achilles. Send me your swift bird of omen on the right, the bird you love best, the strongest in the sky, so I may see it with my own eyes and trust it as I go down to the ships of the Greeks.”

So he prayed, and Zeus the lord of counsel heard him. At once he sent down an eagle, surest of all winged omens, the dark hunter that men also call the dusky eagle. Wide as the well-fitted, well-bolted door of a rich man’s high chamber were its wings, spread on either side; and it swept across the city on their right. They saw it, and their hearts lifted in their breasts. The old man climbed quickly into his chariot and drove out through the gateway and the echoing portico. Before him the mules drew the four-wheeled cart, driven by wise Idaeus, and behind came the horses, which the old man drove with the lash, fast through the city. His kinsmen all followed, weeping for him as if he were going to his death. But when they had come down out of the city onto the plain, the sons and the sons-in-law turned back to Troy.

Priam and Idaeus did not pass unseen out onto the plain. Zeus the wide-seeing looked down on the old man and pitied him, and at once he spoke to his dear son Hermes: “Hermes — escorting a man is the work you love best of all, and you listen to whom you please. Go now, and lead Priam to the hollow ships of the Greeks so that no one sees him, no one of the Greeks marks him, until he reaches the son of Peleus.”

So he spoke, and the guide, the slayer of Argus, obeyed. At once he bound under his feet the lovely golden sandals, undying, that bore him over the water and the boundless earth as fast as the breath of the wind. And he took up the wand with which he lulls men’s eyes to sleep, or wakes the sleeping as he pleases, and flew, holding it, the strong slayer of Argus, until he came to Troy and the Hellespont. There he went on in the likeness of a young prince with the first down on his lip, in the loveliest hour of youth.

Now when the two had driven past the great barrow of Ilus, they halted the mules and horses at the river to let them drink, for darkness was coming down over the land. And the herald looked up and made out Hermes close beside them, and he said to Priam: “Look hard, Priam of Dardanus’ line — this calls for a careful mind. I see a man, and I think we are about to be cut down. Come, let us flee with the horses — or take hold of his knees and beg him to have pity.” At that the old man’s mind reeled and the fear ran cold through him; the hair stood up on his bent body, and he stood there, stunned. But the kindly god came up to him on his own, took the old man by the hand, and asked him: “Where are you going, father, with your horses and mules, through the deep of the night, when other men are asleep? Are you not afraid of the Greeks, breathing their fury so near you, men with no mercy and no relenting? If one of them caught you carrying such treasure through the swift black night — what then? You are not young, and the man with you is old; you could not fend off an attacker. But I will do you no harm — I will keep others off you. You are like my own father in my eyes.”

And the old man, godlike Priam, answered him: “It is just as you say, dear child. But still, some god has held his hand over me, to send a traveler like you across my path at such an hour — for you are beautiful in form and bearing, and your mind is sound. You must be born of blessed parents.”

Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, answered: “Old man, all of that is true. But tell me this, and tell me straight: are you sending all this rich treasure off to safety among foreign people — or are you all abandoning sacred Troy in fear, now that the best of your men has fallen, your son, who never gave ground to the Greeks in battle?”

And the old man, godlike Priam, said: “Who are you, fine young man? Whose son? How is it you speak so truly of the fate of my doomed son?”

And the guide, the slayer of Argus, answered: “You are testing me, old man, asking about noble Hector. Many a time I saw him with my own eyes in the fighting that wins glory, and again when he drove the Greeks back on their ships and cut them down with the bronze. We stood there in wonder, for Achilles in his anger at the son of Atreus would not let us fight. I am Achilles’ man; the same ship carried us here. I am a Myrmidon. My father is Polyctor — a rich man, about as old as you. He has seven sons; I am the seventh. We cast lots, and the lot fell on me to come here with Achilles. I have come up from the ships to the plain just now, for at dawn the bright-eyed Greeks will set the battle around the city. They chafe at sitting idle, and their kings can no longer hold them back from war.”

And the old man, godlike Priam, answered: “If you are truly a man of Achilles, son of Peleus, then tell me the whole truth: is my son still lying by the ships, or has Achilles already hacked him limb from limb and thrown him to the dogs?”

Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, said: “Old man, the dogs have not eaten him, nor the birds. He still lies there by the ship of Achilles, in the shelter, just as he was. This is the twelfth day he has lain there, and his flesh has not rotted, nor have the worms touched him, the worms that eat men killed in war. True, every dawn Achilles drags him without mercy round the tomb of his dear friend — but it does him no harm. You would marvel to come and see how he lies, fresh as dew, the blood all washed from him, no stain on him anywhere. Every wound has closed, all the wounds men dealt him, for many drove their bronze into him. So the blessed gods care for your good son, even dead, for he was dear to their hearts.”

The old man was glad to hear it, and said: “My child, it is a good thing to give the immortals their due. As surely as he was ever mine, my son never forgot, there in our halls, the gods who hold Olympus — and now they remember him in the house of death. Come, take this fine cup from my hands; and in return, guard me, and lead me, with the gods’ help, until I come to the shelter of the son of Peleus.”

Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, answered: “You are testing me, old man, and I am young — but you will not move me, asking me to take a gift behind Achilles’ back. I have too much fear and reverence for him to rob him; some harm might come of it later. But I would be your guide, with all care, even all the way to famous Argos, on a swift ship or on foot, and no one would scorn your escort and attack you.” With that the kindly god leapt up into the chariot, took the whip and the reins in his hands, and breathed fresh strength into the horses and mules.

When they came to the trench and the wall before the ships, the sentries were just then setting out their supper, and the slayer of Argus poured sleep over them all, and drew back the bolts, and opened the gates, and led Priam in, and the splendid gifts on the cart. And they came to the high shelter of the son of Peleus, which the Myrmidons had built for their king, cutting the pine themselves; they roofed it with shaggy reeds they had mown from the meadow, and around it they raised a great courtyard for their king, fenced with close-set stakes. One bar of pine held the gate — it took three men to drive it home and three to draw it back, but Achilles could work it alone. Then Hermes, the kindly god, opened the gate for the old man and brought in the glorious gifts for the swift-footed son of Peleus, and stepped down from the chariot to the ground, and said: “Old man, it is a god who has come with you — I am the immortal Hermes; my father sent me to be your guide. But now I will go; I will not come into Achilles’ sight. It would give offense for a god to show such open favor to mortals. You go in, and take the knees of the son of Peleus, and beg him by his father, by his lovely-haired mother, and by his son, to move his heart.”

With that Hermes was gone, back to high Olympus. And Priam stepped down from the chariot to the ground, leaving Idaeus there to keep the horses and mules. The old man went straight to the shelter where Achilles, beloved of Zeus, was sitting. He found him within, his companions seated apart; only two, the warrior Automedon and Alcimus, son of Ares’ line, were busy near him, waiting on him. He had just finished his meal, eating and drinking, and the table still stood beside him. Great Priam came in unseen, and standing close he took Achilles’ knees in his arms, and kissed his hands — those terrible, man-killing hands that had killed so many of his sons.

As when blind madness takes a man, and in his own land he kills another, and flees to a far country, to a rich man’s house, and all who see him stare — so Achilles stared, amazed, at the sight of godlike Priam. The others looked at one another in wonder. But Priam made his plea: “Remember your own father, godlike Achilles — a man as old as I am, on the bitter threshold of old age. Maybe his neighbors are pressing him hard, with no one to keep off ruin and harm. Yet even so, while he hears that you are still alive, his heart lifts, and every day he hopes to see his beloved son come home from Troy. But I — I am the most cursed of men. I fathered the bravest sons in all wide Troy, and I tell you, not one of them is left. Fifty I had when the Greeks came: nineteen from a single womb, the rest borne to me by women of my house. Furious Ares has cut the knees out from under most of them. And the one who was left, who alone guarded the city and its people — him you have just now killed, fighting for his own land: Hector. For his sake I have come to the ships of the Greeks, to ransom him from you, and I bring a boundless ransom. So have reverence for the gods, Achilles, and pity me, remembering your own father. I am more to be pitied than he. I have brought myself to do what no other man on earth has done — to lift to my lips the hand of the man who killed my son.”

So he spoke, and he stirred in Achilles a longing to weep for his own father. He took the old man’s hand and pushed him gently away. And the two of them remembered: Priam, crouched at Achilles’ feet, wept hard for Hector the killer of men, and Achilles wept now for his father, now again for Patroclus, and the sound of their mourning filled the house. But when noble Achilles had had his fill of grief, and the ache had gone out of his heart and his limbs, he rose from his seat and lifted the old man up by the hand, pitying his gray head and gray beard, and spoke to him with winged words: “Poor man — what your heart has borne. How could you bring yourself to come alone to the ships of the Greeks, to the eyes of the man who killed so many of your fine sons? Your heart is iron. Come, sit on this seat. However much it hurts us, let us let our grief lie quiet in our hearts. There is nothing to be gained by cold mourning. This is the way the gods have spun it for us, for wretched mortals: that we live in sorrow, while they themselves have no cares.

“On the floor of Zeus’s house stand two jars of the gifts he gives — one of evils, the other of blessings. To the man Zeus the thunderer hands a mixture from both, evil and good come by turns. But to the man he gives only from the jar of sorrows, that man he makes a thing of contempt — a raw hunger drives him over the shining earth, and he wanders honored by neither gods nor men. So it was with Peleus. The gods gave him glittering gifts from his very birth: he outshone all men in wealth and good fortune, he was king over the Myrmidons, and the gods gave him, a mortal, a goddess for his wife. But even on him a god laid an evil too — no line of strong sons born in his halls to come after him, only one child, doomed to an early death. And I am no comfort to him in his old age, since here I sit, far from my own country, in Troy, bringing grief to you and to your children. And you, old man — once, we hear, you too were happy. In all the land bounded by Lesbos, Macar’s seat, out to sea and Phrygia inland and the wide Hellespont, they say no one matched you in wealth and in sons. But ever since the gods of heaven brought this affliction on you, there has been nothing around your city but battle and the killing of men. Bear it. Do not grieve on and on without end. You will gain nothing by mourning for your son. You will not raise him up again, and you may suffer some new sorrow first.”

And the old man, godlike Priam, answered: “Do not seat me, beloved of Zeus, while Hector lies uncared-for in your shelter. Give him to me, quickly, let me look on him with my own eyes — and take the great ransom we have brought. May you have the good of it, and come home safe to your own land, since you have let me go on living and looking on the light of the sun.”

Swift-footed Achilles looked at him from under his brows and said: “Push me no further, old man. I mean of my own will to give Hector back to you. My mother came to me from Zeus, the daughter of the old man of the sea. And I see clearly, Priam — you cannot hide it — that some god led you to the ships of the Greeks. No mortal, however young and strong, would dare to come into our camp; he could not pass the sentries unseen, nor lever back the bar of my gate so easily. So do not provoke my grief any further, or I may not spare you, old man, suppliant though you are in my shelter — and I would break the command of Zeus.”

The old man was afraid, and obeyed. Then the son of Peleus sprang to the door like a lion, and not alone — two of his men went with him, the warrior Automedon and Alcimus, whom Achilles honored most of his companions now that Patroclus was dead. They unyoked the horses and mules, and led in the old king’s herald and seated him, and lifted down from the polished cart the boundless ransom for Hector’s head. But they left two cloaks and a fine-woven tunic, to wrap the body for the journey home. Then Achilles called the serving-women out to wash the body and anoint it, but first to carry it apart, so that Priam would not see his son — lest in his grief he could not hold his anger at the sight, and rouse the heart of Achilles, who might then kill him and break the command of Zeus. So the women washed the body and rubbed it with oil and wrapped it in the fine tunic and the cloak, and Achilles himself lifted it and laid it on a bier, and his men raised it onto the polished cart. Then he groaned, and called on the name of his dear friend: “Do not be angry with me, Patroclus, if you learn, even in the house of Hades, that I have given noble Hector back to his father. He has paid a ransom that is not unworthy — and I will share it fairly with you, your due.”

Then noble Achilles went back inside and sat down on the richly worked couch he had risen from, against the far wall, and spoke to Priam: “Your son is given back to you, old man, as you asked. He lies on the bier. At first light you will see him for yourself, and carry him home. For now, let us think of supper. Even lovely-haired Niobe remembered to eat, when twelve of her children had died in her halls — six daughters and six sons in the prime of their youth. Apollo killed the sons with arrows from his silver bow, in anger at Niobe, and Artemis the archer killed the daughters, because Niobe had set herself beside lovely Leto: she said Leto had borne only two, while she herself had borne many. So the two, for all they were only two, destroyed them all. Nine days they lay in their blood, with no one to bury them, for the son of Cronus had turned the people to stone; and on the tenth the gods of heaven buried them. And then Niobe, worn out with weeping, remembered to eat. Now, somewhere among the rocks, in the lonely mountains of Sipylus, where they say the goddesses sleep, the nymphs who dance along the river Achelous — there, though she is stone, she still broods over the sorrows the gods gave her. So come, noble old man, let the two of us think of food. You can weep for your dear son again, once you have brought him into Troy — and he will cost you many tears.”

With that swift Achilles sprang up and killed a white sheep, and his companions skinned it and dressed it well, and cut the meat skillfully, and ran it onto spits, and roasted it through, and drew it off. Automedon took the bread and set it round the table in fine baskets, and Achilles served the meat. They reached out their hands to the good things laid ready before them. And when they had put away their desire for food and drink, Priam, of the line of Dardanus, gazed at Achilles in wonder — how tall, how beautiful he was, the very likeness of a god to look on. And Achilles in turn wondered at Priam, of the line of Dardanus, at his noble face and the words he heard him speak. When they had had their fill of gazing at each other, the old man, godlike Priam, spoke first: “Let me lie down now, beloved of Zeus, and quickly, so we may take our rest in the sweetness of sleep. Not once have my eyes closed beneath their lids since my son lost his life under your hands. Ever since, I have grieved and brooded over my countless sorrows, grinding myself into the dung of my courtyard. Only now have I tasted food, and let the bright wine pass my throat. Until now, I had taken nothing.”

So Achilles told his men and the serving-women to set up beds under the portico, and to lay fine purple rugs on them, and spread blankets over them, and put thick cloaks on top to wrap themselves in. The women went out, torch in hand, and quickly made up the two beds. Then Achilles, the great runner, said to Priam, half in jest: “Sleep outside, dear old man, in case one of the Greek counselors should come — they are always coming to sit with me and lay their plans, as is right. If one of them caught sight of you here through the swift black night, he would tell Agamemnon at once, and the giving-back of the body might be delayed. But tell me this, and tell me true: how many days do you need for the funeral of noble Hector? I will hold back myself, and hold back the army, for that long.”

And the old man, godlike Priam, answered: “If you would truly let me give noble Hector his burial, then, Achilles, you would do me a kindness in this. You know how we are penned inside the city; it is far to bring wood from the mountain, and the Trojans are full of fear. For nine days we would mourn him in our halls; on the tenth we would bury him and the people would feast; on the eleventh we would raise a mound over him; and on the twelfth we will fight again, if we must.”

Then swift-footed noble Achilles answered: “It shall be as you ask, old Priam. I will hold back the war for as long as you have named.” And he clasped the old man’s right hand at the wrist, so that his heart would have no fear. So they lay down to sleep there in the forecourt of the house, Priam and his herald, with much on their minds; but Achilles slept in the inner room of the well-built shelter, and beside him lay fair-cheeked Briseis.

Now all the other gods and the men who fight from chariots slept the whole night through, mastered by gentle sleep. But sleep took no hold of Hermes, the kindly god, as he turned over in his mind how to lead King Priam back from the ships unseen by the strong guards at the gate. He stood over Priam’s head and spoke: “Old man, you have no fear at all of harm, the way you sleep among your enemies, now that Achilles has spared you. You have ransomed your dear son, and paid a great price. But for your life, the sons you left behind would pay three times as much, if Agamemnon, son of Atreus, learned you were here, and all the Greeks knew it.”

At that the old man took fright, and woke his herald. Hermes yoked the horses and mules for them and drove them quickly through the camp, and no one saw. But when they reached the ford of the lovely, eddying river Xanthus, born of immortal Zeus, Hermes left them and went up to high Olympus, as Dawn in her saffron robe was spreading over all the earth. And the two drove the horses on toward the city, mourning and groaning, and the mules drew the body. No one saw them, no man and no fair-belted woman — no one but Cassandra, lovely as golden Aphrodite. She had climbed to the height of Pergamus, and from there she saw her dear father standing in the chariot, and the herald, the city’s crier, beside him, and behind them, on the bier on the cart, the body the mules were drawing. Then she cried out, and called through all the city: “Come, men of Troy, come, women of Troy — look on Hector, if ever you rejoiced to see him come home alive from battle. He was the great joy of the city and all its people.”

At that, not a man, not a woman, was left in the city — a grief past bearing had come over them all. Close by the gates they met Priam bringing in the body. First of all, Hector’s wife and his honored mother flung themselves on the smooth-wheeled cart, tearing at their hair, and laid their hands on his head, and the crowd stood weeping around them. And there before the gates they would have wept for Hector the whole day long, to the setting of the sun, had the old man not spoken to them from the chariot: “Let the mules through. You will have your fill of weeping later, once I have brought him home.” So the people drew apart and made way for the cart.

When they had brought him into the great house, they laid him on a corded bed and set singers beside him to lead the dirge, men who chanted the mourning-song, and the women wailed in answer. And white-armed Andromache led the lament, cradling the head of man-killing Hector in her arms: “My husband — you have died young, and left me a widow in your house, and the boy is still only a baby, the child you and I bore, ill-fated both of us. I do not think he will reach his manhood. Long before that, this city will be sacked from top to bottom. For you who watched over it are gone — you who guarded it, who kept its loyal wives and little children safe. Soon they will be carried off in the hollow ships, and I among them. And you, my child, will come with me, to labor at harsh work for a brutal master. Or some Greek will seize you by the arm and hurl you from the wall to a pitiful death, in his rage that Hector killed his brother, or his father, or his son — for many a Greek bit the earth under Hector’s hands. Your father was no gentle man in the grief of battle; that is why the people mourn him through the city. You have left your parents an unspeakable grief, Hector — and to me, most bitter of all. You did not reach your arms to me from your deathbed, you spoke me no last close word that I might have kept and wept over, day and night, forever.”

So she spoke, weeping, and the women wailed with her. Then Hecuba took up the lament in her turn: “Hector — dearest by far to my heart of all my children. While you lived, the gods loved you well, and even in your death they have not forgotten you. The others of my sons that Achilles caught, he sold across the barren sea, to Samos, to Imbros, to Lemnos lost in mist. But you — when he had taken your life with the long bronze, he dragged you again and again round the tomb of his friend Patroclus, whom you killed; and yet it did not bring him back. Now here you lie in my halls, fresh as dew, like a man whom Apollo of the silver bow has come upon and killed with his gentle arrows.”

So she spoke, weeping, and stirred their endless grief. Then Helen, third of them, led the mourning: “Hector — dearest by far to my heart of all my husband’s brothers. My husband is Paris, godlike to see, who brought me to Troy. I wish I had died first. This is the twentieth year since I came from my own land and left my home, and in all that time I never heard from you one cruel word, one unkind word. If anyone else in the house spoke against me — a brother, a sister, a brother’s fair-robed wife, or my husband’s mother (though Priam was always gentle to me, like a father) — you would check them, you would talk them round with your own gentleness, your kind words. So I weep for you and for my own wretched self together, with grief in my heart. There is no one left in all wide Troy who is kind to me, or my friend. They all shrink from me, and shudder.”

So she spoke, weeping, and the great crowd mourned with her. Then old Priam said to his people: “Now, men of Troy, bring wood into the city, and have no fear of some Greek ambush. Achilles, when he sent me from the black ships, gave me his pledge that he would do us no harm until the twelfth dawn comes.”

So they yoked their oxen and mules to the wagons and gathered before the city. For nine days they hauled in a vast store of wood. And when the tenth dawn came with its light for mortals, they carried out brave Hector, weeping, and laid his body on the top of the pyre, and set fire to it. When the next day’s child, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, the people gathered around the pyre of glorious Hector. First they quenched the embers with bright wine, wherever the fire’s strength had reached. Then his brothers and his companions gathered up his white bones, mourning, the heavy tears running down their cheeks. They wrapped the bones in soft purple cloth and laid them in a golden chest, and set it down in a hollow grave, and roofed it over with great close-set stones. Then they heaped a mound over it quickly, with lookouts posted on every side, in case the Greeks should attack before the time. And when they had raised the mound, they went back into the city, and gathered together, and held a great and fitting feast in the house of Priam, the king cherished by Zeus.

Such was the funeral of Hector, breaker of horses.
