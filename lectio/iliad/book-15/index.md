# The Iliad, Book XV

The Battle at the Ships

When their flight had carried them back past the trench and the row of stakes, and many had gone down under Greek hands, the Trojans halted by their chariots, pale and shaking. And on the heights of Ida Zeus woke, where he had lain beside golden-throned Hera, and he sprang to his feet and looked out and saw the Trojans and the Greeks — the one side scattered in rout, the other driving them headlong with lord Poseidon in their midst. He saw Hector lying on the plain, his comrades crouched around him, struggling for breath, his mind wandering, vomiting blood; for the man who had struck him was not the weakest of the Greeks.

The father of gods and men pitied him, and turned a terrible look on Hera. “This is your work, Hera — your scheming, your treachery. You crippled Hector and routed his army. I have half a mind to be the first to make you taste what your cunning earns: I will lash you for it. Have you forgotten the time I hung you out of heaven, with two anvils chained to your feet and your wrists bound in a band of gold that nothing could break? You swung there in the high air among the clouds. The gods on Olympus raged at the sight, but they could not reach you to set you free — and any one of them I caught, I seized and flung from the threshold of the sky, and down he came half-dead to the earth. Even that did not ease the grief that gnawed at me over godlike Heracles, my son, when you and the North Wind had whipped up a storm and driven him over the barren sea to Cos, out of pure spite. I rescued him from there and brought him back to Argos, for all the labors he had borne. I remind you of this so you will stop your deceiving, and learn how little you stand to gain from the bed you came down from Olympus to lure me into and trick me.”

Hera shuddered at his words, and answered: “Let Earth be my witness, and the wide Heaven above, and the down-flowing water of the Styx — the most binding oath the blessed gods can swear — and your own sacred head, and the bed we share as man and wife, which I would never swear by falsely: it is no doing of mine that Poseidon is breaking Hector and the Trojans and helping the Greeks. He acts on his own, driven by his own heart, because he was grieved to see the Greeks crushed against their ships. If it were up to me, I would tell him to go wherever you, lord of the storm-cloud, lead.”

The father of gods and men smiled at that, and answered: “If only you would always think with me, Hera, when we sit in council, then Poseidon, however much he might wish otherwise, would soon bend his mind to yours and mine. But if you mean what you say and speak the truth, then go among the gods and send me Iris and Apollo, lord of the bow. Iris will go down to the Greek army and tell Poseidon to break off the fighting and come home to his own halls. And Apollo will send Hector back into battle and breathe fresh strength into him, so he forgets the pain that wracks him now, and drives the Greeks back in headlong panic until they fall among the ships of Achilles, son of Peleus. Then Achilles will rouse his comrade Patroclus into the fighting, and bright Hector will kill him in front of Troy, after Patroclus has cut down many strong men — among them my own son, noble Sarpedon. And in fury for Patroclus, brilliant Achilles will kill Hector. From that hour on I will turn the tide and drive the Trojans steadily back from the ships, on and on, until the Greeks take steep Troy through the counsels of Athena. But until then I will not let my anger rest, and I will let no other god help the Greeks, not until the desire of the son of Peleus is fulfilled — as I promised him, bowing my head, on the day Thetis clasped my knees and begged me to give honor to Achilles, sacker of cities.”

Hera did not disobey. She went from the peaks of Ida to high Olympus. Swift as the mind of a man who has traveled over much of the world and thinks, “I wish I were there, or there” — and in a flash his thought has flown across it all — so swiftly did queenly Hera dart upward, until she came to steep Olympus and entered among the immortal gods gathered in the house of Zeus. When they saw her they all rose and lifted their cups toward her in welcome. She passed the others by, but took the cup from lovely Themis, who had run to her first, and who spoke to her with care: “Hera, why have you come? You look stricken. Has the son of Cronus, your husband, been frightening you?”

White-armed Hera answered: “Don’t ask me, Themis. You know yourself how arrogant and hard his temper is. Take your place at the feast among the gods, and you will hear, with all the immortals, what evil things Zeus is announcing. I tell you, this will not gladden every heart, mortal or divine, however cheerfully some of them are feasting now.”

She sat down, and through the house of Zeus the gods were troubled. Her lips were smiling, but above her dark brows her forehead was not, and she spoke out in anger to them all: “Fools that we are, to rage against Zeus like children. We keep wanting to go up to him and stop him, by force or by words — and he sits apart and cares nothing for us, because he knows he is far the strongest of all the immortals. So each of you make the best of whatever grief he chooses to send you. Ares, I think, has already had his share: his son Ascalaphus is dead, fallen in the fighting — the man Ares loved best of all, the man he calls his own.”

When Ares heard this he struck both his sturdy thighs with the flat of his hands and cried out in grief: “Don’t blame me, you gods who live on Olympus, if I go down to the Greek ships to avenge my son’s death — even if I am fated to be struck by the bolt of Zeus and to lie there among the corpses in blood and dust.”

And he gave the order to yoke his horses, Terror and Rout, and he began to put on his armor. Then Zeus would have been roused to an anger against the immortals greater and more bitter than ever — but Athena, fearing for all the gods, sprang from her seat and ran out through the doorway. She tore the helmet from Ares’ head and the shield from his shoulders, took the bronze spear from his strong hand and set it aside, and rounded on furious Ares: “Madman — you’ve lost your mind, you’re ruined. You have ears but you cannot hear; your judgment and your sense are gone. Did you not hear what white-armed Hera said, who came to us straight from the side of Olympian Zeus? Do you want to drink your fill of sorrow and be dragged back to Olympus sick and beaten, with disaster sown for the rest of us? At once Zeus would leave off with the Trojans and the Greeks and come up here to drive us all before him, seizing us one after another, guilty or not. So let go of this rage over your son. Better men than he have been killed already, or will be yet. It is not possible to keep every man’s whole line alive.”

With that she sat raging Ares back in his seat. And Hera called Apollo out of the hall, and Iris with him, the messenger of the gods, and said to them: “Zeus orders you to come to him on Ida at once. When you have seen him, do whatever he commands.”

Queenly Hera went back in and took her seat, and the two of them sped off. They reached Ida, the mother of wild beasts, with its many springs, and found the wide-seeing son of Cronus seated on the highest crest of Gargarus, a fragrant cloud wreathed around him like a crown. They came and stood before him, and he was pleased that they had been so quick to obey his wife’s word.

He spoke to Iris first: “Go, swift Iris. Tell lord Poseidon all this — and tell it true. Order him to break off the fighting and join the company of the gods, or go down into the sea. And if he will not listen, if he disobeys me, then let him think hard whether he is strong enough to hold his ground against me when I come for him. I am the elder, and far the stronger — and yet he does not shrink from setting himself up as my equal, the one whom all the other gods fear.”

Iris, swift as the storm-wind, did not disobey. Down she swept from the heights of Ida to holy Troy, fast as the snow or the cold hail that flies from the clouds before the blast of the North Wind, until she came up close to the great Earthshaker and spoke: “I have come, dark-haired lord who holds the earth in your embrace, to bring you word from Zeus. He orders you to break off the fighting and join the company of the gods, or go down into the sea. And if you will not listen, if you disobey, he threatens to come here himself and fight you — and he warns you to keep clear of his hands, for he is the elder, and far the stronger; and yet you do not shrink from setting yourself up as his equal, the one whom all the other gods fear.”

Poseidon, deeply angered, answered: “No — for all his strength, this is too much, if he would force me down by threats, when I am of equal honor with him. We are three brothers, sons of Cronus by Rhea: Zeus, and myself, and Hades, who is lord of the dead below. The whole world was split three ways, and each of us drew his share. The lot gave me the gray sea to live in forever; Hades drew the misty dark; Zeus drew the wide sky among the clouds and the bright upper air. But the earth and high Olympus are still common to us all. So I will not live by the will of Zeus. Strong as he is, let him stay content with his own third, and not try to frighten me as if I were some nobody. Let him save his bullying for the sons and daughters he fathered — they have no choice but to obey him.”

Iris, swift as the wind, answered: “Dark-haired lord who holds the earth, am I truly to carry this hard and unbending answer back to Zeus? Or will you give a little? The minds of good men can be turned. And you know the Furies always stand with the elder-born.”

Poseidon, who holds the earth, replied: “Iris, you speak well, and in season. It is a fine thing when a messenger has good sense. But it galls me to the heart that he would scold an equal, one who shares his rank, in such angry words. Still — for now, and against my will, I’ll give way. But I tell you this, and I mean it: if, against my wish and Athena’s and Hera’s, against Hermes and lord Hephaestus, Zeus spares steep Troy and will not let the Greeks have the great triumph of sacking it — let him know there will be a wound between us that nothing will ever heal.”

With that the Earthshaker left the field and went down into the sea, and the Greeks felt his loss keenly. Then Zeus who gathers the clouds said to Apollo: “Go now, dear Phoebus, to Hector of the bronze helmet. Poseidon who holds the earth has gone down into the sea, out of reach of my anger — and well for both of us that he did, for it would have cost us a hard struggle, and the gods below with Cronus would have heard the noise of it. Take up the tasselled aegis, and shake it hard, and put the Greek champions to flight. And take great Hector into your own care, far-shooter, and rouse his strength again, until the Greeks are driven back in panic to their ships and to the Hellespont. From there I will see to it myself, how the Greeks may have some relief from their trouble.”

Apollo did not disobey his father. Down he came from the crests of Ida like a falcon, the swift killer of doves, the fastest thing that flies. He found Hector no longer lying on the ground but sitting up, just coming back to himself, knowing the men around him; the sweat and the hard breathing had passed, now that the will of Zeus had revived him. Apollo stood beside him and said: “Hector, son of Priam — why are you sitting here, faint and apart from the rest? Has some trouble come over you?”

Faintly Hector answered: “Which of the gods are you, kind one, who ask me this? Don’t you know that at the Greek ships, while I was killing his comrades, Ajax struck me in the chest with a boulder and took the fight out of me? I was sure this was the day I would breathe my last and go down to the house of the dead.”

Then lord Apollo who strikes from afar said: “Take heart. The son of Cronus has sent you a powerful helper down from Ida, to stand by you and guard you — Phoebus Apollo of the golden sword, who has watched over you and your high city all this time. Up now — order your charioteers to drive their teams hard against the hollow ships. I will go before you and smooth the whole way for the horses, and turn the Greek champions in flight.”

And he breathed great strength into the shepherd of his people. As a stalled horse, full-fed at the manger, snaps his halter and gallops thundering over the plain, headed for his bathing place in the sweet-running river — head high, mane streaming over his shoulders, sure of his own splendor, his legs carrying him to the haunts and the pastures of the mares — so Hector, when he heard the god’s voice, moved his feet fast and called on his charioteers. And the Greeks — as when hunters and their hounds press a horned stag or a wild goat, but it has taken cover under a rock or in dark woods, for it was not their fate to catch it, and then their shouting raises a great bearded lion in their path, and at once they lose all heart for the chase — so the Greeks for a while kept pressing on in a body, thrusting with swords and double-edged spears; but when they saw Hector going up and down the ranks, their courage drained into their feet.

Then Thoas spoke out, son of Andraemon, the best of the Aetolians — a fine spear-thrower, steady in close fighting, and few of the Greeks could beat him in debate when the young men argued out a plan. With good will he spoke: “What is this I’m seeing? It can’t be — is that Hector, back on his feet and alive? Every one of us was sure Ajax, son of Telamon, had killed him. Some god has snatched him up and saved him again. He has killed many of us already, and he’ll kill more — the hand of Zeus is plainly behind him, or he would never stand so far out in front. Listen to me, all of you. Let’s order the main body of the army to fall back on the ships. But those of us who claim to be the best, let us stand our ground here, and try whether we can hold him off when he comes near, meeting him with leveled spears. I think that even he, for all his fury, will think twice before he plunges into the press of the Greeks.”

They heard him and did as he said. The men around Ajax and lord Idomeneus, with Teucer and Meriones and Meges, who matched Ares in war, called all their best fighters together and made a wall against Hector and the Trojans, while behind them the main body fell back on the ships.

The Trojans came on packed close together, Hector striding huge at their head; and before him went Phoebus Apollo, his shoulders wrapped in cloud, holding the terrible aegis, shaggy and bright at the fringe, which Hephaestus the smith had given to Zeus to carry terror into men. With this in his hands he led the Trojans on.

The Greeks held together and stood their ground. The war cry rose sharp from both sides, and arrows leapt from the bowstrings; and many a spear from a strong hand drove home in the bodies of fighting men, while many fell short in the ground between, hungry for flesh they never tasted. As long as Apollo held the aegis steady in his hands, the missiles of both sides struck home and men kept falling. But when he looked the Greeks full in the face and shook it, and let loose his own great war cry, their hearts melted in their chests and they forgot how to fight. As two wild beasts in the dead of night will stampede a herd of cattle or a great flock of sheep, falling on them suddenly while the herdsman is away — so the Greeks were thrown into helpless rout, for Apollo had loosed panic on them and given the glory to Hector and the Trojans.

Now the fighting broke up, and man killed man wherever he could. Hector killed Stichius and Arcesilaus — the one a leader of the Boeotians, the other the loyal friend of Menestheus. Aeneas killed Medon and Iasus: Medon was the bastard son of Oileus and Ajax’s brother, but he lived far from his own country at Phylace, having killed a man, a kinsman of his stepmother Eriopis, Oileus’ wife; and Iasus was a captain of the Athenians, son of Sphelus, the son of Boucolus. Polydamas cut down Mecisteus, and Polites killed Echius in the front rank, and Agenor killed Clonius. And Paris struck Deiochus from behind, low at the base of the shoulder, as he fled among the foremost, and drove the bronze clean through him.

While the Trojans were stripping the armor from these dead, the Greeks went stumbling back into the trench and the line of stakes, fleeing this way and that, and were forced back behind their wall. And Hector shouted to the Trojans in a great voice: “On to the ships — leave the bloody spoils. If I see any man hanging back from the ships, anywhere along this wall, I’ll have him killed on the spot. His own kin, men and women alike, will give him no funeral fire; the dogs will tear him in front of our city.”

With that he laid the lash across his horses’ shoulders and called to the Trojans up and down the ranks; and they all shouted together with a cry that split the air and drove their teams beside his own. And out in front Phoebus Apollo kicked down the banks of the deep trench with his feet, tumbling the earth into the middle to make a long broad causeway, as wide as the cast of a spear when a man throws to test his strength. Over it the Trojan companies poured in a flood, and Apollo led the way before them with the terrible aegis, and threw down the Greek wall as easily as a child at the seashore who builds his sand into toys and then, in play, kicks them flat again with his feet and hands. So you, Apollo, the far-shooter, scattered the long toil and labor of the Greeks, and loosed panic and confusion on them.

So the Greeks were penned in among their ships, calling out to one another, and lifting their hands to all the gods, each man praying aloud. Above them all old Nestor, the Greeks’ tower of strength, lifted his hands to the starry sky and prayed: “Father Zeus — if ever any man in wheat-rich Argos burned for you the fat thighs of a sheep or an ox and prayed to come safely home, and you bowed your head and promised it — remember it now, Olympian, and turn this pitiless day from us. Don’t let the Trojans break the Greeks like this.”

So he prayed, and Zeus of the counsels thundered loud, hearing the prayer of the old son of Neleus. But the Trojans, when they heard the thunder of Zeus, only flung themselves harder at the Greeks, mad for battle. As a great wave of the open sea breaks over a ship’s rail when the wind is high — for the wind is what builds the wave to its full height — so the Trojans came over the wall with a roar and drove their chariots in among the ships, and fought hand to hand with their double-edged spears: the Trojans from their chariots, the Greeks climbing high onto the black ships’ decks and fighting down with the long pikes that lay aboard, jointed and tipped with bronze, kept ready for fighting at sea.

Now Patroclus, all the while the Greeks and Trojans had been fighting around the wall, away from the swift ships, had stayed in the hut of good Eurypylus, keeping him company and laying soothing herbs on his wound to ease its pain. But when he saw the Trojans pouring over the wall and the Greeks crying out in panic and breaking, he groaned aloud and struck both his thighs with the flat of his hands, and said in his grief: “Eurypylus — I can’t stay with you any longer, badly as you need me. The fighting has come to its worst. Let one of your men tend you now; I have to run to Achilles and try to move him to fight. Who knows — with a god’s help I may yet sway his heart. A man does well to listen to a friend who loves him.”

And with that he was gone, running. The Greeks held firm against the Trojans’ attack, but though the Trojans were the fewer, the Greeks could not drive them back from the ships; and the Trojans could not break the Greek line and force their way in among the huts and the ships. As a chalk-line draws a ship’s timber straight and true in the hands of a skilled carpenter, a man Athena has taught the whole of his craft — so even and taut was the strain of the fighting between them, as they fought it out, this group around one ship and that around another.

But Hector went straight for glorious Ajax, and the two of them fought hard over the same ship. Hector could not force Ajax back and set fire to it, and Ajax could not drive Hector off, now that a god had brought him to that spot.

Then Ajax speared Caletor, son of Clytius, in the chest as he was bringing fire to the ship; he crashed down, and the torch fell from his hand. When Hector saw his own cousin fallen there before the ship, he shouted to the Trojans and Lycians: “Trojans, Lycians, Dardanians who fight at close quarters — don’t give an inch here in the thick of it. Save the son of Clytius before the Greeks can strip his armor where he’s fallen by the ships.”

And he cast his spear at Ajax. He missed him, but hit Lycophron, Ajax’s man, the son of Mastor from Cythera, who had come to live with Ajax after killing a man at home, and was honored in the household like one of his own parents. The bronze struck him on the head above the ear, and he toppled headlong from the ship’s stern to the ground, with the life gone out of him. Ajax shuddered, and called to his brother: “Teucer, our trusted friend is dead — the son of Mastor, whom we honored in our house like one of our own. Hector has just killed him. Where are your deadly arrows now, where is the bow Phoebus Apollo gave you?”

Teucer heard him and ran to his side with his bow and his quiver, and at once began raining arrows on the Trojans. He hit Cleitus, son of Pisenor, Polydamas’ comrade, as he held the reins in his hands — he was in the thick of the fighting, doing good service to Hector and the Trojans, but in an instant the evil came on him that none who wished it could turn aside, for the arrow struck him in the back of the neck. He fell from the chariot, and the horses swerved, rattling the empty chariot. Polydamas saw it at once and was first to the team; he gave it over to Astynous, son of Protiaon, ordering him to keep the horses close at hand, and went back into the front ranks.

Then Teucer aimed another arrow at Hector — and there would have been an end to the fighting at the ships, if he had hit him and brought him down. But Zeus, who was watching over Hector, did not miss it; he robbed Teucer of his triumph and snapped the well-twisted bowstring just as he drew it to take aim. The arrow flew wild, and the bow dropped from his hands. Teucer shuddered, and said to his brother: “This is no use — a god is cutting the ground from under everything we try. He has snapped my bowstring out of my hand, and broken the fresh-corded bow I strung only this morning to carry all the arrows I would loose.”

Great Ajax, son of Telamon, answered: “Then let your bow and your arrows lie, since a god, set against the Greeks, has made them useless. Take a long spear in your hands, sling a shield on your shoulder, and fight the Trojans yourself, and call on the others to do the same. Let them not take the ships without a hard fight, even if they break through. Let us think only of the work in front of us.”

Teucer set the bow down in his hut, slung a four-layered shield on his shoulders, set a well-made helmet on his strong head, the horsehair crest nodding above it, took up a stout spear shod with bronze, and went and stood at Ajax’s side.

When Hector saw that Teucer’s arrows were useless to him, he called out to the Trojans and Lycians: “Trojans, Lycians, Dardanians who fight at close quarters — be men, my friends; remember your courage here at the hollow ships. I have seen with my own eyes how Zeus has crippled the weapon of one of their best. It is easy to know when Zeus is helping men — and easy to know whom he leaves behind to ruin. Now he is bringing the Greeks low and standing with us. So close on the ships, all of you, and fight. And if any man among you meets his death by spear or arrow, let him die. There is no shame in dying for your country. His wife will be safe behind him, and his children, his house and his lands unharmed — if only the Greeks can be driven back to the land they came from, in their ships.”

So he put strength into them all. And Ajax on the other side called out to his men: “Shame on you, Greeks. Now it is win or die. Do you think, if Hector takes the ships, that you’ll go home on foot? Can’t you hear him cheering his whole army on to burn the fleet? He isn’t calling them to a dance — he’s calling them to a fight. There’s nothing for us now but to throw our hands and our whole strength into it. Better to settle it once, life or death, than to be ground down slowly, pinned against the ships by lesser men.”

So he too put strength into them all. Then Hector killed Schedius, son of Perimedes, a leader of the Phocians, and Ajax killed Laodamas, captain of foot soldiers, son of Antenor. Polydamas killed Otus of Cyllene, comrade of the son of Phyleus and a chief of the proud Epeians. Meges saw it and lunged at him, but Polydamas ducked aside — Apollo would not let the son of Panthous fall — and the spear caught Croesmus full in the chest, and Meges stripped his armor. As he did, Dolops sprang at him — Dolops son of Lampus, son of Laomedon, a hard fighter who knew every trick of war. He drove his spear into the center of Meges’ shield at close range, but the close-set plates of his breastplate saved him, the corslet Phyleus had brought from Ephyra, from the river Selleis, a guest-gift from King Euphetes to wear in battle; and now it saved his son’s life. Then Meges thrust at the crest of Dolops’ bronze helmet and sheared off the plume, newly dyed scarlet, and it fell in the dust. And while Dolops still fought on, sure of victory, Menelaus came up to help, and got beside him unseen, and speared him from behind in the shoulder; the point drove through into his chest, and he pitched forward on his face. The two ran in to strip his armor — but Hector called on all his brothers, and rounded most of all on Melanippus, son of Hicetaon, who once had grazed his cattle in Percote before the war, but when the Greek ships came had gone back to Troy and won a high name among the Trojans, living near Priam, who treated him like a son. Now Hector rebuked him: “Are we going to slacken now, Melanippus? Does it move you nothing that your kinsman is dead, and that they’re stripping Dolops’ armor? Come with me. There’s no more killing the Greeks from a distance — we close with them now, until either we cut them down or they storm the high towers of Troy and slaughter her people.”

He led on, and Melanippus followed. And Ajax, son of Telamon, was urging the Greeks: “Be men, my friends. Set honor in your hearts; let each fear the shame of looking weak to the rest. When men hold to each other’s good opinion, more come through alive than die. But in flight there is no safety and no glory.”

They needed no telling — they were already set on driving the Trojans back — and they took his words to heart, and fenced the ships about with a wall of bronze, while Zeus drove the Trojans on against them. Then Menelaus of the great war cry called to Antilochus: “Antilochus — there’s no Greek younger than you, none faster on his feet or braver. Spring out and bring down a Trojan.”

And he was gone again, having spurred Antilochus on. Out from the front rank he darted, glanced around, and threw; and the Trojans gave way as the spear flew, and it did not leave his hand for nothing, for it struck Melanippus, the proud son of Hicetaon, in the chest by the nipple as he came on, and he crashed down, and his armor rang about him. Antilochus sprang at him like a hound on a fawn that a hunter has struck as it broke from cover — so Antilochus leapt on you, Melanippus, to strip your armor. But noble Hector saw it and came running through the press, and Antilochus, brave as he was, would not stand to face him, but bolted like a wild thing that has done some harm — killed a dog, or a herdsman among his cattle — and runs before the men can gather against it. So the son of Nestor fled, and the Trojans and Hector after him, with a roar that split the air, showering their weapons. Only when he reached his own comrades did he turn and stand.

And the Trojans, fierce as lions, came storming on toward the ships, fulfilling the will of Zeus, who kept rousing them to new daring while he deadened the hearts of the Greeks and stole away their glory and gave it to the Trojans. For he meant to give the great glory to Hector, son of Priam — to let him hurl ravening fire on the curved ships, and so fulfill the cruel prayer of Thetis. Zeus, the lord of counsel, was only waiting to see with his own eyes the glare of a single burning ship; for from that hour he meant to turn the Trojans back from the ships and give the glory to the Greeks. With this in mind he drove Hector, mad for battle already, against the hollow ships. Hector raged like the war-god Ares with his spear, or like a fire that rages through the thick brush on the mountains; he foamed at the mouth, his eyes blazed under his terrible brows, and the helmet shook fearfully about his temples as he fought. For Zeus himself, out of heaven, was on his side, and gave glory and honor to him alone among so many men — because his life was to be short, and even now Pallas Athena was driving on the day of his death at the hands of the son of Peleus. He kept trying to break the Greek line wherever he saw it thickest and best-armed — but he could not break it, for all his fury; they held like a tower, square and solid, like a great sheer cliff above the gray sea that stands against the screaming wind and the waves that thunder up against it. So the Greeks stood firm against the Trojans and did not run. But Hector, all ablaze, hurled himself on them from every side, and fell upon them as a wave reared mountain-high by the wind and storm comes crashing down on a swift ship and buries it in foam, while the wind howls in the sail and the sailors’ hearts shake in their chests, barely saved from death by a hair — so the hearts of the Greeks were torn within them. And he came at them like a savage lion falling on a herd of cattle grazing by the thousand in the wet meadows of a wide shore, with a herdsman among them who does not yet know how to fight a beast for his cattle, and the lion springs into the midst of the herd and pulls one down, and the rest scatter in terror — so the Greeks were thrown into a wild, unearthly panic by Hector and father Zeus. Yet Hector killed only one of them, Periphetes of Mycenae, the son of Copreus — the man who used to carry King Eurystheus’ orders to mighty Heracles. From that far meaner father came a far better son, swift of foot and brave in battle, and among the wisest men in Mycenae. He it was who handed Hector this triumph: turning back, he tripped on the rim of his own shield, the great shield that reached to his feet and warded off the spears; he stumbled over it and fell on his back, and the helmet rang loud about his head as he went down. Hector saw it and ran in, and drove a spear into his chest, and killed him there among his own companions — who, for all their grief, could not help him, in their own terror of Hector.

Now they were back among the ships, with the sterns of the foremost line all around them, and the Trojans came pouring on. The Greeks were forced back from the first row of ships, but they made a stand by the huts, not scattered, holding together; shame and fear held them. And they kept calling out to one another, and most of all old Nestor, the Greeks’ tower of strength, begged each man by his own parents to stand firm: “Be men, my friends. Set the shame of others’ eyes in your hearts. Think, each of you, of your children, your wives, your goods, your parents, whether they are still living or dead. For their sake — though they are not here — I beg you: hold your ground. Do not turn and run.”

So he put strength into them all. And Athena lifted the thick cloud of darkness from their eyes, and the light poured down on them, on the side of the ships and on the side where the battle still raged; and they could see Hector of the great war cry and all his men — both those behind, out of the fighting, and those at the swift ships.

But Ajax could not bear to fall back with the rest. He went striding from deck to deck along the ships, swinging in his hands a great sea-pike, twenty-two cubits long, jointed together with rings. As a man who has mastered horses harnesses four of them together and comes galloping full speed from the country toward a great town along the public road, and the men and women crowd to watch and marvel as he leaps surely from one horse to the next, never missing his footing, while the team runs flat out — so Ajax went striding from one ship’s deck to another, and his voice went up into the sky as he shouted, over and over, to the Greeks to defend their ships and their huts. And Hector, too, would not stay back in the mass of the Trojans, but as a tawny eagle stoops on a flock of birds feeding by a river — geese, or cranes, or long-necked swans — so Hector hurled himself straight at a dark-prowed ship, charging full at it, for Zeus thrust him on from behind with his mighty hand, and roused the army to come after him.

And the battle blazed up again around the ships. You would have thought these were fresh, untired men, so fiercely did they fall on each other — and this was the mind of each side: the Greeks no longer believed they would escape ruin, but thought themselves doomed; while there was not a Trojan whose heart did not beat high with the hope of firing the ships and killing the Greek champions. With this in their hearts they faced each other.

Then Hector laid hold of the stern of a fine ship — the swift seagoing ship that had brought Protesilaus to Troy, and never carried him home again. Around that ship the Greeks and Trojans fought hand to hand, butchering each other. No more standing off with bows and javelins now: they stood close and cut at one another with one rage in them, with sharp axes and hatchets, with great swords and double-edged spears. Many a good blade, dark-hilted and heavy, fell to the ground, struck from a man’s hand or sheared from his shoulder as he fought, and the earth ran black with blood. And Hector, once he had hold of the stern, would not let go; he gripped the curved sternpost and shouted to the Trojans: “Bring fire — and all together, raise the war cry. Now Zeus has given us the day that pays for everything: to take the ships that came here against the will of heaven and brought us so much grief, through the cowardice of our elders, who held me back when I would have fought at the ships, and would not let the army follow. But if Zeus, the wide-seeing, blunted our wits then, now he himself is driving us on and urging us forward.”

At his words the Trojans flung themselves still harder on the Greeks. And Ajax could no longer hold his ground; he was beaten back by the storm of missiles, and gave himself up for dead. He left the high deck at the stern and stepped down onto the seven-foot rowing bench, and stood there, watching, holding off with his spear every Trojan who came to the ships with fire, and shouting all the while in a terrible voice to the Greeks: “Friends — Greek champions, fighters of Ares — be men, and remember your fighting strength. Do you think there are reinforcements somewhere behind us, or a stronger wall than this to keep off ruin? There is no walled city near where we might fall back and bring up fresh men. We are out on the plain of the Trojans and their bronze, with the sea at our backs, far from our own land. Our deliverance is in our hands and in hard fighting — not in slacking off.”

So he spoke, and drove on with his spear in a still greater fury; and whenever a Trojan came at the hollow ships with blazing fire, hoping to please Hector, Ajax was watching for him, and ran him through with the long spear. Twelve men he killed that way, hand to hand, in front of the ships.
