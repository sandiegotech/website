The Iliad, Book II

The Dream and the Ships

Book II2 of 24

Now the other gods and the men who fought from chariots slept the night through, but Zeus was not held by sleep. He lay turning it over in his mind: how to honor Achilles, and destroy great numbers of the Greeks at their ships. In the end one plan seemed best — to send a deadly Dream to Agamemnon, son of Atreus. He called the Dream to him and spoke winged words: “Go, deadly Dream, to the fast ships of the Greeks. Enter the hut of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, and tell him everything, exactly as I command. Tell him to arm the long-haired Greeks now, with all their numbers — for now he can take the wide streets of Troy. The immortals on Olympus are no longer divided; Hera has bent them all to her will with her pleading, and grief is fixed upon the Trojans.”

The Dream heard him and went. Quickly it came to the fast ships of the Greeks, and sought out Agamemnon, son of Atreus, and found him asleep in his hut, a sweet slumber poured around him. It stood over his head in the likeness of Nestor, son of Neleus — the counselor Agamemnon honored above all the elders — and in that shape the divine Dream spoke:

“You are sleeping, son of Atreus, breaker of horses. A man with the whole army on his shoulders, with so much to weigh and decide, should not sleep the night away. Listen to me now, and be quick: I come as a messenger from Zeus, who, far off as he is, cares for you and pities you. He commands you to arm the long-haired Greeks now, with all their numbers — for now you can take the wide streets of Troy. The immortals on Olympus are no longer divided; Hera has bent them all to her will with her pleading, and grief from Zeus is fixed upon the Trojans. Hold this fast in your mind. When sweet sleep lets go of you, don’t let it slip away.”

So it spoke, and was gone, and left him there turning over in his heart what would never come to be. He thought that on that very day he would take the city of Priam — the fool. He had no idea what Zeus was planning, who meant to lay still more pain and groaning on Trojans and Greeks alike, through hard battle after hard battle. He woke with the god’s voice still ringing around him, and sat upright. He pulled on his soft tunic, fine and new, and threw his great cloak over it; under his shining feet he bound the fine sandals, and slung the silver-studded sword across his shoulders. Then he took up the scepter of his fathers, the imperishable staff, and carried it out among the ships of the bronze-clad Greeks.

Dawn the goddess was climbing up the long slope of Olympus to announce the daylight to Zeus and the other immortals, when Agamemnon ordered the clear-voiced heralds to call the long-haired Greeks to assembly. They cried the summons, and the men came thronging fast. But first he sat the council of elders down beside the ship of Nestor, the king of Pylos, and there he laid before them a cunning plan.

“My friends,” he said, “a Dream came to me in the night, out of heaven, through the divine dark — and in face and build and bearing it was the very likeness of Nestor. It stood over my head and said: ‘You are sleeping, son of Atreus, breaker of horses. A man with the whole army on his shoulders should not sleep the night away. Listen to me: I come from Zeus, who cares for you and pities you, far off as he is. He commands you to arm the long-haired Greeks now, with all their numbers — for now you can take the wide streets of Troy. The immortals on Olympus are no longer divided; Hera has bent them all to her will, and grief from Zeus is fixed upon the Trojans. Hold this in your mind.’ With that it flew off, and sweet sleep released me. So now — let us see if we can get the sons of the Greeks under arms. But first I will test them with words, as is right. I will tell them to run for it, to put out to sea with the ships; and the rest of you, scattered through the army, are to hold them back, each man with his own.”

He sat down, and Nestor stood — the lord of sandy Pylos — and with good will toward them all he said: “Friends, leaders and counselors of the Greeks, if any other man among us had told this dream, we would call it a lie and want nothing to do with it. But the one who saw it claims to be the foremost man in the army. So let us see how we may arm the sons of the Greeks.” With that he led the way out of the council, and the scepter-bearing kings rose and followed the shepherd of the people.

The men were streaming in. As bees pour from a hollow rock in swarm after swarm without end, and cluster in bunches over the flowers of spring, some flying off this way, some that — so the countless tribes of men marched in from their ships and huts, company by company, across the wide beach to the assembly. And among them blazed Rumor, the messenger of Zeus, driving them on; and they gathered. The meeting ground was in an uproar, the earth groaning beneath them as the men took their places, and the din was tremendous. Nine heralds shouted them down, trying to check the noise and make them listen to the kings, the cherished of Zeus. With effort the men were seated and held to their places, and the clamor died.

Then lord Agamemnon stood, holding the scepter that Hephaestus had labored to make. Hephaestus gave it to lord Zeus, son of Cronus; Zeus gave it to the guide and slayer of Argus; lord Hermes gave it to Pelops, driver of horses; Pelops gave it to Atreus, shepherd of the people; Atreus dying left it to Thyestes, rich in flocks; and Thyestes left it to Agamemnon to carry, to be lord of many islands and of all Argos. Leaning on that scepter, he spoke to the Greeks:

“My friends — Greek warriors, men of Ares — Zeus, son of Cronus, has caught me in a cruel snare. The hard god: once he bowed his head and promised me I would sack the strong walls of Troy and sail home again. Now he has worked an ugly deception, and orders me back to Argos in disgrace, with much of my army dead. So it must please almighty Zeus, who has brought low the crowns of many cities, and will bring low still more — for his power is the greatest of all. It is a shameful thing, and men to come will hear of it: that an army of the Greeks, this great, this strong, fought a war for nothing, with no end to show for it, against fewer men than ourselves. For consider — if Greeks and Trojans both swore a truce and were counted, every Trojan who keeps a house in the city set on one side, and we Greeks ranged by tens on the other, and each of our tens chose a single Trojan to pour its wine: there would be many tens left without a man to serve them. That is how far we outnumber the men who live in Troy. But they have allies, spearmen drawn from many cities, and these throw me back and will not let me take the rich city of Ilios, for all my will. Nine of great Zeus’s years have come and gone; the ship timbers have rotted and the ropes worked loose. Our wives and small children sit at home and wait for us, while the work we came here to do stands undone. So let us do as I say: let us run with our ships for our own dear country — for now we will never take Troy, with its wide streets.”

So he spoke, and he stirred the hearts in the crowd, all of them who had not sat in on the secret council. The assembly heaved like the long swells of the Icarian Sea when the east wind and the south wind together rush down on them out of Zeus’s clouds; or as when the west wind comes down on a deep field of grain and the ears bow under the gust — so was the whole assembly shaken. With a great shout they broke for the ships, and the dust rose in clouds from under their feet and hung in the air. They called to one another to lay hold of the ships and drag them to the bright sea; they began to clear the launching channels and knock the props from under the hulls, and their cries to set sail for home went up to heaven.

And now the Greeks would have made it home, against all that fate decreed, if Hera had not spoken to Athena: “This is unbearable, daughter of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, untiring goddess. Are the Greeks to run home like this, over the sea’s broad back, to the land of their fathers — and leave Priam and the Trojans their boast, Argive Helen, for whose sake so many Greeks have died at Troy, far from home? Go now, through the army of the bronze-clad Greeks. Hold each man back with your own gentle words; don’t let them haul the curved ships down to the sea.”

Athena, gray-eyed and unwearied, did not refuse. Down she shot from the peaks of Olympus, and in an instant she stood among the fast ships of the Greeks. There she found Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel, standing still. He had not laid a hand on his black ship — grief had gone deep into his heart. Athena came close and said: “Odysseus, resourceful son of Laertes, descended from Zeus — is this how it ends? You all fling yourselves into the ships and flee for home? You would leave Priam and the Trojans their boast, Argive Helen, for whose sake so many Greeks have died at Troy, far from home? Go now, through the army of the Greeks. Don’t hold back. Stop each man with your own gentle words; don’t let them haul the curved ships down to the sea.”

Odysseus knew the voice of the goddess. He threw down his cloak — his herald, Eurybates of Ithaca, who attended him, caught it up — and went straight to Agamemnon, son of Atreus, and took from him the imperishable scepter of his fathers. With it in his hand he went out among the ships of the bronze-clad Greeks.

Whenever he met a king, or a man of rank, he came up beside him and held him back with gentle words: “You’re no coward — it isn’t like you to flinch like a frightened man. Sit down yourself, and make the rest of the army sit. You don’t yet know the full mind of Agamemnon. He is testing the sons of the Greeks, and soon he will come down hard on them. Not all of us heard what he said in the council. Take care he doesn’t turn his anger on us and do the army harm — the rage of a king, nursed by Zeus, runs high; his honor comes from Zeus, and Zeus the counselor holds him dear.”

But when he caught a common man shouting, he beat him with the scepter and rebuked him: “You — sit still, and listen to your betters. You’re nothing in a fight and nothing in council. We can’t all be kings here. Too many masters is no good thing. Let there be one ruler, one king, the man to whom the son of crooked-minded Cronus has given the scepter and the laws, to rule over the rest.”

So he ranged through the army, taking it in hand. And back they streamed from the ships and huts to the assembly with a roar, like a wave of the loud-thundering sea crashing on a long beach, while the open water booms.

The rest were settled now, and held their seats — all but one. Thersites kept on, a man with no measure to his tongue. His head was full of words, reckless and disorderly, for quarreling with kings — anything he thought would make the Greeks laugh. He was the ugliest man who came to Troy: bow-legged, lame in one foot, his two shoulders rounded and caved in over his chest, his head running up to a peak with a scant fuzz of hair on top. Achilles loathed him most, and Odysseus, for he was forever at the two of them. Now it was glorious Agamemnon he screeched his abuse at, while the Greeks raged and resented him bitterly. But Thersites only bawled the louder:

“Son of Atreus — what’s wrong now? What more do you want? Your huts are stuffed with bronze, and full of women too — the choice ones, that we Greeks hand you first whenever we sack a town. Is it still more gold you’re after, the kind some Trojan brings out of Troy to ransom his son, whom I, or another Greek, have dragged off in chains? Or some young woman to keep for your own bed, to lie with apart from the rest? It is not right for a leader to drag the sons of the Greeks into misery. Cowards! Disgraces! Women of Greece — you are no men — let us sail home with the ships and leave this man here at Troy to chew over his prizes, so he can see whether we ever helped him at all, or not. Look how he has insulted Achilles, a far better man than he is — robbed him of his prize and kept it, taken by his own hand. But there’s no rage in Achilles’ heart; he lets it go. Or this, son of Atreus, would be the last man you ever wronged.”

So Thersites railed at Agamemnon, shepherd of the people. But brilliant Odysseus was on him in an instant, scowling, and lashed him hard: “Thersites, reckless babbler — clear speaker that you think you are — hold your tongue, and don’t take it on yourself to quarrel with kings, you alone against them all. I tell you there is no baser man than you among the whole army that came to Troy with the sons of Atreus. So keep the kings out of your mouth — don’t hurl insults at them, and don’t sit watching for the road home. We don’t yet know how this will turn out, whether the sons of the Greeks go home in triumph or in ruin. And here you sit, abusing Agamemnon, shepherd of the people, because the warriors give him so many prizes — taunting him to his face. Now hear me, and it will be done: if I ever find you playing the fool like this again, then let my own head no longer sit on my shoulders, let me no longer be called the father of Telemachus, if I don’t lay hold of you, strip the clothes from your back — cloak and tunic and all that hides your shame — and whip you out of the assembly and back to the fast ships, howling, beaten with ugly blows.”

So he spoke, and brought the scepter down across his back and shoulders. Thersites doubled over, and a fat tear fell; a bloody welt rose up under the golden staff. He sat down terrified, hurting, helpless, and wiped the tears away, staring stupidly about him. And the rest, for all their gloom, broke into glad laughter at him. A man would glance at his neighbor and say: “There — Odysseus has done a thousand good things, leading us in counsel and setting us in line for war, but this is the best turn he has ever done the Greeks: he has shut the mouth of that foul-mouthed slanderer. The man’s own gall won’t send him railing at the kings again.”

So said the crowd. Then Odysseus, sacker of cities, rose with the scepter in his hand, and beside him gray-eyed Athena, in the likeness of a herald, called the army to silence, so the sons of the Greeks, near and far, might hear him and take in his counsel. With good will toward them all, he said:

“Son of Atreus — my king — the Greeks mean to make you a byword among all mortal men. They will not keep the promise they made you when they set out for here from horse-pasturing Argos: that you would not go home until you had sacked the strong walls of Troy. Now, like little children, like widowed women, they wail to one another and long for the way home. True, the work has been hard enough to break a man’s spirit. A man kept just one month from his wife frets in his benched ship when the winter gales and the rising sea pen him in — and for us this is the ninth year come round, the ninth we have waited here. So I do not blame the Greeks for chafing by the curved ships. And yet — it is a shameful thing to stay so long and go home empty. Bear up, my friends. Hold on a little longer, and let us learn whether Calchas prophesies true or false.

“For this we remember well — every one of us the dark fates have not carried off — as if it were yesterday or the day before: when the Greek ships were gathering at Aulis, bringing ruin to Priam and the Trojans, and we stood around a spring at the holy altars, making perfect sacrifice to the immortals, under a fine plane tree where the bright water ran. There a great sign appeared. A serpent, its back blood-red, terrible — Zeus himself had sent it up into the light — darted from beneath the altar and shot toward the plane tree. And there, on the topmost branch, cowering under the leaves, was a sparrow’s brood, tender chicks: eight of them, and the mother that bore them made nine. The serpent swallowed the little ones as they cried out pitifully, while the mother flew wailing around her dear young; then it coiled and caught her by the wing as she screamed above it. And when it had eaten the sparrow and her chicks, the god who had brought it into the light made it a clear sign: the son of crooked-minded Cronus turned it to stone, and we stood there in wonder at what had happened. So, when this fearful portent broke in upon the gods’ offerings, Calchas at once read out the will of heaven: ‘Why have you fallen silent, long-haired Greeks? Zeus the counselor has shown us this great sign — late in coming, late to be fulfilled, but its fame will never die. As the serpent ate the sparrow’s chicks and the sparrow — eight, and the mother that bore them made nine — so for so many years we will wage war here, and in the tenth we will take the wide-streeted city.’ That is what he said, and now it is all coming to pass. So stay, all of you, you bronze-greaved Greeks, here in your places, until we take the great city of Priam.”

So he spoke, and the Greeks roared their approval — and around them the ships rang fearfully with the shouting men — praising the words of godlike Odysseus. Then Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, spoke among them: “You amaze me. You stand here talking like little boys at play, who care nothing for the work of war. Where will our oaths go, and our pledges? Into the fire, then — the counsels and plans of men, the unmixed wine of our offerings, the clasped right hands we trusted in? We wear ourselves out wrangling with words, and for all our talk here we have found no remedy in all this time. Son of Atreus — hold to your unbending purpose, as you always have, and lead the Greeks through the hard fighting. And let these one or two, who plan apart from the rest of the Greeks, rot where they sit — they’ll get nowhere — scheming to go back to Argos before they so much as learn whether the promise of Zeus, who holds the storm-shield, was true or a lie. For I tell you, the almighty son of Cronus gave his pledge that very day, when we Greeks boarded our fast ships carrying death and doom to the Trojans: he flashed his lightning on the right and showed us signs of good. So let no man be in any haste to go home until he has lain beside the wife of some Trojan and avenged his struggle and his grief over Helen. But if any man is wild to go home, let him lay a hand on his black ship — and so meet his death and his doom before all the rest. My king, take counsel yourself, and listen to another’s too; the word I give you is not one to be thrown away. Sort the men by tribes and by clans, Agamemnon, so that clan stands by clan and tribe by tribe. Do this, and if the Greeks obey, you will know which of your leaders is a coward and which of your men, and which are brave — for they will be fighting each in his own company. And you will know, too, whether it is by the will of heaven that you fail to take the city, or by the cowardice of men and their folly in war.”

Lord Agamemnon answered him: “Once again, old man, you outdo the sons of the Greeks in council. Father Zeus, Athena, Apollo — if only I had ten such counselors among the Greeks! Then the city of lord Priam would soon bow beneath our hands, taken and sacked. But the son of Cronus, who holds the storm-shield, has loaded grief on me, and flung me into useless quarrels and strife. Achilles and I fell out over a girl, hurling words at each other — and I was the one who began it. If we two are ever of one mind again, then there will be no holding off the Trojans’ ruin, not for a moment. For now — go and eat, so that we may join battle. Each man sharpen his spear, set his shield in order, give the swift-footed horses their feed, and look his chariot over on every side, so we are ready for hateful war. We will fight the whole day through, with no break, none at all, until night comes to part the fury of men. The strap of the body-shield will run with sweat on every chest, the hand will go weary on the spear, the horse will steam dragging the polished chariot. And any man I see hanging back, choosing to skulk by the curved ships, will have no hope of escaping the dogs and the birds.”

So he spoke, and the Greeks gave a great shout, like a wave on a steep shore when the south wind drives it against a jutting headland — a rock the waves never leave, whatever quarter the winds blow from. They sprang up and scattered among the ships, lit fires by the huts, and took their meal. Each man sacrificed to one or another of the everlasting gods, praying to come through the battle alive, out of the grind of war. And Agamemnon, lord of men, slaughtered a fat five-year-old bull to the almighty son of Cronus, and called the elders, the best of the whole army: Nestor first, and lord Idomeneus, then the two named Ajax, and the son of Tydeus, and sixth Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel. Menelaus, good at the war cry, came of his own accord, for he knew in his heart how hard his brother was working. They stood around the bull and took up the barley grains, and Agamemnon, lord of men, prayed aloud: “Zeus — most glorious, greatest, god of the dark cloud, who lives in the high air — let the sun not go down, let the dark not come, until I have thrown down the smoke-blackened hall of Priam in ruin and burned its gates with hungry fire; until I have torn the tunic on Hector’s chest, ripped through with bronze; and until many of his companions around him have fallen face-down in the dust and bitten the earth.”

So he prayed, but the son of Cronus would not yet grant it. He took the sacrifice, and only piled on more unending toil. When they had finished the prayer and scattered the barley, they drew back the victim’s head, cut its throat, and flayed it; they cut out the thigh pieces, wrapped them in a double fold of fat, and laid raw meat over them. These they burned on split, leafless wood, and held the entrails over the flame on spits. When the thighs were burned and they had tasted the inner parts, they carved the rest into smaller pieces, ran them on the spits, roasted them through with care, and drew them all off. The work done, they laid out the feast, and no man’s hunger went without its equal share. When they had put away the desire for food and drink, Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, was first to speak: “Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men — let us not stand here talking any longer, nor keep putting off the work the god is laying in our hands. Send the heralds to call the bronze-clad Greeks to gather at the ships, and we will go down together through the broad army and rouse the keen edge of war.”

Agamemnon, lord of men, did not refuse. At once he had the clear-voiced heralds cry the long-haired Greeks to battle, and they cried it, and the men came thronging fast. The kings about the son of Atreus ranged through them, marshaling the ranks, and gray-eyed Athena went among them with the storm-shield — priceless, ageless, undying, with a hundred tassels of pure gold streaming from it, each one finely braided, each worth a hundred oxen. With it she swept through the army of the Greeks like flame, urging them on, and put strength in every man’s heart to fight without ceasing, without rest. And in that instant war grew sweeter to them than sailing home in the hollow ships to the land of their fathers.

As a ravaging fire sets a vast forest blazing on a mountain peak, and the glare is seen far off — so, as they marched, the dazzle of their bronze, wonderful and bright, went up through the sky to heaven. And as the many tribes of winged birds — wild geese, or cranes, or long-necked swans — in the Asian meadow, by the streams of the Caystrian river, wheel this way and that, glorying in the strength of their wings, and settle ever onward with a clamor that fills the meadow — so the many tribes poured from the ships and huts onto the plain of the Scamander, and the ground rang terribly under the tramp of men and horses. They stood in the flowering meadow by the Scamander, countless as the leaves and flowers that come in their season. As the thick swarms of flies that crowd a herdsman’s farmyard in the spring, when the milk drenches the pails — so many were the long-haired Greeks who stood on the plain facing the Trojans, burning to tear them apart.

And as goatherds easily sort their wide flocks of goats when they have mingled at pasture, so the leaders set the men in order this way and that, marshaling them for battle — and among them moved lord Agamemnon, his head and eyes like Zeus who delights in thunder, his waist like Ares, his chest like Poseidon. As a bull in the herd stands far above the rest, towering over the gathered cattle, so Zeus made the son of Atreus that day, set apart, foremost among many, first among the heroes.

Tell me now, Muses who hold the halls of Olympus — for you are goddesses, you are everywhere, and you know all things, while we hear only the rumor and know nothing — who were the leaders and lords of the Greeks? The mass of them I could never name, never tell over, not if I had ten tongues and ten mouths, a voice that would not break, and a heart of bronze inside me, unless you, Muses of Olympus, daughters of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, called to mind all those who came to Troy. So I will tell the captains of the ships, and the ships in all their number.

The Boeotians were led by Peneleos, Leitus, Arcesilaus, Prothoenor, and Clonius — the men who lived in Hyria and rocky Aulis, who held Schoenus, Scolus, and the hill country of Eteonus, with Thespeia, Graia, and the broad dancing-grounds of Mycalessus; the men of Harma, Eilesium, and Erythrae; of Eleon, Hyle, and Peteon; of Ocalea and the strong-built fortress of Medeon; of Copae, Eutresis, and Thisbe of the many doves; of Coronea and grassy Haliartus; of Plataea and Glisas; of the well-built fortress of Lower Thebes; of holy Onchestus, the bright grove of Poseidon; of Arne rich in vineyards, and Midea, and sacred Nisa, and Anthedon on the coast. From these came fifty ships, and a hundred and twenty young Boeotians sailed in each.

The men of Aspledon and Orchomenus of the Minyans were led by Ascalaphus and Ialmenus, sons of Ares — whom Astyoche bore in the house of Actor, son of Azeus, a modest maiden who climbed to an upper room, where Ares lay with her in secret. With these came thirty hollow ships.

The Phocians were led by Schedius and Epistrophus, sons of great-hearted Iphitus, son of Naubolus — the men who held Cyparissus and rocky Pytho, holy Crisa, Daulis, and Panopeus; who lived around Anemorea and Hyampolis, along the divine river Cephissus, and in Lilaea by the Cephissus springs. With them came forty black ships. The Phocians’ leaders set their ranks in order beside the Boeotians, on the left.

The Locrians were led by Ajax, the swift son of Oileus — the lesser Ajax, not nearly so great as Telamonian Ajax, but far smaller, with a corselet of linen, though with the spear he outdid all the Greeks. These were the men of Cynus, Opous, and Calliarus, of Bessa, Scarphe, and lovely Augeae, of Tarphe and Thronium by the streams of the Boagrius. With Ajax came forty black ships of the Locrians, who live across from holy Euboea.

From Euboea came the fierce, fast-breathing Abantes, who held Chalcis and Eretria, Histiaea rich in vines, Cerinthus by the sea, and the steep fortress of Dium, with the men of Carystus and Styra. Their leader was Elephenor, of the line of Ares, son of Chalcodon, lord of the great-hearted Abantes — fast on their feet, their hair grown long behind, fighters keen to lunge with their ashen spears and tear the corselets from their enemies’ chests. With him came forty black ships.

And the men who held Athens, the strong-built city — the land of great-hearted Erechtheus, whom Athena, daughter of Zeus, raised after the grain-giving earth had borne him, and set in her own rich temple at Athens, where year by year the young men of Athens win his favor with bulls and rams — these were led by Menestheus, son of Peteos. No man on earth could match him at marshaling chariots and shield-bearing men; only Nestor rivaled him, and he was the elder. With Menestheus came fifty black ships. From Salamis Ajax brought twelve ships, and drew them up where the lines of the Athenians stood.

The men of Argos and walled Tiryns, of Hermione and Asine that ring the deep gulf, of Troezen, Eionae, and the vineyards of Epidaurus, with the young Greeks of Aegina and Mases — these were led by Diomedes, good at the war cry, and Sthenelus, dear son of glorious Capaneus. With them, as a third leader, came Euryalus, godlike son of lord Mecisteus, son of Talaus; but over them all the great war cry of Diomedes was chief. With these came eighty black ships.

The men who held the well-built fortress of Mycenae, rich Corinth and well-built Cleonae, who lived in Orneae and lovely Araethyrea and Sicyon, where Adrastus reigned of old; the men of Hyperesia and steep Gonoessa and Pellene, of Aegium and all the coast of Helice, and around broad Helice — these sent a hundred ships under lord Agamemnon, son of Atreus. His was the largest force by far, and the finest; and among them he himself put on his gleaming bronze, glorying, the first of all the heroes, for he was the greatest, and led the most men.

The men of the valley of Lacedaemon, hollowed among the hills, of Pharis, Sparta, and Messe of the many doves; of Bryseae and lovely Augeae; who held Amyclae and Helos, the town by the sea; who lived in Laas and around Oetylus — these his brother led, Menelaus, good at the war cry, with sixty ships, mustered apart from the rest. He moved among them himself, trusting his own keen heart, urging them to battle, for above all men he longed to avenge his struggle and his grief over Helen.

The men of Pylos and lovely Arene, of Thryum at the Alpheus ford, and well-built Aipy; the men of Cyparisseis and Amphigenea, of Pteleus and Helos, and Dorium — where the Muses met Thracian Thamyris and put an end to his song. He was coming from Oechalia, from the house of Eurytus of Oechalia, and he boasted that he would win even if the Muses themselves, daughters of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, sang against him. In their anger they crippled him: they took away his divine power of song and made him forget how to play the lyre. These were led by Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, and with him came ninety hollow ships.

The men of Arcadia, under the steep mountain of Cyllene, by the tomb of Aepytus, where men fight hand to hand — the men of Pheneus and Orchomenus rich in flocks, of Rhipe and Stratie and windy Enispe; who held Tegea and lovely Mantinea, who held Stymphelus and lived in Parrhasia — these were led by lord Agapenor, son of Ancaeus, with sixty ships; and many Arcadians, skilled in war, sailed in each. Agamemnon, lord of men, had given them the well-benched ships himself, to cross the wine-dark sea, for the sea and its work were not their own.

The men of Buprasium and bright Elis — all the land that Hyrmine and Myrsinus on the border, the rock of Olene and Alesium hold within them — had four leaders, and ten fast ships followed each, with many Epeans aboard. Two were led by Amphimachus and Thalpius, of the line of Actor, sons one of Cteatus and one of Eurytus; one company by strong Diores, son of Amarynceus; and the fourth by godlike Polyxenus, son of lord Agasthenes, son of Augeas.

The men of Dulichium and the holy Echinean islands, that lie across the sea off Elis, were led by Meges, the equal of Ares — son of the horseman Phyleus, dear to Zeus, who once in anger at his father went off to settle in Dulichium. With him came forty black ships.

Odysseus led the great-hearted Cephallenians, who held Ithaca and Neritum with its trembling leaves, who lived in Crocyleia and rugged Aegilips; who held Zacynthus and Samos, and the mainland too, and the shore that faces the islands. These were led by Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel, and with him came twelve ships, their prows painted red.

The Aetolians were led by Thoas, son of Andraemon — the men of Pleuron and Olenus and Pylene, of Chalcis by the sea and rocky Calydon; for great-hearted Oeneus and his sons were no more, and golden-haired Meleager too was dead, and so the rule of the Aetolians had fallen to Thoas. With him came forty black ships.

The Cretans were led by Idomeneus, famous with the spear — the men of Cnossus and walled Gortys, of Lyctus, Miletus, and chalk-white Lycastus, of Phaestus and Rhytium, fine cities both, and all the rest who lived in Crete of the hundred cities. These were led by Idomeneus, famous with the spear, and by Meriones, the equal of murderous Ares. With them came eighty black ships.

Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, a tall, brave man, brought nine ships of high-hearted Rhodians from Rhodes — those who lived in Rhodes in their three divisions, Lindus and Ielysus and chalk-white Cameirus. These Tlepolemus led, famous with the spear, whom Astyochea bore to the strength of Heracles; he carried her off from Ephyra, by the river Selleis, after he had sacked many cities of warriors in their prime. When Tlepolemus came of age in the well-built halls, he killed his father’s aged uncle, Licymnius, of the line of Ares; and at once he built ships, gathered a great following, and fled across the sea, hunted by the rest of the sons and grandsons of mighty Heracles. After much suffering he came to Rhodes, and settled there in three tribes, and was loved by Zeus, who is lord over gods and men; and the son of Cronus poured great wealth down on them.

Nireus brought three trim ships from Syme — Nireus, son of Aglaia and lord Charopus; Nireus, the handsomest man who came to Troy of all the Greeks, after the matchless son of Peleus — but he was a slight man, and few followed him.

The men of Nisyrus and Crapathus and Casus, of Cos, the city of Eurypylus, and the Calydnian islands, were led by Pheidippus and Antiphus, the two sons of lord Thessalus, son of Heracles. With them was mustered a fleet of thirty hollow ships.

And now the men of Pelasgian Argos — those who lived in Alos and Alope and Trachis, who held Phthia and Hellas of the lovely women, called the Myrmidons, the Hellenes, and the Achaeans. Their fifty ships were Achilles’ command. But they had no thought of the grim clash of battle now, for there was no one to lead them into the lines. Brilliant, swift-footed Achilles lay among the ships, raging over the girl, lovely-haired Briseis, whom he had won with great toil out of Lyrnessus, when he sacked Lyrnessus and the walls of Thebe and brought down the spearmen Mynes and Epistrophus, sons of lord Evenor, son of Selepus. Grieving for her, he lay there still — but soon enough he would rise again.

The men of Phylace and flowering Pyrasus, the sanctuary of Demeter; of Iton, mother of sheep; of Antrum by the sea, and Pteleus deep in grass — these were led, while he lived, by warlike Protesilaus; but already the black earth held him down. He had left behind in Phylace a wife with both cheeks torn in grief, and a house half built, for a Trojan struck him down as he leapt from his ship, first of all the Greeks to touch the soil of Troy. They were not without a leader, though they longed for the one they had lost: Podarces, of the line of Ares, marshaled them — son of Iphiclus, rich in flocks, son of Phylacus, own brother to great-hearted Protesilaus, but younger; the warrior Protesilaus was both the elder and the braver. The men were not leaderless, but they missed him for the brave man he had been. With Podarces came forty black ships.

The men of Pherae by the Boebean lake, of Boebe and Glaphyrae and well-built Iolcus, came in eleven ships, led by the dear son of Admetus, Eumelus, whom Alcestis bore to him — loveliest of the daughters of Pelias.

The men of Methone and Thaumacia, of Meliboea and rugged Olizon, were led by Philoctetes, the skilled archer, in seven ships; fifty oarsmen rowed in each, every one of them skilled with the bow, hard fighters. But Philoctetes lay on an island, suffering terribly — on holy Lemnos, where the sons of the Greeks had left him in agony, bitten by a deadly water snake. There he lay grieving; but soon the Greeks beside their ships would remember lord Philoctetes. They were not without a leader, though they longed for him: Medon set them in their ranks, the bastard son of Oileus, whom Rhene bore to the sacker of cities.

The men of Tricca and rocky Ithome and Oechalia, the city of Oechalian Eurytus, were led by the two sons of Asclepius, healers both and skilled, Podalirius and Machaon. With them came thirty hollow ships.

The men of Ormenius and the spring of Hypereia, who held Asterium and the white crests of Titanus, were led by Eurypylus, the splendid son of Euaemon. With him came forty black ships.

The men of Argissa and Gyrtone, of Orthe, Elone, and the white city of Oloosson, were led by Polypoetes, steady in battle — son of Pirithous, whom undying Zeus fathered; for famous Hippodameia bore him to Pirithous on the day he took his revenge on the shaggy mountain-beasts and drove them from Pelion to the Aethices. He did not lead alone: with him was Leonteus, of the line of Ares, son of proud Coronus, son of Caeneus. With them came forty black ships.

Guneus brought twenty-two ships from Cyphus, leading the Enienes and the steadfast Peraebi, who had made their homes around wintry Dodona, and worked the fields along the lovely Titaresius, that pours its bright water into the Peneus — yet does not mingle with the silver eddies of the Peneus, but runs across the top of it like oil; for it is a branch of the Styx, the dread water of the oath.

The Magnetes were led by Prothous, son of Tenthredon, who lived around the Peneus and Pelion of the trembling leaves. Swift Prothous led them, and with him came forty black ships.

These were the leaders and lords of the Greeks. And who, then, was the best of them all, Muse — of the men and of the horses that followed the sons of Atreus? The best horses by far were the mares of the son of Pheres, driven by Eumelus, fast as birds, matched in coat and age, their backs level to a measure; Apollo of the silver bow had reared them in Perea, two mares, carrying the terror of Ares into battle. Of the men the greatest by far was Telamonian Ajax — while Achilles raged; for Achilles was far the stronger, and his horses too, the horses that carried the matchless son of Peleus. But he lay among his curved seafaring ships, nursing his rage at Agamemnon, son of Atreus, shepherd of the people. His men along the surf passed the time hurling the discus and the javelin and shooting the bow; the horses stood idle, each by its own chariot, cropping clover and the marsh parsley; the chariots stood wrapped and waiting in the huts, and their lords, longing for their warlike leader, wandered up and down the camp and would not fight.

So the whole army marched, as if the entire plain were fire; and the earth groaned beneath them as it groans under the thunder of Zeus in his anger, when he lashes the ground about Typhoeus, in the land of the Arimi, where they say Typhoeus lies. So the earth groaned, deep and loud, beneath their feet, as they crossed the plain at speed.

To the Trojans came Iris, fast as the wind, with her grim message from Zeus who holds the storm-shield. They were gathered in assembly at Priam’s gates, all of them, young and old. Swift Iris came and stood beside them, taking the voice of Polites, son of Priam, who used to sit as a Trojan lookout, trusting in his speed, on the high tomb of old Aesyetes, watching for the Greeks to break out from their ships. In his likeness Iris spoke: “Old man, you love your endless speeches as in the days of peace — but unrelenting war is upon us. I have been in many a battle of men, but I have never seen an army like this, so vast: as many as the leaves, or the sands, they sweep across the plain to fight at the city. Hector — above all I charge you, do this: there are many allies through the great city of Priam, men of every scattered land, every one with his own tongue. Let each leader give the word to his own people, and marshal his own, and lead the citizens out.”

Hector knew the goddess’s voice and broke up the assembly at once. They rushed to arms; all the gates were flung open, and the army poured out, foot and horse, with a great roar rising. Now there is a steep mound that stands by itself out on the plain, with open ground on every side. Men call it Batieia, but the immortals call it the tomb of dancing Myrine. There the Trojans and their allies drew up their lines.

Great Hector of the gleaming helmet, son of Priam, led the Trojans. With him were armed by far the most men, and the best, straining toward the fight with their spears.

The Dardanians were led by the brave son of Anchises, Aeneas, whom shining Aphrodite bore to Anchises in the foothills of Ida — a goddess lying with a mortal man. He did not lead alone, but with the two sons of Antenor, Archelochus and Acamas, skilled in every kind of fighting.

The men who lived in Zeleia, under the lowest foot of Ida, men of wealth, who drank the dark water of the Aesepus — Trojans — were led by Pandarus, the splendid son of Lycaon, to whom Apollo himself had given the bow.

The men of Adresteia and the land of Apaesus, who held Pityeia and the steep hill of Tereia, were led by Adrestus and Amphius in his linen corselet, the two sons of Merops of Percote, who beyond all men knew the art of prophecy. He had refused to let his sons go to the war that kills men, but they would not listen, for the dark spirits of death were drawing them on.

The men of Percote and Practius, who held Sestos and Abydos and bright Arisbe, were led by Asius, son of Hyrtacus, a captain of men — Asius, son of Hyrtacus, whom his great fiery sorrel horses had carried from Arisbe, from the river Selleis.

Hippothous led the tribes of the spear-fighting Pelasgians, who lived in deep-soiled Larissa — Hippothous and Pylaeus, of the line of Ares, the two sons of Pelasgian Lethus, son of Teutamus.

Acamas and the warrior Peirous led the Thracians, all that the strong-flowing Hellespont closes within. Euphemus led the spear-armed Cicones, son of Troezenus, son of Ceas, cherished of Zeus. Pyraechmes led the Paeonians with their curved bows, from far-off Amydon, from the broad waters of the Axius — the Axius, whose stream spreads the loveliest water over the earth.

The Paphlagonians were led by the strong heart of Pylaemenes, from the land of the Eneti, where the wild mules are bred — the men who held Cytorus and lived around Sesamus, in their famous homes by the river Parthenius, in Cromna and Aegialus and lofty Erithini. The Halizones were led by Odius and Epistrophus, from far-off Alybe, the birthplace of silver. The Mysians were led by Chromis and Ennomus, reader of birds — but his bird-lore did not ward off the black death; he went down at the hands of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus, in the river, where Achilles cut down other Trojans too.

Phorcys led the Phrygians, with godlike Ascanius, from far Ascania, both of them straining for the fight. The Maeonians were led by Mesthles and Antiphus, the two sons of Talaemenes, born to him by the Gygaean lake — they who led the Maeonians, born under Mount Tmolus. Nastes led the Carians, men of a strange tongue, who held Miletus and the leafy mountain of Phthires, the streams of the Maeander, and the steep crests of Mycale. Amphimachus and Nastes led them — Nastes and Amphimachus, the splendid children of Nomion. Amphimachus came to the war decked in gold, like a girl — the fool; the gold did nothing to keep off his bitter end. He went down at the hands of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus, in the river, and the great fighter Achilles carried off his gold. Sarpedon and faultless Glaucus led the Lycians, from far-off Lycia, from the eddying Xanthus.
