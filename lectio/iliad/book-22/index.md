# The Iliad, Book XXII

The Death of Hector

So the Trojans, who had run for the city like terrified fawns, leaned against the fine battlements and cooled the sweat from their bodies and drank to quench their thirst. The Greeks came on toward the wall, their shields slung over their shoulders. But Hector stayed where he was, held fast by deadly fate, out in front of Ilium and the Scaean gates. Then Phoebus Apollo spoke to the son of Peleus: “Why do you keep chasing me, son of Peleus, you who are only a man, when I cannot die? You still haven’t grasped that I am a god — that’s how furious your pursuit is. So much for the Trojans you routed; they’re crowded safe inside their walls now, while you’ve been lured out here. You’ll never kill me. There’s no death in me to take.”

Deeply angered, swift-footed Achilles said: “You’ve cheated me, Far-Shooter, cruelest of all the gods — turning me away from the wall when many another man would still have bitten the earth before he reached Ilium. You’ve robbed me of great glory and saved the Trojans easily, since you had nothing to fear in return. I’d pay you back for this, if I had the power.”

With that he set off for the city, full of fury, running like a prize-winning chariot horse that stretches at full stride across the plain — so fast and light did Achilles drive his legs and feet. Old Priam was the first to see him, blazing across the flat ground like the star that comes at harvest-time, the one men call Orion’s Dog: brightest of all the stars, and yet a sign of evil, for it brings raging fever to wretched mortals. Just so the bronze flamed on Achilles’ chest as he ran. The old man groaned and beat his head with his hands and lifted them high, and cried aloud to his beloved son, who stood determined before the gates, burning to fight Achilles. Stretching out his arms, the old man begged him: “Hector — my dear child — don’t stand out there alone, with no one beside you, to meet that man, or you’ll soon meet your death at the hands of the son of Peleus. He is far stronger than you. The monster — I wish the gods loved him only as much as I do; then dogs and vultures would make short work of him where he lay, and a terrible grief would lift from my heart. He has stripped me of so many fine sons, killing them or selling them off to distant islands. Even now I cannot find two of them among the Trojans crowded into the city — Lycaon and Polydorus, the sons Laothoe bore me, that queen among women. If they’re alive and held in the Greek camp, we’ll ransom them with gold and bronze; we have plenty, for old Altes gave his daughter a rich dowry. But if they’re already dead and in the house of Hades, then their mother and I, who gave them life, will grieve — though the rest of the people will grieve less, and not for long, unless you die too at Achilles’ hands. So come inside the wall, my child. Be the guardian of the men and women of Troy. Don’t hand a great triumph to the son of Peleus and lose your own dear life. And pity me, your poor father, while I can still feel — your father, whom the son of Cronus will destroy by a hard fate on the threshold of old age, after I have seen it all: my sons cut down, my daughters dragged off, the bridal chambers ransacked, the little children dashed to the ground in the horror of battle, the wives of my sons hauled away by the brutal hands of the Greeks. And at the last, the dogs will tear me to pieces at my own gates, when someone’s sword or spear has beaten the life out of me — the very dogs I reared at my table to guard my doors, who’ll lap my blood and then lie there in the gateway, out of their minds. When a young man falls in battle, cut down by the bronze, let him lie; everything about him is honorable in death, whatever shows. But when the dogs defile the gray head and gray beard and the nakedness of an old man killed — there is nothing more pitiable in all this wretched world.”

So spoke the old man, and he tore at his gray hair and ripped it from his head — but he could not move Hector’s heart. And nearby his mother wept and wailed, loosening the folds of her gown and baring her breast with the other hand, and through her tears she cried out to him: “Hector, my child — have some respect for this, and pity me. If I ever gave you my breast to quiet you, remember it now, dear son, and come inside the wall to drive off this man. Don’t stand out there to face him. He is without mercy. If he kills you, then I will never weep for you on your bier, my own dear branch, nor will your richly dowered wife; far from us both, the dogs will devour you beside the Greek ships.”

So the two of them pleaded with their son, weeping, calling out again and again — but they could not move Hector’s heart, and he held his ground as huge Achilles came on toward him. As a snake in the mountains waits by its hole for a man, gorged on poisonous herbs, filled with cold fury, glaring terribly as it coils about its den — so Hector, his courage unquenched, would not give way, and leaned his bright shield against the jutting tower and stood his ground. And, troubled, he spoke to his own great heart: “This is bad. If I go inside the gates, Polydamas will be the first to throw it in my face — he who told me to lead the Trojans back into the city that ruinous night, when great Achilles roused himself again. I wouldn’t listen. It would have been far better if I had. Now that my own recklessness has destroyed the army, I am ashamed to face the men and women of Troy, ashamed that some lesser man might say, ‘Hector trusted his own strength too far and brought us to ruin.’ That’s what they’ll say. Better, then, to stand and fight Achilles — to come back having killed him, or die with honor here before the city. But what if I set down my bossed shield and my heavy helmet, lean my spear against the wall, and go out myself to meet the noble son of Peleus? What if I promised to give Helen back, and all the treasure with her — everything Alexandros carried off to Troy in his hollow ships, the very thing that started this war — to hand it all over for the Greeks to take, and beyond that to divide with them, fairly, everything else the city holds; to bind the Trojan elders by oath to hide nothing, but to split in two all the wealth this lovely town contains? But why does my heart even argue this with me? I can’t go up to him and expect any mercy, any respect; he’d cut me down on the spot, defenseless, stripped of my armor, as easily as a woman. There’s no whispering with him now, soft and intimate, from oak tree or rock, the way a young man and a girl whisper to each other, a boy and a girl. Better to clash at once, and see which of us the Olympian hands the glory.”

So he stood and waited, turning it over — and Achilles came on, terrible as Ares, the war-god with the flashing helmet. Over his right shoulder he balanced the dreadful Pelian ash spear, and the bronze flared around him like a blaze of fire, or the light of the rising sun. And as Hector saw him, terror took hold of him, and he could not bear to stay; he left the gates behind and ran, and the son of Peleus rushed after him, sure of his speed. As a hawk in the mountains, fastest of all the birds, swoops light after a trembling dove — the dove darts away beneath him, but the hawk keeps screaming close behind, again and again, his whole heart set on the kill — so Achilles flew straight at Hector in his fury, and Hector ran in terror beneath the wall of Troy, his knees pumping hard.

Past the lookout point and the windswept wild fig tree they raced, on along the wagon-road that ran under the wall, and came to the two clear springs that feed the eddying Scamander. From one of them hot water flows, and steam rises off it like smoke from a fire; the other runs cold even in summer, cold as hail, or snow, or ice frozen out of water. And right there beside the springs are the broad stone troughs, beautiful, where the wives and lovely daughters of the Trojans used to wash their shining clothes in the days of peace, before the Greeks ever came. Past these they ran, one fleeing and one giving chase — a good man in front, but a far better one hunting him down — and they ran hard, for the prize they raced for was no sacrificial beast, no oxhide such as men win in a footrace: they were running for the life of Hector, breaker of horses. As prize-winning horses round the turning-post at full gallop, when some great prize is set out — a tripod, or a woman — at the funeral games of a man who has died, so the two of them circled the city of Priam three times, on flying feet, while all the gods looked on. And the father of gods and men was the first to speak: “This is a hard thing to see — a man I love being chased around the wall, and my heart grieves for Hector, who has burned the thighs of so many oxen for me, now on the ridges of Ida with its many valleys, now on the heights of the city. And there is brilliant Achilles, running him down around Priam’s town. Consider it, gods, and decide: shall we save him from death, or shall we let Achilles, for all Hector’s courage, bring him down at last?”

Then the bright-eyed goddess Athena answered him: “Father — lord of the white lightning, lord of the dark cloud — what are you saying? A man who is mortal, long ago marked for death — would you set him free again from death’s hard grip? Do it, then. But the rest of us gods will not all praise you for it.”

Zeus who gathers the clouds answered her: “Take heart, Tritogeneia, dear child. I don’t mean it with my whole will, and I want to be kind to you. Do as your own mind moves you. Don’t hold back.” So he urged on Athena, who was already eager, and she came swooping down from the peaks of Olympus.

And still swift Achilles drove Hector on, hard and relentless. As a hound in the mountains starts a fawn from its lair and hunts it through the glens and hollows, and even when the fawn ducks shivering under a thicket, the hound keeps tracking it, running it down — so Hector could not lose the fast-footed son of Peleus. Every time he made a dash for the Dardanian gates, to get under the well-built towers where his own men might cover him with their spears from above, Achilles would cut across and head him back toward the open plain, keeping always to the city side himself. As in a dream a man cannot catch the one who flees from him — the one cannot escape, and the other cannot catch him — so Achilles could not run Hector down, nor could Hector pull away. And how could Hector have outrun his death so long, if Apollo had not come close to him, one last time, and roused his strength and his swift knees? And brilliant Achilles signaled to his men with his head: not one of them was to throw at Hector, in case another struck the blow and won the glory, and he came in second. But when they reached the springs the fourth time, then the Father lifted his golden scales and set in them two fates of death that lays men low — one for Achilles, one for Hector, breaker of horses — and he took the balance by the middle and lifted it, and Hector’s day of doom sank down, down toward the house of Hades. And Phoebus Apollo left him.

Then the bright-eyed goddess Athena came up to the son of Peleus and stood beside him, and spoke winged words: “Now, brilliant Achilles, dear to Zeus — now I think the two of us will carry off great glory for the Greeks beside the ships, and bring down Hector, insatiable for battle as he is. There’s no escaping us now, whatever Apollo does, however he grovels before Father Zeus who wields the storm-shield. Stop here and get your breath. I’ll go to Hector and talk him into standing to fight you, face to face.”

So Athena spoke, and Achilles obeyed her gladly and stopped, leaning on his bronze-tipped ash spear. And she left him and went to noble Hector, taking on the shape of Deiphobus and his strong voice. She came close and spoke winged words: “Dear brother — Achilles is wearing you down, chasing you on swift feet around the city of Priam. Come, let’s make a stand here and beat him back.”

And great Hector of the flashing helmet answered her: “Deiphobus — you were always the dearest to me of all my brothers, the sons of Hecuba and Priam. And now I’ll honor you in my heart still more, since for my sake you’ve dared to come outside the wall, seeing me out here, while all the rest stay in.”

Then the bright-eyed goddess Athena answered: “Brother — our father and our honored mother begged me on their knees, and my comrades all around me, to stay inside; such terror has taken hold of every one of them. But my heart was breaking with grief for you. Now let us go at him hard, and fight, and spare nothing of our spears, and find out whether Achilles kills the two of us and carries our bloody armor to the hollow ships — or whether he goes down beneath your spear.”

So Athena lured him on by her cunning. And when the two had come close, facing each other, great Hector of the flashing helmet spoke first: “I’ll run from you no longer, son of Peleus, as I have these three times around the great city of Priam, never daring to wait for your charge. Now my heart drives me to stand and face you — to kill you, or be killed. But come, let us call the gods to witness; they are the best guardians of an oath between us. If Zeus grants me the endurance to take your life, I will do nothing shameful to your body: I will strip your famous armor, Achilles, and give the rest of you back to the Greeks. Swear you’ll do the same for me.”

Glaring at him, swift-footed Achilles answered: “Hector — don’t talk to me of agreements, curse you. There are no binding oaths between lions and men, and wolves and lambs are never of one mind; they hate each other through and through, always. So there can be no love between us, no oaths, until one of us falls and gluts Ares, the war-god with the bull’s-hide shield, with his blood. Call up every bit of fighting you have in you. Now you must prove yourself a spearman and a soldier with nerve. There’s no more getting away from me. Pallas Athena will bring you down beneath my spear this moment, and you’ll pay all at once for the grief of my companions, the men you killed in your raging.”

With that he balanced his long-shadowed spear and let it fly — but glorious Hector saw it coming and dropped beneath it, watching, so the bronze flew over his head and stuck in the ground. But Athena snatched it up and gave it back to Achilles, and Hector, the people’s shepherd, did not see. And Hector said to the matchless son of Peleus: “You missed, godlike Achilles. So you did not know my fate from Zeus after all — though you spoke as if you did. You silver-tongued trickster — you thought you’d shake my nerve with words and make me forget my courage and my strength. Well, you won’t drive your spear into the back of a running man. Send it through my chest as I charge straight at you, if a god lets you. And now dodge this bronze of mine — I’d be glad to see you take the whole length of it in your body. The war would be lighter for the Trojans with you dead, you who have been their worst grief.”

With that he balanced his long-shadowed spear and threw, and his aim was true — he struck the middle of Achilles’ shield, but the spear glanced off it, far away. Hector was furious that the swift cast had left his hand for nothing, and he stood there stunned, for he had no second spear. He shouted out to Deiphobus of the white shield, calling for his long spear — but Deiphobus was nowhere near him. And then Hector understood, in his heart, and said: “This is it. The gods have called me to my death. I thought the warrior Deiphobus was beside me — but he is inside the wall, and Athena has tricked me. Now ugly death is close, no longer far off, and there is no escape. This is what Zeus and the son of Zeus, the far-shooter, wanted all along, the gods who once protected me so readily. Now my fate has caught me. But let me not die without a fight, without glory, without some great deed done for men to come to hear of.”

So he spoke, and drew the keen sword that hung sharp and heavy at his side, and gathering himself he swooped like a high-flying eagle that drops through the dark clouds toward the plain to seize a soft lamb or a cowering hare — so Hector swooped, swinging his sharp sword. And Achilles charged, his heart full of savage fury, holding the splendid, intricate shield before his chest, his bright four-horned helmet nodding, and the golden plumes that Hephaestus had set thick along the crest tossing around it. As the evening star moves among the stars in the dead of night — Hesperus, the loveliest star set in the sky — so the light flashed off the keen spear-point that Achilles balanced in his right hand, his heart set on killing noble Hector, his eyes searching the handsome body for the place to drive it home. Now the rest of Hector was covered by the fine bronze armor he had stripped from mighty Patroclus when he killed him; but there was one gap, where the collarbones hold the neck off from the shoulders, at the throat — the spot where life is destroyed fastest. There, as Hector came on, brilliant Achilles drove the spear, and the point went clean through the soft neck. But the bronze-weighted ash did not cut his windpipe, so that he could still speak, and answer. He crashed down in the dust, and brilliant Achilles cried his triumph over him: “Hector — you thought, when you stripped Patroclus, that you’d be safe; you gave no thought to me, far off. Fool. Down by the hollow ships there was a defender left for him, better by far — me — and I have brought you low. The dogs and the birds will tear you apart in shame, while the Greeks give Patroclus his rites of burial.”

Then, his strength failing, Hector of the flashing helmet answered: “I beg you — by your life, by your knees, by your own parents — don’t let the dogs devour me beside the Greek ships. Take the bronze and gold, all the rich ransom my father and honored mother will give you, and send my body home, so the Trojans and their wives can grant me my share of fire in death.”

Glaring at him, swift-footed Achilles answered: “Don’t beg me, you dog, by my knees or my parents. I only wish my fury and my heart would drive me to carve your flesh and eat it raw, for what you’ve done — that’s how sure it is that no one will keep the dogs off your head. Not if they brought me ten times, twenty times the ransom, weighed it out before me here and promised more. Not if Priam, of the line of Dardanus, told them to give your weight in gold — not even then will your honored mother lay you on the bier and mourn the son she bore. The dogs and the birds will eat every part of you.”

And dying, Hector of the flashing helmet said: “I know you well, looking at you now. I was never going to move you. The heart in you is iron. But take care — I may become a cause of the gods’ anger against you, on the day when Paris and Phoebus Apollo destroy you, brave as you are, at the Scaean gates.”

Even as he spoke, the end that is death covered him over, and his soul slipped from his limbs and went down to the house of Hades, weeping for its fate, leaving behind its youth and strength. And though he was dead, brilliant Achilles spoke to him still: “Lie there dead. I will take my own death whenever Zeus and the other immortal gods choose to bring it.”

He drew the bronze spear out of the body and set it aside, and stripped the bloody armor from Hector’s shoulders. And the other Greeks came running up to gather around, marveling at Hector’s stature and his terrible beauty — and not one of them came near without driving in a fresh wound. And a man would say, glancing at his neighbor: “You can put your hands on Hector more easily now than when he was setting our ships on fire” — and as he said it, he would stab the body again. When swift Achilles had finished stripping the armor, he stood among the Greeks and spoke winged words: “Friends — leaders and counselors of the Greeks — now that the gods have let us bring down this man, who did us more harm than all the rest together, let us go in arms around the city and test it, and learn the Trojans’ minds — whether they’ll abandon the high town now that Hector has fallen, or hold out even with him gone. But why does my heart argue this with me? There by the ships lies a dead man, unwept, unburied — Patroclus, whom I will never forget as long as I am among the living and my own knees move. And even if men forget their dead down in the house of Hades, still I will remember my dear companion even there. Come now, young men of the Greeks — let us go back to the hollow ships singing the song of triumph, and take this man with us. We have won a great glory: we have killed noble Hector, to whom the Trojans prayed throughout their city as if he were a god.”

And then he did a shameful thing to noble Hector. He cut through the tendons of both feet behind, from heel to ankle, and threaded oxhide thongs through them, and bound the body to his chariot, leaving the head to drag. Then he lifted the famous armor aboard and mounted, and lashed the horses on, and they flew, not unwilling. A cloud of dust rose where Hector was dragged, and his dark hair streamed loose on either side, and the head that had once been so handsome lay all in the dust — for Zeus had given him up now to his enemies, to be defiled in his own native land.

So Hector’s head was fouled in the dust. And his mother tore her hair and flung off her shining veil and cried out, a long wail, seeing her son. His dear father groaned pitifully, and all around them the people gave themselves to weeping and wailing through the city. It was as if all of frowning Ilium were burning, top to bottom, in fire. The people could barely hold the old king back as he raged to go out through the Dardanian gates. He rolled in the filth and begged them all, naming each man by name: “Let me go, friends — leave me, for all your care for me. Let me go alone from the city to the Greek ships. Let me beg that violent man, that worker of terror; maybe he’ll feel some shame before his own years, some pity for an old man. He too has a father, Peleus, who fathered and reared him to be a curse to the Trojans — and to me above all others, so many of my sons cut down by him in their first youth. And yet for all of them I do not grieve so much as for this one — Hector — and the sharp grief for him will carry me down to the house of Hades. If only he had died in my arms; then his ill-fated mother who bore him, and I, could have had our fill of weeping and mourning over him.”

So he spoke, weeping, and the people of the city groaned with him. And among the women of Troy, Hecuba led the long lament: “My child — what is left for me, in my misery, now that you are dead? Night and day you were my pride throughout the city, and a help to all in Troy, men and women alike, who welcomed you like a god. You were their great glory while you lived. Now death and fate have come for you.”

So she spoke, weeping. But Hector’s wife knew nothing yet; no true messenger had come to tell her that her husband had stayed outside the gates. She was at her loom, deep in the high house, weaving a wide purple cloth and working into it intricate flowers. And she had called through the house to her fair-haired maids to set a great tripod on the fire, so there would be a warm bath ready for Hector when he came home from the fighting — poor woman, she did not know how far he was now from any bath, how Athena had brought him down beneath the hands of Achilles. Then she heard the wailing and the cries of grief from the wall, and her body shook, and the shuttle fell from her hands to the floor. At once she called again to her fair-haired maids: “Come, two of you, follow me — I must see what has happened. I heard the voice of my husband’s honored mother, and my own heart leaps to my mouth, my knees are frozen under me. Some terrible thing is near for the children of Priam. I pray it never reaches my ears — but I am sick with fear that brilliant Achilles has cut my bold Hector off from the city, alone, and driven him out onto the plain, and put an end to the deadly courage that always took him; for he would never hold back in the crowd of men, but always charged out ahead, yielding to no one in his fighting spirit.”

So she spoke, and rushed out through the hall like a madwoman, her heart pounding, her maids following after. And when she reached the wall and the crowd of men, she stopped on the rampart and looked out — and saw him: Hector, dragged in front of the city, the fast horses hauling him without mercy toward the hollow ships of the Greeks. And black night came down over her eyes, and she fell backward, fainting, and the breath went out of her. Far from her head she flung the shining bands that bound her hair — the frontlet, the netted snood, the woven headband, and the veil that golden Aphrodite had given her on the day Hector of the flashing helmet led her from the house of Eetion, after he had brought countless gifts to win her. Her husband’s sisters and his brothers’ wives crowded around her and held her up, dazed and half-dead between them. But when at last she breathed again and the spirit came back into her breast, she lifted her voice in broken grief among the women of Troy: “Hector — I am lost. So we were both born to one fate, you in Troy in the house of Priam, and I in Thebe, under wooded Placus, in the house of Eetion, who raised me when I was small — an ill-starred father, an ill-starred child. I wish he had never fathered me. Now you go down to the house of Hades, into the hidden places under the earth, and you leave me a widow in your halls, in terrible grief — and the boy is still only a baby, the child we two bore, doomed as we are. You will be no help to him now, Hector, now that you are dead, and he will be no help to you. Even if he comes through this miserable war with the Greeks, his life from now on will be all hardship and sorrow; other men will move his boundary stones and take his land. The day a child is left fatherless cuts him off from all his friends. He goes with his head bowed, his cheeks wet with tears, and in his need he goes to his father’s friends, tugging at one man’s cloak, another’s tunic; one of them, out of pity, may hold a cup to him for a moment, just enough to wet his lips, not enough to wet the roof of his mouth. And a boy with both parents living shoves him away from the feast with his fists and hard words: ‘Get out — your father isn’t dining here.’ And the child goes back crying to his widowed mother — Astyanax, who once sat on his father’s knees and ate only the marrow and the rich fat of sheep, and when sleep took him and he was tired of his play, slept soft in a bed, in his nurse’s arms, his heart full of good things. But now, with his dear father gone, he has much to suffer — Astyanax, ‘lord of the city,’ the name the Trojans gave him, because you alone, Hector, guarded their gates and their long walls. And now, far from your parents, by the curved ships, the writhing worms will eat you when the dogs have had their fill, and you will lie there naked — though in your halls there is fine clothing, soft and lovely, made by women’s hands. All of it I will burn in the fire. It is no use to you now; you will never lie in it again. But let it be your honor, in the eyes of the men and women of Troy.”

So she spoke, weeping, and the women wailed in answer.
