The Iliad, Book X

The Night Raid

Book X10 of 24

All the other Greek captains slept soundly the whole night through, but sleep would not come to Agamemnon, son of Atreus; his mind churned. As when the lord of fair-haired Hera throws his lightning to signal a great storm of rain, or hail, or snow that whitens the fields, or else to open wide the hungry jaws of war — so Agamemnon groaned again and again from the depths of his chest, and his heart shook within him. When he looked out across the plain of Troy he was amazed at all the watchfires burning in front of the city, and at the cry of pipes and flutes and the murmur of men. But when he turned back to his own ships and his own army, he tore at his hair by the handful, calling on Zeus on high, and the great heart in him groaned aloud.

In the end this seemed best to him: to go first to Nestor, son of Neleus, and see whether the two of them together could devise some defense against ruin for the Greeks. So he rose, pulled on his tunic, bound the fine sandals to his feet, and threw over his shoulders the tawny skin of a great lion that hung to his heels, and took up his spear.

Menelaus could not sleep either. He too feared for the Greeks, who for his sake had crossed the wide water to make war on the Trojans. He covered his broad back with the dappled hide of a leopard, set a helmet of bronze on his head, took a spear in his strong hand, and went to wake his brother — the most powerful of all the Greeks, a man the army honored like a god. He found him at the stern of his ship, already settling his armor about his shoulders, and was glad to see him coming.

Menelaus spoke first. “Why are you arming, brother? Are you going to send one of our men to scout the Trojans? I am afraid no one will do you this service — to spy on the enemy alone, in the dead of night. That takes a hard kind of courage.”

Agamemnon, lord of men, answered: “Menelaus, the two of us need sharp counsel, something that will save the Greeks and their ships. Zeus has turned his mind; he leans now toward Hector’s offerings, not ours. I have never seen or heard of any man working such ruin in a single day as Hector has worked against the sons of the Greeks — and all of it alone, son to no god or goddess. The Greeks will remember this grief for a long time. Now run, fast as you can, down the line of the ships, and call Ajax and Idomeneus. I will go to Nestor and tell him to rise and walk among our sentry companies and give them their orders. They will listen to him sooner than to anyone, for his own son and Meriones — Idomeneus’s man — are the captains over them. To them above all we gave that watch.”

Menelaus said: “How do I take your meaning? Am I to wait there with them for you, or come back here once I have given the order?” “Wait,” said Agamemnon, lord of men. “There are so many paths through the camp that we could miss each other in the dark. And call out to every man as you go; wake them. Name each by his lineage and his father’s name; give every man his honor, and do not stand too proudly on your own. We must take our full share of the work. This was the heavy load Zeus laid on us at our birth.”

With that he sent his brother off and went on himself to Nestor, shepherd of his people. He found the old man asleep in his hut by his black ship. His fine armor lay beside him — his shield, his two spears, his helmet — and beside him too the gleaming war-belt the old man buckled on when he armed to lead his men into battle; for age did not hold him back. He raised himself on one elbow, lifted his head, and called out to Agamemnon: “Who is this, going alone through the camp and the ships in the dead of night, when other men are sleeping? Are you looking for a stray mule, or for some friend? Speak — don’t just stand there in silence. What do you want?”

Agamemnon, lord of men, answered: “Nestor, son of Neleus, glory of the Greeks — you see Agamemnon, son of Atreus, on whom Zeus has heaped trouble and grief beyond all others, so long as there’s breath in my body and my legs hold me up. I am wandering here because sleep will not settle on my eyes; the war weighs on me, and the danger to the Greeks. I am sick with fear for them. My heart will not stay in its place; it pounds as if it would leap out of me, and my limbs give way beneath me. If you too cannot sleep, then come — let us go down to the sentries and see whether they have not dropped off, worn out, and forgotten their watch. The enemy is camped close by, and we have no way of knowing he will not strike in the night.”

Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, answered: “Most honored son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men — Zeus will not grant Hector everything he hopes for. He will have his own share of troubles yet, and more of them, if Achilles ever turns his heart back from this bitter anger. I will go with you gladly, and we’ll rouse the others too — the son of Tydeus, and Odysseus, and quick Ajax, and the brave son of Phyleus. And someone should go and call great Ajax and King Idomeneus, whose ships lie farthest off of all, not near at hand. But I will say this plainly, even if it stings — I love and honor Menelaus, but I have to blame him for sleeping and leaving all this trouble on your shoulders alone. He should be out among all the captains, begging their help, now that the need is so dire.”

Agamemnon, lord of men, answered: “Old man, there are times you would be right to blame him. He is often slack, unwilling to put himself forward — not from laziness, not from carelessness, but because he looks to me and waits for me to lead. This time, though, he woke before I did, and came to me on his own. I have already sent him off to call the very men you named. So let us go. We will find them out beyond the gates with the watch; that is where I told them to meet.”

Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, answered: “If so, no Greek will blame him or refuse him, when he urges them on or gives them orders.”

With that he pulled on his tunic, bound the fine sandals to his feet, and clasped about him a purple cloak, doubled over, wide, with a thick shaggy nap. He took up a strong bronze-headed spear and set off down the line of the Greek ships. First he came to Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel, and woke him with a shout — quickly the cry pierced his sleep, and he came out of his hut and said: “Why are you wandering alone like this, down the line of the ships, in the still of the night? What is so urgent?” And Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, answered: “Royal son of Laertes, resourceful Odysseus — don’t take it amiss. The Greeks are in dire straits. Come with me; let us wake another man, one who can help us decide whether to fight or to run.”

At that Odysseus stepped back into his hut, slung his shield over his shoulders, and went with them. They came first to Diomedes, son of Tydeus, and found him outside his hut in his armor, his men sleeping around him with their shields beneath their heads. Their spears stood upright, butt-spikes driven into the ground, and the bronze of them flashed far off like the lightning of Father Zeus. The hero himself was sleeping on an ox-hide, with a bright rug folded under his head. Nestor went and stirred him with his heel and woke him, prodding him, scolding him on: “Up, son of Tydeus. How can you sleep the whole night through? Don’t you see the Trojans are camped on the rise of the plain, close by the ships, with only a little ground left between us?”

At that Diomedes sprang up at once and said: “Old man, you are made of iron. You never rest a moment from your labors. Are there no younger Greeks who could go around and rouse the captains? There is no wearing you out.”

Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, answered: “My son, everything you say is true. I have good sons, and plenty of men who could call the chieftains. But the Greeks stand in the gravest danger; right now everything balances on a razor’s edge — life or death for all of us. Go then, since you are younger than I am, and wake quick Ajax and the son of Phyleus. Be good enough to do it.”

Diomedes threw the skin of a great tawny lion about his shoulders — a skin that hung to his heels — took up his spear, and went. When he had roused the heroes he brought them back, and together they went the round of the watch. They found the captains not asleep at their posts but awake, sitting alert, their weapons in their hands. As sheepdogs keep an anxious watch over the fold when they catch a wild beast coming down through the mountain woods — a clamor of dogs and men breaks out, and sleep is gone — so sleep was driven from the eyes of the men keeping the cruel night’s watch; they kept turning toward the plain, listening for any stir from the Trojans. The old man was glad to see it, and spoke to lift their hearts: “That’s the way, my children. Stay awake, every one of you; let no sleep take hold, or we’ll be the laughter of our enemies.”

With that he crossed the trench, and the other chiefs who had been called to the council went with him — and Meriones too, and Nestor’s brave son, for the princes asked them along. Beyond the dug trench that ran around the wall they came to clear ground, a space free of the dead, where Hector had turned back from killing Greeks when night came down. There they sat and held their council.

Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, spoke first: “My friends — is there a man bold enough to trust his own daring heart and go among the Trojans? He might cut off some straggler on the edge of the camp, or hear what the enemy means to do — whether they plan to hold their ground here by the ships, far from the city, or fall back behind their walls now that they’ve beaten us. If he could learn all this and come back safe, his fame would rise high as heaven in the mouths of men, and his reward would be rich: every captain who commands a ship would give him a black ewe with her lamb at the udder — no possession is worth more — and he would have his place at every feast and gathering.”

They all sat silent. Then Diomedes, master of the war cry, spoke: “Nestor, my heart and my proud spirit drive me to go among the enemy, the Trojans, so close at hand. But I’d go with far more confidence if another man went with me. When two go together, one sees the chance before the other; alone, a man’s sight is narrower and his wits are slower.”

At that, many offered to go with Diomedes. Both Ajaxes, fighters in Ares’ company, wanted to go, and Meriones, and Nestor’s son above all; Menelaus, son of Atreus, wanted to go; and patient Odysseus too, for his heart was always eager to dare. Then Agamemnon, lord of men, said: “Diomedes, son of Tydeus, dear to my own heart — choose your companion yourself. Take the best of those who have offered, for many are willing. And do not, out of some misplaced respect, leave the better man behind and take the worse for the sake of his royal blood.” He said this because he was afraid for Menelaus.

Diomedes, master of the war cry, answered: “If you tell me to choose the man myself, how could I overlook Odysseus? His heart and his bold spirit are equal to any danger, and Pallas Athena loves him. With him beside me, we could come back through fire itself — there is no one quicker to see and to understand.”

Patient, brilliant Odysseus said: “Son of Tydeus, don’t praise me too much, and don’t run me down. You are speaking among Greeks who know me well. Let us go. The night is wearing on, and dawn is near. The stars have wheeled across the sky; two parts of the night are gone, and only the third is left to us.”

Then the two of them armed. Brave Thrasymedes gave the son of Tydeus a two-edged sword — for his own was back at the ship — and a shield, and set on his head a helmet of bull’s hide, without ridge or crest, the kind they call a skullcap, that guards the heads of strong young men. Meriones gave Odysseus a bow, a quiver, and a sword, and set on his head a helmet made of leather, drawn tight inside with thongs and on the outside set thick with the white tusks of a boar, packed close and skillfully worked all around, with a lining of felt fitted in the middle. This helmet had a long history: Autolycus had once stolen it out of Eleon, when he broke into the strong house of Amyntor, son of Ormenus; he gave it to Amphidamas of Cythera to carry to Scandeia; Amphidamas gave it as a guest-gift to Molus, who gave it to his son Meriones; and now it was set on the head of Odysseus.

When the two of them had armed, they set out, leaving the other captains behind. And Pallas Athena sent them a sign — a heron, close by the path on their right. They could not see it in the dark, but they heard its cry. Odysseus was glad at the omen, and prayed to Athena: “Hear me, daughter of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, you who stand beside me in all my labors and miss nothing I do — befriend me now, more than ever. Grant that we come back to the ships covered in glory, having done some great deed the Trojans will not forget.”

Then Diomedes, master of the war cry, prayed as well: “Hear me too, unwearying daughter of Zeus. Come with me now, as you went with my father, brave Tydeus, to Thebes, when the Greeks sent him there as their envoy. He left them on the banks of the Asopus and carried his message of peace to the Cadmeians — and on his way back he did great and terrible things, with you beside him, gracious goddess, ready at his side. So stand by me now, and keep me safe. In return I will sacrifice to you a broad-browed heifer of a year, unbroken, never yet led under the yoke by any man. I will sheathe her horns in gold and offer her to you.”

So they prayed, and Pallas Athena heard them. When they had finished their prayer to the daughter of mighty Zeus, the two went forward like a pair of lions through the black night, through the slaughter and the corpses and the scattered arms and the dark blood.

Meanwhile Hector would not let the Trojans sleep either. He had called together all the leaders and counselors of the Trojans, and laid his plan before them: “Who will undertake the task I have in mind, for a great reward? His pay will be enough. I will give him a chariot and two horses, the finest at the Greek ships, to anyone who will dare it and win glory for himself besides. Let him go to the fast ships and find out whether they are still guarded as before, or whether the Greeks, beaten now, are planning their escape and are too worn out to keep their watch.”

They all sat silent. But there was among the Trojans a man named Dolon, son of Eumedes the herald, a man rich in gold and bronze. He was ugly to look at but quick on his feet, an only son among five sisters. He spoke now to Hector and the Trojans: “Hector, my heart and my proud spirit drive me to go to the fast ships and spy on them. But first hold up your scepter and swear to me you will give me the horses and the chariot, bright with bronze, that carry the matchless son of Peleus. I’ll be no useless scout to you, and I won’t fall short. I’ll go straight through their camp until I reach the ship of Agamemnon, where I expect the captains are taking counsel — whether to fight or to run.”

He spoke, and Hector took the scepter in his hands and swore: “Let Zeus himself be my witness, the loud-thundering husband of Hera — no other Trojan shall ride behind those horses. They are yours; you alone shall have the glory of them, for as long as you live.”

So he swore — an empty oath, but it spurred Dolon on. At once he slung his curved bow over his shoulder, pulled the gray hide of a wolf over his back, set a cap of weasel-skin on his head, took up a sharp javelin, and left the camp for the ships. But he was never to come back from the ships with word for Hector. When he had left the press of men and horses behind, he hurried eagerly down the path — and Odysseus saw him coming, and said to Diomedes: “Diomedes, here is someone from the camp. I can’t tell whether he’s a spy sent for our ships, or a man come to strip the dead. Let him get a little past us along the open ground; then we’ll rush him and run him down. And if he outruns us, keep pressing him toward the ships, away from his own camp, with your spear, so he can’t slip back to the city.”

With that the two of them lay down off the path among the dead. Dolon, suspecting nothing, ran straight past them. But when he had got about as far ahead as the width of a field a team of mules can plow in a day — for mules outwork oxen at dragging the jointed plow through deep fallow — the two ran after him. He heard their feet and stopped, sure they were friends from the Trojan camp, sent by Hector to call him back. But when they were a spear’s throw off, or less, he saw they were enemies and bolted, his legs flying, and they tore after him. As two sharp-toothed hounds, trained to the hunt, press hard on a fawn or a hare through wooded country while it runs squealing before them, so the son of Tydeus and Odysseus, the sacker of cities, ran Dolon down, cutting him off from his own people. But when, in his flight toward the ships, he had nearly reached the sentries, Athena put fresh strength into the son of Tydeus, so that no other Greek might strike first and rob him of the glory, leaving him second. Diomedes sprang on with his spear and shouted: “Stop, or I’ll run you through. You won’t escape death from my hand for long.”

He threw the spear, but missed on purpose. The point passed over the man’s right shoulder and stuck fast in the ground beyond him. Dolon stopped dead, shaking, his teeth chattering in his head, his face gone white with terror. The two came up to him panting and gripped his hands, and he burst into tears: “Take me alive — I’ll ransom myself. There is gold in my father’s house, and bronze, and hard-wrought iron; he will pay you a boundless ransom if he hears I’m alive at the Greek ships.”

Resourceful Odysseus answered him: “Take heart. Let no thought of death weigh on you. But tell me this, and tell it true: where are you going, alone, away from your camp toward the ships, in the dead of night, while other men sleep? Did you come to strip the bodies of the dead, or did Hector send you to spy out everything at the ships? Or did some impulse of your own bring you here?”

Dolon answered, his knees trembling under him: “Hector led me astray, out of all good sense, with his promises. He swore he would give me the strong-hoofed horses of the matchless son of Peleus and his chariot bright with bronze. He sent me through the fast black night to get close to the enemy and find out whether the ships are still guarded as before, or whether the Greeks, beaten now, are planning their escape and are too worn out to keep their watch.”

Resourceful Odysseus smiled at him and said: “You had set your heart on a great prize indeed — the horses of warlike Achilles. They are hard for any mortal man to master or to drive, any man but Achilles himself, whose mother is a goddess. But tell me this now, and tell it true: where did you leave Hector when you came? Where is his war-gear lying, and his horses? How are the watches set among the other Trojans, and where do they sleep? What are they planning? Will they hold their ground here by the ships, away from the city, or fall back behind their walls now that they’ve beaten us?”

Dolon, son of Eumedes, answered: “I will tell you the whole truth. Hector and his counselors are holding their council apart from the noise, beside the tomb of great Ilus. As for the guards you ask about — there is no chosen watch set over the camp. The Trojans themselves keep their fires burning, since they must, and they stay awake and call each other to their posts. But the allies, who have come from many lands, are asleep; they leave the watching to the Trojans, for their own wives and children are not here.”

Resourceful Odysseus said: “And these allies — do they sleep mingled in among the Trojan horsemen, or apart? Tell me clearly; I want to understand.”

Dolon, son of Eumedes, answered: “I’ll tell you the whole truth of that as well. Toward the sea lie the Carians, and the Paeonians with their curved bows, and the Leleges and Cauconians and the noble Pelasgians. Toward Thymbra are the Lycians, and the proud Mysians, and the Phrygians and the Maeonians, masters of horses. But why ask all this? If you mean to slip into the Trojan host — there, at the very edge of the camp, apart from the rest, lie the Thracians, newly come. Among them is their king, Rhesus, son of Eioneus. I have never seen horses like his, so fine, so tall — whiter than snow, swift as the winds when they run. His chariot is worked with gold and silver, and he came carrying golden armor, a marvel of craft, too splendid for mortal men to wear — fit only for the deathless gods. Now take me to the fast ships, or tie me up and leave me here, until you go and prove for yourselves whether what I’ve told you is true or false.”

Diomedes, master of the war cry, glared at him and said: “Don’t imagine you’ll get away now, Dolon, useful as your news has been, once you’re in our hands. If we let you go or ransom you, you’ll be back at the Greek ships another time — to spy again, or to fight us in the open. But if my hand takes your life, you’ll never trouble the Greeks again.”

Dolon reached up, about to take him by the chin and beg for his life — but Diomedes struck him across the middle of the neck with his sword and cut through both tendons. Dolon’s head rolled in the dust while he was still trying to speak. They stripped from him the weasel-skin cap, the wolf hide, the bent bow, and the long spear. Odysseus, sacker of cities, lifted them high in his hands to Athena, giver of spoils, and prayed: “Take these, goddess, and rejoice. Of all on Olympus, you are the first we call on. Now guide us once more — to the horses and the sleeping-ground of the Thracian men.”

With that he lifted the spoils and set them on a tamarisk bush, and marked the spot by pulling up reeds and breaking off branches of the tamarisk, so they would not miss it coming back through the swift dark of the night. Then the two went on, through the scattered arms and the black blood, and came quickly to the band of Thracian men. They were asleep, worn out with the day’s labor; their fine armor lay beside them on the ground in three good rows, and each man had his pair of horses by him. Rhesus slept in the middle, his swift horses tied with their reins to the topmost rail of his chariot. Odysseus saw him first and said: “Diomedes, this is the man, and these are the horses Dolon told us of, the one we killed. Now show your full strength. It is no time to stand idle in your armor. Loose the horses — or kill the men, and leave the horses to me.”

He spoke, and bright-eyed Athena breathed fury into Diomedes, and he killed to the left and the right. Hideous groans rose from the men as the sword went through them, and the ground ran red with blood. As a lion comes on a flock of sheep or goats left without their shepherd and leaps among them, bent on slaughter, so the son of Tydeus fell on the Thracian men until he had killed twelve. And each man Diomedes cut down with his sword, Odysseus, the master of strategy, took by the foot and dragged aside, clearing a way, so the fair-maned horses could pass through without shying — for they were not yet used to walking over the dead. Then the son of Tydeus came to the king himself, and Rhesus made the thirteenth life he took with his sword — Rhesus, gasping in his sleep. For an evil dream had stood that night above his head: the grandson of Oeneus himself, by the design of Athena. Meanwhile Odysseus, the man of patience, loosed the strong-hoofed horses, tied them together with the reins, and drove them clear of the camp, herding them with his bow — for he had forgotten to take the bright whip from the chariot. Then he whistled, a signal to brilliant Diomedes.

But Diomedes stood there still, turning over what bolder thing he might do — whether to seize the chariot, with the king’s fine armor lying in it, and drag it off by the pole, or hoist the armor out and carry it away; or whether to take still more Thracian lives. And while he stood weighing it, Athena came and stood beside him: “Think of going back to the ships, son of great-hearted Tydeus, or you may go back driven before the enemy — should some other god wake the Trojans.”

He knew the voice of the goddess, and swung up at once onto a horse. Odysseus whipped them on with his bow, and they flew toward the fast ships of the Greeks.

But Apollo of the silver bow was keeping no idle watch. He had seen Athena going with the son of Tydeus, and in his anger at her he slipped into the great host of the Trojans and woke a Thracian counselor, Hippocoon, a noble kinsman of Rhesus. The man started up out of sleep, and when he saw the empty ground where the swift horses had stood, and the men gasping out their lives in the bloody slaughter, he cried aloud and called his dear comrade by name. And an uproar rose through the Trojan camp, a confused rush of men running together, and they stared in horror at the deeds those two had done before they got away to the ships.

When the two reached the place where they had killed Hector’s spy, Odysseus, dear to Zeus, reined in the horses, and the son of Tydeus leaped to the ground, put the bloody spoils into Odysseus’s hands, and climbed back up. Then he lashed the horses on, and they flew willingly toward the hollow ships, glad to be going home. Nestor was the first to hear the beat of their hooves, and said: “Friends, leaders and counselors of the Greeks — am I wrong, or am I right? My heart tells me to speak. The sound of swift horses’ hooves is in my ears. I pray it is Odysseus and brave Diomedes driving the strong-hoofed horses back from the Trojans now — but I am terribly afraid the bravest of the Greeks may have come to harm in the uproar of the enemy.”

He had not finished speaking when the two arrived. They dismounted, and the others welcomed them gladly, with handclasps and warm words. Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, was the first to ask: “Tell me, Odysseus, glory of the Greeks — how did the two of you take these horses? Did you slip into the Trojan host, or did some god meet you and give them to you? They are like the rays of the sun. I am always going among the Trojans — old as I am, I never hang back by the ships — but I have never seen or imagined horses like these. Some god must have met you and made you this gift; for Zeus who gathers the clouds loves you both, and so does his daughter, bright-eyed Athena.”

Resourceful Odysseus answered him: “Nestor, son of Neleus, glory of the Greeks — a god, if he wished, could easily give us finer horses than these, for the gods are far stronger than we are. But these horses you ask about, old man, are newcomers from Thrace. Brave Diomedes killed their king and twelve of his men beside him, the best of them. And we took a thirteenth close to the ships — a scout Hector and the other proud Trojans had sent to spy on our camp.”

So he spoke, and he laughed as he drove the strong-hoofed horses across the trench, and the other Greeks went with him, glad at heart. When they reached the well-built quarters of the son of Tydeus, they tied the horses with cut leather reins at the manger, where Diomedes’s own swift horses stood feeding on sweet barley. And Odysseus hung Dolon’s bloody spoils at the stern of his ship, until they could ready a holy offering for Athena. Then the two of them waded into the sea and washed the heavy sweat from their shins and necks and thighs. And when the swell of the sea had rinsed the sweat from their bodies and the heart in each man eased, they stepped into the polished tubs and bathed. Washed and rubbed with oil, they sat down to eat, and from a full mixing-bowl they drew sweet wine and poured the first of it out to Athena.
