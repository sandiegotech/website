The Iliad, Book IX

The Embassy to Achilles

Book IX9 of 24

So the Trojans kept their watch. But Panic, the sister of cold-blooded Rout, had laid hold of the Greeks, and every one of their leaders was sick at heart. As when two winds off Thrace, the north wind and the west, swoop down together on the fish-thronged sea and in an instant heave the dark water into peaks and fling the seaweed scattering up the beach — so were the hearts of the Greeks torn in their breasts.

In his misery the son of Atreus told the heralds to summon the men to assembly — each one called by name, quietly, no shout going up over the camp — and he went among them himself, calling them. They came and sat down heavy-hearted. Agamemnon stood up weeping, the tears running down like a stream of dark water that falls in a sheet from a high cliff, and groaning hard he spoke to the Greeks:

“My friends — leaders and counselors of the Greeks — the hand of Zeus has come down hard on me. The cruel god. Once he bowed his head and promised me I would sack the strong walls of Troy and sail home; now he has played me false, and orders me back to Argos in disgrace, with so many of my men dead. So it must please almighty Zeus, who has thrown down the high crowns of many cities, and will throw down more — his power is the greatest there is. So let us all do as I say: let us run with our ships for our own dear country. We will never take Troy now, with its wide streets.”

He spoke, and for a long while the sons of the Greeks sat without a word, sick at heart and silent. At last Diomedes, master of the war cry, broke it: “Son of Atreus, I’ll fight your folly first — it’s my right, here in the assembly, so don’t take it hard. You attacked my courage once before, in front of the whole army; you called me a coward and no soldier. Every Greek, young and old, knows you said it. The son of crooked-minded Cronus gave you your gifts by halves: he gave you the scepter and the honor of ruling over all of us, but courage — the highest thing there is — he did not give you. You strange man. Do you really think the sons of the Greeks are the cowards you say? If your own heart is set on going home, then go — the road is open, your ships are there on the shore, the whole fleet that came with you from Mycenae. But the rest of us will stay until we’ve sacked Troy. And if they too want to run — let them sail for their own dear country. Sthenelus and I will fight on, the two of us, until we reach the end of Troy. We came here with a god behind us.”

The sons of the Greeks shouted their approval, struck by the words of Diomedes the breaker of horses. Then Nestor the horseman rose and spoke among them: “Son of Tydeus — in war your strength is beyond question, and in council you are the best of the men your age; no Greek will make light of your words or speak against them. And yet you have not gone all the way to the end of the matter. You’re still young — you might be the youngest son I have — but you have spoken sense, and spoken it well, to the kings of the Greeks. So now let me speak; I am older than you, and I will say it all, and no one will make light of it, not even lord Agamemnon. A man who longs for the horror of war among his own people is a man without clan, without law, without a hearth.

“But for now, let us yield to the night and see to our supper. Let the sentries post themselves along the ditch, outside the wall. That is my word to the younger men. And then, son of Atreus, take the lead — you are the most kingly among us. Lay on a feast for the elders; it is right and fitting. Your huts are full of wine, the wine the Greek ships bring across the sea from Thrace every day; you have everything you need to host us, and many men under you. Gather many heads together, and follow the man whose counsel proves best. The Greeks have hard need of good counsel now, wise and shrewd, with the enemy’s watchfires burning so close to our ships. What man could look at them and not be afraid? This night will either ruin the army or save it.”

So he spoke, and they heard him gladly and did as he said. The sentries went out under arms — Nestor’s son Thrasymedes, shepherd of the people, at their head, and Ascalaphus and Ialmenus, the sons of Ares, and Meriones, and Aphareus, and Deipyrus, and Lycomedes, the noble son of Creon. Seven captains of the watch there were, and a hundred young men with each, gripping their long spears; they took their place between the ditch and the wall, lit their fires, and each man made his supper.

Then the son of Atreus led the elders of the Greeks to his quarters and set a good feast before them, and they reached out their hands to the food. When they had put away their hunger and thirst, old Nestor was the first to weave them his counsel — Nestor, whose advice had always shown the best before. With good will toward them all he spoke:

“Most honored son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men — with you I will begin, and with you I will end, for you are king over many. Zeus has put the scepter in your hand and the law in your keeping, so that you may take thought for your people. So it falls to you above all others both to speak and to listen — and to act on another man’s word, when his heart moves him to speak for the good. Whatever a man begins, it comes back to rest on you. So let me say what I judge to be best. No one will think of a better word than the one I have carried in me since the day, my king, that you went into the hut of Achilles and took the girl Briseis, against my own judgment. I argued hard against it, but you gave way to your high temper and dishonored a man the very gods have honored — for you hold his prize still. But even now, let us think how to win him back, with gifts and with gentle words.”

And Agamemnon, lord of men, answered: “Old man, you have not lied; my folly is just as you say. I was blind. I will not deny it. One man, when the gods are with him, is worth a whole army — and Zeus has shown his love for this one by laying the Greeks low. I was blind, I gave way to my own black temper; so now I will make it right, and pay him back with gifts beyond counting. Here, in front of you all, I will name them. Seven tripods never touched by fire. Ten bars of gold. Twenty gleaming cauldrons. Twelve strong horses, prize-winners, who have carried off trophies with their speed — a man would be no poor man, no man short of precious gold, who had as much as these horses have won me. And seven women skilled in fine work, women of Lesbos, that I chose for myself when Achilles took Lesbos — they outshone all their sex in beauty. These I will give him, and with them the one I took from him, the daughter of Briseus. And I will swear a great oath: that I never once went up into her bed, never lay with her, as is the way of men and women.

“All this is his now. And if the gods grant us someday to sack the great city of Priam, let him come when we Greeks are dividing the plunder and load his ship deep with gold and bronze, as much as he likes; and let him pick out for himself twenty Trojan women, the loveliest there are after Helen. And when we reach Greek Argos, the richest land on earth, he will be my son-in-law, and I will hold him in honor like my own son Orestes, who is being raised there in all abundance. I have three daughters in my strong-built house — Chrysothemis, Laodice, and Iphianassa. Let him take whichever he likes, and bring her to the house of Peleus — and no bride-price; I will give him a dowry instead, greater than any man ever gave with his daughter. I will give him seven strong cities: Cardamyle, Enope, and grassy Hire; sacred Pherae, and Antheia in its deep meadows; lovely Aepeia, and Pedasus of the vines. All of them lie near the sea, on the borders of sandy Pylos, and the men who live there are rich in flocks and herds; they will honor him like a god with their gifts, and pay him rich tribute under his rule. All this I will do, if he will let go of his anger. Let him give way. Hades alone is the god who cannot be softened, cannot be turned — and so of all the gods men hate him most. And I am the greater king, and I am the older man.”

Then Nestor the horseman answered: “Most honored son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men — these are no small gifts you offer Achilles. Come, then, let us send chosen men to go without delay to the hut of Peleus’s son. The ones I name, let them take it on. First let Phoenix, dear to Zeus, lead the way; then great Ajax and brilliant Odysseus, and let the heralds Odius and Eurybates go with them. Now bring water for our hands, and call for holy silence, while we pray to Zeus, the son of Cronus, to take pity on us.”

So he spoke, and his words pleased them all. The heralds poured water over their hands, and the young men filled the bowls to the brim and served the wine to every man, pouring the first drops out for the gods. When they had poured the offering and drunk what their hearts desired, they set out from the hut of Agamemnon, son of Atreus. And Nestor, the old horseman, sent them off with a long look at each in turn — at Odysseus most of all — pressing on them again and again to do their best to win over the faultless son of Peleus.

The two of them went along the shore of the sounding sea, praying hard to Poseidon, who holds the earth in his arms, that they might easily bend the great heart of Achilles. They came to the ships and huts of the Myrmidons, and found him there, soothing his heart with a clear-toned lyre — a beautiful thing, finely made, with a crossbar of silver. He had taken it from the spoils when he sacked the city of Eetion. With it he was easing his heart now, singing of the glories of men. Patroclus sat across from him, alone with him, silent, waiting for the moment the grandson of Aeacus would leave off his song. The two envoys came forward, brilliant Odysseus leading, and stood before him. Achilles sprang up, astonished, the lyre still in his hand, and left the seat where he had been sitting; and Patroclus rose too, when he saw the men. Achilles, the swift runner, gave them welcome: “Welcome, both of you. You come as friends — there must be some great need. But you are the dearest of the Greeks to me, even now, in my anger.”

With that brilliant Achilles led them in and sat them down on couches spread with purple cloths, and at once he said to Patroclus, close by him: “Son of Menoetius — set out a larger bowl, mix the wine stronger, and pour a cup for every man. These are the men I love best, here under my roof.”

Patroclus did as his dear friend bade him. He set a great chopping block down in the firelight and laid out on it the back of a sheep, the back of a fat goat, and the backbone of a hog, rich with fat. Automedon held the meat steady while Achilles carved it, cutting it cleanly and running the pieces onto spits, and the son of Menoetius, a man like a god, built the fire high. When it had burned down and the flame died, he raked the embers flat, laid the spits across them on their racks, and sprinkled the meat with holy salt. When it was roasted he heaped it on platters; Patroclus brought bread to the table in handsome baskets, and Achilles served out the meat. Then he took his own seat against the far wall, across from Odysseus, and told his friend Patroclus to make the offering to the gods, and Patroclus threw the first portions into the fire. So they reached out their hands to the food before them. And when they had put away their hunger and thirst, Ajax nodded to Phoenix. Odysseus saw it, and filling his cup with wine he lifted it to Achilles:

“Your health, Achilles. We have had no want of a good feast — not in the hut of Agamemnon, and not here now; there has been plenty to eat and drink. But it is not feasting we have on our minds. We are looking disaster in the face, my lord, and without you we cannot tell whether our ships will be saved or lost. The Trojans and their allies are camped right up against the wall and the ships; their watchfires are burning all through the camp, and they say nothing can stop them now from falling on the black ships. And Zeus has shown them his sign, the lightning on their right. Hector storms on in his glory, raging, trusting in Zeus, with no fear of god or man left in him — a kind of madness has him. He prays for dawn to come quickly. He swears he will hack the high sterns from our ships, set the hulls ablaze, and slaughter the Greeks where they stand, choking in the smoke. And I am terribly afraid the gods will make good his boast, and our fate will be to die here at Troy, far from the horse-pastures of Argos. Up, then — late as it is, if you mean to save the worn-out sons of the Greeks from the Trojan storm. You yourself will grieve for it later if you don’t; once the harm is done, there is no cure for it. Think, while there is still time, how to keep this evil day from the Greeks.

“My friend — when your father Peleus sent you off from Phthia to Agamemnon, did he not charge you, saying: ‘My son, strength is a thing Athena and Hera will give you, if they choose. But you — hold down the high temper in your chest. A kind heart is the better way. Let go of quarreling, the breeder of trouble, and old and young among the Greeks will honor you the more.’ That was the old man’s charge, and you have let it slip. Even now, let it go — give up this anger that eats the heart. Agamemnon will make you fit amends, if only you give up your rage. Listen, and I will tell you everything he named in his hut. Seven tripods never touched by fire, and ten bars of gold; twenty gleaming cauldrons, and twelve strong horses, prize-winners — a man would be no poor man, no man short of precious gold, who had as much as these horses have won Agamemnon. And seven women skilled in fine work, women of Lesbos, that he chose for himself when you took Lesbos — they outshone all their sex in beauty. These he will give you, and with them the one he took from you, the daughter of Briseus. And he will swear a great oath that he never went up into her bed, never lay with her, as is the way of men and women, my lord. All this is yours at once. And if the gods grant us someday to sack the great city of Priam, you may come when we Greeks are dividing the plunder and load your ship deep with gold and bronze, and pick out for yourself twenty Trojan women, the loveliest there are after Helen. And when we reach Greek Argos, the richest land on earth, you will be his son-in-law, and he will hold you in honor like his own son Orestes, who is being raised there in all abundance. He has three daughters in his strong-built house — Chrysothemis, Laodice, and Iphianassa — and you may take whichever you like, with no bride-price, to the house of Peleus; and he will give a dowry besides, greater than any man ever gave with his daughter. And he will give you seven strong cities: Cardamyle, Enope, and grassy Hire; sacred Pherae, and Antheia in its deep meadows; lovely Aepeia, and Pedasus of the vines, all of them near the sea, on the borders of sandy Pylos, and the men who live there rich in flocks and herds, who will honor you like a god with their gifts and pay you rich tribute under your rule. All this he will do, if you will give up your anger. But if the son of Atreus is too hateful to you, gifts and all, then have pity at least on the rest of the Greeks, worn down through the whole army, who will honor you like a god. You would win great glory in their eyes. You might even kill Hector — he would come right into your reach now, mad as he is, sure there is no man among us the ships have brought who can match him.”

Then Achilles, the swift runner, answered him: “Royal son of Laertes, resourceful Odysseus — I had better say it straight out, the way I see it, the way it is going to be, so you don’t sit here cooing at me one after another. I hate like the gates of death the man who hides one thing in his heart and says another. So here is what I think. The son of Atreus will not win me over, and neither will the rest of the Greeks. There is no thanks in it, none — fighting the enemy on and on without rest. The man who hangs back gets the same share as the man who fights his hardest. The coward and the brave man are held in one honor. Death comes the same to the man who has done nothing and the man who has done much. And I have got nothing by it, for all the pain I’ve put my heart through, day after day staking my life in battle. Like a bird that brings every scrap she finds to her unfledged chicks, and goes hungry herself — that is how it has been with me: long sleepless nights, and bloody days of fighting against men defending their wives. I have sacked twelve cities of men from my ships, and eleven more, I tell you, on foot across the deep soil of Troy. From every one of them I took out great treasure, fine treasure, and carried it all back and put it in the hands of Agamemnon, son of Atreus — and he, hanging back the whole time by his fast ships, would take it, dole out a little, and keep the most for himself.

“Oh, he handed out prizes well enough to the other kings and chiefs, and they have theirs still, untouched. From me alone of all the Greeks he took it — took the woman I loved. Well, let him keep her. Let him lie with her and be glad. But tell me — why must the Greeks make war on the Trojans at all? Why did the son of Atreus gather an army and lead it here? Wasn’t it for Helen of the lovely hair? Are the sons of Atreus the only men alive who love their wives? Any man worth anything loves his own woman and cares for her, as I loved mine with my whole heart, spear-won though she was. He has taken her from my hands, cheated me — so let him not try me again. I know him now. He will not move me. No, Odysseus — let him take counsel with you and the other kings how to keep the fire off the ships. He has done a great deal already without me: he has built a wall, and dug a ditch beside it, broad and deep, and driven stakes into it. And even so he cannot hold back man-killing Hector. As long as I was fighting among the Greeks, Hector wouldn’t carry the battle far from his own wall — he came only as far as the Scaean gates and the oak tree, and there he once waited for me alone, and barely got away. But now, since I have no will to fight him, tomorrow at dawn I will make sacrifice to Zeus and all the gods, and haul my ships down and load them. And in the morning, if you care to look, you will see my ships at first light riding out over the Hellespont, my men bending hard at the oars. If the great Earthshaker grants me a clear passage, on the third day I will be home in the deep soil of Phthia.

“I have everything there that I left when I came here, to my sorrow — and I’ll bring home more besides, gold and red bronze, fine-belted women and gray iron, all I drew by lot — though my prize, the prize that he gave, lord Agamemnon, son of Atreus, in his arrogance has taken back again. Tell him all of it, exactly as I say, and out loud, so the rest of the Greeks may be angry too, if he ever thinks to cheat another of them — shameless as he is to the bone. And yet, dog that he is, he would not dare look me in the eye. No. I will share no counsel with him, no work with him. He cheated me once and wronged me; he will not talk me round a second time. Once is enough. Let him go to his ruin in peace — Zeus has stolen his wits. His gifts are hateful to me; I value them at the price of a single hair. Not if he gave me ten times, twenty times all he has now, and all that might ever come to him from anywhere — not all the wealth that pours into Orchomenus, or into Egyptian Thebes, where the houses are stacked with treasure, Thebes of the hundred gates, two hundred fighters driving out through each with their chariots and horses — not if his gifts were as many as the grains of sand or the dust of the earth, even then Agamemnon will not bend my heart, not until he has paid me back in full for the bitter outrage to my honor.

“And I will not marry the daughter of Agamemnon. Not if she rivaled golden Aphrodite for beauty and gray-eyed Athena for the work of her hands — even so I will not have her. Let him pick some other Greek, a man more his equal, a greater king than I am. If the gods keep me safe and I reach home, Peleus himself will find me a wife. There are Greek women all through Hellas and Phthia, daughters of the lords who hold the towns; I can take any of them I want and make her my wife. My heart has turned that way often, back home — to marry a fit wife, a true bride, and enjoy what my old father Peleus built. For nothing is worth my life — not all they say the great city of Troy held in the old days of peace, before the sons of the Greeks ever came, nor all the treasure locked behind the stone threshold of Apollo the archer at rocky Pytho. Cattle and fat sheep can be had for the taking; tripods and the tawny heads of horses can be won. But a man’s life cannot be raided back, cannot be seized or bought, once it has crossed the barrier of his teeth.

“My mother Thetis, the silver-footed goddess, tells me two fates carry me toward the day of my death. If I stay here and fight on around the city of Troy, there is no homecoming for me, but my glory will never die. If I go home, to my own dear country, my glory is lost, but I will live long, and death will be slow in coming. And to the rest of you I say this too: sail home. There is no end of Troy to be reached now. Zeus the wide-thunderer holds his hand over her, and her people have taken heart. So go, and carry my message to the chief men of the Greeks — that is the privilege of elders — and tell them to think of some better plan to save their ships and the army camped beside them, since the one they have hit on cannot serve while my anger holds. As for Phoenix, let him stay and sleep here, so he may sail home with me in the morning, if he is willing. I will not take him by force.”

So he spoke, and they all fell silent, struck dumb by his words; for he had refused them with great force. At last the old horseman Phoenix spoke, bursting into tears, in dread for the ships of the Greeks: “If you really mean to go home, brilliant Achilles — if you will do nothing at all to keep the consuming fire from the ships, because the anger has fallen on your heart — how can I stay here behind you, dear child, all alone? Your father Peleus sent me with you the day he sent you off, a boy still, from Phthia to Agamemnon, knowing nothing of leveling war, nothing of the assembly where men come to stand out among others. He sent me to teach you all of it — to be a speaker of words and a doer of deeds. So I could never be willing to stay behind without you, child — not if a god himself promised to strip the old age from me and make me young and fresh as the day I first left Hellas, the land of lovely women, fleeing the wrath of my father Amyntor, son of Ormenus.

“He was in a fury with me over a beautiful woman, a mistress he loved, while he dishonored his own wife, my mother. And my mother begged me without end, on her knees, to lie with the woman first, so that she would loathe the old man. I did as she asked. My father knew it at once, and cursed me bitterly, calling on the dread Furies, that no son of mine should ever sit on his knees — a son of my begetting. And the gods made the curse good, Zeus of the underworld and dread Persephone. I thought to kill him with the sharp bronze; but some god checked me, and set before my heart the talk of men and all the names they would call me — a father-killer among the Greeks. Yet I could no longer bear to stay in the house of a father so enraged at me. My cousins and kinsmen crowded around, begging me to stay; they killed many fat sheep and shambling oxen, and set many fat hogs to singe and roast over the fire, and drank down jar after jar of the old man’s wine. Nine nights running they slept around me, taking the watch in turns, and kept two fires burning — one in the porch of the walled court, one in the hall, before the door of my room. But on the tenth night, in the dark, I broke the close-fitted doors of the chamber, and was over the courtyard wall, slipping past the watchmen and the women slaves before any of them saw me. Then I fled far through the wide land of Hellas and came at last to deep-soiled Phthia, mother of flocks, to King Peleus. And he took me in gladly, and loved me as a father loves his only son, his late-born heir to great wealth. He made me rich and set many people under me, and I lived on the borders of Phthia, lord over the Dolopians.

“And I made you what you are, Achilles, godlike as you are — I loved you from my heart. You would never go off to a feast with another, or eat at home, until I had set you on my knees and cut your meat small for you and held the wine to your lips. Many a time you wet the front of my shirt, spitting up the wine in your helpless little way. I went through a great deal for you, took a great deal of trouble, thinking that since the gods would give me no son of my own, I would make you my son, godlike Achilles, so that one day you might keep the cruel day from me. So master your great spirit, Achilles. You should not have a pitiless heart. Even the gods can be turned, and theirs is the greater excellence, the greater honor and strength. And yet with sacrifices and soft prayers, with libations and the savor of burnt offering, men turn them, begging when a man has gone wrong and overstepped.

“For there are Prayers, too — the daughters of great Zeus. Lame, and wrinkled, and their eyes turned aside, they come limping along behind Ruin. But Ruin is strong, and quick on her feet, and so she outruns them all by far and gets first to every land, and there she does her harm to men; and the Prayers come after, healing what she has done. Now, the man who reveres these daughters of Zeus when they come near — him they help, and they hear him when he prays. But the man who refuses them, who hardens against them and turns them away — they go to Zeus, son of Cronus, and ask that Ruin walk with that man, so that he is hurt, and pays. So you, Achilles — see that you too grant the daughters of Zeus the honor that bends the minds of other good men. If the son of Atreus were not bringing gifts and naming more for later, if he were still hot in his anger, I would not be the one to tell you to throw your wrath aside and stand by the Greeks, however hard their need. But he is giving much now, and pledging more to come, and he has sent the best men to plead his cause, picked out from the whole army, the men dearest to you of all the Greeks. Don’t shame their words or their coming. Until now no one could blame your anger. But of the heroes of old we have heard the same — that even men in a towering rage could be won by gifts and turned by words.

“There is an old tale I remember — an old one, not a new one — and since you are all friends here, I’ll tell it. The Curetes and the Aetolians were fighting, killing each other around the city of Calydon, the Aetolians defending it, the Curetes burning to bring it down. Artemis of the golden throne had set this evil on them, angry that Oeneus had not offered her the first fruits of his orchard. The other gods all feasted on his sacrifices; to her alone, the daughter of great Zeus, he gave nothing — he forgot, or it slipped his mind somehow, and his heart went badly astray. So in her anger the archer goddess sent a wild boar against him, a great white-tusked thing, and it did much harm to Oeneus’s orchard, throwing tall apple trees flat on the ground, roots and blossoms and all. Meleager, the son of Oeneus, killed it at last, gathering hunters and hounds from many cities — for it was no small band that could bring down a beast so huge; it set many a man on the bitter funeral pyre. But over its body the goddess raised a clash and an uproar, the Curetes and the great-hearted Aetolians fighting for the boar’s head and its shaggy hide.

“Now, as long as Meleager, dear to Ares, was in the fighting, it went badly for the Curetes, and for all their numbers they could not hold their ground outside the wall. But there came a time when anger swelled in Meleager — the anger that swells the breast even of wise men. He was enraged at his mother Althaea, and so he lay idle beside his wedded wife, lovely Cleopatra. She was the daughter of Marpessa of the fine ankles and of Idas, the strongest man on earth in his day — Idas, who took up his bow against lord Apollo himself for the sake of fair Marpessa. Her father and mother in their house had named the girl Alcyone, the halcyon, because her mother had wept like the halcyon bird, the bird of grief, when Apollo the far-shooter carried her off. Beside this Cleopatra Meleager lay, nursing the bitter anger of his mother’s curse. For she, in her grief over her dead brother, prayed to the gods, beating the nourishing earth with her hands, calling on Hades and dread Persephone, down on her knees, her breast wet with tears, that they should bring death to her son. And the Fury that walks in the dark, the one whose heart knows no pity, heard her out of Erebus.

“Soon the noise of battle was at the gates, the thudding of the Curetes against the towers. The elders of the Aetolians sent the holiest of their priests to Meleager, begging him to come out and save them, and promising a great gift: wherever the richest land lay in the plain of lovely Calydon, there they bade him cut himself fifty acres, half vineyard, half open plowland. The old horseman Oeneus pleaded with him, standing on the threshold of his high room, rattling the close-fitted doors, begging his son. His sisters pleaded, and his honored mother; he refused them harder still. His comrades pleaded, the ones nearest and dearest to him of all; not even they could move his heart — not until his own room was being battered, and the Curetes were climbing the towers and setting the great city alight. Then at last his wife, in her grief, begged him, and laid out for him all the sorrows that fall on a people whose city is taken: the men cut down, the city burned to ash, the children and the deep-belted women dragged away. His heart stirred as he heard those evils, and he rose and went out and put on his shining armor. So Meleager kept the day of ruin from the Aetolians, giving way to his own heart — but they never gave him the rich and gracious gifts; he saved them, and got nothing.

“So don’t you think that way, dear child; let no god turn your mind down that road. It will be a harder thing to save the ships once they are burning. Come for the gifts. The Greeks will honor you like a god. But if you go into the slaughter later, without the gifts, you will not be held in the same honor, even if you beat the war back.”

Then Achilles, the swift runner, answered him: “Phoenix, old father, dear to Zeus — I have no need of that kind of honor. I think I am honored already by the will of Zeus, and that honor will keep me here by the curved ships as long as the breath is in my chest and my knees have their spring. And I’ll tell you another thing — fix it in your mind. Don’t go confusing my heart with this weeping and grieving, doing it all for the son of Atreus. You shouldn’t love that man; you’ll lose the love I bear you if you do. It would be the fine thing to wound, with me, the man who wounds me. Be king at my side, share half my honor. These men will carry the answer back. You stay here, sleep on a soft bed, and at first light we’ll decide whether to go home or stay.”

And he gave a quiet nod to Patroclus, with his brows, to spread a thick bed for Phoenix — a sign for the others to think about leaving. Then Ajax, the godlike son of Telamon, spoke: “Royal son of Laertes, resourceful Odysseus — let’s go. I don’t think we’ll reach the end of this errand on this road. We had better take the answer back, hard as it is, to the Greeks who sit there waiting for it. Achilles has turned the great heart in his chest savage and proud — a hard man, who cares nothing for the love of his comrades, the love we gave him at the ships beyond all others. Pitiless. Why, a man takes a blood-price even for a brother killed, even for his own dead son; the killer pays it and stays on among his people, and the other holds back his heart and his pride once he’s taken it. But in you the gods have set a hard, unyielding spirit, and all of it for one girl, a single one — when here we are offering you seven, the best there are, and a great deal more besides. Let your heart turn gentle. Respect your own roof. We are under it as messengers of the whole Greek host, and we would be the nearest and dearest to you of all the Greeks.”

Then Achilles, the swift runner, answered: “Ajax, royal son of Telamon, lord of the people — everything you’ve said is close to my own mind. But my heart swells with anger every time I think of it, the way the son of Atreus shamed me in front of the Greeks, as if I were some worthless drifter with no rights at all. So go, the two of you, and carry the message. I will give no thought to bloody war until the son of wise Priam, until Hector himself comes through, killing as he comes, to the huts and the ships of the Myrmidons, and sets them on fire. But at my own hut, by my own black ship, I think I will hold Hector, hungry for battle though he is.”

So he spoke, and each of them took up a double cup, poured the offering, and went back along the line of ships, Odysseus leading. Patroclus told his men and the serving women to make up a thick bed for Phoenix at once, and they laid it down — fleeces, a coverlet, and a sheet of fine linen — and there the old man lay and waited for the bright dawn. But Achilles slept in the inner room of the well-built hut, and beside him lay a woman he had carried off from Lesbos, Diomede, the lovely daughter of Phorbas. Patroclus lay across the room, and with him fair-belted Iphis, whom brilliant Achilles had given him when he took steep Scyros, the city of Enyeus.

When the envoys came back to the huts of the son of Atreus, the Greeks rose to meet them, lifting cups of gold all down the line, and pressed them with questions. Agamemnon, lord of men, asked first: “Tell me, Odysseus, glory of the Greeks — will he keep the consuming fire off the ships, or did he refuse, and is the anger still in his proud heart?”

And the long-suffering, brilliant Odysseus answered: “Most honored son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men — that man has no will to put out his anger. He is fuller of rage than ever, and he waves you off, you and your gifts. He tells you to take counsel with the Greeks yourself, how to save the ships and the army. As for himself, he threatens that at first light he will haul his ships down to the sea. And he said he’d advise the rest of us to sail home too, since we will never reach the end of Troy now. ‘Zeus the wide-thunderer holds his hand over her,’ he said, ‘and her people have taken heart.’ That is what he said; and these who came with me will tell you the same — Ajax, and the two heralds, both of them men of sense. Old Phoenix stayed behind to sleep there, for so Achilles wanted it, that he might sail home with him in the morning, if he is willing; but he will not take him by force.”

So he spoke, and they all fell silent, struck dumb by his words; for he had brought back a hard answer. For a long while the sons of the Greeks sat in silence, sick at heart. At last Diomedes, master of the war cry, spoke: “Most honored son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men — you should never have begged the great-hearted son of Peleus, nor offered him gifts beyond counting. He is proud enough on his own; now you have driven him deeper into his pride. But let him be — let him go or stay as he pleases. He will fight again, soon enough, when the heart in his chest stirs him and a god drives him to it. So now let us all do as I say. Lie down and sleep — you have had your fill of food and wine, and in those a man finds strength and heart. But when lovely rosy-fingered Dawn shines out, get the army and the horsemen drawn up before the ships, and rouse them on; and you yourself, fight in the front line.”

So he spoke, and all the kings approved his words, marveling at Diomedes the breaker of horses. Then they poured the offering and went, each man to his own hut, and lay down and took the gift of sleep.
