The Iliad, Book XVII

The Fight over Patroclus

Book XVII17 of 24

Menelaus, son of Atreus, dear to Ares, did not fail to see that Patroclus had gone down under the Trojans in the fighting. He came forward through the front ranks in his gleaming bronze and took his stand astride the body — the way a cow stands over her first calf, lowing low and tender, a young mother who has never known birth before. So yellow-haired Menelaus stood over Patroclus, holding his spear and his round shield out in front of him, set to kill any man who dared come near.

But the son of Panthous, Euphorbus of the good ash spear, had marked the body too. He came up to Menelaus and said: “Menelaus, son of Atreus, lord — pull back. Leave the body, leave the bloody spoils. I was first of the Trojans and their famous allies to drive my spear into Patroclus in the hard fighting. Let me win the glory I’m owed among the Trojans — or I’ll take aim and kill you.”

Yellow-haired Menelaus answered him in anger: “Father Zeus, there’s an ugly thing — a man who boasts. The leopard has no such pride, nor the lion, nor the wild boar, whose heart is fiercest of all — and yet the sons of Panthous in their high spirits outdo them all. But the strength of Hyperenor, breaker of horses, did him no good when he made light of me, when he stood and faced me and called me the worst of fighters among the Greeks. He never walked home on his own feet to gladden his wife and his honored parents. So I’ll break you too, if you stand against me. Get back into the crowd; don’t face me, before something worse happens. Even a fool learns once it’s done.”

Euphorbus would not listen. “Now, Menelaus,” he said, “now you’ll pay for my brother — the one you killed and then crowed over, the one whose wife you widowed in her new bridal chamber, leaving his parents in grief beyond all words. I’ll bring some comfort to those poor people if I carry your head and your armor and lay them in the hands of Panthous and lovely Phrontis. Enough delay — let’s fight it out and see which way it falls.”

With that he struck Menelaus full on the shield. But the bronze did not break through; the point bent back against the strong shield. Then Menelaus came on in his turn, praying to father Zeus, and as Euphorbus drew back he struck him at the base of the throat and threw his whole weight behind the spear, trusting his heavy hand. The point drove clean through the soft neck. He fell with a crash, and his armor rang around him. His hair, lovely as the hair of the Graces, his locks braided tight with gold and silver, were soaked now with blood. As a man raises a strong young olive shoot in a lonely place where the water runs clear — a fine thing, full of promise, swaying in the breath of every wind, breaking into white blossom — and then a sudden storm comes screaming out of nowhere and tears it from its trench and lays it flat on the earth: so Menelaus, son of Atreus, killed the son of Panthous, Euphorbus of the good ash spear, and began to strip his armor.

As a lion bred in the mountains, sure of his own strength, snatches the best cow out of a grazing herd — first he breaks her neck in his strong jaws, then tears her open and gulps the blood and the inward parts, while around him the dogs and the herdsmen raise a great noise but hang back, not one with the nerve to come close, for pale fear has them — so not one of the Trojans had the heart to go up against glorious Menelaus. And the son of Atreus would have carried off the splendid armor of the son of Panthous easily, if Phoebus Apollo had not begrudged it him. Apollo roused Hector against him, swift as Ares, taking the shape of Mentes, lord of the Cicones, and said: “Hector, you’re chasing what can’t be caught — the horses of the proud grandson of Aeacus. They’re hard for any mortal man to master or to drive, for any but Achilles, whose mother is a goddess. Meanwhile Menelaus, son of Atreus, stands astride Patroclus and has killed the best of the Trojans, Euphorbus son of Panthous — he’s stopped his fighting for good.”

Then the god went back into the toil and the killing, but a black cloud of grief came down over Hector’s heart. He looked along the ranks and saw the one man stripping the bright armor and the other lying on the ground, the blood still running from the wound. He went forward through the front, blazing in his bronze, with a sharp cry like a flame that nothing puts out. The son of Atreus heard it, and spoke to his own great heart in dismay: “What now? If I leave this splendid armor and Patroclus, who fell here fighting for my honor, then any Greek who sees it will cry shame on me. But if for very shame I take on Hector and the Trojans alone, they’ll surround me — one man against many, for Hector is bringing them all up. And yet — why is my heart even debating this? When a man fights against the god’s own will, against a man a god is backing, the ruin comes down on him fast. No Greek will blame me for giving way to Hector, since Hector fights with heaven behind him. If only I could find Ajax, good at the war cry — the two of us together might remember our fighting strength even against the god, and bring the body off for Achilles. That, of these bad choices, would be the least bad.”

While he turned it over in his mind, the Trojans came on with Hector at their head. Menelaus drew back and left the body — turning his head as he went, like a bearded lion that men and dogs are driving from a farmyard with spears and shouting, until his proud heart freezes inside him and he slinks off, sore against his will. So Menelaus, son of Atreus, gave up Patroclus. He reached his own men and turned to look for huge Ajax, son of Telamon, and found him quickly on the far left of the fighting, steadying his men and urging them on, for Phoebus Apollo had thrown a great panic into them. He ran to him and said: “Ajax — here, friend, this way, fast. We have to fight for dead Patroclus. We may at least bring the body back to Achilles, the bare body — Hector has the armor already.”

The words stirred the heart of warlike Ajax, and he strode out through the front ranks with Menelaus beside him. Hector, having stripped Patroclus of his glorious armor, was hauling him away to hack the head from the shoulders with his bronze and throw the trunk to the dogs of Troy. But Ajax came up close with his shield like a tower in front of him, and Hector fell back into the press of his own men and leaped onto his chariot. He handed the beautiful armor to the Trojans to carry into the city, a great glory for himself. And Ajax spread his broad shield over the son of Menoetius and stood astride him — like a lion over its cubs, when hunters meet it in the woods as it leads its young, and it draws itself up tall in its strength, dragging its brows down low till they curtain its eyes. So Ajax stood over the warrior Patroclus, and beside him stood Menelaus, son of Atreus, his heart swelling with grief.

Then Glaucus, son of Hippolochus, captain of the Lycians, gave Hector a black look and rebuked him hard: “Hector — fine to look at, but you fall short in a fight. That great name of yours is wasted on a man who runs away. Now think how you’ll save your city and your stronghold with only your own people, the ones born in Ilium — for not one Lycian will go out to fight the Greeks for your city, when there’s never been any thanks in it, fighting your enemies day in, day out, without rest. How are you going to save a lesser man in the crush, you ungrateful man, when you let Sarpedon — your own guest, your own comrade in arms — be left as spoil and prey to the Greeks? While he lived he did your city and you good service, and now you hadn’t the stomach even to keep the dogs off his body. So if the Lycians will hear me, we’re going home, and the steep ruin of Troy can come. If the Trojans had the daring, the fearless heart, that fills men who fight and labor for their own land, we’d have dragged Patroclus inside Ilium by now. If we could get this dead man into the great city of Priam, hauled out of the fighting, the Greeks would hand back Sarpedon’s armor at once, and we’d carry his body home as well. For the man whose squire was just killed is the finest fighter at the ships of the Greeks — he and his close-fighting men. But you — you hadn’t the heart to stand and face great-hearted Ajax, to look him in the eye in the battle’s roar, because he’s the better man.”

Hector of the flashing helm scowled at him and said: “Glaucus — that you, of all men, should talk so out of turn. I’d thought you the wisest head in Lycia, the whole of it; but now I think nothing of you, saying I won’t stand and face huge Ajax. I don’t shrink from the fight or the thunder of horses. It’s the mind of Zeus who wields the storm-shield that’s stronger always — Zeus, who makes even a brave man run and strips the victory from him, then turns and drives him on to fight. Come here, then, friend; stand by me and watch the whole day through, and see whether I’m the coward you call me, or whether I won’t bring more than one of these high-spirited Greeks to a stop, for all his eagerness, here over the body of dead Patroclus.”

So he spoke, and called aloud to the Trojans: “Trojans, Lycians, Dardanians who fight up close — be men, my friends, remember your wild fighting strength, while I put on the splendid armor of brilliant Achilles, the armor I stripped from Patroclus when I killed him.”

With that Hector of the flashing helm left the bitter fighting and ran full speed after his men, who were carrying the famous armor of the son of Peleus toward the city; they had not gone far. He caught them, and standing apart from the war and its tears he changed his armor. His own he gave to the warlike Trojans to carry to sacred Ilium, and he put on the immortal armor of Achilles, son of Peleus — the armor the gods of heaven had given to his father Peleus, and Peleus when he was old had given to his son. But the son did not grow old in his father’s armor.

Zeus, who herds the clouds, saw him from far off, arming himself in the armor of the godlike son of Peleus, and he shook his head and said to his own heart: “Poor man — death is nowhere in your thoughts, and it’s already standing close to you. You put on the immortal armor of the best of men, the man other men shake before. You’ve killed his comrade, gentle and strong, and stripped the armor wrongly from his head and shoulders. Well — for now I’ll grant you great power, in payment for this: that you will never come home out of the fighting, and Andromache will never take the splendid armor of the son of Peleus from your hands.” And the son of Cronus bowed his head, his dark brows, in confirmation.

Zeus fitted the armor to Hector’s body, and dread Ares entered into him, and his limbs filled with strength and fighting fury. He went in among the famous allies with a great shout, and to all of them he flashed in the armor of the great-hearted son of Peleus, until they took him for Achilles himself. Going from man to man he roused them — Mesthles and Glaucus, Medon and Thersilochus, Asteropaeus, Deisenor and Hippothous, Phorcys, Chromius, and Ennomus the bird-reader. To all of them he called out, his words flying: “Hear me, you countless tribes of allies from the lands around — it was not to swell a crowd that I called you here, each from your own city. I called you to guard the wives and little children of the Trojans, with all your heart, from the war-loving Greeks. That is why I wear out my own people with the food and the gifts that keep your spirit high. So turn now, straight at the enemy, and live or die — that is the game of war. Whoever drags Patroclus, dead as he is, into the hands of the horse-breaking Trojans, and makes Ajax give ground before him, I’ll give half the spoils and keep half myself; and his glory will be equal to my own.”

At his word they bore down with their full weight on the Greeks, leveling their spears, and the hope ran high in every man that he would haul the body out from under Ajax, son of Telamon. Fools — Ajax was about to take the life out of many of them over that body. Then Ajax spoke to Menelaus, good at the war cry: “My friend, Menelaus — I no longer think the two of us will come out of this fighting alive. It isn’t the body of Patroclus that I fear for most — he’ll soon be glutting the dogs and birds of Troy — it’s my own head, and yours, that I fear may go down. Hector is a thundercloud of war wrapped around everything, and sheer ruin is hanging over us. Call out to the best of the Greeks — see if any can hear.”

Menelaus, good at the war cry, did as he said and shouted across the Greeks in a great voice: “Friends — leaders and counselors of the Greeks, all you who drink the public wine beside Agamemnon and Menelaus, each of you commanding your own men, with honor and glory from Zeus — the fighting is so thick I can’t pick any one of you out by sight. So come unbidden, every man, and burn with shame that Patroclus should become a plaything for the dogs of Troy.”

Swift Ajax, son of Oileus, heard him clearly and was first to come, running up through the fighting; and after him Idomeneus and Idomeneus’s man Meriones, a match for the man-killing war-god. As for the rest who came after to wake the Greek attack — what man could call them all by name?

The Trojans came on in a body, Hector leading. As at the mouth of a river fed from heaven the great wave roars in against the current, and the headlands on either side bellow as the salt sea breaks and is flung back — with such a roar the Trojans came. But the Greeks stood firm around the son of Menoetius, of one mind, walled in behind their bronze shields. And the son of Cronus poured a thick mist over their shining helmets, for he had borne the son of Menoetius no grudge while he lived and was the squire of the grandson of Aeacus; he hated to see him made a feast for the enemy’s dogs, and he stirred his comrades to defend him.

At first the Trojans pushed the bright-eyed Greeks back, and they let the dead man go and gave ground; yet for all their spears the high-hearted Trojans killed none of them — they only began to drag the body off. But the Greeks would not leave it to them long. Ajax turned them fast — Ajax, who in beauty and in deeds outstripped all the other Greeks after the matchless son of Peleus. He drove through the front like a wild boar that scatters dogs and strong young men with one easy turn, wheeling at bay through the mountain glens. So the lordly son of Telamon charged in among the Trojan ranks and scattered those who had crowded over Patroclus, all of them set on dragging him to their city and winning the glory. There Hippothous, lordly son of Pelasgian Lethus, was dragging the body off by the foot through the brutal fighting, a strap looped around the tendon at the ankle, doing a favor for Hector and the Trojans — and the ruin came on him fast, and not one of them who longed to could keep it off. The son of Telamon sprang through the press and struck him hand-to-hand on the bronze-cheeked helmet. The crested helm split open around the spearhead, struck by the great spear and the heavy hand, and the brain ran out along the socket, mixed with blood. The strength went out of him; he let Patroclus’s foot drop from his hands and fell forward across the body, dead — far from the rich soil of Larissa, never to pay his dear parents back for raising him; his life was cut short under the spear of great-hearted Ajax.

Hector hurled his bright spear at Ajax, but Ajax saw it coming and just dodged it, and the spear struck Schedius, son of high-hearted Iphitus, the best of the Phocians, who lived in famous Panopeus and ruled many men. It hit him under the collarbone, and the bronze point ran straight through and out at the base of the shoulder. He fell with a crash, and his armor rang around him. Then Ajax struck warlike Phorcys, son of Phaenops, full in the belly as he stood astride Hippothous, and broke the plate of his corselet, and the bronze tore through his guts; he clutched the dust in his fist as he went down. Hector and the front-fighters gave ground, and the Greeks raised a great cry and hauled off the bodies of Phorcys and Hippothous and began stripping the armor from their shoulders.

And now the Trojans, beaten by the war-loving Greeks, would have been driven back up into Ilium, worn down by their own weakness, and the Greeks would have won glory even against the will of Zeus, by their own force and fighting heart — if Apollo himself had not roused Aeneas, taking the shape of the herald Periphas, son of Epytus, who had grown old serving Aeneas’s aged father, a man Aeneas loved. In that shape Apollo said: “Aeneas — how would you ever hold high Ilium if a god were against you? I’ve seen men do it on their own daring, their own strength, their own numbers, with Zeus against them. But Zeus wants the victory for us, far more than for the Greeks — and you, all of you, are afraid to fight.”

Aeneas looked him in the face and knew Apollo who strikes from afar, and called aloud to Hector: “Hector, and you other captains of the Trojans and the allies — shame on us, if we’re driven back up into Ilium, beaten by the war-loving Greeks through our own weakness. Just now a god stood at my side and told me that Zeus, the highest counselor, is fighting for us. Straight at the Greeks, then — don’t let them carry dead Patroclus easily to the ships.”

With that he sprang far out ahead of the front rank, and the rest rallied and wheeled to face the Greeks. There Aeneas speared Leiocritus, son of Arisbas, the brave companion of Lycomedes; and Lycomedes, watching him fall, felt the pity catch in him, and came up close and threw, and struck Apisaon, son of Hippasus, shepherd of his people, in the liver below the midriff, and dropped him on the spot — a man come from rich Paeonia, the best of them after Asteropaeus. Warlike Asteropaeus sprang to defend him and rushed at the Greeks — but it was no use, for they were fenced on every side with shields around Patroclus, their spears leveled out before them. Ajax had gone all along the line with hard orders: no man was to fall back from the body, and no man was to push forward ahead of the rest to fight; they were to hold close around it and fight hand to hand. So huge Ajax commanded, and the earth ran red with dark blood, and the dead fell thick — Trojans and proud allies on one side, Greeks on the other; for the Greeks did not fight a bloodless fight either, though far fewer of them fell, since they remembered always to ward off sheer death from one another in the press.

So they fought on, like a running fire, and you would not have thought the sun was still safe in the sky, or the moon — such a darkness lay over all that part of the field where the best men stood and fought around the dead son of Menoetius. Elsewhere the other Greeks and Trojans fought at their ease under a clear sky, the sharp sunlight all around them, not a cloud on the whole plain or on the mountains; and they would even pause and rest from the painful work, standing well apart, beyond the reach of one another’s spears. But the men in the middle suffered from the war and the dark together; the best of them were worn down under the cruel weight of the bronze. Two good men, though — Thrasymedes and Antilochus — had not yet heard that faultless Patroclus was dead. They still thought him alive and fighting the Trojans at the head of the press, and were holding back in reserve against the rout or death of their own comrades, watching from the flank, as Nestor had ordered them when he sent them out from the ships.

So all day long the great struggle of the bitter battle raged; and without rest the sweat ran down the knees and shins and feet, the hands and the eyes of the men who fought over the brave squire of swift-footed Achilles. As when a man gives his people a great bull’s hide, soaked through with fat, to stretch — and they take it and stand around it in a ring and pull, until the wet is drawn out of it and the grease soaks in, with so many hands hauling, and the whole hide is stretched out tight — so the two sides hauled the body this way and that in a little space, hearts high with hope: the Trojans set on dragging it into Ilium, the Greeks on getting it back to the hollow ships. And a savage fighting raged about it — Ares who whips men on could not have faulted it, nor Athena, not even in their full fury. Such was the harsh struggle of men and horses that Zeus strung tight that day over Patroclus.

And all this while brilliant Achilles knew nothing of Patroclus’s death. The fighting was far off, under the wall of Troy, a long way from the ships, and it never crossed his mind that Patroclus had fallen. He thought he would come back alive once he had reached the gates and turned away; for Achilles knew he was not fated to sack the city, not with Patroclus’s help and not without it. His mother had told him so, again and again, when he sat alone with her — telling him the mind of great Zeus. But this time she had not told him of the great evil that had come on him: that the dearest of all his comrades was dead.

Around the body the others kept driving in with their sharp spears, killing one another. And a man among the bronze-clad Greeks would say: “Friends — there’s no glory for us in going back to the hollow ships now. Better the black earth opened under all of us, here on the spot, than that we let the horse-breaking Trojans drag this man off to their city and win the glory.” And on their side a high-hearted Trojan would say: “Friends — even if it’s fated that every one of us die beside this man, let no one fall back from the fighting.” So they spoke, and stirred each other’s hearts. They fought, and the iron din rose up through the empty air to the bronze sky.

But the horses of the grandson of Aeacus stood apart from the fighting and wept, once they learned that their charioteer had fallen in the dust under man-killing Hector. Automedon, brave son of Diores, beat them hard, again and again, with the lash; he coaxed them with soft words, again and again he cursed them; but they would not go back to the ships at the wide Hellespont, and they would not go forward into the fighting. As a gravestone stands fixed over the mound of a dead man or a dead woman, so they stood, fixed, holding the beautiful chariot still, their heads bowed to the ground. Hot tears ran from their eyes and fell to the earth as they grieved, longing for their charioteer; and their thick manes spilled down, fouled with dust, on either side of the yoke-pad, drooping from under the yoke.

The son of Cronus saw them weeping and was moved with pity, and he shook his head and said to his own heart: “Poor things — why did we give you to lord Peleus, a mortal man, when you have no age and no death? Was it so you could grieve among unhappy men? For of all things that breathe and move upon the earth, there is nothing more wretched than a man. But Hector, son of Priam, will not ride behind you in your patterned chariot — that I will not allow. Isn’t it enough that he holds the armor, and brags of it so emptily? No — I’ll put strength into your knees and your hearts, to carry Automedon safely out of the fighting to the ships. For I mean to give the Trojans glory still, and let them go on killing until they reach the well-benched ships, until the sun goes down and the holy darkness comes on.”

With that he breathed great strength into the horses. They shook the dust from their manes and ran lightly, carrying the swift chariot into the fighting between the Trojans and the Greeks. Behind them Automedon fought, grieving for his comrade — swooping in his chariot like a vulture among geese, easily out of the Trojans’ way as he fled, easily charging back through the thick of the press. But for all his rush he killed no man, since he could not, alone in the sacred chariot, both drive at them with the spear and hold the swift horses. At last a comrade saw him — Alcimedon, son of Laerces, son of Haemon — and came up behind the chariot and called: “Automedon — what god has put this empty plan in your chest and stolen your good sense? You’re fighting the Trojans in the front rank, alone, with your comrade dead, and Hector glorying in the armor of Achilles on his own shoulders.”

Automedon, son of Diores, answered him: “Alcimedon — what other Greek is your equal at handling and holding these immortal horses, except only Patroclus, a match for the gods in counsel, while he lived? But now death and fate have him. Here — take the whip and the shining reins, and I’ll get down from the chariot and fight.” Alcimedon leaped onto the war-chariot and took up the whip and reins, and Automedon jumped down.

Hector saw it and at once said to Aeneas, who was close by: “Aeneas, counselor of the bronze-clad Trojans — I see the horses of swift-footed Achilles coming into the fighting with feeble drivers. I think the two of us could take them, if your heart is willing; they wouldn’t dare hold their ground and face us in a fight.” The brave son of Anchises agreed, and the two went straight on, their shoulders hidden under shields of tough oxhide overlaid with thick bronze. With them went Chromius and godlike Aretus, both together, and their hearts were high with the hope of killing the men and driving off the strong-necked horses. Fools — they were not to come back from Automedon without blood. He prayed to father Zeus, and his dark heart filled with strength and courage, and he said to Alcimedon, his trusted comrade: “Alcimedon, hold the horses close — keep them breathing right at my back. For I don’t think Hector, son of Priam, will check his fury until he’s killed the two of us and climbed behind the lovely-maned horses of Achilles, and scattered the ranks of the Greeks — or gone down himself among the front-fighters.”

With that he called to the two Ajaxes and to Menelaus: “Ajaxes, captains of the Greeks, and you, Menelaus — leave the dead man to the best men you have to stand over him and hold the ranks off, and come keep death from us, the living. For here, where the fighting breaks like a wave, Hector and Aeneas are bearing down on us — the two best of the Trojans. Well — these things lie in the lap of the gods. I’ll make my throw, and leave the rest to Zeus.” He balanced his long-shadowed spear and threw, and struck the round shield of Aretus. The shield did not hold it; the bronze drove on through, through the belt and into the lower belly. As a strong young man with a sharp axe strikes a field-ox behind the horns and cuts straight through the tendon, so that the beast leaps forward and drops — so Aretus leaped forward and fell on his back, and the keen spear stood quivering in his guts and unstrung him. Hector hurled his bright spear at Automedon, but he saw it coming and ducked low under it; the long spear flew over and stuck in the ground behind him, the butt-end shivering, until the war-god drained its force. They would have closed and fought with swords, but the two Ajaxes forced their way through the crowd at their comrade’s call and broke them apart — and Hector and Aeneas and godlike Chromius drew back in fear, leaving Aretus lying there with his heart cut through. And Automedon, a match for swift Ares, stripped the armor and cried over him: “There — I’ve eased my heart a little for the death of the son of Menoetius, though the man I killed was the lesser man.” With that he gathered up the bloody spoils and laid them in the chariot, and climbed up himself, his hands and feet running with blood, like a lion that has fed on a bull.

And once more over Patroclus the grim fight raged, heavy with tears, for Athena came down from heaven and woke it; far-seeing Zeus had sent her to put heart into the Greeks, for his mind had turned. As Zeus stretches a glowing rainbow out of the sky to mortals, a sign of war, or of a cold storm that stops men’s work in the fields and troubles the flocks — so Athena wrapped herself in a glowing cloud and went down among the Greek ranks and roused each man. First she came to the son of Atreus, to strong Menelaus, who was near her, and taking the shape and the tireless voice of Phoenix she said: “Menelaus — it will be your shame and your disgrace, if the dogs drag the brave comrade of glorious Achilles under the wall of Troy. So hold fast, and drive on all your people.”

Menelaus, good at the war cry, answered her: “Phoenix, old friend, father — if only Athena would give me strength and keep the storm of spears off me. Then I’d gladly stand over Patroclus and defend him; his death has gone deep into my heart. But Hector rages like a fire that nothing puts out, and never stops killing, for Zeus is granting him the glory now.” It pleased the goddess, gray-eyed Athena, that of all the gods he had named her first. She put strength into his shoulders and his knees, and into his chest she set the brashness of a fly — the fly a man keeps brushing off his skin, and back it comes to bite, so sweet to it is human blood. With that boldness she filled his dark heart, and he stood over Patroclus and let his bright spear fly. There was among the Trojans a man named Podes, son of Eetion, rich and brave; of all the Trojans Hector honored him most, for he was a dear companion at the feast. Yellow-haired Menelaus struck him on the belt as he turned to run, and drove the bronze clean through; he fell with a crash, and Menelaus, son of Atreus, dragged the body out from the Trojans into the ranks of his own people.

Then Apollo came and stood by Hector and stirred him on, in the shape of Phaenops, son of Asius, who lived in Abydos and was the dearest of all Hector’s guest-friends. In his shape Apollo who strikes from afar said: “Hector — what other Greek will fear you now, when you’ve shrunk back from Menelaus, who used to be a soft spearman? Yet he’s come and carried a dead man off from the Trojans single-handed, and killed your own true comrade, a brave man among the front-fighters — Podes, son of Eetion.” A black cloud of grief came down over Hector, and he went forward through the front-fighters, armed in his blazing bronze. And then the son of Cronus took up his bright tasseled storm-shield, and shrouded Ida in cloud, and lightened and thundered hard, and shook the shield — and gave the victory to the Trojans and routed the Greeks.

Peneleos the Boeotian was the first to break and run. He had been hit on the upper shoulder, a grazing wound to the surface of the bone, by the spear of Polydamas, who had come up close and thrown. Then Hector wounded Leitus, son of great-hearted Alectryon, on the hand at the wrist, and ended his fighting; Leitus shrank back, looking about him, for his heart no longer believed he could hold a spear and battle the Trojans. And as Hector went after Leitus, Idomeneus struck him on the corselet, on the chest beside the nipple — but the long spear snapped at the socket, and the Trojans gave a great shout. Then Hector threw at Idomeneus, son of Deucalion, as he stood on the chariot, and only just missed him; but the spear struck Coeranus, the companion and driver of Meriones, who had come with him from well-built Lyctus. Idomeneus had come on foot from the ships and would have handed the Trojans a great triumph, if Coeranus had not driven up fast and saved him — bringing him life and rescue, but losing his own to man-killing Hector. For Hector struck him below the ear, on the jaw, and the spearhead drove the teeth out and split the tongue in two; he tumbled from the chariot and let the reins spill to the ground. Meriones stooped and gathered them up in his own hands and said to Idomeneus: “Lay on with the whip now, till you reach the fast ships. You can see for yourself the day is no longer ours.” So Idomeneus lashed the lovely-maned horses back toward the hollow ships, for fear had fallen on his heart.

Great-hearted Ajax and Menelaus did not fail to see that Zeus had turned the tide of the fight to the Trojans. And huge Ajax, son of Telamon, was the first to speak: “Look — by now even a fool, a child, would know that father Zeus himself is backing the Trojans. Every weapon of theirs strikes home, whether a brave man throws it or a coward — Zeus speeds them all alike; and ours fall to the ground and do nothing. Come, then — let’s think how to do two things at once: bring off the body, and get home ourselves to gladden our friends, who must be grieving as they look this way, sure that nothing will hold off the murdering hands of Hector now, that he’ll fall on the black ships. If only there were a man to carry the word to the son of Peleus, fast — for I don’t think he’s so much as heard the bitter news, that his dearest comrade is dead. But I can’t see such a man anywhere among the Greeks: the men and their horses alike are wrapped in the dark. Father Zeus — lift this mist off the sons of the Greeks, make the sky clear, give us back our eyes; and if it’s your will to destroy us, then destroy us — but in the light.”

So he spoke, with the tears running, and the Father pitied him, and at once scattered the mist and pushed the darkness back. The sun blazed out, and the whole battle lay open. Then Ajax said to Menelaus, good at the war cry: “Look now, Menelaus, prince — if Antilochus, son of great-hearted Nestor, is still alive, get him to run to brilliant Achilles with the word that the dearest by far of all his comrades is dead.”

Menelaus, good at the war cry, did as he said, and turned to go — like a lion turning from a farmyard, worn out at last with baiting the men and the dogs, who keep watch the whole night through and will not let him take the fat of the herd; in his hunger for meat he charges in, but it comes to nothing, for thick spears fly to meet him from bold hands, and blazing torches, and he flinches from them for all his fury, and at dawn he slinks off, sore at heart. So Menelaus turned, sore against his will, from Patroclus — afraid the Greeks in a terrible rout might leave the body to the enemy. He gave hard orders to Meriones and the two Ajaxes: “Ajaxes — captains of the Greeks — and Meriones — now, every one of you, remember poor Patroclus, how gentle he was while he lived. He had a kind heart for all men. Now death and fate have caught him.” With that yellow-haired Menelaus went off, glancing round on every side like an eagle — the sharpest-eyed, they say, of all the birds under heaven; high as he is, the swift hare crouching under a leafy bush does not escape him; he stoops on it and snatches its life away. So your bright eyes, Menelaus, prince, ranged everywhere over the crowd of your many companions, to find if Nestor’s son were still alive. He saw him quickly, on the far left of the fighting, heartening his comrades and driving them on, and he came up close and said: “Antilochus — come here, prince, and hear bad news, the kind I wish had never been. You can see for yourself, I think, that the god is rolling disaster down on the Greeks and the victory to the Trojans. Patroclus has fallen, the best of the Greeks, and a great loss to us all. Run, fast, to the ships and tell Achilles, in the hope he can save the bare body for the ships — for the armor, Hector of the flashing helm has it already.”

Antilochus shuddered at the words. For a long time he could not speak; his eyes filled with tears and his strong voice was choked. But even so he did what Menelaus asked. He set off running, after handing his armor to a faultless comrade, Laodocus, who was wheeling his horses close by. So he ran from the field in tears, to carry the terrible news to Achilles, son of Peleus.

And you, Menelaus, prince, had no heart to defend the hard-pressed Pylians when Antilochus left them — and they missed him sorely. But Menelaus sent them godlike Thrasymedes, and himself went back again to the warrior Patroclus. He came running up to the two Ajaxes and said: “I’ve sent Antilochus off to the ships, to brilliant Achilles. But I don’t think he’ll come now, for all his terrible rage at Hector — he can’t fight the Trojans without armor. So let’s think for ourselves how to do the best we can — both to bring off the dead man, and to get clear of death and the war cry of the Trojans ourselves.” Great Ajax, son of Telamon, answered him: “Well said, glorious Menelaus. Quick, then — you and Meriones, stoop down and take the body up and carry it out of the fighting; the two of us behind you will hold off the Trojans and Hector — we two of one heart and one name, who have stood our ground side by side in the hard fighting many times before.”

So Menelaus and Meriones gathered the dead man up in their arms and lifted him high, with a great heave; and the Trojan host behind them raised a great cry the moment they saw the Greeks lifting the body, and rushed after them like hounds that fly at a wounded boar ahead of the young hunters — for a while they tear in as if to rip him apart, but every so often he wheels on them in his strength, and they scatter and bolt in all directions. So the Trojans pressed on after them in a body, stabbing with swords and double-edged spears; but every time the two Ajaxes turned and stood to face them, the color drained from the Trojans, and not a man dared spring forward and fight over the dead.

So with all their strength the two carried the body off the field toward the hollow ships. And behind them the battle stretched out savage as fire — fire that breaks out and races over a city until the houses fall in the great glare of it, and the rush of the wind makes it roar — such was the unending roar of men and horses that came after them as they bore Patroclus along. And as a team of mules puts out all its strength to drag a beam or a great ship’s timber down a rough mountain track, panting and pouring sweat — so Menelaus and Meriones strained and sweated, carrying the body. Behind them the two Ajaxes held the line — as a wooded ridge thrown across a plain holds back even a great river, turning aside the cruel current of strong streams and sending them out across the flat, and not one of them, for all its force, can break the ridge through. So the two Ajaxes held back the Trojans behind them. But the Trojans kept pressing on, two of them above all — Aeneas, son of Anchises, and glorious Hector. And as a cloud of starlings or jackdaws bursts into screaming flight when it sees a hawk coming on, the killer of small birds — so before Aeneas and Hector the young Greeks ran screaming, all their old fighting heart forgotten. Much fine armor of the fleeing Greeks fell about the ditch and on either side of it, and there was no rest from the fighting.
