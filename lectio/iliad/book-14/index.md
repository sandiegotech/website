# The Iliad, Book XIV

The Deception of Zeus

Nestor was sitting over his wine, but the cry of battle did not pass him by. He turned to the son of Asclepius and said: “Tell me, noble Machaon, what does all this mean? The shouting of men fighting by the ships keeps growing louder. Stay here, sit, and drink your wine, while lovely Hecamede heats a bath and washes the clotted blood off you. I’ll go up to the lookout and see for myself.”

He took up the gleaming bronze shield of his son Thrasymedes, who had gone off with his father’s, gripped a stout bronze-tipped spear, and stepped outside — and saw the ruin laid bare: the Greeks in headlong rout, their wall thrown down, the Trojans driving them on from behind. As when the open sea heaves with a heavy, silent swell, holding its breath for the shrieking winds that may come, but going neither this way nor that until one of them sweeps down from Zeus to settle it — so the old man stood torn, whether to make for the crowd of the Greeks or go straight to Agamemnon. In the end he judged it best to go to the son of Atreus. And all the while men were killing one another, the hard bronze ringing on their bodies as they thrust with swords and double-edged spears.

The wounded kings met him on the way — Diomedes, Odysseus, and Agamemnon, son of Atreus — coming up from their ships. Their ships were drawn up far from the fighting, on the shore itself, for they had been beached first, and the wall built behind the last of them. Wide as the beach was, it could not hold the whole fleet, and the army was cramped for room; so they had hauled the ships up in rows, one behind another, filling the long mouth of the bay between its two headlands. The kings came leaning on their spears to look out over the fight, sick at heart. The sight of old Nestor filled them with dread. Agamemnon, lord of men, called to him: “Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Greeks — why have you left the battle and come here? I’m afraid the words of fierce Hector are coming true. He boasted to the Trojans that he’d never go back to Troy until he had burned our ships and slaughtered us all. That’s what he said — and look, it’s happening. The rest of the Greeks must be nursing the same anger Achilles does, if they won’t fight for the ships.”

Nestor the horseman answered: “It’s true, all of it, and happening now — not even Zeus who thunders on high could turn it back. The wall is down, the wall we trusted to be an unbreakable shield for us and the fleet. The Trojans are fighting at the ships without rest or pause; look where you will, you can’t make out from which side the rout is coming — the men are dying in a confused mass, and the war cry climbs to heaven. Let’s think what to do, if thinking can still help. But I don’t advise us to go into the fight ourselves: a wounded man can’t fight.”

Agamemnon, lord of men, answered: “Nestor, if the Trojans are really fighting at the sterns of our ships, and neither the wall nor the trench has saved us — the trench the Greeks broke their backs to dig, sure it would be an unbreakable shield for us and the fleet — then it must be the will of Zeus that the Greeks die here without glory, far from Argos. I knew it when he was willing to defend us; I know it now, when he lifts the Trojans up like the blessed gods and ties our hands. So do as I say. Let’s drag the ships nearest the water down into the sea and ride them at anchor a little way out, until the holy night comes on — if even night will make the Trojans break off — and then we can launch the rest. There’s no shame in running from ruin, even by night. Better to run and be saved than be caught and killed.”

Odysseus glared at him and said: “Son of Atreus, what is this you’re saying? You should be giving orders to some other army, some lesser one — not ruling over us, men Zeus has set to wind the bitter skein of war from youth to old age, until we die to the last man. So this is how you’d walk away from Troy, the wide-streeted city we’ve suffered so much to take? Be quiet, before another Greek overhears the kind of thing no man with sense in his head, no king with this great an army under his command, should ever let past his lips. I have no respect left for your judgment, none, after what you’ve just said. With the battle raging, you want us to haul the ships into the sea — to hand the triumphant Trojans the one thing more they want, and drop the doom on our own heads? Once the men see the ships being launched, the Greeks won’t go on fighting; they’ll keep glancing back at them and slacken off. Your plan, commander, would destroy us.”

Agamemnon, lord of men, answered: “Odysseus, you’ve cut me to the heart with your rebuke, and it was deserved. No — I’m not ordering the Greeks to drag the ships down against their will. Let someone speak who has a better plan, young or old; I’d be glad to hear it.”

Then Diomedes, master of the war cry, spoke up: “The man is here — you need not look far — if you’ll listen and not take it ill that I’m the youngest of you. I too come of a noble father, Tydeus, who lies buried at Thebes. Portheus had three good sons, who lived in Pleuron and steep Calydon — Agrius, Melas, and the horseman Oeneus, my father’s father, the bravest of the three. Oeneus stayed in his own land, but my father wandered to Argos — so Zeus and the gods willed it. He married into the house of Adrastus and lived in plenty, with broad fields of wheat and orchards all around and many flocks, and no Greek could match him with a spear. You must have heard all this is true. So when I give good counsel, don’t despise it as if I were a coward or low-born. I say this: let’s go back to the fighting, wounded as we are — we must. But once there, let’s keep clear of the throw of the spears, out of range, so we take no fresh wounds on the old ones. We can still drive the others on — the men who have been standing back, indulging their grudges, holding off from the fight.”

They heard him gladly and did as he said, and went off, with Agamemnon, lord of men, leading the way.

Poseidon, the great Earth-Shaker, had kept no idle watch. He came to them in the likeness of an old man, took Agamemnon’s right hand in his own, and spoke winged words: “Son of Atreus, I’d guess Achilles is glad in his heart now, watching the Greeks routed and dying — there’s no feeling left in him, not a grain. May he rot for it, may the gods bring him low. But with you the blessed gods are not so bitterly angry yet. The day will come when the captains and counselors of the Trojans go raising dust across the wide plain, and you’ll see them flying from the ships and the tents, back toward the city.”

With that he gave a great cry and sped off across the plain. Loud as the shout of nine thousand men, ten thousand, joining battle in the thick of the fighting — such was the voice that broke from the lord Earth-Shaker’s chest, and he poured fresh strength into every Greek heart to wage the war without pause.

Hera of the golden throne stood on a peak of Olympus and looked down, and her heart leaped to see him — her own brother and brother by marriage — hard at work, ranging through the battle where men win glory. Then she turned her eyes to Zeus, where he sat on the highest crest of Ida of the many springs, and she hated the sight of him. And the ox-eyed lady Hera began to plot how she might cheat the mind of Zeus who wields the storm-shield. The best way, she decided, was this: to make herself beautiful and go down to Ida, in the hope that he would burn to lie with her — and that, while he did, she might pour a warm and trusting sleep over his eyes and his cunning mind.

So she went to her chamber, the room her dear son Hephaestus had built for her, with a secret bolt on the doors that no other god could open. She went in and closed the gleaming doors. First she cleansed every stain from her lovely body with ambrosia, then rubbed herself with rich oil, sweet, divine, scented for her alone — stir it but once in the bronze-floored house of Zeus, and the fragrance would breathe out across all heaven and earth. With this she anointed her soft skin, and combed out her hair, and with her own hands plaited the bright, shining braids — immortal, beautiful — that streamed from her undying head. She put on the wondrous robe that Athena had worked for her with all her art, sewn over with many a figure, and pinned it across her breast with brooches of gold; she belted herself with a girdle hung with a hundred tassels, fixed the earrings in her pierced ears — three drops to a cluster, glinting with grace — and over it all a fresh veil, fair as the new sun. On her shining feet she bound her lovely sandals. And when she had dressed herself in all her finery, she left the room and called Aphrodite aside, away from the other gods, and said to her: “Will you do something for me, dear child — or will you refuse, because I take the side of the Greeks while you stand with the Trojans?”

Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, answered: “Hera, honored goddess, daughter of mighty Cronus — say what you want. My heart bids me do it, if I can, and if it can be done at all.”

And Hera, scheming, told her a lie: “Give me the love and the longing with which you yourself bring the whole world to its knees, gods and mortal men alike. I am going to the ends of the earth, to visit Ocean, the source of all the gods, and mother Tethys — they took me from Rhea and raised me in their own house, when Zeus thrust Cronus down beneath the earth and the barren sea. I must go and see them and end their quarrel. They’ve been at odds so long that they’ve held back from each other’s bed in their anger. If I can win them round with words and bring them back into each other’s arms, they’ll love me and call me dear forever.”

Aphrodite, who loves laughter, said: “I cannot refuse you, and I should not — you sleep in the arms of Zeus, the greatest of us all.”

And she loosed from her breast the embroidered band, the band into which all her powers had been worked: in it was love, in it was longing, in it was the soft, coaxing talk of lovers — the whispered word that steals the wits even of the wise. She put it into Hera’s hands and said: “Here — take this band and lay it in the fold of your robe. Everything is wrought into it, and I promise you won’t come back with your errand undone, whatever your heart is set on.”

At that the ox-eyed lady Hera smiled, and still smiling she laid the band in the fold of her robe.

Aphrodite went back into the house of Zeus, and Hera darted down from the peak of Olympus. She passed over Pieria and lovely Emathia, on over the snowy mountains of the Thracian horsemen, skimming the highest crests, her feet never touching the ground. From Athos she crossed the swelling sea and came to Lemnos, the city of godlike Thoas. There she found Sleep, the brother of Death, and caught him by the hand, and called him by name: “Sleep, lord over all gods and all men — if you ever heard a prayer of mine before, do this for me now, and I’ll be grateful all my days. Lull the bright eyes of Zeus to sleep under his brows the moment I lie down in his arms. And I’ll give you a beautiful throne for it, gold, that will never break — my own son, the lame smith Hephaestus, will make it and fit it with a footstool, where you can rest your shining feet when you sit at the feast.”

Sleep answered her: “Hera, honored goddess, daughter of mighty Cronus — any other of the everlasting gods I would gladly put to sleep, even the streams of Ocean, the source of them all. But Zeus, son of Cronus, I will not go near; I will not lull him, unless he tells me to himself. A command of yours taught me that once before — the day his great-hearted son Heracles sailed from Troy, after he had sacked the city. That day I poured myself sweet and soft over the mind of Zeus who wields the storm-shield and laid him to rest, while you plotted ruin against Heracles and stirred the angry winds across the sea, and drove him off to Cos, far from all his friends. Zeus woke in a fury and flung the gods about his house, hunting for me above all — he’d have hurled me out of the sky into the sea, lost forever, if Night had not saved me, Night who masters gods and men alike. I ran to her, and Zeus, angry as he was, held back, for he would not do anything to offend swift Night. And now you’re asking me for something else I dare not do.”

The ox-eyed lady Hera said: “Sleep, why take such fears to heart? Do you think Zeus will defend the Trojans the way he raged for his own son Heracles? Come — I’ll give you one of the younger Graces to marry, to be your own. Pasithea — the one you’ve longed for all your days.”

Sleep was glad to hear it, and answered: “Then swear it to me — by the dread water of the Styx. Lay one hand on the nourishing earth, the other on the shining sea, so that all the gods below with Cronus may be our witnesses, that you truly will give me one of the younger Graces — Pasithea, whom I have longed for all my days.”

Hera, white-armed goddess, did as he asked. She swore it, and named to witness all the gods below, the Titans, as they are called. When she had sworn the oath in full, the two of them wrapped themselves in mist and went lightly on their way, leaving Lemnos and Imbros behind. They came to Ida of the many springs, mother of wild beasts, to Lecton, where they left the sea and went on by land, and the tops of the forest trees stirred and whispered under their feet. There Sleep halted, before the eyes of Zeus could find him, and climbed a tall pine — the highest that had grown on Ida, reaching up through the air to heaven. He hid himself in the thick branches, in the likeness of the shrill mountain bird the gods call chalcis and men call cymindis.

Hera went on to Gargarus, the topmost peak of Ida, and Zeus who herds the clouds saw her. And the moment he saw her, desire wrapped close around his cunning heart — the same as the first time they lay together and crept into each other’s bed, with their dear parents knowing nothing of it. He stood up before her and spoke: “Hera, what brings you down here from Olympus? And with no chariot, no horses to carry you?”

And Hera, scheming, told him a lie: “I am going to the ends of the earth, to visit Ocean, the source of all the gods, and mother Tethys, who took me in and raised me in their house. I must go and see them and end their quarrel. They’ve been at odds so long they hold back from each other’s bed in their anger. My horses are waiting at the foot of Ida of the many springs, to carry me over land and sea. I’ve come here to you from Olympus on purpose — I didn’t want you angry with me afterward, if I slipped away to the house of deep-flowing Ocean without a word.”

Zeus who herds the clouds answered: “Hera, you can go there another time. Come — let us lie down and take our joy in love. Never has desire for goddess or for woman so flooded me and overmastered the heart in my breast — not for the wife of Ixion, who bore me Pirithous, a match for the gods in counsel; not for Danae of the slim ankles, daughter of Acrisius, who bore me Perseus, most renowned of men; not for the far-famed daughter of Phoenix, who bore me Minos and godlike Rhadamanthus; not for Semele, nor for Alcmene in Thebes, who bore my lion-hearted son Heracles, while Semele bore Dionysus, the joy of mortals; not for lady Demeter of the lovely hair, nor for glorious Leto — nor even for you, as I desire you now and sweet longing takes hold of me.”

And Hera, scheming, answered him again: “Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing to say. Lie down with you here, and take our joy on the heights of Ida, where everything is open to view? What if one of the everlasting gods caught sight of us asleep together and went off and told the rest? I could never rise from this bed and go back into your house again — the shame of it. But if this is your wish, if your heart is set on it, there is a chamber my dear son Hephaestus built for me, with strong doors fitted to their posts. Let us go there and lie down, since the bed is what you long for.”

Zeus who herds the clouds answered: “Hera, have no fear that god or man will see. I will hide us in a cloud of gold, so thick that not even Helios could see through it — and his light is the keenest thing there is for looking.”

With that the son of Cronus caught his wife in his arms. And beneath them the holy earth sent up fresh new grass — dewy clover and crocus and hyacinth, soft and thick, that lifted them clear of the ground. There they lay, and a beautiful cloud of gold closed over them, and from it fell glittering drops of dew.

So the father of all slept in peace on the crest of Ida, mastered at once by love and by sleep, his wife in his arms. And Sleep ran lightly to the ships of the Greeks, to bring his word to Poseidon, the Earth-Shaker. He came up close and spoke winged words: “Now, Poseidon, help the Greeks with all your heart, and give them the glory — even if only for a little while, while Zeus is still asleep. I’ve wrapped him in a soft slumber; Hera beguiled him into her bed.”

With that Sleep was gone, off among the tribes of men, and he left Poseidon more eager than ever to help the Greeks. At once the god sprang to the front ranks and shouted: “Greeks — are we going to hand the victory to Hector, son of Priam, and let him take our ships and cover himself with glory? That’s what he says he’ll do, now that Achilles sits sulking by the hollow ships. But we won’t miss him much, if the rest of us spur one another on and stand fast together. Now do as I say. The best, biggest shields in the whole army — let us take them up, put the bright helmets on our heads, grip the longest spears we have, and go. I’ll lead the way, and I tell you, Hector, son of Priam, for all his fury, will not stand against us. Any tough soldier holding a small shield — let him hand it to a lesser man and take a bigger one for himself.”

They heard him and obeyed. The kings themselves marshaled the lines, wounded as they were — Diomedes, Odysseus, and Agamemnon, son of Atreus — going through the ranks and ordering the exchange of armor, the best fighters into the best gear, the worse to the worse. When they had clad themselves in gleaming bronze they marched out, Poseidon, the Earth-Shaker, at their head, a long terrible sword in his strong hand, edged keen and flashing like lightning. No man may close with it in the grim work of battle; dread holds them all back.

And on the other side glorious Hector marshaled the Trojans. Then Poseidon, dark-haired god, and Hector strained the cord of war to its breaking point, the one bracing the Trojans, the other the Greeks. The sea came surging up to the tents and the ships, and the two armies clashed with a roar. Not so loud does the wave thunder on the shore, driven in from the deep by the cruel breath of the North Wind; not so loud does a forest fire roar when it leaps up the mountain glens to burn the woods; not so loud does the gale scream through the high crests of the oaks when it blows its hardest — as the great cry of the Trojans and the Greeks, terrible to hear, as they hurled themselves on one another.

Glorious Hector cast his spear first at Ajax, who was turned straight toward him, and did not miss — but it struck where the two straps crossed his chest, the baldric of his shield and the strap of his silver-studded sword, and they saved his flesh. Hector drew back among his men, raging that the throw had gone for nothing. As he went, Ajax, son of Telamon, caught up one of the many stones that lay rolling under the fighters’ feet — props for the beached ships — and hurled it. It caught Hector over the rim of his shield, high on the chest near the throat, and sent him spinning like a top, reeling round and round. As an oak goes down headlong, torn up by the lightning-stroke of father Zeus, and a terrible reek of brimstone rises from it, and the man who stands near loses his nerve, for the bolt of great Zeus is a fearful thing — so all at once mighty Hector dropped in the dust. The spear fell from his hand; his shield and helmet went down with him; and the bronze armor clashed about his body.

The sons of the Greeks ran at him with a great shout, hoping to drag him off, and showered their spears — but no one could reach him with a thrust or a throw, for the bravest were already around him: Polydamas and Aeneas and Agenor, Sarpedon, lord of the Lycians, and noble Glaucus. None of the rest forgot him either; they held their round shields over him. His comrades lifted him in their arms and carried him out of the fighting, to where his swift horses stood waiting at the rear with their charioteer and the painted chariot. They bore him toward the city, groaning, in deep pain. When they reached the ford of the fair-flowing Xanthus, the eddying river that immortal Zeus begot, they took him down from the chariot and laid him on the ground and poured water over him. He drew breath again and opened his eyes; he came up on his knees and vomited dark blood — then fell back, and darkness closed over his eyes again, for the stone had stunned him still.

When the Greeks saw Hector carried from the field, they pressed the Trojans harder and threw themselves into the fight with fresh fury. First of all, swift Ajax, son of Oileus, sprang on Satnius and wounded him with his spear — Satnius, whom a flawless naiad nymph had borne to Enops as he herded his cattle by the banks of the Satnioeis. The son of Oileus came up and struck him in the side, and he fell, and a hard fight broke out over his body, Trojans and Greeks together. Polydamas, son of Panthous, came to avenge him, brandishing his spear, and struck Prothoenor, son of Areilycus, on the right shoulder; the heavy spear drove clean through, and he went down clutching the earth in his fist. Polydamas shouted over him in triumph: “This time too, I’d say, the spear has not flown for nothing from the strong hand of the proud son of Panthous. A Greek has taken it in his body — it’ll serve him for a staff as he goes down into the house of Hades.”

The Greeks were stung to fury by his boast, and Ajax, son of Telamon, most of all — the man had fallen right beside him. As Polydamas drew back, Ajax let fly at him; Polydamas swerved aside and escaped, but the spear caught Archelochus, son of Antenor — the gods had marked him for death. It struck where the head joins the neck, at the topmost vertebra, and cut through both sinews, so that his head and mouth and nostrils hit the ground before his shins and his knees as he fell. And Ajax shouted to Polydamas: “Think, Polydamas — tell me the truth: was this man not worth killing to pay for Prothoenor? He doesn’t look low-born or of low stock — a brother of Antenor the horseman, maybe, or a son; the family likeness is strong.”

He knew well who it was, and grief seized the Trojans. Then Acamas, standing astride his brother’s body, drove his spear into Promachus the Boeotian, who was trying to drag the corpse away by the feet. Acamas shouted over him in triumph: “You Greeks — archers, big talkers, never sated with threats — it won’t be only we who toil and suffer; you too will die, sooner or later, just like this. Look how your Promachus sleeps now, brought down by my spear. The blood-price for my brother didn’t wait long. That’s why a man prays to leave a kinsman behind in his house, to avenge his fall.”

His boast cut the Greeks to the quick, and Peneleos most of all. He charged Acamas, who would not stand to meet him, and instead Peneleos struck Ilioneus, the only son of Phorbas, rich in flocks, whom Hermes had loved above all the Trojans and given great wealth. He struck him in the eye, beneath the brow, and the spearhead drove the eyeball out and on through the socket into the nape of the neck, and Ilioneus sank down, both arms thrown wide. Then Peneleos drew his sharp sword and hewed through the neck, and the head with its helmet dropped to the ground, the heavy spear still fixed in the eye. He held it up like the head of a poppy and showed it to the Trojans, crying in triumph: “Trojans — go tell the father and mother of proud Ilioneus to weep for him in their halls; for the wife of Promachus, son of Alegenor, will never gladden her eyes with her husband’s coming, when we Greeks sail home from Troy.”

Fear ran cold through them at his words, and every man looked around for some way out of sheer destruction. Tell me now, Muses who hold the homes of Olympus — who was the first of the Greeks to carry off bloody spoils, once the Earth-Shaker had turned the tide of battle? Ajax, son of Telamon, was first; he wounded Hyrtius, son of Gyrtius, captain of the stout-hearted Mysians. Antilochus killed Phalces and Mermerus; Meriones brought down Morys and Hippotion; Teucer killed Prothoon and Periphetes. And the son of Atreus struck Hyperenor, shepherd of his people, in the side, and the bronze tore through his bowels and ripped them out, and his life rushed from the wound, and darkness closed over his eyes. But Ajax, swift son of Oileus, killed the most by far — no man was as fast as he to run down a fleeing enemy, once Zeus had loosed the panic among them.
