The Iliad, Book IV

The Broken Truce

Book IV4 of 24

Now the gods sat with Zeus in council on the golden floor of heaven, and Hebe went among them pouring out nectar, and they pledged one another from their golden cups and looked down on the city of Troy. And the son of Cronus set out to needle Hera, talking past her to provoke her: “Menelaus has two goddesses to back him — Hera of Argos and Athena of Alalcomenae — but the two of them only sit here and watch the show, while laughter-loving Aphrodite never leaves Paris’s side and turns aside every danger from him. Look — she has just saved him when he thought he was a dead man. And yet the victory belongs to Menelaus. So we have to decide what to do. Shall we set them at each other’s throats again, or make peace between the two sides? If you’d all agree to that last, the city of Priam could go on standing, and Menelaus could take Helen home.”

So he spoke, and Athena and Hera muttered under their breath, sitting close together, plotting trouble for the Trojans. Athena said nothing — she scowled at her father, a wild fury working in her — but Hera could not hold it in. “Dread son of Cronus,” she said, “what kind of talk is that? How can you want to make all my work come to nothing — the sweat I sweated, the horses I wore out, mustering the army against Priam and his sons? Do as you please. But the rest of us gods will not all of us approve.”

Deeply angered, Zeus who gathers the clouds answered: “You strange, impossible woman — what have Priam and Priam’s sons ever done to you, that you rage on and on to sack the well-built city of Troy? Would nothing satisfy you but to break through the gates and the great walls and eat Priam raw, and his sons, and all the Trojans besides? Then have it your way. Only don’t let this quarrel grow into a lasting bitterness between us. And mark what I tell you now, and take it to heart: when the day comes that I want to destroy a city — and it will be one of yours, full of men you love — don’t try to hold back my anger. Let me have my way, as I am letting you have yours, against my own will. For of all the cities men live in under the sun and the starry sky, none has stood dearer to my heart than holy Troy, and Priam, and the people of Priam who fight with the good ash spear. My altar there never went without its fair share of the feast, the wine poured out and the savor of burning fat — the honors that are our due.”

Ox-eyed lady Hera answered him: “There are three cities I love best of all — Argos, and Sparta, and Mycenae of the wide streets. Raze them whenever they come to anger you. I will not stand in front of them; I will not grudge it. And even if I did grudge it, and tried to stop you, I would gain nothing, since you are far the stronger. But you must not let my labor go for nothing. I too am a god, and I come from the same stock as you: crooked-minded Cronus fathered me, his eldest daughter, and I am honored twice over — by my birth, and because I am called your wife, and you rule over all the immortals. So let us give way to each other in this, you to me and I to you, and the rest of the gods will follow our lead. Now order Athena down to the fighting at once, and let her work it so that the Trojans are the first to break their sworn oath and strike at the proud Greeks.”

The father of gods and men did not refuse her. At once he spoke winged words to Athena: “Go quickly, down into the armies, among the Trojans and the Greeks, and work it so that the Trojans are the first to break their oath and strike at the proud Greeks.”

It was the very thing Athena was burning to do, and down she shot from the peaks of Olympus. She came like a star that the son of crooked-minded Cronus sends as a sign to sailors, or to some great army of men, a blazing thing that throws off sparks as it goes — and in that shape Athena flashed to earth, dropping between the two armies. Awe took the men who saw it, Trojans and bronze-clad Greeks alike, and a man would turn to the one beside him and say: “This means hard war and the din of battle again — or else Zeus, who deals out war to men, is about to make peace between the two sides.”

So the men were saying to one another. Then Athena went into the Trojan ranks in the likeness of Laodocus, son of Antenor, a strong spearman, hunting for godlike Pandarus. She found him standing among the stout ranks of shield-bearers who had followed him from the banks of the Aesepus. She came up close and spoke winged words: “Will you do as I say, brave son of Lycaon? Then you would dare to send a fast arrow at Menelaus — and win the thanks and the glory of all the Trojans, and most of all of prince Paris. He, before any of them, would load you with shining gifts, if he saw Menelaus, the warlike son of Atreus, laid out on the funeral pyre, brought down by an arrow from your hand. Come — let fly at Menelaus, and make a vow to Apollo, the wolf-god, the lord of the bow, that you will offer him a fine hecatomb of firstling lambs when you come home to your holy city of Zeleia.”

So Athena spoke, and won over the fool’s heart in him. At once he uncovered his polished bow — made from the horns of a wild ibex he had shot one day as it sprang from a rock; he had been lying in wait, and the arrow caught it under the breast and it dropped backward off the cliff. Its horns had grown sixteen palms long, and a craftsman in horn had worked them, fitted them together, smoothed them down, and capped them with gold. Pandarus bent the bow and braced it carefully against the ground, and his brave men held their shields up in front of him, so the warlike Greeks would not rush him before he had hit Menelaus. He lifted the lid of his quiver and took out an arrow, never shot, fledged with the agonies of death. Quickly he set the bitter shaft to the string and made his vow to Apollo, the wolf-god, the lord of the bow — that he would offer a fine hecatomb of firstling lambs when he came home to his holy city of Zeleia. He notched the arrow and drew the ox-gut string back to his chest, the iron point to the bow; and when he had bent the great bow into a circle, it sang, and the string cried out, and the sharp arrow leapt away, hungry to fly through the crowd.

But the blessed, undying gods did not forget you then, Menelaus. First of all, the daughter of Zeus who drives the spoils of war stood in front of you and turned the piercing arrow aside. She brushed it from your skin the way a mother brushes a fly from her child while he lies in sweet sleep, and she steered it down to where the golden clasps of your belt held it tight, where the corselet was doubled over on itself. The bitter arrow struck the fitted belt and drove straight through it, and on through the finely worked corselet, and through the guard of mail he wore underneath next to his skin to keep off spears and arrows — the thing that saved him most of all, and yet the arrow tore through even that, and grazed the surface of the skin, and at once the dark blood came running from the wound.

As when a woman of Maeonia or Caria stains ivory with crimson dye, to make a cheek-piece for a horse’s bridle — it lies stored in a treasure-room, and many a horseman longs to carry it, but it is kept aside as a king’s treasure, a glory to the horse and a pride to the man who drives him — so, Menelaus, your shapely thighs were stained with blood, and your legs, and your fine ankles below.

When Agamemnon, lord of men, saw the dark blood running from the wound, a shudder went through him; and warlike Menelaus shuddered too — until he saw that the barbs of the point and the binding-thread still stood clear of the flesh, and then the heart came back into his chest. But Agamemnon, holding his brother by the hand, groaned from the depths of him, and the men around them groaned along with him. “Dear brother,” he said, “so it was your death I sealed when I swore that truce — when I sent you out alone, in front of the Greeks, to fight for us. The Trojans have trampled the oath; they have shot you down. But the oath is not dead — not the blood of the lambs, not the unmixed wine we poured, not the right hands we clasped and trusted in. If the Olympian does not bring it home now, he will bring it home later, and they will pay in full — with their own lives, with their wives, with their children. For this I know in my heart and my mind: the day is coming when holy Troy will fall, and Priam, and all the people of Priam who fight with the good ash spear; when the son of Cronus, throned high in the bright air, will shake his dark storm-shield over the whole city in his anger at this treachery. All this will come to pass. But the grief will be mine, Menelaus, beyond bearing, if you die now and fill out the measure of your life. I would go back to thirsty Argos in disgrace — for the Greeks will turn their minds at once to the land of their fathers. We would leave Argive Helen for Priam and the Trojans to gloat over, and your bones would rot in the ground here at Troy, your work undone. And some swaggering Trojan would jump on the grave of glorious Menelaus and say: ‘May Agamemnon’s anger always end like this — the way he brought his Greek army here for nothing, and now has sailed home to his own country with empty ships, and left brave Menelaus behind.’ That is what one of them will say. On that day, let the wide earth open and swallow me.”

But fair-haired Menelaus reassured him: “Take heart, and don’t frighten the men. The point hasn’t fixed in a fatal spot. The bright belt turned it first, and under that the kilt of mail and the guard the bronze-smiths beat out for me.”

And Agamemnon, lord of men, answered: “I pray it’s so, dear Menelaus. But let a healer look at the wound and lay drugs on it to ease the dark pain.”

He turned to the herald Talthybius: “Talthybius — go, quick as you can, and call Machaon, the son of Asclepius the great healer, to come and see warlike Menelaus. Some Trojan or Lycian, a man skilled with the bow, has shot him — a glory for the archer, a grief for us.”

The herald heard and obeyed. He went through the ranks of the bronze-clad Greeks, searching for the warrior Machaon, and found him standing among the strong lines of shield-men who had followed him from Tricca, where they pasture horses. He came up beside him and spoke winged words: “On your feet, son of Asclepius — Agamemnon, lord of men, is calling for you, to come and see warlike Menelaus. Some Trojan or Lycian, a man skilled with the bow, has shot him — a glory for the archer, a grief for us.”

So he spoke, and stirred the heart in Machaon’s chest. They went back through the wide army of the Greeks, and came to where fair-haired Menelaus had been hit, and the captains were gathered in a ring around him. Machaon stepped into the middle of them, and at once drew the arrow out of the fitted belt; and as he pulled, the sharp barbs bent back. He undid the bright belt, and under it the kilt of mail and the guard the bronze-smiths had made; and when he had found the wound where the bitter arrow had struck, he sucked the blood from it and spread on it, with sure skill, the soothing drugs that Chiron had once given his father out of friendship.

While they were busy over Menelaus, who roars in battle, the lines of the Trojans, shields up, came on against them, and the Greeks took up their armor again, with a will for war.

And now you would not have seen Agamemnon dozing, or hanging back, or shy of the fight — no, he was straining for the battle where men win glory. He left his horses and his chariot bright with bronze, and his man Eurymedon, son of Ptolemaeus, son of Peiraeus, held the snorting team off to one side, with orders to keep them close at hand for whenever his legs grew weary of ranging up and down and giving commands; for he went among the lines on foot. Wherever he saw the men keen and quick, he stood beside them and spurred them on: “Greeks — don’t slacken your fury for a moment. Father Zeus will be no friend to liars. The men who were first to break the oath and strike us — the vultures will eat their soft flesh, and we’ll carry off their dear wives and their little children in our ships, on the day we take their city.”

But wherever he saw men shrinking from the hateful war, he tore into them in anger: “You Greeks — you boasters, you disgraces — have you no shame? Why do you stand here stunned, like fawns that run themselves out across a wide plain and then stop, helpless, with no fight left in them? That’s how you stand here, dazed, with no will to fight. Are you waiting for the Trojans to reach the sterns of our fine ships, drawn up there on the gray shore — waiting to see whether the son of Cronus will stretch his hand over you and save you?”

So he ranged through the ranks, taking command. He came through the press to the Cretans, arming around fiery Idomeneus, who stood at their head, brave as a wild boar, while Meriones was driving up the rear companies. Agamemnon, lord of men, was glad to see them, and spoke warmly to Idomeneus: “Idomeneus — I honor you above all the fast-horsed Greeks, in war and in everything else, and at the table too. When the other captains mix the bright wine and each is given his measured share, your cup stands full like my own, to drink whenever your heart moves you. So now — into the fight, and show yourself the man you have always been proud to be.”

Idomeneus, lord of the Cretans, answered: “Son of Atreus — I will be the trusty friend I promised to be from the first. Go now, rouse the rest of the long-haired Greeks, and let us join battle at once, since the Trojans have trampled their oath. Death and ruin are theirs in the days to come, for being the first to break faith and strike us.”

So he spoke, and the son of Atreus passed on, glad at heart. He came through the crowd to the two named Ajax, arming themselves with a thick mass of foot-soldiers around them. As when a goatherd from a lookout above the sea watches a storm come on across the deep before the West Wind — out on the water it stands black as pitch and bears down on him, a great whirlwind, and he is afraid and drives his flock into a cave — so under the two Ajaxes the close ranks of the strong young men moved into battle, a dark mass bristling with shields and spears. Agamemnon, lord of men, was glad to see them, and his words flew out: “You two — captains of the bronze-clad Greeks — I’ll give you no orders; that would be wrong, for of your own selves you drive your men to fight with all their strength. Father Zeus, Athena, Apollo — if only every man in the army had a heart like yours! Then the city of lord Priam would soon bow under our hands, taken and sacked.”

With that he left them and went on to Nestor, the clear speaker of Pylos, who was marshaling his men and urging them to the fight — and around him were Pelagon and Alastor, Chromius, Haemon, and Bias, shepherd of the people. He set the horsemen with their chariots and teams in the front rank, and behind them the foot-soldiers, brave men and many, the wall of the war; and the cowards he drove into the middle, so that a man would have to fight whether he wanted to or not. He gave his orders to the horsemen first, telling them to hold their teams hard in hand and not let the lines fall into confusion: “Let no man, trusting his horsemanship or his strength, charge out ahead of the rest to take on the Trojans alone — and let no man fall back; that only weakens us. When a man comes within reach of an enemy chariot from his own, let him stab with his spear — that is far the best way. This is how the men of old took walls and cities: this resolve, this spirit was in their chests.”

So the old man urged them on, schooled in war from long ago, and Agamemnon, lord of men, was glad to see it. His words flew out to him: “Old man — I wish the strength in your limbs matched the spirit in your chest, and your body were as sure as your judgment. But age, the common burden of all men, has come down on you. I wish it had fallen on some other man, and that you were still young.”

And Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, answered: “Son of Atreus — I too would gladly be the man I was the day I cut down great Ereuthalion. But the gods do not give a man everything at once. I was young then; now old age is on me. Even so, I’ll go among the horsemen and guide them with counsel and with words — for that is the privilege left to the old. The young men will throw the spears, the men who came after me, who can still trust their strength.”

So he spoke, and the son of Atreus passed on, glad at heart. He found Menestheus, son of Peteos, the driver of horses, standing idle, and around him the Athenians, who raise the war cry in battle. And nearby stood resourceful Odysseus, with the ranks of his strong Cephallenians around him. They were standing still, for the war cry had not yet reached them — the lines of Trojans and Greeks had only just begun to stir — and so they waited, watching for some other Greek column to go forward against the Trojans and open the fight. When Agamemnon, lord of men, saw this, he rounded on them: “Son of Peteos, king bred up by Zeus — and you, master of evil cunning, your mind set on your own gain — why do the two of you hang back, cowering, waiting on the rest? It would suit you two to stand among the very first and meet the scorching fight. You’re the first to hear when I call you to the feast, when we elders of the Greeks lay out a banquet. Glad enough then to eat your fill of roast meat and drink the honeyed wine as long as you please. But now you’d be glad to watch ten Greek columns go in ahead of you with the pitiless bronze.”

Resourceful Odysseus glared at him and answered: “Son of Atreus — what kind of word has got past your teeth? How can you say I hold back from the fight, when we Greeks wake the keen edge of war against the Trojans? You’ll see — if you care to, and if it’s any concern of yours — the dear father of Telemachus closing with the front ranks of the horse-taming Trojans. This is empty talk you’re making.”

When Agamemnon, lord of men, saw that he was angry, he smiled, and took his words back: “Royal son of Laertes, resourceful Odysseus — I have no blame to lay on you, no orders to give you. I know the heart in your chest is set on what is right; you and I are of one mind. Come, we’ll make this good later. And if any harsh thing has been said now, may the gods scatter it to the winds.”

With that he left them and went on to others. He found the son of Tydeus, proud Diomedes, standing by his horses and his bolted chariot, with Sthenelus, son of Capaneus, beside him; and he tore into him too: “Son of Tydeus — tamer of horses — why do you stand here cowering, peering down the lanes of the battle? It was not Tydeus’s way to shrink like this. He fought far out ahead of his men, against the enemy — so they say who saw him at his work, for I never met him or set eyes on him myself; but they say there was no man like him. Once he came to Mycenae, not as an enemy but as a guest, with godlike Polynices, raising an army; they were marching on the holy walls of Thebes, and they begged us hard to give them picked men to help. Our people were willing to grant it, but Zeus turned them against it with signs of ill omen. So Tydeus and Polynices went on their way. They came as far as the deep-grassed, rush-grown banks of the Asopus, and there the Greeks sent Tydeus ahead as their envoy. On he went, and found a great crowd of the sons of Cadmus feasting in the house of mighty Eteocles. Stranger though he was, the horseman Tydeus felt no fear, alone among so many: he challenged them all to contests, and won every one, easily — Athena was that great a help to him. The horse-driving Cadmeans were stung, and on his way back they set an ambush for him, fifty young men under two captains — godlike Maeon, son of Haemon, and Polyphontes, son of Autophonus, steady in battle. But Tydeus brought a foul death down on all of them; he killed every man but one — Maeon, whom he sent home in obedience to the gods’ signs. That was Tydeus of Aetolia. But the son he fathered is a worse man in battle, and a better one only at talk.”

So he spoke. Strong Diomedes said nothing — he took the king’s rebuke and held his peace, in awe of him. But the son of glorious Capaneus answered back: “Son of Atreus — don’t tell lies, when you know how to speak the truth. We claim to be far better men than our fathers were. We took seven-gated Thebes, the two of us, leading a smaller force against a stronger wall, trusting the gods’ signs and the help of Zeus — while they died through their own blind folly. So never set our fathers in the same honor as us.”

Strong Diomedes turned a hard look on him: “Hold your tongue, friend, and listen to me. I don’t blame Agamemnon, shepherd of the people, for driving the Greeks on to fight. The glory will follow him if we Greeks cut the Trojans down and take holy Troy — and the great grief will be his too, if the Greeks are beaten. So come — let us turn our minds to our own fighting fury.”

He spoke, and sprang to the ground from his chariot in his armor, and the bronze rang terribly on the body of the rushing king — a sound to put fear into the bravest heart.

As when, on a thundering shore, wave after wave of the sea comes driving in before the West Wind — far out on the deep it lifts its crest first, then breaks roaring on the dry land, and arches high over the headlands and spits the salt foam into the air — so, wave on wave, the close ranks of the Greeks moved without stopping into battle. Each captain called the orders to his own; the rest came on in silence — you would not have thought so vast a host had a voice in it at all, so quietly they went, in dread of their commanders. The worked armor blazed on them as they marched in their lines. But the Trojans — like the countless ewes of a rich man, standing penned to be milked, bleating without end at the sound of their lambs — so the cry of the Trojans went up across the wide army, no single shout, no one speech; their tongues were mixed, men called from many lands. These Ares drove on; the Greeks were driven by gray-eyed Athena — and with them went Terror, and Rout, and Strife, who rages on and never tires, the sister and companion of man-killing Ares: Strife, who rears up small at first, then plants her head in the sky while her feet still walk the earth. She it was who went among them now, casting her hatred into the thick of them, the cause of men’s groaning, and made the bitterness grow on every side.

And when they had come together into one ground, shield clashed against shield, spear against spear, the fury of men in their bronze armor; the bossed shields rammed one another, and a great roar went up. There were screams of the dying and shouts of the men who killed them, and the earth ran with blood. As when two rivers in winter spate come down the mountains and hurl their racing waters together into one ravine, out of their great springs, deep in a gorge — and far off on the hills the shepherd hears the thunder of it — so was the shouting and the toil as the two armies met.

Antilochus was the first to bring down a Trojan in his armor, a brave man in the front line, Echepolus, son of Thalysius. He struck him first on the ridge of his crested helmet, and the bronze point drove into his forehead and through the bone, and the dark closed over his eyes, and he toppled like a falling tower in the hard fighting. As he dropped, lord Elephenor, son of Chalcodon, captain of the great-hearted Abantes, caught him by the feet and began dragging him out from under the flying spears, in a rush to strip his armor — but his rush was short-lived. Great-hearted Agenor saw him hauling the body away, and as Elephenor bent over it his flank was left bare beside his shield, and Agenor drove the bronze-shod spear into it and brought him down. So he died, and over his body the deadly work of Trojans and Greeks flared up, and they leapt at one another like wolves, man grappling man.

Then Ajax, son of Telamon, struck down the son of Anthemion, a fine young man in his bloom, Simoeisius — whom his mother had borne by the banks of the Simois, coming down from Ida where she had gone with her parents to look at the flocks; and so they named him Simoeisius. But he never paid his dear parents back for raising him; his life was cut short under the spear of great-hearted Ajax. As he came on among the front-rankers, Ajax struck him in the chest beside the right nipple, and the bronze spear drove straight through his shoulder, and he fell in the dust like a poplar — a smooth-trunked tree grown tall in the wet ground of a great water-meadow, with branches springing only at the crown. A wheelwright fells it with his bright iron to bend a wheel-rim for a fine chariot, and it lies seasoning by the riverbank. So Simoeisius, son of Anthemion, lay where godlike Ajax had felled him. Then Antiphus of the gleaming corselet, a son of Priam, hurled his sharp spear at Ajax through the crowd — and missed him, but struck Leucus, Odysseus’s brave comrade, in the groin as he was dragging Simoeisius’s body to the other side. Leucus fell across it and let it slip from his hands. Odysseus’s heart blazed at his death, and he strode out through the front ranks in his shining bronze and came up close, and stood and threw his bright spear, looking carefully about him — and the Trojans gave ground before the throw. The spear did not fly for nothing: it struck Democoon, a bastard son of Priam, who had come to him from Abydos, from his post over the swift mares. Odysseus, raging for his dead friend, struck him with the spear on one temple, and the bronze point came out through the other; the dark closed over his eyes, and he fell with a crash, and his armor clattered on him. Then the front-line Trojans gave way, glorious Hector with them; and the Greeks raised a great shout, and dragged off the dead, and pushed on, further forward. But Apollo looked down from Pergamus and was angry, and he called to the Trojans: “Up, you horse-taming Trojans! Don’t give in to the Greeks. Their skin is not stone, and it is not iron, to turn the cutting bronze when you strike them. And remember — Achilles, the son of lovely-haired Thetis, is not fighting. He sits by the ships, nursing his anger.”

So the dread god cried to them from the high city. And on the other side the daughter of Zeus, glorious Athena, ranged through the army of the Greeks, urging them on wherever she saw them giving ground.

Then doom closed over Diores, son of Amarynceus. A jagged stone struck him by the ankle of his right leg, hurled by the captain of the Thracians, Peirous, son of Imbrasus, who had come from Aenus. The pitiless stone crushed both the bone and the two tendons utterly, and he went down on his back in the dust, stretching out both hands to his companions, gasping out his life. And Peirous, the man who had hit him, ran in and stabbed him with the spear beside the navel, and his bowels spilled to the ground, and the dark closed over his eyes. But as Peirous sprang back, Thoas of Aetolia caught him with a spear in the chest above the nipple, and the bronze fixed in his lung. Thoas came up close, wrenched the heavy spear out of his chest, and drew his sharp sword and drove it into the middle of his belly and took his life — but he could not strip the armor, for the Thracians stood thick around the body, men who wear their hair in a topknot, holding their long spears out, and for all his size and his courage and his force they shoved him back, and he gave ground. So the two lay stretched in the dust beside each other, the captain of the Thracians and the captain of the bronze-clad Epeans, and around them many more were cut down.

And now no man could have come into that fight and made light of it — not even one who walked through the middle of it untouched, unwounded, with Pallas Athena leading him by the hand and warding off the storm of flying weapons. For that day many Trojans and many Greeks lay side by side, face-down in the dust.
