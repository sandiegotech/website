The Iliad, Book XII

The Storming of the Wall

Book XII12 of 24

So inside the hut the son of Menoetius was tending the wound of Eurypylus, while out beyond, the Greeks and Trojans fought on without pause. And now the ditch was not going to hold the Trojans back any longer, nor the high wall above it. The Greeks had raised it to shield their ships, and dug the trench around it to guard the ships and the rich spoils they had taken — but they had not made the gods their burnt offerings. It went up without the blessing of the immortals, and so it was not built to last.

While Hector lived and Achilles nursed his rage, and while Priam’s city stood unsacked, the great Greek wall held firm. But once the bravest of the Trojans had fallen, and many of the Greeks with them — once, in the tenth year, the city was sacked and the Greeks had sailed home in their ships — then Poseidon and Apollo laid their plans to tear the wall down. They turned against it every river that runs from the heights of Ida down to the sea: Rhesus, Heptaporus, Caresus, Rhodius, Grenicus, Aesopus, and bright Scamander, and Simois too, where so many shields and helmets had dropped in the dust, and so many men of the half-divine generation had fallen. Apollo swung all their mouths together and drove them against the wall for nine days, while Zeus rained without pause to wash it the sooner into the sea. Poseidon himself went before them, trident in hand, and swept into the surf every foundation of log and stone the Greeks had laid with such labor; he leveled it all along the rushing Hellespont, then buried the whole stretch under a wide beach of sand. When the wall was gone, he turned the rivers back into their old channels.

That was what Poseidon and Apollo would do, in time to come. For now the fighting and the uproar still raged around the wall, and its timbers groaned under the blows raining on them. The Greeks, broken down under the lash of Zeus, were penned against their ships in dread of Hector, that master of rout, who fought as he always did, with the force and fury of a whirlwind. As a lion or a wild boar wheels among the dogs and hunters that close on him, and they lock themselves into a wall of bodies and pour their javelins at his face — and his great heart never flinches, though that same high spirit will be the death of him; again and again he charges to scatter them, and wherever he drives, they give way — so Hector ranged through the ranks, urging his men on, pressing them to cross the ditch.

But the horses would not. They balked at the brink, neighing, frightened by the width of it. They could neither leap the trench nor pick their way across, for it ran with steep overhanging banks on both sides, and set close along the top were the sharp stakes the Greeks had planted thick and strong against any assault. A horse could not go down into it and haul a chariot after, but the men on foot kept straining to do what they could. Then Polydamas came up to Hector and said, “Hector, and you other captains of the Trojans and the allies — it is madness to drive our horses across the ditch. It will fight us every step: it bristles with stakes, and just beyond them is the wall. There is no room for chariots down there, and no use for them; it is a narrow place, and we would only be cut to pieces. If Zeus in his anger truly means to help us and destroy the Greeks, then let it come now, here, far from Argos. But if they turn and we are driven back from the ships in a rout, back into the trench — not one of us will make it home to tell the city. So do as I say. Let the squires hold the horses at the ditch, and the rest of us follow Hector on foot, in full armor, in one mass. If their day of doom has come, the Greeks will not stand against us.”

Polydamas had spoken well, and it pleased Hector. He leapt down from his chariot to the ground in full armor, and when the other Trojans saw it, they too left their chariots. Each man handed his horses to his driver to hold ready at the ditch. Then they sorted themselves into companies, armed, and went forward behind their leaders in five bodies. The largest and the bravest, the ones most set on breaking through and fighting at the ships, marched with Hector and Polydamas, and Cebriones was their third in command, for Hector had left a lesser man in charge of his chariot. The second company was led by Paris, Alcathous, and Agenor; the third by two sons of Priam, Helenus and Deiphobus, and with them the hero Asius — Asius son of Hyrtacus, whom his great sorrel horses had carried from Arisbe, from the river Selleis. Aeneas, the brave son of Anchises, led the fourth, and with him the two sons of Antenor, Archelochus and Acamas, men skilled in every kind of fighting. And Sarpedon was captain of the allies; he took with him Glaucus and Asteropaeus, whom he judged the best men after himself — for he was far the best of them all. They locked their ox-hide shields together and drove straight at the Greeks, certain they could hold out no longer and would break before the ships.

The rest of the Trojans and their allies did as Polydamas advised. But Asius, son of Hyrtacus, would not leave his horses and his driver behind. In his recklessness he took them on toward the ships — and so he came to his death, and never again returned to windy Ilium, glorying in his chariot and his horses. Before that, doom with its dark name closed over him, and he fell to the spear of Idomeneus, the lordly son of Deucalion. He had driven for the left wing of the ships, the way the Greeks came back from the plain with their chariots and horses, and there he found the gates flung wide and the great bar drawn — for the gatemen kept them open to let in any of their own who came fleeing toward the ships. That was the road Asius chose, on purpose, and his men charged behind him with a shout, sure the Greeks would not hold. They did not know that two of the bravest fighters stood at the gates: the proud sons of the warlike Lapiths, Polypoetes, the strong son of Pirithous, and Leonteus, the equal of murderous Ares. The two of them waited there like a pair of high oaks on the mountains, that stand on their wide-spreading roots and year after year ride out the wind and the rain — so these two awaited the charge of great Asius, steady and unafraid. The Trojans came on under Asius and Iamenus and Orestes, and Adamas son of Asius, and Thoon and Oenomaus, raising the war cry, their shields of dry ox-hide held over their heads, straight for the wall. For a while the two defenders stayed within and rallied the Greeks to fight for the ships. But when they saw the Trojans storming the wall and their own men crying out and breaking, they burst out and fought in front of the gates like two wild boars in the hills that take the rush of men and dogs, and charging from both sides snap the brush around them, tearing it up by the roots, and you can hear the gnashing of their tusks — until a man strikes them down. So the bright bronze rang on the breasts of the two as the blows came at them; for they fought hard, trusting their own strength and the men on the wall above. And those men hurled great stones from the heights in defense of themselves and their tents and their ships. The stones fell thick as snowflakes that a screaming wind drives down from the dark clouds and pours in sheets on the earth — so thick flew the missiles from the hands of Trojans and Greeks alike. Helmet and shield boomed under the pounding of the great stones, and Asius, son of Hyrtacus, struck both his thighs in dismay and cried out, “Father Zeus — so you too are a friend to lies. I was certain the Greeks could never stand against our fury, and here, like wasps with narrow waists, or bees that nest in a rock by the road, they will not abandon the hollow they have built but fight for their young against every raider — two men, only two, and they will not be moved from the gates until they kill or are killed.”

So he spoke, but he did not move the mind of Zeus, whose purpose now was to give the glory to Hector. Meanwhile, around the other gates, the rest of the fighting raged on — and I am no god, to tell all of it; everywhere the battle blazed about the stone wall like a furnace. The Greeks, hard pressed, were forced to fight for their ships, and all the gods who stood for them grieved in their hearts. But the Lapiths fought on with all their strength.

There Polypoetes, the strong son of Pirithous, struck Damasus through the bronze-cheeked helmet with his spear; the helmet did not hold, the point drove through and shattered the bone, and the brain was scattered inside it, and Damasus went down in mid-charge. Then Polypoetes killed Pylon and Ormenus. And Leonteus, of the line of Ares, ran Hippomachus, son of Antimachus, through the belt with his spear; then he drew his sword and came first on Antiphates, cut him down hand to hand, so that he fell on his back in the dust, and after him killed Menon, Iamenus, and Orestes, one upon another, and laid them in the earth.

While the two were stripping the armor from these dead, the young men who followed Polydamas and Hector — the greatest part of them, and the bravest, the ones most bent on breaking the wall and burning the ships — were still halted at the ditch, in doubt. For a sign from heaven had stopped them just as they made to cross: an eagle, riding high along the left of their host, with a great blood-red snake in its talons, alive and still fighting. The snake had not given up; it twisted back and struck the bird that held it, on the neck, by the breast, and the eagle in its pain let go and dropped it into the middle of the host, then wheeled away down the wind with a sharp cry. The Trojans shuddered when they saw the writhing snake lying among them, the sign of Zeus who bears the storm-shield. And Polydamas came up to fearless Hector and said, “Hector, in our councils you are always quick to rebuke me, even when my counsel is good — because it suits no one, you think, for a man of the people to cross you, in the field or at the council. Still I will say what I judge is best. Let us not march on now to fight the Greeks at their ships. I think I know how this will end, if it was truly an omen for us, this eagle that crossed high on the left of our host as we made to cross the trench, with the living blood-red snake in its grip. It did not hold its prey; it never carried it home to the chicks in its nest. And so it will be with us: even if by some great effort we break through the gates and the wall, and the Greeks give way, we will not come back the same road in good order — we will leave many a Trojan behind us, cut down by the Greeks defending their ships. That is how a seer would read it, a man who truly knew such signs and whom the people trusted.”

Hector glared at him and said, “Polydamas, I have had enough of this. You can do better than this if you try. But if you mean it in earnest, then the gods themselves have taken your wits. You would have me forget the will of Zeus, the promise he made me and sealed with a nod of his head, and steer instead by the wings of birds — which way they fly, toward the dawn or toward the dark, to my right hand or my left. What is that to me? Let us trust the will of great Zeus, who is lord of all mortals and all the gods. One omen is best: to fight for your country. Why are you so afraid of the war? Even if the rest of us are killed at the Greek ships, you need not fear dying yourself — you have no stomach for a stand, no heart for the fight. But if you hang back from the battle now, or talk another man out of it, my spear will find you on the spot, and the life will go out of you.”

With that he led the way, and the others came after him with a roar that split the air. And Zeus, lord of thunder, sent down a blast of wind from the mountains of Ida that drove the dust straight at the ships; he dulled the Greeks’ resolve and handed the glory to Hector and the Trojans, who trusted his signs and their own strength and threw themselves at the great wall. They wrenched the breastworks off the ramparts and toppled the battlements; they pried up the buttress-beams the Greeks had set in the ground to brace the wall, and as these gave way they were sure of breaking through. But still the Greeks did not yield. They fenced the battlements with their ox-hide shields and struck down at every man who came under the wall.

The two Ajaxes ranged everywhere along the walls, rousing the Greeks, fair words for one man, hard words for any they saw flagging. “Friends,” they cried, “Greeks, every one of you — the best, the middling, and the worst, for no two men were ever equal in a fight — there is work here for all of you now, as you well know. Let no man turn back toward the ships because the enemy is shouting. Press forward, hold one another up, and Olympian Zeus, the lord of lightning, may yet let us beat back this attack and drive them home to their city.”

So the two went on shouting, calling the Greeks to the fight. And as snowflakes fall thick on a winter day, when Zeus the counselor stirs himself to snow and to show men his arrows — he stills the winds and pours it down hour after hour until he has covered the high mountain peaks and the jutting headlands and the clover meadows and the rich tilled fields of men, and it lies deep over the harbors and shores of the gray sea, only the breaking wave holding it off, while everything else is wrapped under it, weighed down from the sky — so thick flew the stones from both sides, some hurled at the Trojans, some by the Trojans at the Greeks, and the whole length of the wall was an uproar.

Even so, the Trojans and bright Hector would not yet have broken through the gates and the great bar, had not Zeus driven his own son Sarpedon at the Greeks, like a lion on a herd of horned cattle. He held before him his round shield of hammered bronze, beaten smooth by the smith and lined inside with layers of ox-hide stitched with golden rivets all around the rim. Holding it before him and shaking two spears, he came on like a mountain lion long starved for meat, whose proud heart drives him even into a close-fenced fold to try for the sheep. He may find the shepherds there, keeping watch over the flock with dogs and spears — but he will not be turned away without one charge at it; he will leap in and seize a sheep, or take a spear from a strong hand and fall. So Sarpedon’s spirit drove him to rush the wall and tear down its battlements. And he said to Glaucus, son of Hippolochus, “Glaucus, why is it that the two of us are held in such honor back in Lycia — the seats of honor, the choice cuts of meat, the cups kept full — and men look on us as gods? Why do we hold a great estate along the banks of the Xanthus, fine land of orchard and wheat? It is so that now we may take our stand at the front of the Lycians and meet the fire of the battle head-on, and one of them may say, ‘These are no idle lords who rule us in Lycia, who eat the fat sheep and drink the best of the honeyed wine — their strength is great, for they fight in the front of the Lycians.’ My friend, if escaping this war we could be sure of living forever, ageless and undying, I would not fight in the front line myself, nor send you into the battle where men win glory. But as it is, the fates of death stand over us in their thousands, and no man can run from them or escape — so let us go forward, and give the glory to another, or another give it to us.”

Glaucus heard him and did not turn aside, and the two led on the host of the Lycians. Menestheus, son of Peteos, saw them coming and was filled with dread, for it was his stretch of the wall they came at, bringing ruin with them. He looked along the rampart for some captain who could shore up his men, and saw the two Ajaxes, never weary of the fight, and Teucer just come from his hut, standing nearby — but he could not make his voice carry to them, so loud was the din: the crash of shields and helmets, the gates hammered, the whole tumult of it climbing to the sky. For all the gates were shut, and the Trojans stood pounding at them to force a way through. So Menestheus sent Thootes running to Ajax with a message: “Run, Thootes — call Ajax, or better, the two of them, for it will soon be over with us here. The Lycian captains are on us, men who have always fought like fury. But if they have too much on their hands to come, then at least send Ajax, son of Telamon, and let Teucer the great archer come with him.”

The messenger heard and obeyed, and set off along the Greek wall, and when he reached the Ajaxes he said, “You two, lords of the Greeks — the son of brave Peteos asks you to come and bear a share of the fighting with him, both of you if you can, for it will soon be over with him; the Lycian captains are on him, men who have always fought like fury. But if you have too much on your hands here, then let Ajax, son of Telamon, come at least, and Teucer the great archer with him.”

Great Ajax, son of Telamon, heard and answered the son of Oileus at once: “Ajax — you and brave Lycomedes stay here and keep the Greeks fighting hard. I will go over there and take my part in the battle, and come straight back when I have given them the help they need.” With that, Ajax son of Telamon set out, and his brother Teucer went with him, and Pandion to carry Teucer’s bow.

They went along inside the wall, and when they came to the tower where Menestheus was — and they found him hard pressed indeed — the brave captains and leaders of the Lycians were already storming the parapet like a dark, dense storm-cloud, fighting at close quarters, the war cry rising. Ajax, son of Telamon, was the first to kill — Epicles, a comrade of Sarpedon — striking him with a jagged stone that lay among the rubble at the very top of the wall. No man as men are now, not even one in the bloom of youth, could lift it easily with both hands; but Ajax raised it high and flung it down, and it smashed the four-ridged helmet and crushed the bones of the head together, and Epicles dropped from the high wall like a diver, the life gone out of him. Then Teucer hit Glaucus, the brave son of Hippolochus, as he climbed at the wall: he caught his arm bare and shot an arrow into it, and Glaucus broke off the attack. He sprang back down out of sight, so that no Greek would see he was hit and taunt him. Sarpedon felt the pang when he saw Glaucus go, but he did not give up the fight. He drove his spear at Alcmaon, son of Thestor, struck home, and as he wrenched the spear back Alcmaon came down headlong after it, his bronze armor clanging around him. Then Sarpedon caught the parapet in his strong hands and hauled, and the whole length of it gave way at once, leaving the wall stripped bare on top and a path open for many to cross.

Ajax and Teucer both turned on him at once. Teucer hit the strap across his chest that held the great shield, but Zeus turned death away from his son, that he might not fall there at the sterns of the ships. And Ajax leapt at him and drove his spear into the shield; it did not punch clean through, but it checked him in mid-charge, and he fell back a little from the parapet — not all the way, for his heart was still set on winning glory. He wheeled and shouted to the Lycians, “Lycians, why are you failing me? Strong as I am, I cannot break through alone and open the road to the ships. Come with me, all of you — the more hands, the better the work.”

Stung by their king’s rebuke, the Lycians pressed closer around the man who led them and counseled them. And the Greeks, on their side, drew up their ranks tight inside the wall, and there was deadly work between them. The Lycians could not break through and force a road to the ships, and the Greeks could not drive the Lycians off the wall once they had a hold on it. As two men with measuring rods in hand will quarrel over the boundary in a field they share, each fighting for his rights in a narrow strip of ground, so the two sides fought across the battlements, hacking at each other’s round shields over a wall’s-breadth of contested space. Many a man took the pitiless bronze in his body — some struck in the back as they turned and left it bare, many run clean through the shield. And the wall and the parapets ran everywhere with the blood of Trojans and Greeks alike. Even so, the Trojans could not break the Greeks, who held their line; and as an honest working woman holds the balance with the wool and the weight, leveling the two until they hang true, to earn some poor wage for her children, so the fight hung even between them — until Zeus lifted the greater glory to Hector, son of Priam, who was the first to leap inside the Greek wall. In a piercing voice he cried to the Trojans, “Up, Trojans, breakers of horses — break through the Greek wall and throw blazing fire on their ships.”

So he spurred them on, and they all heard it, and rushed straight at the wall in one body and went swarming over the parapets, the sharp spears in their hands. And Hector laid hold of a stone that lay before the gates, broad at the base and tapered to a point — no two of the strongest men in a town, as men are now, could easily lever it off the ground onto a wagon, but Hector hefted it alone and with ease, for the son of crooked-minded Cronus made it light for him. As a shepherd lifts a ram’s fleece in one hand and barely feels the weight, so easily Hector raised the great stone and carried it against the doors that closed the gates, doors set strong and high, two leaves fastened by two cross-bars with a single bolt holding them. He came up close, set his feet wide for the full force of the blow, and struck them square in the middle, throwing all his weight behind it. He broke both hinges, and the stone crashed through under its own great weight. The gateway boomed with the sound, the bars gave, the doors flew apart under the blow, one leaf this way and one that. And bright Hector leaped inside, his face dark as the falling night. The bronze blazed terribly about his body, and he held two spears in his hands. No one could have stood against him as he sprang through the gate, no one but a god, and his eyes glared like fire. He wheeled to the Trojans and called them on to climb the wall, and they did as he said — some scaling the rampart, others pouring through the open gates. And the Greeks fled in panic toward their ships, and the whole place was an uproar without end.
