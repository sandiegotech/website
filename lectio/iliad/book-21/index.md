# The Iliad, Book XXI

The Battle at the River

When the fleeing Trojans reached the ford of the swirling river Xanthus — Xanthus, whom deathless Zeus had fathered — Achilles split them in two. Half he drove across the plain toward the city, along the same road where the Greeks had run in panic the day before, with Hector glorying behind them; this way they streamed in a rout, and Hera flung a thick mist down in front of them to slow them. The other half were penned against the deep silver-eddying stream, and they fell into it with a great crash. The water roared, the banks rang, and they thrashed this way and that, screaming, caught in the whirlpools. As locusts driven before a grass fire pour into a river — the flame rolls on and on until it overtakes them and they huddle in the water — so the eddying current of Xanthus filled with the uproar of men and horses, all of them churning together before Achilles.

He left his spear on the bank, leaning it against a tamarisk, and plunged into the river like a god, his sword alone in his hand. His heart was set on slaughter. He hacked them down on every side, and their dying screams went up horribly as the blade fell, and the water ran red with blood. As fish flee in terror before a great dolphin and pack into the corners of some safe harbor — for the dolphin eats whatever it catches — so the Trojans cowered under the banks of the roaring river. And when his arms grew tired with killing, Achilles dragged twelve young men alive out of the water, to be cut down in payment for Patroclus, son of Menoetius. He hauled them out stunned as fawns, bound their hands behind them with the belts off their own tunics, and gave them to his men to take back to the ships. Then he sprang into the river again, still thirsting for blood.

There he came on Lycaon, a son of Priam, struggling up out of the water. Achilles had taken this man prisoner once before, by night, in his father’s orchard, where he was cutting young shoots from a wild fig tree to make the rail of a chariot. He had shipped him to Lemnos and sold him there, and Jason’s son had paid the price; then a guest-friend, Eetion of Imbros, had ransomed him for a great sum and sent him to Arisbe, and from there he had slipped home to his father’s house. Eleven days he had spent happily with his people since coming back from Lemnos; on the twelfth, a god put him back into Achilles’ hands, to send him down to the house of Hades against his will. He was unarmed now — no helmet, no shield, no spear; he had thrown all of it onto the bank, soaked and gasping, his knees buckling, his strength almost gone from fighting the current.

Achilles saw him and marveled to himself: “What is this? My eyes don’t believe it. If this man can come back alive after I sold him into slavery, then the Trojans I’ve killed will be climbing up out of the dark earth next. Not even the gray sea could hold him — and that holds many a man against his will. Well — this time let him taste my spear, so I can find out for certain whether the earth will keep him down for good, the earth that holds even the strong, or whether he’ll come back from there too.”

So he stood waiting, turning it over. And Lycaon came toward him dazed, desperate to clasp his knees — anything to live, anything but die. Achilles drove the long spear at him, meaning to kill, but Lycaon ducked under it and ran in and caught him by the knees, so the spear went over his back and stuck in the ground, hungry though it was for a man’s flesh. With one hand he gripped Achilles’ knees, begging; with the other he clung to the spear and would not let go. And he pleaded: “Achilles, I’m at your knees — have mercy, spare me. I am your suppliant, and you owe a suppliant respect. It was at your table that I first ate bread, the day you took me in the orchard. Then you sold me far from my father and my friends, off to Lemnos — I brought you the price of a hundred oxen. I’ve bought my freedom back for three times that, and it’s only twelve days since I came home to Troy after all I suffered, and now a cruel fate has put me in your hands again. Father Zeus must hate me, to give me to you twice. My mother bore me to a short life — Laothoe, daughter of old Altes, Altes who rules the warlike Lelegae and holds steep Pedasus on the Satnioeis. Priam married her along with his other wives, and she bore two sons, and you’ll have killed us both. You cut down godlike Polydorus in the front line already, and now my own death is here. I can’t escape you, since a god has set me in your reach. But hear me, and take it to heart: don’t kill me. I am not from the same womb as Hector — Hector, who killed your gentle, your brave companion.”

So the noble son of Priam begged, and the answer he got was iron. “Fool,” said Achilles, “don’t talk to me of ransom. Don’t even say the word. Before Patroclus met his day, I had some taste for sparing Trojans; I took plenty alive and sold them overseas. But now — now not one of them escapes death, not one whom a god delivers into my hands before the walls of Troy, and least of all the sons of Priam. So die, friend. Why cry about it? Patroclus died too, and he was a far better man than you. And look at me — do you see how big I am, how beautiful? My father is a great man, and a goddess was my mother. Yet death is coming for me too, and a fate there’s no fighting. There will be a morning, or an evening, or a noon, when someone takes my life in battle as well — with a spear cast, or an arrow off the string.”

He spoke, and the strength went out of Lycaon’s knees and heart. He let the spear go and sat back, both hands spread wide. But Achilles drew his sharp sword and struck him at the collarbone, beside the neck, and the two-edged blade sank in to the hilt. Lycaon pitched forward and lay full length on the ground, and the dark blood ran out of him and soaked the earth. Then Achilles caught him by the foot and slung him into the river to be carried away, and he jeered over him as the water took him: “Lie there among the fish. They’ll lick the blood from your wound and feel nothing. No mother will lay you out on a bier to weep over you; the swirling Scamander will carry you down into the wide lap of the sea. Some fish will rise through the dark ripple and feed on Lycaon’s white fat. Die, all of you — die till we reach the high city of Troy, you running, me killing behind you. This broad silver river won’t save you, for all the bulls you’ve thrown into it, all the live horses you’ve drowned in its pools. You’ll die anyway, and die hard, every last one of you, until you’ve all paid for Patroclus and for the slaughter of the Greeks you cut down by the fast ships while I stayed out of the war.”

He spoke, and the river grew angrier and angrier, brooding on how to stop Achilles and save the Trojans from ruin. Meanwhile the son of Peleus, spear in hand, sprang at Asteropaeus to kill him — Asteropaeus, son of the broad river Axius and Periboea, the eldest daughter of Acessamenus, whom the river had once lain with. He stood up out of the water to face Achilles, a spear in each hand, for Xanthus had filled him with daring, raging over the young men Achilles was butchering in his stream without pity. When they had closed, Achilles spoke first: “Who are you, and where do you come from, to dare to stand against me? Cursed are the parents whose sons face my fury.” And Asteropaeus answered: “Great son of Peleus, why ask my line? I come from deep-soiled Paeonia, far off, and I lead the Paeonians; this is my eleventh day at Troy. My blood is the river Axius, Axius the fairest stream that runs on the earth. He fathered the famous warrior Pelegon, and they call me Pelegon’s son. Now let us fight, Achilles.”

So he challenged him, and Achilles raised his spear of Pelian ash. But Asteropaeus threw with both hands at once, for he was right- and left-handed alike. One spear struck the shield and did not get through — the gold layer, the god’s gift, stopped it; the other grazed Achilles’ right forearm and drew dark blood, then drove past him into the earth, cheated of the body it wanted. Then Achilles hurled his own spear, hungry to kill, but missed the man and struck the high bank, sinking the ash half its length in the ground. He drew his sword and rushed in. Asteropaeus was straining to wrench Achilles’ spear out of the bank — three times he heaved at it with all his strength and could not, and the fourth time he tried to bend it and snap it off; but before he could, Achilles was on him with the blade and killed him, opening his belly by the navel so that all his guts spilled out on the ground, and the dark closed over his eyes as he lay gasping. Achilles set a foot on his chest and stripped his armor, jeering: “Lie there. It’s a hard thing to fight the children of Zeus, even for the son of a river. You said you came from a broad river — but I am of the seed of mighty Zeus. The man who fathered me is Peleus, son of Aeacus, lord of the Myrmidons, and Aeacus was a son of Zeus. So just as Zeus is greater than any river that empties into the sea, the children of Zeus are greater than any river’s children. You have a great river right beside you — let it help you if it can. But there is no fighting Zeus, the son of Cronus. Not even lordly Achelous matches him, nor the vast strength of deep Oceanus, from whom all rivers and seas and springs and deep wells flow — even Oceanus dreads the lightning of great Zeus and the terrible thunder when it cracks down out of the sky.”

He pulled his bronze spear from the bank and left Asteropaeus dead on the sand, the dark water washing over him, the eels and fish busy at him, nibbling the fat around his kidneys. Then he went after the Paeonians, who were running in terror along the river when they saw their captain killed by the son of Peleus. He cut down Thersilochus, Mydon, Astypylus, Mnesus, Thrasius, Oeneus, and Ophelestes, and he would have killed more, if the river had not risen in anger. It took on the shape of a man and spoke to him out of the deep current: “Achilles — you are mightier than any man, and crueler too, and the gods are always at your side. If the son of Cronus has granted you the killing of every Trojan, at least drive them out of my water and do your slaughter on the plain. My lovely channels are choked with the dead; I can’t pour myself into the sea, I’m so clogged with corpses, and still you go on killing without mercy. Enough — leave me in peace, commander of the host. You sicken me.”

Achilles answered: “It will be as you say, Scamander, child of Zeus. But I won’t stop killing Trojans until I’ve penned them in their city and tested Hector face to face — to find out whether he breaks me or I break him.” And with that he flung himself at the Trojans like a god. Then the river turned to Apollo: “Lord of the silver bow, son of Zeus — you’re not keeping the orders of Zeus, who told you flatly to stand by the Trojans and shield them until evening came and the shadows fell over the rich earth.”

Achilles leapt from the bank into mid-channel, and the river came at him in a high wave. He swelled his whole stream into a torrent and heaved up the dead — the many Achilles had killed and left in him — flinging them out onto the land, bellowing like a bull; but the living he saved, hiding them in his great eddies. The terrible, towering wave gathered over Achilles and crashed down on his shield, so that he lost his footing. He grabbed at a tall elm, but it tore out by the roots and ripped away the bank and went down, damming the river with its thick branches, a bridge of wreckage across the flood. Achilles dragged himself out of the channel and ran across the plain at full speed, in terror.

But the great god did not let up. He rose behind him with a dark-crested wave, set on stopping his hands and saving the Trojans. The son of Peleus shot ahead a spear’s-throw, fast as the black hunting-eagle, strongest and swiftest of all birds; the bronze rang loud on his chest as he flew, and he swerved out from under the wave and ran. But the river came roaring after him. As a man digging a channel from a dark spring leads the water through his plants and his garden, mattock in hand, clearing the debris from the ditch — and the water runs and rolls the small stones along, gliding downhill faster than the man who guides it — so the flood kept overtaking Achilles, fast as he was, for the gods are stronger than men. Every time he braced to make a stand and see whether all the immortals were driving him, the great wave came smashing down on his shoulders again, and he had to leap on, sick at heart, the angry water wearing him down, dragging the ground out from under his feet.

Then the son of Peleus cried up to the sky: “Father Zeus — is there no god who will pity me and pull me out of this river? After that I’d take anything. None of the others on Olympus is so much to blame as my own dear mother, who beguiled me with lies. She told me I’d die under the walls of Troy by the flying arrows of Apollo. I wish Hector had killed me — Hector, the best of the men bred here. Then a brave man would have killed a brave man and stripped him. Instead I’m to die a wretched death, trapped in a swollen river like some swineherd’s boy who gets swept away crossing a stream in a storm.”

He had barely spoken when Poseidon and Athena came and stood beside him in the shape of two men. They took his hand to steady him, and Poseidon spoke first: “Son of Peleus, don’t shake so. Don’t be afraid. The two of us are gods, and Zeus has sent us to stand by you — myself and Pallas Athena. It is not your fate to be drowned in a river; he’ll soon fall back, and you’ll see it. And here is our hard advice, if you’ll take it: don’t pull your hands from the fighting until you’ve penned the whole Trojan army inside the famous walls of Troy — as many as escape you. Then kill Hector, and go back to the ships. We grant you the glory of him.”

When they had said it they went back to the other gods, and Achilles, lifted by the gods’ command, pushed on across the plain. The whole flat was a sheet of water now, fine armor of the slain drifting in it, and bodies; but he forced his way upstream, driving straight ahead, and the wide water could not hold him, for Athena had put great strength in him. Still Scamander did not slacken — he raged at the son of Peleus harder than ever, reared his water into a crest, and called out to Simois: “Brother — let the two of us together hold this man, or he’ll sack King Priam’s great city, and the Trojans won’t stand against him. Help me now! Fill yourself from the springs, rouse all your torrents, throw up a great wave, send down a thunder of logs and stones, so we can finish this savage who’s lording it now as if he were a god. His strength won’t save him, nor his beauty, nor that fine armor — it’ll lie deep in the mud at the bottom soon enough. I’ll wrap the man himself in sand and pour a mountain of shingle over him, and the Greeks won’t know where to gather his bones for all the silt I’ll heap on him. That will be his grave; they won’t need to raise a mound when they bury him.”

And he hurled his churning flood at Achilles, towering, seething with foam and blood and corpses. The dark water of the river stood up in a wall and was about to overwhelm the son of Peleus — but Hera, terrified that the great torrent would sweep Achilles away, cried out to her son Hephaestus: “Up, my crippled child! We always reckoned eddying Xanthus a fair match for you. Help, fast — flare up your fire! I’ll raise a hurricane out of the sea, the west wind and the bright south wind, to carry the flames against the Trojans, against their heads and their armor, and burn them. You burn the trees along Xanthus’s banks and set the god himself ablaze; don’t let him talk you off it, fair words or threats. Don’t slacken until you hear me shout — then call your flames back.”

So Hephaestus loosed a raging fire. First it broke out over the plain and burned the heaped dead, the many Achilles had killed, until the ground was dry and the flood drew back. As the north wind dries an orchard soaked by autumn rain, and the man who owns it is glad — so the whole plain dried and the dead were consumed. Then he turned his tongues of flame on the river itself. The elms burned, the willows, the tamarisks; the clover burned, the rushes, the galingale, all the lush growth along the banks; the eels and fish in the water writhed under the heat, twisting through the eddies, tormented by the breath of Hephaestus’s cunning fire. And the river himself was scalded, and at last he cried out: “Hephaestus — no god can stand up to you. I give in. I can’t fight a thing that blazes like this. Stop — let Achilles drive the Trojans out of their city this instant. What is this quarrel to me? Why should I fight for anyone?”

He was boiling as he spoke; all his water seethed. As a cauldron set over a roaring fire boils when it renders the fat of a fatted hog, bubbling up everywhere with dry wood crackling under it — so the fair streams of Xanthus burned and his water boiled, and he could not flow but stood still, broken by the breath of Hephaestus’s fire. And he prayed hard to Hera: “Hera, why has your son singled out my stream for this? I’m not so much to blame as all the others who have helped the Trojans. I’ll stop, if that’s your will — but make him stop too. And I swear this oath: never again will I lift a hand to save the Trojans from ruin, not even when all of Troy is burning in the fire the Greeks will set.”

When Hera heard that, she called to her son Hephaestus: “Stop now, my glorious child. It isn’t right to batter an immortal god this hard for the sake of mortals.” At her word Hephaestus quenched his marvelous fire, and the river ran back down into its own fair channel.

So Xanthus was beaten, and the two of them broke off — Hera held them back, angry as she still was. But among the other gods a bitter quarrel broke out, for their hearts were split two ways, and they crashed together with a great din: the wide earth groaned and the sky rang out around them like a trumpet. Zeus heard it from his seat on Olympus and laughed in his heart for joy to see the gods going at one another. They did not hold off long. Ares, the shield-piercer, opened it — he sprang at Athena, sword in hand, and threw insults at her: “You shameless hornet — what is it this time, that you set the gods brawling, drunk on your own nerve? Have you forgotten how you put Diomedes up to wounding me, and took a spear in your own hand and drove it straight into me, in plain sight, and tore my lovely flesh? Now you’ll pay for all of it.”

And he struck at her terrible tasseled storm-shield — the one not even the lightning of Zeus can pierce. There bloodstained Ares stabbed with his long spear. But Athena gave ground and caught up in her strong hand a stone lying on the plain — black, jagged, huge, set there by men of old to mark a boundary — and with it she hit charging Ares on the neck and dropped him. He went down covering seven acres, his hair fouled in the dust, his armor clanging around him. And Athena laughed and stood over him with her boast: “You fool — even now you haven’t learned how much stronger I am, that you’d set yourself against me. So your mother’s curses are coming home to roost on you — she’s furious, and means you harm, because you abandoned the Greeks for the arrogant Trojans.”

She turned her bright eyes away. Then Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, took Ares by the hand and tried to lead him off, groaning, barely able to gather his wits. But white-armed Hera saw her and called to Athena: “Look — daughter of Zeus who carries the storm-shield, you tireless one — there goes that hornet again, leading man-killing Ares out of the war through the crush of battle. After her!” So she spoke, and Athena went after Aphrodite gladly, and struck her on the breast with her strong hand, so that her knees gave and her heart with them, and the two of them lay stretched on the all-nourishing earth. And Athena boasted over them: “Let every god who fights for the Trojans be as good a soldier as this — as bold and as hard as Aphrodite was, coming to help Ares against my fury. We’d have finished this war long ago and sacked the strong city of Troy.” White-armed Hera smiled to hear it.

Then lord Poseidon, the earth-shaker, turned to Apollo: “Phoebus — why are we two keeping clear of each other? It isn’t right, when the rest have begun. It would be a shame to go back to the bronze-floored house of Zeus on Olympus without a blow struck between us. Come on — you’re younger; I won’t taunt you with my age. Have you no memory at all, no sense of how the two of us, alone of the gods, suffered around Troy, when we came at Zeus’s order and served proud Laomedon a whole year for a set wage, and he gave us our orders? I built the Trojans a wall around their city, wide and beautiful, to make it impregnable; you herded his shambling cattle in the glens of wooded Ida. But when the glad seasons brought the day of payment, savage Laomedon cheated us of all our wages and sent us off with threats. He swore he’d bind us hand and foot and ship us to be sold on some far island; he was going to lop the ears off both of us with the bronze. So we went home seething, sick over the pay he’d promised and held back. And now you do his people favors — you won’t join us to wipe out the arrogant Trojans, root and branch, with their wives and their little children?”

And lord Apollo who works from afar answered: “Earth-shaker, you’d call me out of my mind if I went to war with you over wretched mortals, who flare up like leaves — one day full of fire, eating the fruit of the field, the next lifeless and gone. Let’s break this off and let them fight it out themselves.” And he turned away, for he was ashamed to trade blows with his father’s brother. But his sister, Artemis of the wild, mistress of the beasts, rounded on him in fury: “So you run, Far-Shooter, and hand Poseidon the victory outright, a cheap glory for nothing? You baby — why carry that useless bow? Don’t ever let me hear you again in our father’s halls, boasting in front of the gods that you’d stand and fight Poseidon to his face.”

Apollo gave her no answer. But Hera, the honored wife of Zeus, turned on Artemis in anger and rebuked her: “How dare you stand against me, you brazen thing? You’ll find me hard to match, bow and all. Zeus made you a lion among women and lets you kill whichever of them you please — but it’s easier to bring down beasts and deer in the mountains than to fight your betters. Still, if you want a lesson in war, come and learn how much stronger I am, since you choose to test me.” And she caught both of Artemis’s wrists in her left hand, snatched the bow off her shoulders with her right, and laughing beat her about the ears with her own weapon while she twisted and squirmed under it, her quick arrows spilling out on the ground. She fled in tears, the way a dove flies before a hawk into a cleft of hollow rock, escaping because it is not yet her day to be caught — so Artemis ran weeping and left the bow lying there.

Then Hermes, the guide, the slayer of Argus, said to Leto: “Leto, I will not fight you. It’s a dangerous thing to come to blows with a wife of Zeus who gathers the clouds. Go ahead — boast among the immortals as much as you like that you beat me in a fair fight.” So Leto gathered up the bow and the arrows that had fallen scattered in the swirling dust, and when she had them she went after her daughter. Artemis had reached the bronze-floored house of Zeus on Olympus and sat down in tears on her father’s knees, her divine robe trembling around her. The son of Cronus drew her close and asked her, laughing softly: “Which of the heaven-dwellers has done this to you, dear child, so cruelly, as if you’d been caught in some open wrong?” And the fair-crowned huntress answered: “It was your wife who struck me, Father — Hera, white-armed Hera, who starts every quarrel among the immortals.”

So they talked on Olympus. Meanwhile Phoebus Apollo went down into the strong city of Troy, anxious for the wall, afraid the Greeks might take the city that very day, before its hour. The rest of the immortal gods went back to Olympus, some still angry, some in triumph, and took their seats beside Zeus, lord of the dark cloud. But Achilles was still killing — Trojans and their horses both. As the smoke of a burning city goes up to heaven when the anger of the gods has lit it, bringing labor on all and grief on many — so Achilles brought labor and grief on the Trojans.

Old King Priam stood on the high tower of the wall and saw huge Achilles below, and the Trojans scattering in panic before him, with no fight left in them. He came down from the tower groaning and went along the wall, calling out to the men who held the gates: “Keep the gates open with your hands, until the people come pouring in from the rout — Achilles is right behind them, driving them, and I think the end is on us. As soon as they’re inside the walls and can breathe, shut the close-fitted gates again, hard. I’m terrified that terrible man will come bounding in with them.” They threw back the bolts and shoved the gates wide, and the open gates were salvation. Apollo charged out of the city to meet the runners and shield them. Straight for the wall they fled, parched and gray with dust, and Achilles came on raging behind them with his spear, like a man possessed, on fire for glory.

And now the Greeks would have taken high-gated Troy, if Apollo had not roused Agenor — Agenor, the brave noble son of Antenor. The god put courage in his heart and stood beside him to ward off the heavy hands of death, leaning against the oak, wrapped in thick mist. When Agenor saw Achilles bearing down, he halted, and his heart darkened with foreboding, and he said to himself in distress: “This is bad. If I run from mighty Achilles where the others are running in panic, he’ll catch me all the same and cut my throat like a coward’s. What if I let him chase the rest, and slip away from the wall the other way, across the plain behind Troy, until I reach the spurs of Ida and hide in the brush? Then at dusk I could wash the sweat off in the river and come back to the city. But why am I talking to myself? He’d spot me bolting across the open ground and run me down — there’s no outrunning him, he’s the fastest man alive. What if I face him instead, out in front of the city? His flesh can be cut by sharp bronze too. There’s only one life in him, and men say he’s mortal, for all the glory Zeus the son of Cronus hands him.”

So he braced himself and stood his ground, his bold heart set on fighting. As a leopardess comes out of a deep thicket to meet a hunter and feels no fear, hears the baying hounds and is not dismayed — even when the man strikes first with a thrust or a cast and the spear goes through her, she will not quit until she has him in her grip or lies dead — so noble Agenor, son of Antenor, would not run before he had tested Achilles. He held his round shield in front of him and took aim, shouting: “You really believe, glorious Achilles, that you’ll sack the proud Trojans’ city this very day? Fool — there is plenty of grief left in it for you. There are many brave men inside, and we’ll stand in front of our parents and our wives and children to defend Troy. Here, for all your size and your terror, here is where you die.”

He spoke, and his strong hand let the javelin go, and it struck Achilles on the shin below the knee. The new tin greave rang out sharply, but the bronze sprang back from the man it had hit and did not go through — the god’s gift held it. Then Achilles lunged at noble Agenor in turn, but Apollo would not let him have the glory: he snatched Agenor away and hid him in a deep mist and sent him out of the battle unharmed. And then, by a trick, he drew the son of Peleus away from the army. He put on the very likeness of Agenor and stood in front of Achilles, who broke into a run to chase him, hunting him over the wheatlands of the plain, swerving him toward the deep current of the Scamander. Apollo kept just a little ahead, always seeming about to be caught, drawing him on by his cunning. And meanwhile the routed mass of the Trojans was glad to crowd, thankful, back inside the walls, until the city was packed full of them. They no longer dared to wait for one another outside, to learn who had come through and who had fallen — every man whose feet and knees could still carry him poured headlong into the town.
