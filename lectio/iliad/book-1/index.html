The Iliad, Book I

The Quarrel

Book I1 of 24

Rage — sing, goddess, the rage of Achilles, son of Peleus, the ruinous rage that brought the Greeks endless suffering and hurled down to the house of the dead the strong souls of many heroes, leaving their bodies as carrion for the dogs and all the birds — and the will of Zeus was working itself out. Begin where the two first quarreled: Agamemnon, lord of men, and brilliant Achilles.

Which of the gods set them fighting? Apollo, son of Zeus and Leto. Furious with the king, he sent a foul plague through the camp, and the soldiers were dying — all because Agamemnon had dishonored Chryses, his priest.

Chryses had come to the fast Greek ships to free his daughter, carrying a boundless ransom, holding in his hands, high on a golden staff, the wreaths of Apollo who strikes from afar. He begged the whole army, but most of all the two sons of Atreus, the marshals of the people:

“Sons of Atreus, and you other Greeks in your fine armor — may the gods who live on Olympus grant you the sack of Priam’s city and a safe voyage home. But set my dear child free, and take this ransom, out of respect for the son of Zeus, Apollo who strikes from afar.”

All the other Greeks shouted their assent: respect the priest, take the splendid ransom. But it did not please Agamemnon. He sent the old man away harshly, with a hard command:

“Old man, don’t let me catch you by the hollow ships again — not lingering now, not coming back later — or your staff and the god’s wreaths won’t save you. The girl I will not free. Old age will come on her first, far from home, in Argos, in my house, working the loom and sharing my bed. Now go, and don’t provoke me, if you want to leave here whole.”

The old man was afraid and obeyed. He walked in silence along the shore of the sounding sea, and when he had gone some way he prayed hard to lord Apollo, whom lovely-haired Leto bore:

“Hear me, god of the silver bow, you who guard Chryse and holy Cilla and rule Tenedos with your strength — Smintheus! If I ever roofed a temple that pleased you, if I ever burned for you the rich thighs of bulls and goats, grant me this: make the Greeks pay for my tears with your arrows.”

So he prayed, and Phoebus Apollo heard him. Down he came from the peaks of Olympus, raging in his heart, his bow and sealed quiver on his shoulders. The arrows rang against his shoulders as he moved in his fury, and he came on like nightfall. He settled apart from the ships and let an arrow fly, and the silver bow sang with a terrible sound.

First he struck the mules and the running dogs, then loosed his sharp arrows on the men themselves. Day after day the corpse-fires burned thick. For nine days the god’s arrows swept through the army. On the tenth, Achilles called the soldiers to assembly — white-armed Hera had put the thought in his mind, for it grieved her to watch the Greeks dying.

When they had gathered, swift-footed Achilles stood and spoke: “Son of Atreus, I think we’ll be beaten back and driven home now, if we even escape death — if war and plague together are going to break the Greeks. Let’s ask a prophet or a priest, or a reader of dreams, since dreams too come from Zeus, to tell us why Apollo is so enraged: whether he blames us for some vow or sacrifice, and whether the smoke of lambs and full-grown goats might move him to keep ruin from us.”

He sat. Then Calchas rose, the best of the bird-readers, who knew what is, what will be, and what has been, and who had guided the Greek ships to Troy by the gift of prophecy Phoebus Apollo gave him. With good will he spoke: “Achilles, dear to Zeus, you tell me to explain the rage of Apollo, the far-shooting lord. I will — but first promise me, swear, that you’ll defend me with words and with your hands. I’m about to anger a man who rules all the Greeks, a man the soldiers obey. A king is too strong for a lesser man; even if he swallows his anger today, he keeps the grudge until he pays it off. Tell me you’ll protect me.”

Swift-footed Achilles answered: “Speak. Tell us whatever you know. By Apollo, whom you pray to when you reveal the gods’ will, no one — while I’m alive and see the light of day — will lay a hand on you by the hollow ships. Not even Agamemnon, who claims to be the greatest of the Greeks.”

Then the blameless seer took courage and spoke: “It isn’t a broken vow or a missed sacrifice. It’s the priest, whom Agamemnon dishonored — he wouldn’t free the man’s daughter or take the ransom. That’s why the far-shooter sends us grief, and will keep sending it. He won’t lift the ugly plague until we give the bright-eyed girl back to her father, unbought, unransomed, and send a holy offering to Chryse. Only then might we win him over.”

He sat. Then up rose the warrior son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon, in misery, his dark heart brimming with fury, his eyes like blazing fire. He rounded on Calchas first: “Prophet of disaster — you’ve never once told me anything good. You love to foretell catastrophe; you’ve never once said a good word, or made one come true. And now, in front of the Greeks, you announce that the far-shooter afflicts them because of me, because I wouldn’t take the splendid ransom for the girl Chryseis. Of course I’d rather keep her — I want her more than Clytemnestra, my own wife; she’s not beneath her in body or mind or work. Still, I’ll give her back, if that’s better. I want the army safe, not dead. But get me another prize at once, so I’m not the only Greek left empty-handed. That isn’t right, and you all can see it: my prize is walking away from me.”

Swift-footed brilliant Achilles answered: “Most honored son of Atreus, greediest man alive — how can the great-hearted Greeks hand you a prize? We have no common hoard lying around. What we took from the towns has been shared out, and it isn’t right to gather it all back. Give the girl up to the god now, and we Greeks will pay you back three and four times over, if Zeus ever lets us sack the strong-walled city of Troy.”

Lord Agamemnon answered: “Don’t try it, Achilles, brave and godlike as you are — you won’t outwit me or talk me round. You’d keep your own prize while I just sit here with nothing? Is that it? You order me to give the girl back? Fine — if the great-hearted Greeks give me another that suits me, a fair exchange. And if they don’t, I’ll come and take one myself — yours, maybe, or Ajax’s, or Odysseus’s; and whoever I visit will not be happy. But all that later. For now, haul a black ship to the sea, get rowers aboard, load the offerings, and bring lovely-cheeked Chryseis. Let one of our leaders take charge — Ajax, or Idomeneus, or brilliant Odysseus, or you, son of Peleus, most terrifying of men — and make peace with the far-shooter for us.”

Glaring at him, swift-footed Achilles said: “You, wrapped in shamelessness, always scheming for gain — how could any Greek gladly obey you, on the march or in a hard fight? I didn’t come here to make war on the Trojans; they’ve done nothing to me. They never drove off my cattle or horses, never ruined a harvest in Phthia, where the deep soil feeds men — too many mountains and the roaring sea lie between. No, we came for you, you dog, to win honor for you and Menelaus from the Trojans. You don’t think of that, you don’t care. And now you threaten to strip me of my prize, the one I sweated for, that the Greeks gave me. I never get a prize to match yours when the Greeks sack some rich Trojan town. My hands do the worst of the fighting, but when the spoils are handed out, yours is always the bigger share, and I go back to my ships with some small thing, small but my own, worn out from war. I’m going home to Phthia now. Far better to sail back with my curved ships than to stay here insulted, piling up wealth and riches for you.”

Agamemnon, lord of men, shot back: “Run, then, if your heart is set on it. I won’t beg you to stay on my account. I have others who’ll honor me — above all, Zeus, the lord of counsel. Of all the kings, you’re the most hateful to me: strife is always dear to you, and fighting, and war. So you’re strong — a god gave you that. Go home, then, with your ships and your men, and lord it over your Myrmidons. I don’t care about you, and I shrug off your anger. But hear my threat: since Apollo is taking Chryseis from me, I’ll send her on my own ship, with my own men — and I’ll come to your hut myself and take Briseis, your prize, with my own hands, so you’ll know how much greater I am than you, and so the next man will think twice before standing up to me as an equal.”

So he spoke, and grief seized Achilles. Inside his shaggy chest his heart was torn two ways: should he draw the sharp sword at his thigh, scatter the others, and cut down the son of Atreus — or hold his fury and master himself? As he turned it over, half-drawing the great sword, Athena came down from the sky. White-armed Hera had sent her, who loved and cared for both men alike. She stood behind him and caught him by his fair hair — visible to him alone; not one of the others saw her.

Achilles wheeled around, stunned, and knew her at once — Pallas Athena, her eyes blazing terribly. His words flew out: “Why now, child of Zeus who wields the storm-shield? To see Agamemnon’s arrogance? I tell you, and I mean it: his own pride is going to get him killed.”

The bright-eyed goddess answered: “I came from heaven to check your rage, if you’ll listen. White-armed Hera sent me, who loves you both alike. Stop this. Let go of the sword. Wound him with words instead — tell him how it will be. And I promise you this, and it will come true: one day three times as many glittering gifts will be set before you for this insult. Hold back, and obey us.”

Swift-footed Achilles answered: “When the two of you command, goddess, a man has to obey, however bitter his heart. It’s better that way. The gods listen to the one who listens to them.” He stayed his heavy hand on the silver hilt and thrust the great sword back into its sheath, obeying Athena. And she was gone, up to Olympus, to the house of Zeus who wields the storm-shield, among the other gods.

But Achilles turned on Agamemnon again, his anger far from spent: “You drunk, with a dog’s eyes and a deer’s heart — you’ve never had the nerve to arm with the army for battle, or to go out on ambush with the best of the Greeks. That looks like death to you. Far easier, all through the wide camp, to rob the prizes of any man who speaks against you. A king who feeds on his own people — because the men you rule are worthless; otherwise, son of Atreus, this would be your last outrage. But I’ll tell you, and I’ll swear a great oath on it: by this scepter — which will never again grow leaves or bark, now that it’s been cut from its stump in the mountains, and will never bud again, for the bronze has stripped its leaves and bark, and now the Greek judges who guard the laws of Zeus carry it in their hands — by this I swear: someday every Greek will long for Achilles, and on that day, for all your grief, you’ll be helpless to save them, as they drop in heaps before man-killing Hector. And you’ll tear your heart out in rage that you dishonored the best of the Greeks.”

So spoke the son of Peleus, and he threw the scepter to the ground, studded with golden nails, and took his seat. Across from him the son of Atreus went on raging. Then Nestor rose between them — the clear speaker of Pylos, whose voice ran sweeter than honey from his tongue. Two generations of mortal men had passed away in his time, the ones born and raised with him in old Pylos, and now he ruled among the third. With good will he spoke:

“This is a disaster. Great grief is coming to the land of Greece. How Priam would rejoice, and Priam’s sons, how the Trojans would lift their hearts, if they heard the two of you at each other’s throats — you who are first among the Greeks in counsel and in war. Listen to me. You’re both younger than I am. In my day I kept company with better men than you, and they never made light of me. I’ve never seen, and never will see, men like Peirithous and Dryas, shepherd of the people, and Caeneus and Exadius and godlike Polyphemus. They were the strongest men the earth ever raised, the strongest, and they fought the strongest — the wild mountain beasts — and destroyed them utterly. And I was of their company: I came from Pylos, from a far-off land, because they summoned me, and I fought on my own account. No one alive on earth today could fight those men. And they listened to my advice and heeded my word. So listen to me too. It’s the better way. Agamemnon, great as you are, don’t take the girl. Leave her — the prize the Greeks first gave him. And you, Achilles, don’t push a quarrel with the king. A king who holds the scepter, to whom Zeus has given glory, has never had an ordinary share of honor. You may be the stronger, with a goddess for a mother — but he is the greater, for he rules over more. Son of Atreus, cool your anger; I’m begging you, let go of your rage at Achilles, who is the great bulwark of the Greeks in this evil war.”

Lord Agamemnon answered: “Old man, everything you say is fair enough. But this man wants to be above everyone — to rule everyone, command everyone, give everyone orders. There’s someone here who won’t take it. So the everlasting gods made him a spearman — does that license him to throw insults?” Brilliant Achilles cut across him: “Yes — and I’d be called a coward and a nobody if I gave in to you on every point. Give your orders to others; don’t tell me what to do — I won’t obey you anymore. And one more thing, fix it in your mind: I won’t lift a hand to fight you, or anyone, over the girl. You gave her, and now you take her away. But touch nothing else of mine by my swift black ship, against my will. Try it, and your dark blood will run down my spear.”

So the two of them, having fought it out with bitter words, stood up and broke the assembly by the Greek ships. Achilles went off to his huts and his trim ships with Patroclus and his men. The son of Atreus hauled a fast ship to the sea, picked twenty rowers, loaded the offerings for the god, and led lovely-cheeked Chryseis aboard. Resourceful Odysseus took command, and they climbed in and sailed the watery roads.

Then Agamemnon ordered the army to purify itself. They washed the filth from their bodies into the sea and made perfect sacrifices to Apollo, bulls and goats, along the shore of the barren water; and the savor rose to heaven, curling in the smoke.

But Agamemnon did not let go of the threat he’d flung at Achilles. He called his heralds, Talthybius and Eurybates, who waited on him: “Go to the hut of Achilles, son of Peleus. Take lovely-cheeked Briseis by the hand and bring her here. And if he won’t give her up, I’ll come with more men and take her myself — and that will be the worse for him.” He sent them off with that hard order.

The two went unwilling along the shore of the barren sea, and came to the huts and ships of the Myrmidons. They found Achilles sitting by his hut and his black ship, and he was not glad to see them. They stopped, afraid, in awe of the king, and said nothing, asked nothing. But he understood, and spoke: “Welcome, heralds — messengers of Zeus and of men. Come closer. You’re not to blame in my eyes; Agamemnon is, who sends you for the girl, for Briseis. Patroclus, bring the girl out and give her to them to take. And let the two of them be my witnesses, before the blessed gods and mortal men and that brutal king, if there’s ever need of me again to save the rest from shameful ruin. The man rages with a ruined mind. He can’t think backward or forward at once, to see how the Greeks might fight in safety by their ships.”

Patroclus obeyed his dear friend. He led lovely-cheeked Briseis out of the hut and gave her over to be taken, and they went back along the Greek ships, and the woman went with them, unwilling. Then Achilles burst into tears and drew away from his men, and sat down apart on the shore of the gray sea, staring out over the wine-dark water. Again and again he stretched out his hands and prayed to his beloved mother: “Mother — since you bore me to live so short a life, the Olympian owed me at least some honor, Zeus who thunders on high. And now he’s given me nothing. The son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon, has dishonored me: he’s seized my prize and keeps it, taken by his own hand.”

So he spoke through his tears, and his honored mother heard him, far down in the sea’s depths where she sat beside her aged father. Quickly she rose like mist from the gray water and sat beside her weeping son, stroked him with her hand, and said: “Child, why are you crying? What grief has reached your heart? Tell me. Don’t hide it, so we both can know.” With a heavy groan swift-footed Achilles answered, and told her everything — the priest, the plague, the quarrel, Briseis carried off — and begged her: “Go to Olympus and plead with Zeus, if you ever warmed his heart in word or deed. Often in my father’s house I heard you boast that you alone among the gods saved the son of Cronus from shameful ruin, when the other Olympians wanted to put him in chains — Hera, and Poseidon, and Pallas Athena. You came, goddess, and freed him from the chains: you quickly called the hundred-handed one to high Olympus, the one the gods call Briareus and men call Aegaeon, stronger even than his father. He sat down beside the son of Cronus, glorying in his strength, and the blessed gods were afraid of him and did not bind Zeus. Remind him of that now. Sit beside him, clasp his knees, and beg him to side with the Trojans — to pen the Greeks against their ships and the sea, dying, so they all get the good of their king, and Agamemnon, wide-ruling son of Atreus, sees his own blindness, that he dishonored the best of the Greeks.”

Thetis answered him, weeping: “My child — why did I raise you, born to such sorrow? If only you could sit by your ships without tears, without pain, since your span of life is so short, so soon over. Instead you’re doomed to a quick death and to misery beyond all men. To a hard fate I bore you in our halls. Still, I’ll go to snowy Olympus and speak to Zeus who loves the thunder, and hope he listens. You stay here by your fast ships, keep your anger at the Greeks, and hold yourself out of the fighting altogether. Zeus went yesterday to the river Ocean, to feast among the blameless Ethiopians, and all the gods went with him. In twelve days he’ll come back to Olympus. Then I’ll go to his bronze-floored house and clasp his knees, and I think I’ll win him over.” With that she left him there, his heart still raging over the fair-belted girl they’d torn from him by force.

Meanwhile Odysseus came to Chryse with the holy offerings. When they had sailed into the deep harbor, they furled the sail and stowed it, dropped the mast into its cradle, and rowed the ship to her mooring. They threw out the anchor stones, made the stern cables fast, and stepped ashore, and led out the cattle for Apollo the far-shooter; and Chryseis stepped down from the seagoing ship. Resourceful Odysseus led her to the altar, placed her in her father’s hands, and said: “Chryses — Agamemnon, lord of men, has sent me to bring back your daughter and to make holy sacrifice to Apollo on behalf of the Greeks, to win back the god, who has loosed grief and groaning on us.” He gave the girl into his arms, and the old man took his dear child with joy.

Then they set the holy offerings in order around the well-built altar, washed their hands, and took up the barley grains. Chryses lifted his hands and prayed aloud for them: “Hear me, god of the silver bow, you who guard Chryse and holy Cilla and rule Tenedos with your strength. You heard me before, when I prayed — you honored me, and struck the Greeks hard. Now grant me this again: lift the foul plague from them.” So he prayed, and Phoebus Apollo heard him.

When they had prayed and scattered the barley, they drew back the victims’ heads, cut their throats, and flayed them; they cut out the thigh pieces, wrapped them in fat in a double fold, and laid raw flesh on top. The old man burned them on split wood and poured bright wine over them, and the young men stood by with five-pronged forks. When the thighs were burned and they had tasted the inner parts, they carved the rest into smaller pieces, ran them on spits, roasted them with care, and drew them off. The work done, they made the feast, and no one’s hunger went unsatisfied at the shared meal. When they had put away desire for food and drink, the young Greeks filled the bowls to the brim and served the wine to all, pouring the first drops for the god. All day long they sang to win him over, the young men of the Greeks, chanting the lovely paean, hymning the far-shooter; and his heart was glad to hear it.

When the sun went down and darkness came on, they lay down to sleep by the ship’s stern cables; and when the early-born, rosy-fingered Dawn appeared, they set sail for the wide Greek camp. Apollo the far-shooter sent them a following wind. They stepped the mast and spread the white sail, and the wind filled its belly, and the dark wave hissed loud around the keel as the ship ran, cutting her way through the swell toward home. When they reached the broad camp of the Greeks, they hauled the black ship high up the sand and braced it with long props, and scattered to their own huts and ships.

But Achilles, the swift runner, the god-born son of Peleus, sat beside his fast ships and went on nursing his rage. He no longer went to the assembly where men win glory, nor into battle. He stayed there, eating his own heart out, aching for the war cry and the fighting.

When the twelfth dawn came, the everlasting gods returned together to Olympus, Zeus at their head. Thetis did not forget her son’s plea. She rose from the swell of the sea and climbed at dawn to the great sky and to Olympus, and found the wide-seeing son of Cronus sitting apart from the rest, on the highest peak of the ridged mountain. She sat down before him, took his knees with her left hand, touched his chin with her right, and pleaded with lord Zeus, the son of Cronus: “Father Zeus, if ever I helped you among the gods, in word or deed, grant me this prayer: honor my son, doomed to the shortest life of all men. Agamemnon, lord of men, has dishonored him — he’s seized his prize and keeps it. Avenge him, Olympian, lord of counsel: give the Trojans the upper hand, until the Greeks pay my son back and lift him high with honor.”

Zeus who gathers the clouds said nothing. He sat a long while in silence, while Thetis clung to his knees just as she’d taken hold, and pressed him again: “Promise me truly. Nod your assent — or refuse, since you have nothing to fear — so I’ll know clearly how far I am the most dishonored of all the gods.” Deeply troubled, Zeus answered: “This is a miserable business. You’ll set me against Hera, when she provokes me with her cutting words. Even now she’s forever at me in front of the gods, claiming I help the Trojans in the fighting. Go now, before she notices, and leave the rest to me. Look — I’ll bow my head to you, so you’ll believe me. Among the gods this is the greatest pledge I can give; whatever I nod to is never revoked, never false, never left unfulfilled.” And the son of Cronus nodded with his dark brows; the divine locks streamed forward from the king’s immortal head, and great Olympus shook.

So the two made their plan and parted: Thetis dove from bright Olympus into the deep sea, and Zeus went to his own house. The gods all rose together from their seats to meet their father; not one dared keep his seat as he came, but they all stood. So he took his throne. But Hera had seen — she knew silver-footed Thetis, daughter of the old man of the sea, had been laying plans with him. At once she went at Zeus with her barbs: “Who’s been scheming with you this time, you trickster? You always love to settle things behind my back. You can never bring yourself to tell me a single thing you mean to do.”

The father of gods and men answered: “Hera, don’t expect to know all my thoughts. They’d be too much for you, even as my wife. Whatever it’s right for you to hear, no one — god or man — will know before you do. But what I choose to plan apart from the gods — don’t go probing it, question by question.” Ox-eyed lady Hera answered: “Dread son of Cronus, what a thing to say. I’ve never pried before, never questioned you; you settle whatever you please. But now I’m terribly afraid that silver-footed Thetis, daughter of the old man of the sea, has won you over. She sat beside you at dawn and clasped your knees. I think you’ve bowed your head that you’ll honor Achilles and kill Greeks in their thousands by their ships.”

Zeus who gathers the clouds answered: “You strange, impossible woman — always imagining, always watching me. You’ll get nothing by it, except to push yourself further from my heart, and that will be the harder on you. If it’s as you say, then it’s because I want it so. Sit down, be quiet, and do as I tell you — or every god on Olympus won’t be enough to help you when I come and lay my invincible hands on you.” So he spoke, and ox-eyed lady Hera was afraid; she sat down in silence and bent her heart to his will. And through the house of Zeus the gods of heaven were troubled.

Then Hephaestus, the master craftsman, spoke up, to comfort his mother, white-armed Hera: “This will be a sorry, unbearable business, if the two of you quarrel like this over mortals and start a brawl among the gods. There’ll be no pleasure left in a good feast if such ugliness wins out. Mother, I’m begging you, hard as it is — give in to Father Zeus, so he won’t start in on you again and wreck our banquet. The Olympian, the lord of lightning, could blast us all from our seats if he wanted; he’s far the strongest. Soften him with gentle words, and he’ll turn kind to us again.”

He sprang up and put the two-handled cup in his dear mother’s hands: “Bear up, Mother. Be patient, hard as it is. I don’t want to see you struck before my eyes — I’d be helpless to save you, for all my grief. The Olympian is a hard one to stand against. Once before, when I tried to defend you, he caught me by the foot and hurled me from the threshold of heaven. All day I fell, and at sunset I dropped on Lemnos with hardly a breath left in me, and the Sintians took me up and cared for me where I’d fallen.” At that, white-armed Hera smiled, and smiling took the cup from her son.

Then he went down the line, left to right, drawing sweet nectar from the mixing bowl and pouring for all the other gods. And unquenchable laughter rose among the blessed gods as they watched Hephaestus bustling and puffing through the hall. So all day long, until the sun went down, they feasted, and no one’s heart went without its share of the feast, or of the beautiful lyre Apollo held, or of the Muses, who sang in turn with their lovely voices. And when the bright light of the sun had set, they each went home to sleep, where the famous lame god, Hephaestus, had built each one a house with all his cunning skill. And Zeus the Olympian, lord of lightning, went to his own bed, where he always slept when sweet sleep came over him. There he climbed and slept, and golden-throned Hera beside him.
