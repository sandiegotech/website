The Iliad, Book XIX

The Rage Laid Down

Book XIX19 of 24

Dawn rose in her saffron robe out of the streams of Ocean, bringing light to gods and to mortal men, and Thetis came to the ships with the gift of the god. She found her son fallen across the body of Patroclus, weeping without restraint, and around him many of his men were weeping too. The goddess stepped into their midst and took her son’s hand in hers and said: “My child, for all our grief we must let this man lie. He has fallen by the will of heaven. Take now from Hephaestus this armor, splendid beyond anything a man has ever carried on his shoulders.”

She set the armor down before Achilles, and all its worked metal rang. A tremor ran through the Myrmidons; not one of them could look straight at it, and they shrank back in fear. But Achilles, when he saw it, blazed up the harder — his eyes flashed out from under his brows like flame, and his heart filled with joy to hold in his hands the glory the god had made. When he had fed his eyes on it, he said to his mother: “Mother, the god has given me armor such as only the immortals make, that no living man could have forged. I’ll arm now. But it cuts at me — I’m afraid that flies will get into the wounds of the brave son of Menoetius and breed worms in them, and foul the body now the life is gone out of it, and rot the flesh.”

Silver-footed Thetis answered: “My child, don’t let that trouble your heart. I will keep the cruel swarms off him, the flies that feed on men killed in war. He could lie a full year and his flesh stay as sound as now, or sounder. Only call the Greek fighters to assembly, give up your rage against Agamemnon, arm yourself at once, and put on your strength for the fight.” With that she breathed force and daring into him; and she dripped ambrosia and red nectar into Patroclus through the nostrils, so the body would not change.

Then Achilles went out along the shore and gave a great shout to call the Greek fighters together. And now even those who had always stayed back among the ships — the steersmen who hold the helms, the stewards who served out the rations — even they came to the assembly, because Achilles had shown himself at last, after holding so long out of the fighting. Two men came limping, Odysseus and the son of Tydeus, both still in pain from their wounds, leaning on their spears; and they took their seats in the front. Last of all came Agamemnon, lord of men, wounded too — Coon, son of Antenor, had wounded him with a spear in the battle.

When the Greeks were all gathered, Achilles stood and spoke among them: “Son of Atreus — was this better, for you or for me, that the two of us, sick at heart over a girl, tore into each other in a quarrel that ate our hearts out? I wish Artemis had shot Briseis dead at the ships the day I took her, when I sacked Lyrnessus. Then so many Greeks would not have bitten the earth under enemy hands while I nursed my anger. It was Hector and the Trojans who gained by it; the Greeks, I think, will remember our quarrel a long time. But let it go. It is finished. Hard as it is, I beat down the heart in me, because I must. Here, now, I put an end to my rage. It is not right to go on burning forever. So — call the army to arms, and let me go out against the Trojans and find out whether they still have any wish to spend the night by our ships. There’s many a man, I think, who’ll be glad to rest his knees, if he gets clear of my spear when I’m in the fight.”

So he spoke, and the Greeks were glad that the great-hearted son of Peleus had set his rage aside.

Then Agamemnon, lord of men, spoke to them — rising in his place, not coming out into the middle: “Fighters of the Greeks, servants of Ares: when a man stands up to speak, it is right to hear him out and wrong to break in; that is hard even on a skilled speaker. Who can hear, who can say a word, in an uproar? The clearest voice is drowned. To the son of Peleus I will explain myself; the rest of you, listen, and mark what I say. The Greeks have thrown this in my face before, again and again. But it was not I who did it. Zeus did it, and Fate, and the Fury who walks in the dark — they cast a wild blindness on my mind, that day in the assembly when I tore from Achilles the prize that was his. What could I do? A god brings everything to its end. Ate, eldest daughter of Zeus, is the one who blinds us all — ruin herself. Her feet are soft; she never touches the ground, but moves across the heads of men and trips them, and snares first one and then another.

“Once she even blinded Zeus, who they say is greatest of gods and men. Even him Hera fooled, woman though she was, on the day Alcmene was about to bring forth mighty Heracles in the strong-walled city of Thebes. Zeus, exulting, made his boast to all the gods: ‘Hear me, every god and goddess, while I say what the heart in me moves me to say. This day Eileithyia, who brings women to their pangs, will bring into the light a man-child who will be lord over all the people around him — one of the line of men sprung from my own blood.’ But Hera, with all her cunning, set a trap: ‘You’ll break your word — you won’t hold to this. Come, Olympian, swear me a great oath that the child who falls this day between a woman’s feet, born of your blood and line, shall be lord over all the people around him.’

“So she spoke, and Zeus saw nothing of the guile in it, but swore the great oath — and bitterly he paid for it after. Down she darted from the high peak of Olympus and came in haste to Achaean Argos, where she knew the noble wife of Sthenelus, son of Perseus, was carrying a child. The woman was in her seventh month, but Hera brought the boy to birth before his time, while she held back Alcmene’s labor and kept the birth-goddesses away. Then she went to Zeus herself with the news: ‘Father Zeus, lord of the bright bolt, I have a word for your ear. A fine boy is born this day, Eurystheus, son of Sthenelus, son of Perseus — of your own line. It is fitting that he should rule the Argives.’ At that a sharp grief struck Zeus to the very heart. In his rage he caught Ate by the bright hair of her head and swore a great oath that never again should she come back to Olympus and the starry sky — she, the ruin of all. He swung her round and hurled her out of the starry heaven, and down she fell among the works of men. And he groaned over her ever after, whenever he watched his own son crushed under the shameful labors Eurystheus laid on him.

“It was the same with me. While great Hector kept killing Greeks at the ships, I could not put Ate out of my mind — the blindness she first cast on me. I was blind; Zeus took my wits away. But now I want to make it right, and to give freely besides, to make amends. So go out to the fight, you and your men with you, and I’ll bring you everything Odysseus offered yesterday in your hut. Or wait, if you’d rather, hot as you are for battle, and my men will fetch the gifts from my ship, so you can see for yourself that what I give you is enough.”

Swift-footed Achilles answered: “Son of Atreus, most honored Agamemnon, lord of men — give the gifts, as is right, or keep them: that’s yours to decide. But now let us think about the fight. There’s no time to stand here trading words; there’s work still to be done. Let every man see Achilles again among the front ranks, breaking the lines of the Trojans with the bronze — and let each of you keep that in mind, and fight.”

But Odysseus, the man of many counsels, said: “Not so fast, brave as you are, godlike Achilles. Don’t send the Greeks against Troy to fight on empty stomachs. The battle, once it’s joined, will be no short one, when a god breathes fury into both sides. No, tell the men to take food and wine first by the ships — there’s strength in that, and staying power. No one can fight all day to sundown with no food in him; however much his heart is set on fighting, his limbs grow heavy before he knows it, and hunger and thirst find him out, and his knees go weak under him. But a man who’s had his fill of meat and wine can fight all day; his heart stays high, and his strength holds until both sides break off. So dismiss the army and tell them to make their meal. And let Agamemnon bring out the gifts before the whole assembly, so every man can see them and your heart be satisfied. Let him stand up before the Greeks and swear an oath that he never went up into Briseis’ bed, never lay with her as a man lies with a woman. And you, Achilles — show a gracious heart in turn. Let Agamemnon set a feast of reconciliation before you in his hut, so nothing is owed you that you haven’t had in full. And you, son of Atreus, deal more justly with men hereafter. There’s no shame, even for a king, in making amends when he was the one in the wrong.”

Agamemnon, lord of men, answered: “Son of Laertes, your words please me well; you’ve gone through it all rightly. I’ll swear the oath gladly, of my own will, and I will not swear falsely before the gods. Let Achilles wait, then, hot as he is for the fight, and the rest of you wait too, until the gifts come from my hut and we seal the oath with sacrifice. And this I charge you, Odysseus: pick out the best of the young Greeks and bring from my hut the gifts I promised Achilles yesterday, and bring the women too. And let Talthybius find me a boar somewhere in the camp and make it ready to sacrifice to Zeus and to the Sun.”

Swift-footed Achilles answered: “Son of Atreus, most honored Agamemnon, lord of men — see to all this some other time, when there’s a lull in the fighting and the fury in me has cooled. The dead are still lying out there on the plain, hacked and broken, men Hector cut down when Zeus gave him the glory — and you tell me to eat? No. I say the Greeks should fight fasting, with no food, and avenge them first; and when the sun goes down, then let them eat their fill. Until then nothing will pass my lips. My friend lies dead in my hut, torn by the bronze, his feet toward the door, and his comrades mourn around him. Food and drink are nothing to me now — only killing, and blood, and the death-groans of men.”

Odysseus, the man of many counsels, answered: “Achilles, son of Peleus, greatest of the Greeks — you are a better man than I am with a spear, and by no small measure; but in counsel I’m well ahead of you, for I’m older and I’ve seen more. So let your heart bear with my words. Men come quickly to their fill of battle, where the bronze strews thick on the ground the straw of the slain, and the harvest is thin, once Zeus tips the scales — Zeus, who is the steward of war for men. The Greeks cannot mourn the dead by going hungry. Too many fall, day after day, one on another — when would there ever be a rest from grief? No. Let us bury the man who has died, and harden our hearts, and weep for him one day and no more; and let those of us still living think of food and drink, so we can arm and fight our enemies all the harder, in tireless bronze. Let no man hold back then, waiting for a second call — it will go badly for anyone we find lagging by the ships. Let us go out as one and bring the hard edge of war down on the Trojans.”

When he had spoken he took with him the sons of Nestor, and Meges son of Phyleus, and Thoas, and Meriones, and Lycomedes son of Creon, and Melanippus, and they went to the hut of Agamemnon, son of Atreus. No sooner said than done: they brought out the seven tripods Agamemnon had promised, and the twenty gleaming cauldrons and the twelve horses; they led out the seven women skilled in fine handwork, and Briseis with her lovely face made eight. Odysseus weighed out the ten full talents of gold and led the way back, and the young Greeks carried the rest of the gifts and set them down in the middle of the assembly.

Then Agamemnon rose, and Talthybius, whose voice was like a god’s, stood beside him holding the boar. The son of Atreus drew the knife that always hung by the great scabbard of his sword, and cut the first bristles from the boar, and lifting his hands he prayed to Zeus. The Greeks all sat in their places, silent and still, listening to the king as is right; and Agamemnon looked up into the wide sky and prayed: “Let Zeus be my witness first, highest and greatest of the gods, and Earth, and the Sun, and the Furies who under the ground take vengeance on a man who swears falsely — I never laid a hand on the girl Briseis. I never went to her bed, never asked anything of her. She stayed untouched in my huts. And if any word of this oath is false, may the gods give me all the suffering they give to men who swear a lie.”

As he finished he cut the boar’s throat with the pitiless bronze, and Talthybius swung the body round and flung it into the gray sweep of the sea, food for the fish. Then Achilles stood up among the Greeks and said: “Father Zeus — the blindness you send on men is terrible. Otherwise the son of Atreus would never have stirred up such anger in my breast, never carried off the girl against my will, set on it as he was. No — Zeus must have meant the death of a great many Greeks. Go now, and eat, so we can join battle.”

With that he broke up the assembly, and the men scattered, each to his own ship. The Myrmidons took up the gifts and carried them off to Achilles’ ship; they stored them in his huts and settled the women there, and the grooms drove the horses in among the rest.

But Briseis, lovely as golden Aphrodite, when she saw Patroclus lying torn by the bronze, threw herself down over him with a cry, and tore at her breast and her soft throat and her beautiful face with both her hands. Weeping, the woman who was like a goddess said: “Patroclus — dearest to my unhappy heart — I left you alive when I was led away from this hut, and now I come back, prince, to find you dead. So for me sorrow comes on the heels of sorrow, with no end. The husband my father and mother gave me to, I saw cut down before our city, and my three brothers, my own mother’s sons, all met their day of death together. But you — when swift Achilles killed my husband and sacked the city of godlike Mynes — you would not let me weep. You told me you’d make me the wedded wife of Achilles, that you’d take me by ship to Phthia and hold a wedding feast for me among the Myrmidons. You were always gentle with me. That’s why I cannot stop weeping for you, now you are dead.”

So she spoke, weeping, and the women wailed with her — for Patroclus to all seeming, but each of them in truth for her own griefs. Around Achilles the elders of the Greeks gathered and begged him to eat, but he refused them, groaning: “I beg you — if any of you who love me will listen — don’t ask me to taste food or drink. A terrible grief is on me. I’ll hold out fasting until the sun goes down.”

And he sent the other chiefs away, keeping only the two sons of Atreus and Odysseus, and Nestor, and Idomeneus, and the old horseman Phoenix, who stayed to comfort him in the depth of his grief. But there was no comforting him until he should fling himself into the jaws of war. Sigh after sigh broke from him, as his mind ran always on Patroclus, and at last he said:

“Once, my doomed and dearest friend, it was you who would set a fine meal before me, quick and easy, in the hut, when the Greeks were hurrying to bring tearful war on the Trojans. And now you lie there torn, and the heart in me has no wish for food or drink, though they’re here — for missing you. There is no heavier grief I could suffer, not if I were to hear that my own father had died, who back in Phthia is shedding soft tears now for want of a son like me, while I fight here in a foreign land for the sake of Helen, who makes my blood run cold — not even if I heard that my son was dead, who is being raised for me on Scyros, if godlike Neoptolemus is even still alive. Until now the heart in me had hoped that I alone would die here, far from Argos and the grazing-lands of horses, and that you would go back to Phthia, and bring my boy home from Scyros in your swift black ship, and show him everything that’s mine — my household, my servants, the high roof of my house. For by now Peleus, I think, is either dead and gone, or barely clinging to the worn-out edge of life, weighed down with hateful old age and the endless dread of hearing the grim news of my death.”

So he spoke, weeping, and the elders wept with him, each remembering what he had left behind at home. And the son of Cronus, looking down, took pity on them, and said at once to Athena: “My child, you have gone and abandoned your man entirely. Has Achilles slipped out of your thoughts altogether? There he sits by the high-prowed ships, breaking his heart for the friend he loved, while the others have gone to their meal — and he will not eat or drink. Go to him, and drop nectar and sweet ambrosia into his breast, so that hunger does not break him.”

With these words he spurred on Athena, who was already eager. Down she shot from the sky through the high air like a falcon, broad-winged and shrill-crying. Meanwhile the Greeks were arming all through the camp, and when Athena had dropped the nectar and sweet ambrosia into Achilles, so that grim hunger would not weaken his limbs, she went back to the strong house of her mighty father. Then out from the ships the men streamed — thick as the cold snowflakes that come driving down from Zeus on the keen breath of the north wind. Helmet after gleaming helmet, bossed shield after shield, breastplate after strong-plated breastplate, spear after ashen spear — the sheen of it leapt up into the sky, and the whole earth laughed under the flash of bronze, and the ground rang under the tramp of feet. And in their midst Achilles armed. His teeth ground together; his eyes blazed out like fire; an unbearable grief had gone down into his heart. Burning with fury against the Trojans, he put on the gift of the god, the armor Hephaestus had labored to make him.

First he set the greaves about his shins, fine ones, fitted with clasps of silver at the ankle. Next he buckled the breastplate around his chest. Over his shoulders he slung the bronze sword with its studs of silver, and then he took up the shield, huge and strong, and the light from it shone far off like the moon. As when a fire blazes high in the mountains on a lonely farmstead, and sailors out at sea see the gleam of it while the storm-winds carry them, against their will, far from those they love — so the light from Achilles’ shield, beautiful and finely worked, rose up into the high air. He lifted the heavy helmet and set it on his head, and it shone out like a star, and the golden plumes that Hephaestus had set thick along the crest waved around it. Then Achilles tried himself in the armor, to see if it fitted, whether his limbs ran free under it — and it lifted him like wings and bore the shepherd of the people up.

Last he drew his father’s spear from its stand — heavy, huge, and strong, a spear no other Greek could wield, only Achilles. It was the Pelian ash that Chiron had cut for his dear father from the crest of Mount Pelion, to be the death of heroes. Automedon and Alcimus busied themselves yoking the horses; they put the fine breast-straps on them and forced the bits between their jaws and drew the reins back taut to the fitted chariot. Then Automedon took up the bright whip and leapt into the chariot, and behind him Achilles mounted, fully armed, glittering like the blaze of Hyperion the sun. And in a terrible voice he called out to his father’s horses: “Xanthus and Balius, far-famed colts of Podarge — see that you bring your driver back alive to the Greek lines this time, when we’ve had our fill of battle. Don’t leave him dead on the field the way you left Patroclus.”

And from under the yoke the gleaming horse Xanthus answered him — for white-armed Hera had given him a human voice. He bowed his head until his whole mane spilled forward from under the yoke-pad and brushed the ground, and he said: “Yes, mighty Achilles, this time we will save you — but the day of your death is near. We are not the cause of it: a great god is, and the heavy hand of fate. It was no slowness or sloth of ours that let the Trojans strip the armor from Patroclus’ shoulders. It was the strongest of the gods, the son lovely-haired Leto bore, who killed him among the front ranks and gave the glory to Hector. We two could run with the West Wind itself, the swiftest wind there is, men say — but you are fated to be brought down, by a god and by a man.”

He had no sooner said it than the Furies stopped his voice. And in deep grief swift-footed Achilles answered him: “Xanthus — why do you foretell my death? You needn’t. I know well enough that it is my fate to die here, far from my own father and mother. All the same, I will not stop until I have driven the Trojans to their fill of war.” And with a great cry he drove his strong-footed horses to the front.
