# The Iliad, Book V

The Day of Diomedes

Now Pallas Athena filled Diomedes, son of Tydeus, with courage and fire, so that he would stand out among all the Greeks and win himself glory. She made a flame stream from his shield and helmet, steady and unwearying, like the star that burns brightest of all in late summer when it has bathed in the streams of Ocean. Such a fire she kindled on his head and his shoulders, and she drove him into the thick of the fighting, where the press was densest.

There was a Trojan named Dares, a rich and honored man, priest of Hephaestus, and he had two sons, Phegeus and Idaeus, both skilled in every kind of fighting. These two broke from the ranks and came at Diomedes together, riding in their chariot while he stood on foot. When they were close, Phegeus let his spear fly first — but it passed over Diomedes’ left shoulder and missed. Then Diomedes threw, and his bronze did not fly for nothing: it struck Phegeus full in the chest, by the nipple, and knocked him from the chariot. Idaeus did not dare to stand over his dead brother’s body; he leaped from the chariot and ran, or he too would have died there. But Hephaestus saved him, wrapping him in darkness so the old priest would not be utterly crushed with grief. And the son of Tydeus drove off the horses and gave them to his men to take back to the ships.

When the Trojans saw the two sons of Dares — one in flight, the other dead beside his chariot — their hearts shook. Then Athena took Ares by the hand and said: “Ares, Ares — butcher of men, blood-soaked stormer of walls — why don’t we leave the Trojans and the Greeks to fight it out alone, and let Zeus hand the victory to whichever side he pleases? Let’s stand aside, and keep clear of his anger.” With that she led raging Ares out of the battle and sat him down on the high banks of the Scamander.

Now the Greeks drove the Trojans back, and every leader among them killed his man. First Agamemnon, lord of men, hurled mighty Odius, the captain of the Halizones, out of his chariot: as he turned to run, the spear struck him square between the shoulders and drove on through his chest, and he fell with a crash, his armor clanging around him.

Then Idomeneus killed Phaestus, son of Borus the Maeonian, who had come from Tarne. As Phaestus was climbing into his chariot, Idomeneus’ long spear caught him in the right shoulder, and the darkness of death closed over him as he toppled from the chariot; and Idomeneus’ men stripped the armor from his body.

Menelaus, son of Atreus, killed Scamandrius, son of Strophius, a fine huntsman — Artemis herself had taught him to bring down every wild thing that the mountain forests breed. But the goddess of the chase could not save him now, nor could his own famed skill with the bow: Menelaus’ spear took him in the back as he ran, struck him between the shoulders, and drove on through his chest, and he pitched forward, his armor ringing around him.

Meriones killed Phereclus, son of Tecton, son of Harmon, a man whose hands knew every kind of clever craft, for Pallas Athena had loved him dearly. It was Phereclus who had built the ships for Paris — the ships that began all the trouble, that brought ruin on the Trojans and on Paris himself; for he had not understood what the gods had decreed. Meriones ran him down as he fled and struck him in the right buttock; the point drove through the bone into the bladder, and he went down on his knees, screaming, and death took him.

Meges killed Pedaeus, son of Antenor. He was a bastard, but Theano had raised him as one of her own children, out of love for her husband. The son of Phyleus closed on him and drove a spear into the back of his neck; it cut on through, under his tongue and among his teeth, and he bit the cold bronze and fell dead in the dust.

And Eurypylus, son of Euaemon, killed Hypsenor, son of proud Dolopion — a man who had been made priest of the river Scamander and was honored by his people like a god. Eurypylus chased him as he fled, swung his sword, and lopped off his strong arm at the shoulder. The bloody hand dropped to the ground, and red death and overpowering fate closed his eyes.

So they labored on in the hard fighting. But you could not have told which side the son of Tydeus was on — whether he ran with the Trojans or the Greeks — for he swept across the plain like a winter river in flood, a torrent that has burst its banks and tears down the dikes in its rush. No piled-up embankment holds it, no wall of a fruitful vineyard, when it comes on suddenly, swollen with the rain of Zeus, and lays waste the work of many strong men in a moment. So before Diomedes the close ranks of the Trojans were scattered and broken, and for all their numbers they could not stand against him.

But the son of Lycaon — Pandarus — saw him storming over the plain, driving whole ranks before him. He bent his bow and shot, and struck him on the right shoulder, on the plate of his cuirass; the arrow tore straight through, and the blood ran down over the bronze. Pandarus shouted in triumph: “On, Trojans, spur your horses on! The best of the Greeks is hit — and he won’t last long under that wound, if it was truly lord Apollo who sped me here from Lycia!”

So he boasted; but the arrow had not killed Diomedes. He pulled back to his chariot and his horses, to Sthenelus, son of Capaneus, and said: “Up, dear son of Capaneus — get down and draw this arrow out of my shoulder.” Sthenelus jumped from the chariot and pulled the shaft clear, and the blood came spurting out through his plaited tunic. Then Diomedes prayed: “Hear me, child of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, tireless one. If ever you stood by my father in the thick of battle and loved him, stand by me now. Bring this man within a spear’s throw — let me kill the one who shot me before I could see him, and now boasts I won’t look much longer on the light of the sun.”

So he prayed, and Pallas Athena heard him. She made his limbs light, his feet and his hands quick, and she came close and spoke: “Take heart, Diomedes — go and fight the Trojans. I’ve put your father’s spirit in your chest, the fearless drive of Tydeus the horseman. And I’ve taken the mist from your eyes that was on them before, so you can tell a god from a man. If any other god comes here to test you, do not fight him — but if Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, comes into the battle, her you may wound. Drive your bronze into her.”

With that the bright-eyed goddess was gone, and the son of Tydeus went back into the front ranks. He had been hot to fight the Trojans before; now he burned with three times the fury, like a lion that a shepherd has wounded but not killed as it sprang into the sheepfold — he has only stung it to rage. The shepherd cannot fight it off; he hides among the buildings, and the abandoned sheep panic and pile on one another, while the lion leaps back out over the high wall. So Diomedes, raging, fell on the Trojans.

He killed Astynous and Hypeiron, shepherd of his people — one he struck above the nipple with the spear, the other he hacked with the sword at the collarbone, shearing the shoulder clean from neck and back. He left them and went after Abas and Polyidus, sons of old Eurydamas, the reader of dreams; but the old man read no dreams for them when they did not come home, for mighty Diomedes made an end of them both. Then he chased down Xanthus and Thoon, the two late-born sons of Phaenops — sons of his old age, with no other to inherit. Diomedes took both their lives and left their father nothing but grief and tears, for he never welcomed them home alive, and strangers divided his wealth.

Then he came on two sons of Priam in a single chariot, Echemmon and Chromius. As a lion springs on a cow or a heifer grazing in the brush and breaks its neck, so Diomedes threw them both from the chariot for all their struggling, stripped their armor, and gave the horses to his men to drive back to the ships.

Now Aeneas saw him cutting whole ranks to pieces, and he went through the storm of spears looking for Pandarus. When he found the brave son of Lycaon he said: “Pandarus — where is your bow now, and your feathered arrows, and the fame that no man here can match, and no man in Lycia either? Lift your hands to Zeus and let an arrow fly at this man who’s lording it over the field and has done us so much harm — unless he’s some god, angry with the Trojans over a sacrifice, and gods are hard to fight.”

And the son of Lycaon answered: “Aeneas, counselor of the bronze-clad Trojans, he looks to me like the son of Tydeus — I know him by his shield, by the visor of his helmet, by his horses. But I can’t be sure he isn’t a god. If he is the man I think, he’s not raging like this without some god beside him, hidden in cloud, who turned my arrow when it had already hit him. For I shot him — struck him square on the right shoulder, clean through the plate of his cuirass — and I was sure I’d sent him down to Hades, and still I haven’t killed him. Some god is angry with me. And I have no chariot, no horses here — eleven fine new chariots stand in my father Lycaon’s halls, fresh from the builder, cloths spread over them, a team of horses by each, champing white barley. Again and again the old man told me, when I left, to bring chariots and lead the Trojans from horseback. I wouldn’t listen — I was sparing the horses, afraid they’d go hungry in a great siege, used as they were to eating their fill — and so I came on foot to Ilios, trusting in my bow. And the bow has failed me. I’ve hit two of their champions now, the son of Atreus and the son of Tydeus, and drawn real blood from both — and only made them fiercer. It was an evil day I took this bow from its peg to lead my men to Troy for Hector’s sake. If I ever get home and lay eyes on my country and my wife and my great house again, let some stranger cut my head off if I don’t snap this bow with my own hands and throw it on the fire — for all the good it’s done me, it might as well be wind.”

Aeneas answered: “Talk no more like that. Nothing will change until the two of us go against this man with chariot and horses and test him with our weapons. Get up beside me — see how the horses of Tros can run, how they can chase and wheel and flee across the plain. They’ll carry us safe back to the city if Zeus hands the glory to Diomedes again. Take the whip and the reins, and I’ll dismount to fight — or you face him, and I’ll handle the horses.”

“Aeneas,” said the son of Lycaon, “keep the reins yourself and drive. The horses will pull better under the hand they know, if we have to run from the son of Tydeus. If they miss the sound of your voice, they may bolt and refuse to carry us out, and Diomedes will be on us and kill us both and take the team. So drive your own horses, and I’ll meet the man with my spear.”

They mounted the gleaming chariot and drove the fast horses straight at the son of Tydeus. Sthenelus saw them coming and called out: “Diomedes, dearest to my heart — I see two strong men bearing down on you, men of great might. One is the archer Pandarus, son of Lycaon; the other is Aeneas, whose father is great-hearted Anchises and whose mother is Aphrodite. Mount, and let us pull back. Don’t go raging on like this in the front — you’ll get yourself killed.”

But Diomedes scowled at him: “Don’t talk to me of running — you won’t talk me into it. It’s not in my blood to fight from behind or cower; my strength is still in me. I won’t even climb into the chariot — I’ll go at them on foot. Pallas Athena will not let me be afraid. Their horses won’t carry both of those men away from us — not even if one of them gets clear. And listen, fix this in your mind: if Athena grants me the glory of killing both, rein in our own horses here, lash the reins to the chariot rail, and then make a rush for the horses of Aeneas and drive them out from the Trojans to the Greeks. They come from the stock that Zeus once gave to Tros, the best horses under the sun and the dawn — King Anchises bred from that line on the sly, putting his mares to them behind Laomedon’s back. To take them would be great glory.”

While the two of them were talking, the others came on fast, and the son of Lycaon spoke first: “Tough, brave son of noble Tydeus — so my arrow didn’t bring you down. Now I’ll see what my spear can do.” He balanced it and hurled it; it struck Diomedes’ shield, and the bronze point drove through and on to the breastplate. Pandarus shouted: “Now you’re hit clean through the belly — you won’t hold out long, and the glory is mine!” But Diomedes, not shaken at all, answered: “You’ve missed, not hit. And I don’t think the two of you are done with this until one of you, at least, has glutted hard-fighting Ares with his blood.”

With that he threw, and Athena guided the spear to Pandarus’ nose, beside the eye. It crashed in among his white teeth, and the merciless bronze sheared through the root of his tongue and came out at the base of the chin. He fell from the chariot, his bright armor clashing around him, and the swift horses shied in terror; and there his life and his strength were undone.

Aeneas leaped down with his shield and his long spear, afraid the Greeks would drag the body away. He stood over it like a lion sure of its strength, his spear and his round shield held before him, ready to kill whoever came against him, with a terrible cry. But the son of Tydeus caught up a stone in his hand — a huge thing, a mass that two men could not lift, men such as live now; yet he raised it alone, with ease — and he hurled it and struck Aeneas where the thigh turns in the hip, in the joint men call the cup-bone. The stone crushed the socket and snapped both tendons, and the jagged edge tore the skin away. The hero sank to his knees, propped himself on the ground with one heavy hand, and the dark night came down over his eyes.

And now Aeneas, lord of men, would have died there, had his mother not seen it — Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, who had borne him to Anchises among the cattle. She threw her white arms around her dear son and drew a fold of her shining robe over him, a shield against the spears, so that no Greek could drive bronze into his chest and take his life. So she carried her son out of the fighting. But the son of Capaneus did not forget the orders Diomedes had given him. He tied his own horses clear of the uproar, lashing the reins to the chariot rail; then he sprang at the horses of Aeneas, drove them from the Trojans to the Greeks, and gave them to Deipylus — the comrade he prized above all the rest, for they were of one mind — to take to the hollow ships. And then he mounted his own chariot again, gripped the reins, and drove hard after the son of Tydeus.

But Diomedes was already after the Cyprian with his pitiless bronze, for he knew her for a weak goddess, not one of those who rule the battles of men, like Athena or Enyo the sacker of cities. He chased her through the great crowd, and when he caught her at last he lunged and stabbed at the surface of her soft hand. The spear tore through the divine robe that the Graces themselves had woven for her, and pierced the skin at the base of the palm, above the wrist; and out flowed the immortal blood of the goddess — ichor, such as runs in the blessed gods, for they eat no bread and drink no gleaming wine, and so they have no blood as we do, and are called the deathless ones. She shrieked and let her son fall — but Phoebus Apollo caught him up in his arms and hid him in a dark cloud, so that no Greek could drive bronze into his chest and take his life. And Diomedes shouted after her: “Give way, daughter of Zeus — leave war and battle alone. Isn’t it enough that you seduce soft women? Come near the fighting again, and I think you’ll learn to shudder at the very word.”

She fled, beside herself with pain, in agony, her fair skin fouled. Iris, swift as the wind, drew her out of the press and away. She found furious Ares sitting off to the left of the battle, his spear and his fast horses resting on a cloud. Aphrodite dropped to her knees before her brother and begged him for his team: “Dear brother, save me — give me your horses, so I can reach Olympus, where the gods live. I’m in terrible pain. A mortal wounded me, the son of Tydeus, who would fight Father Zeus himself now.” So Ares gave her his gold-bridled horses, and she climbed into the chariot, sick at heart, with Iris beside her. Iris took the reins and lashed them on, and they flew willingly up to high Olympus, the home of the gods. There fleet Iris reined them in, unyoked them, and set ambrosial fodder before them, and Aphrodite threw herself into the lap of her mother, Dione.

Dione folded her daughter in her arms and stroked her, and said: “Which of the gods of heaven has treated you like this, dear child, as if you’d been caught doing some open wrong?” And Aphrodite, who loves laughter, answered through her tears: “It was proud Diomedes, the son of Tydeus, who wounded me — because I was carrying my dear son out of the fighting, Aeneas, whom I love beyond all others. This is no longer a war of Trojans and Greeks; now the Greeks are fighting the gods themselves.”

“Bear up, my child,” Dione answered, “and endure it, hard as it is. Many of us who live on Olympus have suffered at the hands of men, and we lay heavy griefs on one another too. Ares suffered, when Otus and Ephialtes, the sons of Aloeus, bound him in cruel chains and shut him in a bronze jar for thirteen months — and Ares, insatiable for war, would have died there, had not their stepmother, lovely Eeriboea, told Hermes, who stole him out half-spent. And Hera suffered, when the mighty son of Amphitryon struck her in the right breast with a three-barbed arrow, and the pain would not let go of her. And monstrous Hades suffered, when that same man — the son of Zeus who holds the storm-shield — shot him with a swift arrow at the gates of the dead and gave him over to agony. He went to the house of Zeus on high Olympus, sick with pain, the shaft driven deep in his great shoulder, and the arrow tormented him until Paeon spread soothing herbs on the wound and healed him; for he was not made to die. Reckless man, brute, who thought nothing of doing wrong, who used his bow to wound the gods who hold Olympus. And now the bright-eyed goddess Athena has set this Diomedes on you — fool that he is, not to know in his heart that a man who fights the gods does not live long, and no child climbs his knees and calls him father when he comes home from war and the dread fighting. So let the son of Tydeus take care, strong as he is, that no one stronger than you comes to fight him — or one day his good wife Aegialeia, daughter of Adrestus, will wake the whole house with her wailing as she mourns the husband of her youth, the best of the Greeks: Diomedes, breaker of horses.”

With that Dione wiped the ichor from her daughter’s wrist with both her hands; the wound closed, the heavy pain eased. But Athena and Hera, watching, began to needle Zeus with their mockery. Athena spoke first: “Father Zeus — will you be angry if I say something? I think the Cyprian must have been coaxing some Greek woman to run off with the Trojans she loves so well, and while she stroked one of those fine-robed Greek ladies she scratched her delicate hand on a golden pin.” The father of gods and men smiled, and called golden Aphrodite to him: “Fighting is not your work, my child. See to the lovely business of marriage, and leave all this to swift Ares and to Athena.”

So they talked. But Diomedes leaped again at Aeneas, though he knew Apollo himself held his arms around him. He had no fear even of the great god — he was set on killing Aeneas and stripping that famous armor. Three times he charged, hot to cut him down, and three times Apollo struck back his shining shield. But when he came on a fourth time, like something more than a man, Apollo who works from afar cried out at him in a voice of terror: “Think, son of Tydeus, and fall back. Don’t try to set yourself level with the gods. The race of immortal gods and the race of men who walk the ground are not the same thing.”

So the son of Tydeus gave ground a little, to escape the wrath of Apollo who strikes from afar. And Apollo carried Aeneas out of the crowd and set him down in sacred Pergamus, where his own temple stood; and there, in the great sanctuary, Leto and Artemis the archer healed him and made him glorious to look on. Meanwhile Apollo of the silver bow made a phantom in the very likeness of Aeneas, armed as he had been, and around this image Trojans and Greeks hacked at one another’s shields, the great round ones and the light, fluttering ones of hide. Then Phoebus Apollo called to Ares: “Ares, Ares — butcher of men, blood-soaked stormer of walls — won’t you go in and drag this man out of the fighting, this son of Tydeus, who would fight Father Zeus himself now? First he closed with the Cyprian and wounded her on the hand by the wrist, and then he sprang at me too, like something more than a man.”

Apollo took his seat on the height of Pergamus, and murderous Ares went down among the Trojan ranks to rouse them, in the likeness of swift Acamas, the captain of the Thracians. He called to the sons of Priam: “Sons of Priam, kings the gods have nurtured — how long will you let the Greeks slaughter your people? Until they’re fighting at the gates of Troy? A man we honored like Hector himself lies fallen — Aeneas, son of great-hearted Anchises. Come, let us pull our brave comrade out of the carnage.” So he put fresh heart and fury into them all.

Then Sarpedon turned on Hector with a hard rebuke: “Hector — where has your courage gone? You used to say you could hold this city alone, without troops or allies, you and your brothers and your brothers-in-law. Well, I can’t find one of them now; they slink off like dogs from a lion. It’s we, your allies, who carry the fight. I came a long way to help you — from Lycia, from the eddying Xanthus, where I left my wife and my baby son and all the wealth a poor man dreams of. And still I drive my Lycians on, and stand to meet any man, though I have nothing here for the Greeks to plunder. But you stand idle, and you don’t even tell your own men to hold the line and defend their wives. Take care the whole pack of you isn’t caught like fish in a dragnet, and your fine city sacked and stripped. This should be on your mind night and day; you should be begging the captains of your allies to hold without flinching — and so put off the blame they lay on you.”

The words stung Hector to the heart. He leaped down from his chariot in his armor, and went up and down through the army shaking his two sharp spears, urging the men to fight and rousing the terrible war cry. They turned and rallied and faced the Greeks again — and the Greeks held, massed and firm, and did not break. As wind scatters the chaff over the holy threshing floors, when men are winnowing and golden Demeter sifts the grain from the husk in the driving air, and the chaff heaps whiten — so the Greeks turned white with the dust the horses’ hooves kicked up into the brazen sky, as the drivers wheeled them back into the fight. They drove their strength straight at the enemy, and furious Ares wrapped the field in darkness to help the Trojans, ranging everywhere among them; for Apollo had told him, when he saw Pallas Athena leave the battle, to put fury into the Trojans — since she had been the one defending the Greeks. And Apollo sent Aeneas himself out of his rich sanctuary and filled his chest with courage, and he took his place among his men, who were glad to see him alive and whole and full of fight. But they had no time to ask him how it had happened: there was too much to do, with the work Ares stirred up, and Strife, and Terror who never tires.

On the other side the two Ajaxes, and Odysseus, and Diomedes drove the Greeks on, fearless of the Trojan rush and roar. They stood like clouds that the son of Cronus sets on the mountaintops in still air, while the north wind sleeps with all the boisterous winds whose shrill blasts scatter the shadowing clouds — so the Greeks stood and faced the Trojans and would not run. Agamemnon went up and down among them with his orders: “Be men, my friends. Take a strong heart, and think of your good name in one another’s eyes in the hard fighting. When men hold to their honor, more of them come through than are killed; but the ones who run win neither safety nor glory.”

As he spoke he hurled his spear and hit a frontline man, Deicoon, son of Pergasus, a comrade of Aeneas whom the Trojans honored like a son of Priam, for he was always quick to fight up front. Agamemnon’s spear struck his shield and drove clean through it, on through the belt, into the lower belly, and he fell with a crash, his armor clanging around him.

Then Aeneas killed two of the best of the Greeks, Crethon and Orsilochus. Their father was a rich man of Phere, descended from the river Alpheus, whose broad stream flows through Pylian land. The river fathered Orsilochus, lord of many men; Orsilochus fathered Diocles; and Diocles had twin sons, Crethon and Orsilochus, skilled in every kind of war. Grown to manhood, they had sailed to Ilios with the Greek fleet for the honor of the sons of Atreus, and there they died. Like two lions reared by their mother in the deep of the mountain forest, who ravage the farms and carry off the cattle and the fat sheep until they fall to a man’s hand — so these two were beaten down by Aeneas, and dropped like tall pines.

Menelaus, dear to Ares, pitied them as they fell, and strode out through the front, helmed in flashing bronze, brandishing his spear — for Ares drove him on, hoping to see him killed by Aeneas. But Antilochus, son of Nestor, saw it and sprang after him, afraid the king might come to harm and waste all their effort. The two were already squaring up, spears leveled, hungry to fight, when Antilochus stepped in beside Menelaus; and bold as Aeneas was, he gave ground when he saw the two of them standing together. They dragged the bodies of Crethon and Orsilochus back to the Greek lines, laid the poor men in their comrades’ arms, and turned and went on fighting up front.

There they killed Pylaemenes, a match for Ares, the leader of the great-hearted Paphlagonians. Menelaus struck him on the collarbone as he stood in his chariot, while Antilochus hit his charioteer Mydon, son of Atymnius, who was just turning the horses to flee — caught him on the elbow with a stone, and the ivory-bright reins dropped from his hands into the dust. Then Antilochus rushed in and struck him on the temple with his sword, and he toppled gasping from the chariot headfirst, and stuck there a while in the deep sand, head and shoulders down, until his own horses kicked him flat; and Antilochus lashed the team and drove it off to the Greeks.

But Hector spotted them across the ranks and charged with a great shout, and the strong Trojan companies came on behind him. Ares led them, and dread Enyo, she with the merciless din of battle, while Ares wielded a monstrous spear and ranged now in front of Hector, now behind. Diomedes shuddered when he saw him. As a man crossing a great plain stops short at the bank of a flooding river, sees it boiling toward the sea, and recoils, so the son of Tydeus gave ground, and called to his men: “My friends — no wonder we marvel at how Hector handles the spear. There’s always a god at his side to ward him, and now it’s Ares, in the shape of a man. Keep your faces to the Trojans, but give ground, back, step by step — we will not fight with gods.”

Even as he spoke the Trojans closed, and Hector killed two men in one chariot, Menesthes and Anchialus, both skilled in war. Ajax, son of Telamon, pitied them as they fell; he came up close and hurled his spear and hit Amphius, son of Selagus, a rich man from Paesus who owned much grain land, but whose fate had drawn him to fight for Priam and his sons. Ajax caught him in the belt; the long spear drove into the lower belly, and he fell with a crash. Ajax ran to strip the armor, but the Trojans rained spears on him, many sticking in his shield. He set his heel on the body and pulled his spear free, but he could not get the fine armor from the shoulders for the storm of weapons; and the strong Trojans pressed in around him, many and bold, with their spears leveled, so that for all his size and strength and courage they drove him back.

So they labored on. And the strong hand of fate drove Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, a brave man and a tall one, against Sarpedon — and the two, son and grandson of Zeus, drew near each other. Tlepolemus spoke first: “Sarpedon, counselor of the Lycians — why do you skulk here, you who have no stomach for war? They lie who call you a son of Zeus who holds the storm-shield. You fall far short of the men born to him in the old days. My father Heracles was another kind of man — lionhearted, daring — who came here once for the horses of Laomedon, and though he had only six ships and a handful of men, he sacked the city of Ilios and left her streets a wilderness. But you’re a coward, and your people are dying around you. You’ll be no help to the Trojans, big as you are, come all the way from Lycia — my hand will send you through the gates of Hades.”

And Sarpedon, captain of the Lycians, answered: “Tlepolemus, your father did bring down holy Ilios — but through the folly of proud Laomedon, who paid a good man with insults and kept back the horses he had crossed the world to earn. As for you, my spear will deal you death and dark fate here; you’ll give the glory to me, and your soul to Hades of the famous horses.” They threw at the same moment. Sarpedon struck him full in the throat, and the spear drove straight through, and the dark of death came over his eyes. Tlepolemus’ spear had caught Sarpedon in the left thigh, and the point tore raging through to graze the bone — but his father, for now, kept death away from him.

His brave comrades carried Sarpedon out of the fight, the long spear dragging from the wound and weighing him down, for in their haste and grief no one thought to draw the shaft from his thigh so he could walk upright. And on the other side the Greeks bore off Tlepolemus. Odysseus, the man of long endurance, saw it and burned for the fight; but his heart was torn — should he chase the son of Zeus, or kill more of the Lycian rank and file? It was not fated that he should bring down the son of Zeus, so Athena turned his fury against the common Lycians. He killed Coeranus, Alastor, Chromius, Alcander, Halius, Noemon, and Prytanis, and would have killed more, had not great Hector seen him and come storming to the front in his flashing bronze, striking terror into the Greeks. Sarpedon was glad to see him come, and called out in pity for himself: “Son of Priam — don’t leave me here to lie at the Greeks’ mercy. Help me. And if I’m not to see my own home again, and gladden my wife and my baby son, then let me at least die inside your walls.”

Hector gave him no answer, but rushed on, hot to throw back the Greeks and kill as many as he could. So Sarpedon’s men carried him and laid him under Zeus’ great oak, and Pelagon, his dear friend, pulled the spear from his thigh. He fainted then, and a mist came over his eyes; but he breathed again, for the breath of the north wind blew over him and brought him back from the swoon.

Meanwhile the Greeks, with Ares and Hector bearing down on them, neither broke for the ships nor pressed forward; once they knew Ares was with the Trojans, they fell back, but kept their faces to the foe. Who, then, was the first and who the last that Ares and Hector killed? Godlike Teuthras, and Orestes the horseman; Trechus, the Aetolian spearman, and Oenomaus; Helenus, son of Oenops; and Oresbius of the bright belt, a rich man who lived by the Cephisian lake among the Boeotians, lords of fertile land.

Now when white-armed Hera saw the Greeks dying like this, she spoke at once to Athena: “This is unbearable, child of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, tireless one. We promised Menelaus he’d go home only after he had sacked strong-walled Troy — and it comes to nothing if we let murderous Ares rage on. Come — let us think of our own war-fury too.” Athena did not refuse. Hera, the queenly goddess, daughter of great Cronus, went and harnessed her gold-bridled horses. Hebe fitted the curved bronze wheels to the iron axle, eight-spoked, with golden rims and bronze tires fitted over them, a wonder to see, and silver hubs turning on either side; the chariot was woven of gold and silver straps, with a double rail running round, and a silver pole; to its end Hebe bound the golden yoke and the fine golden harness, and Hera led her swift horses under it, hungry for war and the battle cry.

Meanwhile Athena let her soft embroidered robe slip from her — the robe she had made with her own hands — to the floor of her father’s house, and put on the war-shirt of Zeus the cloud-gatherer, and armed herself for the misery of battle. Around her shoulders she slung the tasseled storm-shield, the aegis, terrible to see, ringed about with Rout, and on it Strife, and Strength, and the cold horror of Pursuit, and the head of the Gorgon, that grim and dreadful monster, the dire sign of Zeus who holds the storm-shield. On her head she set the golden helmet with its double crest and its four bosses, broad enough for the fighting men of a hundred cities. Then she stepped into the flaming chariot and took the spear — the great, heavy, massive spear with which she breaks the ranks of warriors who anger the daughter of a mighty father. Hera lashed the horses on, and the gates of heaven groaned open of their own accord — the gates the Hours keep, who hold great heaven and Olympus, to roll back the thick cloud or to shut it. Through these they drove the willing horses.

They found the son of Cronus sitting apart from the other gods on the highest peak of Olympus. There white-armed Hera reined the horses in and spoke to Zeus on high: “Father Zeus — aren’t you angry at Ares for all this savagery? What a great fine host of the Greeks he has destroyed, recklessly, against all right — to my grief — while the Cyprian and Apollo of the silver bow look on and enjoy it, having loosed this madman who knows no law. Father Zeus — will you be angry with me if I beat Ares hard and drive him out of the battle?” And Zeus the cloud-gatherer answered: “Go to it — set Athena on him, the bringer of spoil; she’s the one who’s used to giving him his worst hurts.”

Hera obeyed. She lashed the horses, and they flew willingly between the earth and the starry sky. As far as a man on a lookout can see across the wine-dark sea into the haze, so far at a single bound spring the loud-neighing horses of the gods. When they reached Troy and the two rivers, where the Simois and the Scamander run together, white-armed Hera reined them in, unyoked them, and hid them in a thick mist, and Simois made ambrosia spring up for them to graze. Then the two goddesses went on, stepping like trembling doves in their eagerness to help the Greeks. When they came to where the best and the most of them stood massed about mighty Diomedes, breaker of horses — packed close like ravening lions or wild boars, whose strength does not easily give way — there Hera halted and raised a great shout, in the likeness of bronze-voiced Stentor, whose cry was as loud as fifty other men: “Shame, you Greeks — fine to look at, worthless cowards! As long as brilliant Achilles came to the fighting, the Trojans never dared come past the Dardanian gates, in terror of his spear; and now they fight far from the city, at your very ships.” So she stung the heart and fury of every man.

And Athena went straight to the son of Tydeus. She found the king beside his chariot and horses, cooling the wound that Pandarus’ arrow had dealt him; for the sweat under the broad strap of his shield was galling it, and his arm ached with the weight, and he was lifting the strap to wipe away the dark blood. The goddess laid her hand on the yoke of his horses and said: “Tydeus got himself a son who takes little after him. Tydeus was a small man in body — but he could fight. Once, when I forbade him to fight or show off, when he went alone as an envoy to Thebes among the swarming Cadmeans, and I told him to feast quietly in their halls — that bold spirit of his rose up, and he challenged the young Cadmeans, and beat them at everything, easily; that’s how strongly I stood by him. And I stand by you too, and guard you, and gladly tell you to fight the Trojans. But either weariness has gone deep into your limbs, or some heartless fear is holding you — and if so, you are no son of Tydeus, the fighting son of Oeneus.”

Diomedes answered her: “I know you, goddess, daughter of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, and I’ll hide nothing from you. It’s no fear holding me, no slackness. I’m only keeping to your own command. You told me not to fight any of the blessed gods face to face — except that if Aphrodite came into the battle, I should wound her with the bronze. That’s why I’m giving ground, and why I’ve ordered all the Greeks to draw together here. I can see Ares lording it over the field.” “Diomedes, son of Tydeus, dearest to my heart,” Athena answered, “don’t be afraid of Ares now, or any of the other immortals — I’ll stand by you. Drive your horses straight at Ares first, and strike him hand to hand. Don’t shrink from this raging madman, this born evil, this turncoat. Only just now he was promising Hera and me, to our faces, that he’d fight the Trojans and stand with the Greeks — and now he’s forgotten all that and gone over to the Trojans.”

With that she took hold of Sthenelus and shoved him down off the chariot to the ground — he was off in an instant — and she mounted the chariot herself, beside the godlike Diomedes, an eager goddess. The oak axle groaned aloud under the weight, for it bore a dread goddess and the best of men. Pallas Athena took up the whip and the reins and drove the horses straight at Ares. He was just then stripping the armor from huge Periphas, son of Ochesius, the best of the Aetolians. Blood-soaked Ares was at the body; but Athena put on the cap of Hades, so the great god could not see her. When Ares saw Diomedes coming, he left huge Periphas lying where he had killed him and made straight for the son of Tydeus. As soon as they were in range, Ares lunged over the yoke and the reins with his bronze spear, hungry to take Diomedes’ life — but the bright-eyed goddess caught the spear in her hand and shoved it up over the chariot, to fly off and waste itself. Then Diomedes drove in with his own bronze, and Pallas Athena leaned on it and rammed it home into Ares’ belly, low, where the war-belt girded him. There Diomedes wounded him, tearing the fair flesh, and wrenched the spear back out.

And Ares, god of bronze, bellowed — loud as the war cry of nine thousand men, ten thousand, when they clash in battle — and a trembling fear took hold of the Greeks and the Trojans alike, so terrible was the cry of Ares, insatiable for war. As a black haze rises off the clouds when, after the heat, a storm wind comes up to blow — so to the eyes of Diomedes, son of Tydeus, Ares looked as he rose into the wide sky on the clouds. Fast he came to the home of the gods, to steep Olympus, and sat down beside Zeus, son of Cronus, sick at heart, and showed him the immortal blood running from his wound, and poured out his complaint in winged words: “Father Zeus — does none of this anger you, all this savagery? We gods are forever suffering the cruelest things at one another’s hands, whenever we do men a kindness. And every one of us blames you. You fathered that mad, ruinous girl, who is always set on some outrage. The rest of us, all the gods on Olympus, obey you — but her you never rein in with a word or a blow; you let her run loose, because you gave that wrecker life yourself. Now she’s egged on the son of Tydeus, proud Diomedes, to vent his rage on the immortal gods. First he closed with the Cyprian and wounded her on the hand by the wrist; and then he came at me, like something more than a man. My quick feet carried me off, or I’d be lying there a long time yet in agony among the foul heaps of the dead — or living on, gutted and unstrung by the strokes of the bronze.”

Zeus the cloud-gatherer scowled at him: “Don’t come whining to me, you two-faced thing. Of all the gods who hold Olympus, you are the most hateful to me. Forever strife is dear to you, and wars, and fighting. You have your mother Hera’s own unbending, ungovernable temper — I can barely master her with words. It’s her doing, I think, that you’re in this state. Still, I won’t let you go on suffering — you are my own child; your mother bore you to me. But if you had been born so destructive to any other god, you’d long since have been flung down lower than the sons of Heaven.” With that he told Paeon to heal him, and Paeon spread soothing herbs on the wound and cured him, for he was not made to die. As fig juice curdles white milk, liquid one moment and thickened in a moment under the stirring hand, so swiftly he healed furious Ares. Hebe bathed him and dressed him in fine clothes, and he sat down beside Zeus, son of Cronus, glorying in his splendor. And Argive Hera and Athena of Alalcomenae, now that they had stopped murderous Ares from his slaughter of men, drove back to the house of great Zeus.
