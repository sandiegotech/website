The Iliad, Book XVI

The Death of Patroclus

Book XVI16 of 24

So they fought on around the ship of Protesilaus. And Patroclus came to Achilles, shepherd of the people, the tears running hot down his face like a dark spring that spills its water down the face of a cliff. Achilles, swift of foot, saw him and pitied him, and spoke: “Why are you crying, Patroclus, like a little girl who runs beside her mother and begs to be picked up, clutching at her dress, holding her back though she’s in a hurry, and looks up at her in tears until she lifts her? Those are the tears you’re shedding now. Have you something to tell the Myrmidons, or me? Some news from Phthia that only you have heard? They say Menoetius, son of Actor, is still alive, and Peleus, son of Aeacus, still lives among the Myrmidons — we’d grieve hard for either of them. Or is it the Greeks you’re weeping for, the way they’re dying at the hollow ships, paying for their own arrogance? Don’t hide it. Tell me, so we both can know.”

With a heavy groan you answered him, Patroclus, master of horses: “Achilles, son of Peleus, far the bravest of the Greeks — don’t be angry. The Greeks are in too much pain. The best of them, all the men who were our shield, are lying among the ships, hit by spear or sword. Diomedes, Tydeus’s strong son, is wounded; Odysseus is speared, and Agamemnon too; Eurypylus has an arrow in his thigh, and the healers are crowded around them with their drugs, tending their wounds. But you — there’s no reaching you, Achilles. May no rage like the one you nurse ever take hold of me, you with your ruinous courage. What use will you be to anyone in the years to come, if you won’t save the Greeks from this shameful ruin now? You have no pity in you. Peleus was never your father, nor Thetis your mother — the gray sea bore you, and the sheer cliffs, your heart is so hard. But if some prophecy holds you back, something your mother brought you from Zeus, then at least send me out, and the rest of the Myrmidons with me, and maybe I’ll be a light to the Greeks. And give me your armor to wear on my shoulders — the Trojans might take me for you and pull back from the fight, and the worn-out sons of the Greeks could breathe again. There’s little enough breathing room in war. We’re fresh; we could easily drive tired men back from our ships and tents to their own city.”

So he begged, the fool — for what he was begging for was his own death, his own black end. Achilles, deeply troubled, answered him: “What are you saying, Patroclus, prince that you are? No prophecy is holding me back, none that I know of; my mother has brought me nothing from Zeus. It’s only this — a terrible grief comes over my heart and spirit when a man wants to rob one who is his equal and tear away the prize, just because he holds more power. After all I’ve been through, that is more than I can bear. The girl the sons of the Greeks chose out for me, that I won with my own spear when I sacked a walled city — King Agamemnon snatched her back out of my hands, as if I were some drifter with no honor at all. But let it go; what’s done is done. I never meant to keep my anger forever. I said I wouldn’t let it rest until the war cry reached my own ships. So now — put my famous armor on your shoulders and lead the war-loving Myrmidons out, since the dark cloud of the Trojans has broken over the ships, and the Greeks are penned against the lip of the sea with only a thin strip of ground left to them, and the whole city of Troy has come boldly out against them. They no longer see the gleam of my helmet flash before their eyes. If only King Agamemnon had treated me right, they’d have filled the ditches with their own dead as they ran. As it is, the Trojans are fighting around our camp, and the spear of Diomedes no longer rages in his hands to keep death from the Greeks, and I’ve not heard the voice of the son of Atreus shouting from his hateful head — only murderous Hector’s, ringing out everywhere as he calls the Trojans on, and they hold the whole plain with their cry, beating the Greeks down. Even so, Patroclus, fall on them hard and save the ships, before they burn them and cut off our way home. But do exactly as I tell you, so you win me great honor and glory from all the Greeks, and they hand the girl back to me with splendid gifts besides. Once you’ve driven them from the ships, come back. Even if Hera’s loud-thundering husband puts the chance of triumph in your hands, don’t go fighting the Trojans on your own, with me away — you’ll only strip me of my honor. And don’t, in the joy of slaughter, keep killing your way toward Troy and lead the Greeks up to the city — some god from Olympus may come down against you. Apollo, who strikes from afar, loves the Trojans dearly. Turn back the moment you’ve saved the ships, and let the rest of them fight it out on the plain. By Father Zeus, and Athena, and Apollo — if only not one of all the Trojans could escape death, and not one of the Greeks, and the two of us alone could be left to tear away the holy veil from the brow of Troy.”

So they spoke to each other. But Ajax could hold his ground no longer; the storm of missiles was beating him down. The will of Zeus and the spears of the proud Trojans were too much for him. The bright helmet around his temples rang with the constant clatter of weapons striking the well-made cheek-pieces, and his left shoulder ached from holding the broad gleaming shield so long; yet for all they hurled at him, they could not shake him from his place. His breath came hard, the sweat poured from every limb, and there was no rest anywhere — only danger heaped on danger.

Tell me now, you Muses who hold your homes on Olympus, how fire first fell on the ships of the Greeks. Hector came in close and struck Ajax’s ashen spear with his great sword, just behind the head where the point joins the shaft, and sheared it clean through. Ajax was left brandishing a headless pole, while the bronze point flew off and rang on the ground far away. He knew the work of the gods in this, and his heart shuddered: Zeus, who thunders on high, had cut his fighting short and willed the victory for the Trojans. So he gave way, and they threw tireless fire on the ship, and the flame poured over it in an instant and would not be quenched.

The fire was already licking around the stern when Achilles struck both his thighs with his hands and cried out to Patroclus: “Up now, prince Patroclus, master of horses — I see the glare of the enemy’s fire at the ships. Don’t let them take our ships and leave us no way home. Get into your armor, fast — I’ll call the men together.”

And Patroclus armed himself in gleaming bronze. First he fastened the greaves around his shins, fine ones, fitted with silver ankle-clasps. Around his chest he buckled the breastplate of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus, starry and richly worked. Over his shoulders he slung the bronze sword with its silver studs, and then the great strong shield. On his proud head he set the well-made helmet with its horsehair crest, and the plume nodded down terribly above it. He took up two stout spears that fit his grip — but not the spear of Achilles himself, heavy and huge and strong, for no other Greek could wield it; Achilles alone could swing it, that Pelian ash that Cheiron had cut on a mountaintop and given to his father, to deal out death to heroes. He told Automedon to yoke the horses quickly — the man he honored most after Achilles, the one he trusted most to stand by him in the crush of battle. So Automedon yoked the swift horses Xanthus and Balius, who flew like the wind; the storm-mare Podarge had borne them to the West Wind as she grazed in a meadow by the stream of Ocean. And in the side-trace he set the matchless horse Pedasus, that Achilles had brought back when he sacked Eetion’s city — a mortal horse, but he kept pace with the immortal pair.

Meanwhile Achilles went through the huts and ordered all the Myrmidons to arm. Like ravening wolves with the fury still in their hearts, that have brought down a great antlered stag in the hills and are tearing at it, their jaws all red — and then they go in a pack to lap the dark water from a spring with their thin tongues, belching blood and slaughter, the heart in their chests unflinching, their bellies full — so the leaders and captains of the Myrmidons swarmed around the brave companion of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus, and among them stood Achilles himself, urging on the horses and the men with their shields.

Fifty fast ships Achilles had brought to Troy, and in each there were fifty men at the oars. Over them he set five captains he could trust, while he commanded them all. Menesthius of the glittering breastplate led the first company, son of the heaven-fed river Spercheius; lovely Polydora, Peleus’s daughter, bore him to the tireless river, a woman lying with a god, though in name he was the son of Borus, son of Perieres, who married her openly and gave a great bride-price. The second was led by warlike Eudorus, son of an unwed girl: Polymele, Phylas’s daughter, lovely in the dance, bore him — the strong slayer of Argus, Hermes, had desired her when he saw her among the singing girls at a dance for Artemis of the golden arrows, and went up with her into an upper room and lay with her in secret, and she bore him a brilliant son, swift on his feet and fierce in battle. When Eileithyia, goddess of childbirth’s pains, had brought him into the light and he saw the rays of the sun, mighty Echecles, Actor’s son, took the mother home for a great price, but old Phylas raised the boy and cherished him as if he were his own. The third company was led by warlike Peisander, Maemalus’s son, the finest spearman among all the Myrmidons after Achilles’ own companion. The old horseman Phoenix led the fourth, and Alcimedon, Laerces’s noble son, the fifth.

When Achilles had ranked them all in good order behind their captains, he gave them a hard charge: “Myrmidons — don’t forget the threats you flung at the Trojans down by the ships through all the time of my anger, and how you blamed me. ‘Cruel son of Peleus,’ you’d say, ‘your mother nursed you on bile, you’re so merciless — you hold us here at the ships against our will. If you’re so set on your rage, we’d be better off sailing home.’ You said such things, gathered in your groups. Well, here it is — the great work of war you’ve been aching for all along. So let every man fight the Trojans with the heart he has in him.”

With those words he fired the spirit in every one of them, and the ranks pulled tighter when they heard their king. As a man fits stone to stone in the wall of a high house, to keep out the force of the winds, so close were the helmets and the bossed shields packed — shield leaning on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man, the horsehair plumes on the bright ridges touching as the heads bent together.

Out in front of them all, two men armed with a single mind to lead the Myrmidons — Patroclus and Automedon. Then Achilles went into his hut and opened the lid of a beautiful, well-wrought chest that silver-footed Thetis had set on his ship for him to take, filled with tunics and cloaks against the wind and thick warm rugs. In it lay a cup of rare make; no other man drank wine from it, and he poured a libation from it to no god but Father Zeus. He took it out, cleansed it with sulphur, rinsed it in clear running water, washed his own hands, and drew the bright wine. Then he stood in the middle of the yard and prayed, his eyes lifted to the sky, pouring out the wine — and Zeus, who delights in the thunder, did not fail to see him. “Lord Zeus of Dodona, Pelasgian, you who dwell far off and rule over wintry Dodona, where your interpreters the Selloi live around you, men with unwashed feet who sleep on the ground — you heard me once before when I prayed, and you honored me and struck the Greeks hard. Grant me this too, now. I will stay here where the ships are gathered, but I’m sending my companion out to fight with the Myrmidons in their numbers. Send glory out with him, far-seeing Zeus; put courage in his heart, so Hector may learn whether my companion knows how to fight alone, or whether his hands only rage so unstoppably when I myself go into the storm of war. And when he has driven the battle and the war cry from the ships, then let him come back to me unharmed, with his armor and all his close-fighting men.”

So he prayed, and Zeus, the lord of counsel, heard him. The Father granted him one half and refused the other. He granted that Patroclus would beat back the battle and the fighting from the ships — but he refused to let him come safe out of the fight.

When he had poured the libation and made his prayer, Achilles went back into his hut and put the cup away in the chest. Then he came out again and stood there, for his heart still longed to watch the terrible struggle of Trojans and Greeks.

And the men around brave Patroclus marched out and fell on the Trojans full of fight. They came pouring forth like wasps along the road that thoughtless boys love to torment, stirring them up where they nest by the way — the silly fools, and they make trouble for many a passerby, for if some traveler brushes them by accident, every wasp comes flying out with a fierce heart to defend its young. So the Myrmidons came swarming from the ships with hearts and courage like theirs, and their war cry rose to heaven. Patroclus shouted to his men with all his voice: “Myrmidons, companions of Achilles, son of Peleus — be men, my friends, remember your fighting strength, so we win glory for the son of Peleus, far the best of the men at the ships of the Greeks, he and his close-fighting men — and so the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon, learns his blindness, that he gave no honor to the best of the Greeks.”

With those words he fired the spirit in every one of them, and they fell on the Trojans in one mass, and the ships rang terribly around them with the shout of the Greeks. When the Trojans saw the brave son of Menoetius and his companion blazing in their armor, their hearts shook and the ranks broke into confusion — they thought the swift-footed son of Peleus had thrown off his anger by the ships and made his peace, and every man looked around for some way to escape sheer death.

Patroclus was the first to throw, straight into the thick of the crowd where they were jammed most densely, by the stern of brave Protesilaus’s ship. He hit Pyraechmes, who had led his Paeonian horsemen from Amydon by the broad waters of the Axius; the spear caught him in the right shoulder, and he fell back in the dust with a groan, and his men scattered around him, for Patroclus had killed their leader, the best of them all, and struck the fight out of them. So he drove them off the ships and quenched the blazing fire, leaving the half-burned ship to lie there. The Trojans fled with a deafening cry, and the Greeks poured out after them among the hollow ships, and the noise never stopped. As when Zeus, who gathers the lightning, lifts a thick cloud off the peak of a high mountain, and all the lookout points and jutting headlands and forest glades stand out, and the boundless air breaks open from the sky — so the Greeks, having beaten the deadly fire back from the ships, caught their breath a little. But the fight was not yet over: the Trojans were not yet turned in headlong rout, but still gave way only because they had to, fighting all the while.

Then the battle scattered, leader hunting leader, and the killing began. First the brave son of Menoetius drove his spear into the thigh of Areilycus as he turned away; the bronze went clean through and broke the bone, and he pitched forward on his face. Menelaus, dear to Ares, struck Thoas in the chest where it showed above the shield’s rim, and his limbs gave way beneath him. Phyleus’s son saw Amphiclus coming for him and got in first, hitting him high on the leg where a man’s muscle is thickest; the spearpoint tore through the sinews, and darkness covered his eyes. Of Nestor’s sons, Antilochus speared Atymnius through the flank, and he fell forward; and Maris, in fury over his brother, lunged at Antilochus across the body, but brilliant Thrasymedes was too quick and struck before he could — caught him square in the shoulder, and the spearpoint sheared the muscle from the arm and tore it down to the bone, and he fell with a crash and darkness took his eyes. So those two brothers, comrades of Sarpedon, went down into the dark, the warrior sons of Amisodarus, the man who reared the raging Chimaera, the ruin of many. And Ajax, son of Oileus, rushed Cleobulus and took him alive, tangled in the press, then loosed his strength on the spot with a sword-stroke to the neck; the whole blade ran warm with blood, and dark death and strong fate closed over his eyes.

Peneleos and Lycon ran together, for they had both missed with their spears, and now they drew their swords. Lycon struck the ridge of the other’s plumed helmet, and his blade snapped at the hilt; but Peneleos caught him in the neck under the ear, and the sword sank in so deep that the head hung by nothing but a fold of skin, and the life went out of him. Meriones chased Acamas down on foot and caught him just as he was climbing to his chariot, and ran a spear through his right shoulder; he tumbled from the chariot, and the mist closed over his eyes. Idomeneus speared Erymas through the mouth; the pitiless bronze drove straight on under the brain, splitting the white bones, and his teeth were shaken loose, and both his eyes filled with blood, and the blood came spurting up through his mouth and nostrils as the black cloud of death wrapped him round.

So these leaders of the Greeks each killed his man. As ravening wolves fall on lambs or kids, snatching them from under the flock when a careless shepherd has let them stray on the hills, and the wolves take them at once because they have no fight in them — so the Greeks fell on the Trojans, who turned to shameful flight and forgot their fighting strength.

And all this while great Ajax kept trying to hurl his spear at bronze-helmeted Hector, but Hector knew his craft of war: he kept his broad shoulders covered behind his bull’s-hide shield, watching for the hiss of arrows and the thud of spears. Well he knew the day’s tide had turned, but even so he held his ground and tried to save his trusted men.

As when a cloud climbs from Olympus into the bright sky after fair weather, when Zeus stretches out the storm — so the Trojans broke from the ships now in panic and rout, and there was no order in their going. Hector’s swift horses carried him out of the fight, armor and all, and he left the Trojan army trapped behind them, pinned against the trench they could not cross. Many a chariot’s pole snapped in the trench, and the horses tore loose, leaving their masters’ chariots behind. Patroclus came on hard after them, shouting to the Greeks, his heart full of death for the Trojans, who filled all the ground between with their cries as they fled, broken; the dust rose in a cloud to the sky, and the horses strained at full stretch back toward the city, away from the ships and the tents.

Wherever Patroclus saw the men most jammed in their panic, he drove his horses straight at them, shouting all the while; under his wheels men kept pitching headlong from their chariots, and the chariots crashed and overturned. Straight across the trench at a single bound went his immortal horses, the splendid gift the gods had given to Peleus, for he was set on reaching Hector — he was wild to spear him — but Hector’s swift horses were already carrying him away. As the whole black earth groans under a storm in the autumn, when Zeus pours the hardest rain in his anger at men who give crooked judgments in the assembly and drive justice out, with no regard for the watching gods — and all the rivers run in flood and the torrents cut new channels down the hillsides, roaring headlong to the dark sea, and the works of men are ruined — so loud the Trojan horses groaned and labored in their flight.

Patroclus cut off the front of their broken ranks and drove them back toward the ships; he would not let them reach the city they were straining for, but pressed them in the space between the ships and the river and the high wall, and there he killed and killed and made them pay for the dead. First he hit Pronous in the chest where it showed beside the shield’s rim, and his limbs gave way; then he sprang on Thestor, son of Enops, who sat huddled in his polished chariot, out of his wits, the reins gone from his hands. Patroclus came up and drove the spear into his right jaw and through his teeth, and hauled him by it over the rail of the chariot — the way a man on a jutting rock pulls a great fish from the sea on his line and bright hook — so he dragged him gaping from his chariot, and threw him down on his face, and the life left him as he fell. Next, as Erylaus charged him, he caught him full on the head with a stone, and the whole head split inside the heavy helmet, and he dropped face-down on the ground, and death that breaks the spirit poured over him. Then one after another he laid low Erymas, Amphoterus, Epaltes, Tlepolemus, Echius and Pyris, Ipheus and Euippus and Polymelus, son of Argeas — heaped them all on the nourishing earth.

But when Sarpedon saw his comrades going down under the hands of Patroclus, the son of Menoetius, he called out to the godlike Lycians and rebuked them: “Shame, Lycians — where are you running? Stand and fight. I’ll face this man myself and find out who it is who rules the field here and has done the Trojans so much harm, cutting the knees out from under so many brave men.”

He sprang from his chariot in his armor, and on the other side Patroclus saw him and leapt down too. They flew at each other crying aloud, like hook-clawed, hook-beaked vultures that scream and tear on a high rock. And the son of crooked-counseling Cronus looked down and pitied them, and said to Hera, his sister and his wife: “This is bitter — that Sarpedon, the man I love most, is fated to die at the hands of Patroclus, the son of Menoetius. My heart is torn two ways. Shall I snatch him up out of the tears of battle, alive, and set him down in the rich land of Lycia? Or shall I let him fall now under Patroclus’s hands?”

And ox-eyed lady Hera answered: “Dread son of Cronus, what a thing to say. A mortal man, marked for death long ago by his fate — and you want to free him from death and all its sorrow? Do it, if you must, but the rest of us gods won’t praise you for it. And I tell you this, and lay it to your heart: if you send Sarpedon home alive, then think — some other god will want to carry his own son out of the hard fight too, for there are many sons of gods battling around Priam’s great city, and you’ll set bitter envy among them. No. If you love him, if your heart grieves for him, then let him fall in the strong battle under Patroclus’s hands; but when the life and the spirit have left him, send Death and gentle Sleep to carry him away, until they bring him to the broad land of Lycia, where his brothers and his kin will bury him with mound and pillar — for that is the honor owed the dead.”

So she spoke, and the father of gods and men agreed. And he rained drops of blood down on the earth, in honor of his dear son, whom Patroclus was about to kill on the deep soil of Troy, far from his own home.

When the two had come up close to each other, Patroclus struck the famous Thrasymelus, the brave companion of lord Sarpedon, in the lower belly, and brought him down. Then Sarpedon cast and missed Patroclus, but his spear caught the horse Pedasus in the right shoulder; the horse screamed as the life went out of him, then fell in the dust with a groan, and his spirit flew away. The other two reared apart, the yoke creaked, the reins tangled where the trace-horse had gone down in the dust. But Automedon, famous spearman, found the cure: he drew the long sword from his sturdy thigh, leapt in, and cut the trace-horse loose, and the other two settled and pulled straight in the reins again. And the two men closed once more in the heart-eating struggle.

Again Sarpedon cast, and again he missed; the spearpoint passed over Patroclus’s left shoulder and did not touch him. Then Patroclus drove home in his turn, and the bronze did not fly from his hand in vain: he caught Sarpedon where the midriff guards the beating heart. He fell as an oak falls, or a white poplar, or a tall slender pine that shipwrights cut down in the mountains with their sharp axes for ship-timber — so he lay stretched out before his horses and his chariot, groaning, clutching at the bloody dust. As a lion goes in among a herd and kills a bull, a great tawny high-hearted bull among the shambling cattle, and it bellows and dies under the lion’s jaws — so under Patroclus the leader of the Lycian shield-bearers struggled, dying, and called his dear companion by name: “Glaucus, dear friend, fighter among fighters — now is the time to be a spearman, a man of nerve. Now, if ever, let this evil war be your heart’s desire. First go through all the Lycian captains and rouse them to fight for Sarpedon, and then fight for me yourself, over my body, with the bronze. I’ll be your shame, your reproach, all your days forever, if the Greeks strip the armor from me here, fallen among the ships. Hold hard, and call all the men to me.” As he spoke, the end that is death closed over his eyes and nostrils. And Patroclus set his heel on his chest and pulled the spear from the flesh, and the midriff came with it — he drew out the spearpoint and Sarpedon’s life in a single motion. And close by, the Myrmidons held the snorting horses, wild to bolt now that they had left their masters’ chariot.

Terrible grief came over Glaucus when he heard Sarpedon’s voice, and it tore his heart that he could not save him. He gripped his own arm and held it, for it pained him — the wound that Teucer’s arrow had dealt him from the wall, defending his men, when Glaucus had come against it. So he prayed to Apollo, who strikes from afar: “Hear me, lord, wherever you are — in the rich land of Lycia, or here in Troy — for you can hear a man in distress everywhere, and distress is on me now. I have this cruel wound; sharp pains shoot all through my arm, the blood won’t stop, my shoulder is heavy with it, and I cannot hold a spear steady or go and fight the enemy. And the best of men is dead, Sarpedon, son of Zeus — and Zeus did not even defend his own son. But you, lord — heal this cruel wound of mine, lull the pains, give me strength, so I can call the Lycians to the fight and do battle myself over the body of the dead.”

So he prayed, and Phoebus Apollo heard him. At once he stilled the pains, staunched the dark blood in the cruel wound, and put strength into his heart. Glaucus knew it, and rejoiced that the great god had heard his prayer so quickly. He went among the Lycian captains and roused them to fight for Sarpedon; then he strode off into the Trojans, to Polydamas, son of Panthous, and to brilliant Agenor, and to Aeneas and bronze-helmeted Hector, and stood beside them and let his words fly: “Hector — you have utterly forgotten your allies, the men wasting their lives for you, far from their friends and their own land, and you will not lift a hand to save them. Sarpedon lies dead, the leader of the Lycian shield-bearers, who held Lycia safe by his judgment and his strength — brazen Ares has brought him down under the spear of Patroclus. Stand by him, friends, and let your hearts burn with shame, in case the Myrmidons strip his armor and shame his body, in revenge for all the Greeks we have killed by the ships.”

So he spoke, and a grief past bearing and past control swept through the Trojans, for Sarpedon, though a foreigner, had been a great pillar of their city; he had brought many men, and was the best of them all. Led by Hector, raging now over the fall of Sarpedon, they came straight at the Greeks with all their fury. And the unflinching heart of Patroclus, son of Menoetius, drove the Greeks on. He spoke first to the two Ajaxes, men who needed no urging: “Ajaxes — now let it please you to fight as you have always fought, or better. The man who was first to break over the wall of the Greeks lies dead — Sarpedon. Now let us take him, and shame his body, and strip the armor from his shoulders, and cut down with the bronze any of his comrades who try to save him.”

But they were already burning for it. And both sides braced their ranks — Trojans and Lycians, Myrmidons and Greeks — and joined in the fight over the dead man with a fearful shout, and the clash of their armor was huge. And Zeus stretched a deadly night over the bitter struggle, so the toil over his dear son would be all the worse.

At first the Trojans drove the bright-eyed Greeks back, for a man was killed who was far from the worst of the Myrmidons, brave Epeigeus, son of great-hearted Agacles, who had once been king in well-built Budeum, but had killed a noble kinsman and fled for refuge to Peleus and silver-footed Thetis, and they had sent him with Achilles, breaker of men, to Troy of the fine horses, to fight the Trojans. As he laid hold of the corpse, glorious Hector struck him on the head with a stone, and the whole head split inside the heavy helmet, and he fell face-down across the body, and death that breaks the heart poured over him. And grief for his dead companion seized Patroclus, and he charged through the front ranks like a swift hawk that scatters jackdaws and starlings — straight at the Lycians, Patroclus, master of horses, and at the Trojans, your heart enraged for your friend. He caught Sthenelaus, the dear son of Ithaemenes, on the neck with a stone, and tore the tendons from the bone. And Hector gave ground, and the front ranks with him — as far as a long javelin flies when a man hurls it, trying his strength in a contest or in war, so far did the Trojans give way before the Greeks. Then Glaucus, leader of the Lycian shield-bearers, was the first to turn, and he killed great-hearted Bathycles, the dear son of Chalcon, who had lived in Hellas, the richest of the Myrmidons. Glaucus wheeled suddenly and speared him through the middle of the chest just as he closed to overtake him, and he fell with a crash. The Greeks felt the loss of a brave man hard, and the Trojans rejoiced and crowded around him, but the Greeks did not forget their fighting strength, and bore straight down on them.

Then Meriones killed an armed warrior of the Trojans, Laogonus, the brave son of Onetor, priest of Zeus of Ida, honored by his people like a god. Meriones struck him under the jaw and ear, and the life rushed out of his limbs and the hateful dark took him. Then Aeneas cast at Meriones, hoping to catch him under the shield as he came on, but Meriones, watching, ducked the bronze, leaning forward, and the long spear stuck quivering in the ground behind him, until raging Ares drained the force from it. And Aeneas, his heart hot, called out: “Meriones — you may be a fine dancer, but my spear would have stopped your dancing for good if I’d hit you.” And Meriones, famous spearman, answered: “Aeneas, strong as you are, you can hardly quench the fight in every man who comes against you. You are mortal too. If I caught you square with the bronze, then for all your strength and the trust in your own hands, you would give me the glory, and your life to Hades of the famous horses.” But the brave son of Menoetius rebuked him: “Meriones, why talk like this, brave as you are? My friend, the Trojans won’t draw off from the body for hard words — the earth will hold many a man first. War is settled with the hands; counsel with words. No more talk now — fight.”

And he led the way, and the godlike man went with him. As the din of woodcutters rises from a mountain glade, and the sound of it carries far, so a clamor rose from the wide earth — the clash of bronze and good ox-hide shields, as the swords and double-edged spears struck home. By now no man, however keen his eyes, could have known noble Sarpedon, for he was buried from head to foot in weapons and blood and dust. And still they thronged around the body, like flies that buzz in a sheepfold around the brimming milk-pails in the season of spring, when the milk overflows. So they swarmed around the corpse. And Zeus never turned his shining eyes from the fierce fight, but watched it all the while, his mind working over the death of Patroclus, weighing whether glorious Hector should kill him then and there, in the struggle over godlike Sarpedon, and strip the armor from his shoulders, or whether he should let him bring still more men to ruin first. And in the end he judged it better that the brave companion of Achilles, son of Peleus, should drive the Trojans and bronze-helmeted Hector back toward the city once more, and tear the life from many. So first he made Hector’s heart turn weak; he mounted his chariot and fled, and called the rest of the Trojans to flee, for he saw that the sacred scales of Zeus had turned. And then not even the strong Lycians stood; they all gave way when they saw their king lying with a spear through his heart, in the heap of the dead — for many had fallen over him when the son of Cronus drew the hard fight tight. So the Greeks stripped the gleaming armor from Sarpedon’s shoulders, and the brave son of Menoetius gave it to his men to carry to the hollow ships. Then Zeus, who gathers the clouds, said to Apollo: “Up now, dear Phoebus — go, lift Sarpedon out of range of the weapons, wipe the dark blood from him, and carry him far off and wash him in a running river, and anoint him with ambrosia, and wrap him in immortal clothing. Then give him to the swift escorts to carry, Sleep and Death, the twin brothers, who will set him down quickly in the rich land of Lycia, where his brothers and his kin will bury him with mound and pillar — for that is the honor owed the dead.”

So he spoke, and Apollo did not fail to hear his father. He came down from the hills of Ida into the dread struggle, and lifted godlike Sarpedon clear of the weapons, and carried him far off and washed him in a running river, anointed him with ambrosia, and wrapped him in immortal clothing. Then he gave him to the swift escorts to carry, Sleep and Death, the twin brothers, who set him down quickly in the rich land of Lycia.

But Patroclus, shouting to his horses and to Automedon, drove on after the Trojans and Lycians — the great blindness on him, the fool. Had he kept the word of the son of Peleus, he would have escaped the black fate of death. But the mind of Zeus is always stronger than a man’s; he frightens even a brave man and snatches the victory from him, and at other times drives a man to fight — as now he put the fury in Patroclus’s heart. Who then was the first you killed, Patroclus, and who the last, when the gods had called you to your death? Adrestus first, then Autonous, and Echeclus, and Perimus, son of Megas, and Epistor, and Melanippus; and after them Elasus, and Mulius, and Pylartes. Those he killed; the rest were glad to run.

And now the sons of the Greeks would have taken high-gated Troy by the hands of Patroclus, so wild was his spear in every direction — had not Phoebus Apollo taken his stand on the strong-built tower, with deadly intent toward him and help for the Trojans. Three times Patroclus charged the angle of the high wall, and three times Apollo flung him back, beating his bright shield with his immortal hands. But when he came on a fourth time, like something more than human, Apollo cried out to him in a terrible voice: “Back, prince Patroclus. It is not your fate to sack the city of the proud Trojans with your spear — no, nor Achilles’, who is far the better man than you.” At that Patroclus drew well back, dreading the anger of Apollo, who strikes from afar.

Hector was holding his horses at the Scaean gates, in two minds: should he drive back into the rout and go on fighting, or call the army inside the walls? While he wavered, Phoebus Apollo came up beside him in the shape of a young strong man, Asius, who was Hector’s own uncle, full brother to Hecuba and son of Dymas, who lived in Phrygia by the streams of the Sangarius. In his likeness Apollo, son of Zeus, said to him: “Hector — why have you left off fighting? You should not. If only I were as much stronger than you as I am weaker, you’d soon be sorry you hung back. Come — drive your horses straight at Patroclus, and see if Apollo grants you the kill and the glory.”

With that the god went back into the toil of men. And glorious Hector told Cebriones to whip the horses into the fight. And Apollo went in among them and struck panic into the Greeks, and gave the glory to the Trojans and to Hector. Hector let the other Greeks be and killed none of them; straight at Patroclus he drove his horses. And Patroclus, on the other side, leapt from his chariot to the ground, a spear in his left hand, and in his right a jagged stone, all his hand could hold. He braced and threw it — and it did not travel far without finding a man, nor was the cast wasted: it struck Cebriones, Hector’s charioteer, a bastard son of glorious Priam, full on the forehead as he held the reins, and the stone crushed both his brows together and the bone gave way, and his eyes dropped at his feet into the dust, and he plunged from the well-wrought chariot like a diver, and the life left his bones. And you mocked him then, Patroclus, master of horses: “Well now — there’s a nimble man, the way he dives. If he were out at sea, this fellow would feed a whole crew, diving for oysters, going over the side in any weather, the way he’s just dived from his chariot onto the plain. So — there are divers among the Trojans too.”

And he sprang at the body of Cebriones with the spring of a lion that, ravaging a fold, takes a spear in the chest, and his own courage destroys him — so you leapt, Patroclus, on Cebriones. Hector, on the other side, leapt down from his chariot, and the two fought over Cebriones like two lions on a mountain height, fighting over a fallen deer, both starved, both high of heart — so over Cebriones the two masters of the war cry, Patroclus, son of Menoetius, and glorious Hector, strained to cut each other with the pitiless bronze. Hector had hold of the head and would not let go; Patroclus gripped the feet; and around them the rest of the Trojans and Greeks joined in the hard struggle. As the east wind and the south wind strive against each other in a mountain glen to shake a deep wood — oak and ash and smooth-barked cornel, and the long boughs lash one another with a great roar, and the snapping of the broken branches carries far — so the Trojans and the Greeks fell on each other, hacking, and neither side thought of ruinous flight. Many sharp spears stood fast in the ground around Cebriones, and many winged arrows sprang from the strings, and many great stones beat on the shields of the men fighting around him — and there in the whirl of dust he lay, mighty in his might, his horsemanship forgotten.

While the sun stood astride mid-heaven, the weapons of both sides reached their mark and the men fell on both sides alike. But when the sun came round to the hour when the oxen are unyoked, then the Greeks proved the stronger, beyond all fate; they dragged the hero Cebriones out from under the weapons and the Trojan shouting, and stripped the armor from his shoulders. And Patroclus charged the Trojans with murder in his heart. Three times he charged, fast as the war god Ares, with a terrible cry, and three times he killed nine men. But when he came on a fourth time, like something more than human — then, Patroclus, the end of your life appeared. For Phoebus met you in the hard fight, terrible now. Patroclus did not see him coming through the rout, for the god came against him wrapped in thick mist. He stood behind him and struck his back and his broad shoulders with the flat of his hand, and Patroclus’s eyes spun. And Phoebus Apollo knocked the helmet from his head, and it rolled clattering away under the horses’ feet, and the plumes were fouled with blood and dust — that helmet which the gods had never let be soiled before, that had guarded the head and noble brow of godlike Achilles. But now Zeus gave it to Hector to wear on his own head, and his own death was near. And the long heavy spear, bronze-tipped, broke in Patroclus’s hands, and the great fringed shield with its strap fell from his shoulders, and lord Apollo, son of Zeus, loosed his breastplate.

Blindness took his mind, and his shining limbs gave way beneath him, and he stood there stunned. Then from close behind a Dardanian struck him in the back with a sharp spear, between the shoulders — Euphorbus, son of Panthous, who outdid all the young men of his age with the spear and the horses and his running feet; already he had brought twenty men down from their chariots, the first time he came out to learn the war. He was the first to hit you with a spear, Patroclus, master of horses, but he did not bring you down. He pulled the ashen spear out of the wound and ran back into the crowd; he would not stand and face Patroclus, naked of armor though he was, in the open fight. And Patroclus, broken by the god’s blow and the spear, drew back into the company of his men, away from death. But Hector, when he saw great-hearted Patroclus giving ground, wounded by the sharp bronze, came at him through the ranks and struck him with a spear, low in the belly, and drove the bronze clean through, and he fell with a crash, and a great anguish took hold of the Greeks. As a lion overpowers a tireless boar in a fight, when the two of them battle with high hearts on a mountain ridge over a little spring where they both want to drink, and the lion’s strength masters the panting boar — so Hector, son of Priam, came up close and tore the life from the brave son of Menoetius, who had killed so many. And he stood over him and let his words fly: “Patroclus — so you thought you would sack our city, and rob the Trojan women of the day of their freedom, and carry them off in your ships to your own land. You fool. In front of all that, Hector’s swift horses go out to fight; and I, of all the war-loving Trojans, am the best spear to keep the day of slavery from them. As for you, the vultures will eat you here. Poor wretch — Achilles, for all his courage, was no help to you. I’m sure he charged you sternly, staying behind while you went out: ‘Don’t come back to me, Patroclus, master of horses, at the hollow ships, until you’ve torn the bloody shirt on murderous Hector’s chest.’ That, I think, is what he told you, and your foolish heart said yes.”

Then, with the life going out of you, you answered, Patroclus, master of horses: “Boast loud now, Hector. Zeus, the son of Cronus, and Apollo gave you the victory, and they beat me — it was easy for them; they stripped the armor from my shoulders themselves. If twenty men like you had come against me, all of them would have died here under my spear. No — it was deadly fate and the son of Leto who killed me, and of men, Euphorbus; you come only third in the killing of me. And I’ll tell you one thing more; lay it to your heart. You yourself won’t live long now. Already death and strong fate are standing close at your side, and you will go down under the hands of blameless Achilles, the grandson of Aeacus.”

As he spoke, the end that is death closed over him, and his spirit flew from his limbs and went down to the house of Hades, mourning its fate, leaving manhood and youth behind. And though he was dead, glorious Hector still answered him: “Patroclus, why do you foretell my death? Who knows whether Achilles, the son of fair-haired Thetis, may not lose his own life first, struck down by my spear?” And as he spoke he set his heel on the body, drew the bronze spear from the wound, and shoved him off it onto his back. Then he went straight for Automedon, the godlike companion of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus, eager to spear him too — but the immortal horses bore him away, the swift, splendid horses the gods had given to Peleus.
