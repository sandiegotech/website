The Iliad

Homer

  • 800–701 BCE
  • Western tradition
  • Epic poem
  • Lectio Edition 2026

Homer's poem of a few weeks in the tenth year of the war at Troy. It is about what a man does with anger, with grief, and with the certainty that he will die. The Greeks sang it for centuries before anyone wrote it down.

Why this editionGoal and purpose

Homer is fast. The old English versions are slow.

The two public-domain Iliads most people find are Pope's couplets of 1715 and Butler's prose of 1898. Pope is a great English poem and not much like Homer. Butler is plain, but he gives the gods their Roman names and writes "car" for chariot.

A reader who starts either one tends to stop in Book II. The story is not the obstacle. The English is.

The Lectio Edition is a prose Iliad in current English, all twenty-four books. It keeps the repeated epithets, because the repetition is how the poem is built. It restores the Greek names. Pope and Butler stay in the app beside it.

The choicesPolicies and divergences

What this edition decided.

These are the rules the review settled for the whole poem.

  • The first word, menis, is "rage". The hot human anger Achilles later curses, cholos, is "anger". The two are kept apart, as Homer keeps them.
  • The army is mostly "the Greeks". Homer's three names for it, Achaeans, Argives and Danaans, mean the same coalition and were chosen to fit the meter.
  • Gods and heroes have their Greek names: Zeus, Hera, Athena, Odysseus.
  • Fixed epithets are kept each time they recur: swift-footed Achilles, Hector breaker of horses.
  • A patronymic that names a remote ancestor is given as descent. Achilles is "grandson of Aeacus", not his son.
  • No Biblical or Christian idiom in a pagan poem, and no modern slang. The draft had "the spirit may be willing", "a time to mourn" and "blowhard". All were replaced with what the Greek says.
  • "Car" became "chariot" in 53 places, and leftover archaisms such as "chine" and "a whit" were replaced.

Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 73 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 7. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

μῆνις (menis)

Book I, line 1

Older
"wrath" (Pope), "anger" (Butler)
Lectio
rage

The poem's first word. Homer uses it almost only for the anger of gods. "Rage" is the current word nearest to its size.

Ἀχαιοί, Ἀργεῖοι, Δαναοί

Throughout

Older
Achaeans, Argives, Danaans
Lectio
the Greeks

Three names for one army. Keeping all three puzzles a new reader and tells him nothing. The introduction records the variety.

οἵη περ φύλλων γενεή

Book VI, lines 146 to 149

Older
"Like leaves on trees the race of man is found" (Pope)
Lectio
The generations of men are like the leaves of the trees.

The most quoted lines in the book. Butler has the forest bud with "fresh vines", which is a slip. The Greek is leaves falling and the same tree leafing again.

οὐ ληϊστή, οὐδ' ἑλετή

Book IX, lines 408 to 409

Older
"it can neither be bought nor harried back again" (Butler)
Lectio
a man's life cannot be raided back

Achilles uses of his life the same words he has just used of cattle and tripods. Everything Agamemnon offers can be replaced. A life cannot. The edition keeps the raiding words.

χόλος (cholos)

Book XVIII, lines 107 to 110

Older
"anger" or "wrath" (Butler, Pope)
Lectio
"anger", never "rage"

When Achilles curses the passion that rose in him against Agamemnon, Homer does not use the word of line one. The edition reserves "rage" for menis so the reader can feel the difference.

ἀλλὰ φίλος, θάνε καὶ σύ

Book XXI, line 106

Older
"Therefore, my friend, you too shall die" (Butler)
Lectio
So die, friend

Achilles calls the boy he is about to kill philos, one of his own. He is not being kind. He is counting Lycaon among the doomed, himself included. The address is kept bare.

Ἕκτορος ἱπποδάμοιο

Book XXIV, line 804

Older
"And peaceful slept the mighty Hector's shade" (Pope, who rewrites the ending)
Lectio
Such was the funeral of Hector, breaker of horses.

The poem ends on Hector, on a burial, and on an epithet. The edition ends where Homer ends.

The other editionsOn hand in the app

The other editions on hand.

The Lectio Edition is what opens by default. Two other editions of The Iliad are in the bundle, and switching keeps your place.

  • Alexander Pope, 1715. Stately heroic couplets: 18th-century grandeur.
  • Samuel Butler, 1898. Plain, clear Victorian prose.

The original Greek is not in the bundle yet. Bundling the original for every work is part of the plan, so that review can check against it directly.

Still open

What the first review left for the editor to decide or restore.

  • The editor's own read of all twenty-four books.
  • Book I: Achilles' retelling of the quarrel to Thetis is 28 lines of speech in Homer. The edition still gives it as a short narrated list. It has to be restored as speech, and that is new writing the editor should read.
  • Three segments are set aside, in Books V, X and XXIII. The editor has to decide whether to retire them for good or replace them with Homer's own closing lines.
  • The note that stood at the end of Book XXIII should move to the margin notes.
