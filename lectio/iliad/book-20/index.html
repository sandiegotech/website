The Iliad, Book XX

The Gods Go to War

Book XX20 of 24

So the Greeks armed for battle by the curved ships, around you, son of Peleus — around Achilles, who could not get enough of war; and across from them, on the rise of the plain, the Trojans armed too.

And Zeus, from the top of Olympus with its many folds and valleys, told Themis to call the gods to assembly. She went everywhere and summoned them to the house of Zeus. Not one river stayed away except Ocean; not one of the nymphs who haunt the lovely groves, the springs of rivers, the green meadows. They came to the house of Zeus who gathers the clouds, and took their seats in the polished marble colonnades that Hephaestus had built for their father with all his skill.

So they gathered in the house of Zeus. And Poseidon, who shakes the earth, did not ignore the goddess’s call either; he came up out of the sea to join them, and sat down in the middle of them, and asked what Zeus had in mind. “Why,” he said, “lord of the lightning, have you called the gods to council? Are you turning something over about the Trojans and the Greeks? The fighting is about to blaze up between them now.”

Zeus who gathers the clouds answered him: “Shaker of the earth, you know what I’m thinking, and why I called you here. They are about to be destroyed, and even so I care about them. As for me, I’ll sit here on a fold of Olympus and watch, and enjoy the watching. But the rest of you — go down among the Trojans and the Greeks, and help either side, whichever each of you likes. If Achilles fights the Trojans alone, with no one to hold him back, they won’t stand against him for a moment — even before this they trembled just to see him, and now that he’s wild with grief over his friend, I’m afraid he’ll storm the wall before its time, against fate itself.”

So Zeus spoke, and roused them all to a war they could not be stopped from now. The gods went down to the fighting, their hearts divided. To the Greek side and the ships went Hera, and Pallas Athena, and Poseidon who circles the earth, and Hermes the bringer of luck, sharpest of mind; and with them went Hephaestus, blazing in his strength, limping, his thin legs working hard beneath him. To the Trojan side went Ares of the flashing helmet, and with him Apollo of the long unshorn hair, and Artemis who showers arrows, and Leto, and Xanthus the river, and Aphrodite who loves laughter.

As long as the gods stayed apart from the mortal men, the Greeks had the upper hand, for Achilles had come back to them at last after his long absence from the fighting. And there was not a Trojan whose knees did not shake with fear when he saw the swift son of Peleus shining in his armor, terrible as Ares the killer of men. But the moment the Olympians came down into the crowd of men, then hard Strife rose up, the rouser of armies, and Athena cried out — now standing by the dug trench outside the wall, now shouting along the shore of the sounding sea. And from the other side Ares bellowed, dark as a black storm-cloud, calling to the Trojans at the top of his voice, now from the highest point of the city, now running along the bank of the Simois to the hill of Callicolone.

So the blessed gods drove both sides on against each other, and broke open a bitter quarrel among themselves as well. The father of gods and men thundered terribly from above; below, Poseidon made the wide earth shudder and the steep mountain peaks shake. Every spur and crest of Ida with its many springs trembled, and the city of the Trojans, and the Greek ships. Down below, the lord of the dead, Hades, was seized with fear, and sprang from his throne with a cry, terrified that Poseidon who shakes the earth would split the ground open above him and lay bare to gods and men those houses of the dead, dank and dreadful, that even the gods shrink from. Such was the crash when the gods came together in war.

Against lord Poseidon stood Phoebus Apollo with his winged arrows; against Ares stood Athena with her gray eyes; facing Hera came Artemis who showers arrows, the huntress of the golden shafts, sister of the far-shooting god; against Leto stood Hermes the strong bringer of luck; and against Hephaestus stood the great deep-eddying river the gods call Xanthus and men call Scamander.

So god was matched against god. But the heart of Achilles was set on one man — Hector, son of Priam; it was with his blood, above all, that he longed to glut Ares, the stubborn god of war. Meanwhile Apollo, who drives armies on, set Aeneas against the son of Peleus and breathed fury into him, taking the voice of Lycaon, Priam’s son. In his shape Apollo spoke: “Aeneas, counselor of the Trojans — where are they now, those bold words you made over your wine among the Trojan lords, when you swore you’d stand and fight Achilles face to face?”

And Aeneas answered him: “Son of Priam, why do you push me to fight the proud son of Peleus when I have no wish to? This wouldn’t be the first time I’ve stood against him. His spear already drove me running off Ida, that day he came down on our cattle and sacked Lyrnessus and Pedasus. Zeus saved me then — he gave me the strength to run — or I’d have gone down under the hands of Achilles and of Athena, who went in front of him lighting his way, driving him on to kill Trojans and Lelegians with his bronze. No man can fight Achilles. One of the gods is always at his side to keep harm off him; and even without that, his spear flies straight and doesn’t stop until it has gone through a man’s flesh. If a god would only hold the scales even between us, he would not beat me easily — not though he boasts he’s made all of bronze.”

Then lord Apollo, son of Zeus, said: “Hero, then pray, you too, to the everlasting gods. They say you yourself were born of Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, while Achilles is the son of a lesser goddess — Aphrodite is Zeus’s child, and Thetis is only a daughter of the old man of the sea. So drive your spear at him, and don’t let him turn you back with his hard words and his threats.”

With that he breathed great strength into the shepherd of the people, and Aeneas strode through the front ranks in his shining bronze. But white-armed Hera did not miss the son of Anchises going out through the crowd to meet Achilles. She gathered the gods around her and said: “Look to this now, you two — Poseidon and Athena — and think hard how it should go. Phoebus Apollo has sent Aeneas out in full armor against the son of Peleus. Come, let’s turn him back ourselves, right now — or else let one of us stand by Achilles and give him such strength that his heart fails in nothing, so he knows the greatest of the immortals love him, and that the gods who have shielded the Trojans all this while are nothing but empty wind. We all came down from Olympus to take part in this fight so that Achilles would suffer no harm from the Trojans today. Afterward, let him bear whatever fate spun for him with her thread on the day his mother bore him. But if Achilles doesn’t learn all this from the voice of a god, he may be afraid when one of the immortals comes against him in the fighting — for the gods are terrible when they show themselves face to face.”

Poseidon who shakes the earth answered her: “Hera, don’t be so furious; there’s no need. I have no wish to push the rest of us into a war with the others — not when we’re so much the stronger. Let’s go and sit down out of the way, on some lookout, and leave the war to the mortals. But if Ares begins it, or Phoebus Apollo, or if they hold Achilles back and won’t let him fight, then we too will raise the war-cry at once, and I think they’ll soon break off and go back beaten to Olympus, to the company of the other gods.”

So the dark-haired god spoke, and led the way to the high earthwork of Heracles, the great mound walled round with stone that the Trojans and Pallas Athena had once raised for him to run to when the sea-monster chased him from the shore across the plain. There Poseidon and the gods with him sat down, and wrapped an unbreakable cloud around their shoulders; and across from them the others sat on the brow of Callicolone, around you, lord Apollo, and Ares the sacker of cities.

So the gods sat on either side, laying their plans, and neither side was willing to be the first to begin the grievous war — and Zeus, from his seat on high, had command of them all. But down on the plain the whole field filled with men and horses and blazed with the gleam of bronze, and the earth rang under their feet as they rushed together. And out between the two armies came two men, the best of all by far, hungry to fight each other — Aeneas, son of Anchises, and brilliant Achilles.

Aeneas came on first, striding forward, his heavy helmet nodding, holding his strong shield up in front of his chest, shaking his bronze spear. And from the other side the son of Peleus sprang to meet him, like a lion the whole countryside has come out to kill — a great hunting party. At first he goes on his way, paying them no mind; but when one of the quick young men spears him, then he gathers himself with his jaws gaping, foam stands on his teeth, his great heart groans within him, his tail lashes his ribs and flanks left and right as he whips himself up to fight, and with blazing eyes he charges straight ahead, to kill — or be killed in the front of the fighting. So the fury and the proud heart of Achilles drove him on to face Aeneas.

When they had come close, facing each other, Achilles spoke first: “Aeneas, why have you come so far out from the crowd to stand against me? Is it that your heart is set on ruling the Trojans, on Priam’s throne and all his honors? Even if you kill me, Priam won’t lay his kingdom in your hands for that. He has sons of his own, and his mind is sound, not foolish. Or have the Trojans marked you out some fine plot of land, the best of it — orchard and plowland — for you to live on, if you kill me? You won’t find that easy, I think. I’ve already sent you running once with my spear. Don’t you remember the day I caught you alone and chased you headlong down the slopes of Ida, away from your cattle? You didn’t once look back as you ran. You got away into Lyrnessus, but I came down on the city and sacked it, with the help of Athena and father Zeus, and led its women off into slavery, robbed of their day of freedom — though Zeus and the other gods pulled you out of it. They won’t save you now, whatever you imagine. So I tell you, go back into the crowd, get out of my way before you suffer for it. Even a fool learns once a thing is done.”

Then Aeneas answered him: “Son of Peleus, don’t think you can scare me with words as if I were a child. I can throw insults too, if I want, and ugly ones. We both know each other’s blood and parents — old stories, told and told again — though you have never set eyes on my parents, nor I on yours. They say you are the son of noble Peleus, and that your mother is Thetis of the lovely hair, daughter of the sea. I claim great-hearted Anchises for my father, and my mother is Aphrodite. Before this day is over, one of these will be weeping for a dead son, because I don’t think we two are going to settle this with childish talk and walk away from the field. But if you’d like to hear it, here is my lineage — and many men know it.

“First there was Dardanus, son of Zeus, and he founded Dardania, for sacred Ilios had not yet been built on the plain as a city for men — they still lived on the lower slopes of Ida with its many springs. Dardanus had a son, King Erichthonius, who became the richest of mortal men. Three thousand mares grazed his water-meadows, glorying in their young foals. And the North Wind, Boreas, fell in love with them as they grazed, and went to them in the shape of a dark-maned stallion; and they conceived and bore him twelve fillies. When those fillies skimmed across the grain-giving fields they would run along the top of the ripe ears and never break them; and when they played on the broad back of the sea, they would skim the crests of the breaking surf. Erichthonius fathered Tros, king of the Trojans, and Tros had three faultless sons — Ilus, and Assaracus, and godlike Ganymede, the most beautiful of mortal men, whom the gods caught up to pour the wine for Zeus and live among the immortals, for his beauty’s sake. Ilus fathered Laomedon, and Laomedon fathered Tithonus and Priam, and Lampus and Clytius and Hicetaon, the offshoot of Ares. Assaracus fathered Capys, and Capys fathered Anchises, and Anchises fathered me — while Hector is the son of Priam.

“That is the blood I claim, the line I was born from. As for a man’s courage, Zeus gives it or takes it as he pleases, for he is the strongest of all. Now, enough — let’s not stand here in the middle of a battle trading words like children. We could both fling insults, more than a hundred-benched ship could carry. The tongue runs every way; it has a thousand things to say, a wide grazing-ground of words this way and that, and whatever you say, that’s what you’ll hear back. But what’s the use of our snarling and bickering at each other like a pair of women so angry they spill out into the street to wrangle, half of it true, half lies, with rage driving them on? No words of yours will turn me back now that I mean to fight. So come — let’s test each other with the bronze.”

He spoke, and drove his heavy spear against the great and terrible shield, and the point rang loud against it. The son of Peleus held the shield away from him with his strong hand, afraid, for he thought the long spear of brave Aeneas would punch straight through it — fool that he was, not to see in his heart that the glorious gifts of the gods do not break easily under the hands of mortal men. And so the spear of fierce Aeneas did not go through; the gold stopped it, the god’s gift. It drove through two layers, but three were left, for the lame god had made the shield of five — two of bronze, two of tin underneath, and one of gold; the gold was where the spear was stopped.

Then Achilles threw in his turn, and struck the perfectly round shield of Aeneas at the very rim, where the bronze ran thinnest and the bull’s-hide backing was thinnest too. The Pelian ash spear punched clean through, and the shield rang under it. Aeneas had crouched and held the shield up and away in his fear, and the spear flew over his back and stuck in the ground, after tearing through both circles of the shield that should have covered him. He had dodged the long spear, but he stood there stunned, with grief and terror flooding his eyes, that the weapon had come down so near. Now Achilles drew his sharp sword and sprang at him, screaming his battle-cry, and Aeneas heaved up a stone in his hand — a great one, so huge that no two men, such as men are now, could lift it; but he swung it easily, alone.

And now Aeneas would have struck Achilles as he charged in — on the helmet, or on the shield that warded off death — and Achilles, closing, would have torn the life out of him with his sword; but Poseidon, who shakes the earth, saw it sharply and at once spoke among the immortals: “This is a grief to me — great-hearted Aeneas, who will go down now to the house of Hades, beaten by the son of Peleus, because he listened to Apollo who shoots from afar. Poor fool — and Apollo will do nothing to save him from the bitter end. But why should this man suffer, when he has done no wrong, for sorrows that aren’t his own? He has always given the gods who hold the wide sky the gifts they love. Come, let’s pull him out of death ourselves, so the son of Cronus doesn’t rage when Achilles kills him. It is fated that he escape — fated, so that the line of Dardanus should not die out, gone without seed, lost from the world. For Zeus loved Dardanus above all the sons born to him of mortal women. And now Zeus has come to hate the house of Priam; but mighty Aeneas will be king over the Trojans, and his sons’ sons, and all who are born after them.”

Then the ox-eyed lady Hera answered him: “Shaker of the earth, decide it for yourself, whether you’ll save Aeneas or let him go down, brave as he is, under the hands of Achilles. As for us two — I and Pallas Athena — we have sworn it over and over, in front of all the immortals, that we will never lift a hand to keep the evil day from the Trojans, not even when all of Troy is burning in the fire the Greeks will set.”

When Poseidon who shakes the earth heard that, he went down through the clash of spears and the flying weapons to where Achilles and Aeneas were. At once he poured a mist across the eyes of the son of Peleus, and drew the ash spear with its bronze head from the shield of brave Aeneas and laid it down at the feet of Achilles. Then he caught Aeneas up high off the ground and swung him through the air — over the heads of many ranks of fighting men, many lines of horses — flung from the god’s own hand, until he set him down at the very edge of the battle, where the Cauconians were arming for war. And Poseidon, who shakes the earth, came close to him and spoke: “Aeneas, which of the gods drove you so far out of your mind that you’d fight the proud son of Peleus head-on — a man stronger than you, and dearer to the immortals? Pull back, whenever you run into him, so you don’t go down to the house of Hades before your time. But once Achilles has met his death and his fate, then go fight up in the front line, with no fear; for no other Greek will bring you down.”

He told him this, and left him there, and lifted the strange mist again from the eyes of Achilles. And Achilles stared, and a hard anger came over him, and he said to his own brave heart: “No — what is this I’m looking at? My spear, here on the ground — and the man I threw it at to kill, gone, nowhere. So Aeneas too, it seems, is dear to the deathless gods. And here I thought his boasting was just empty noise. Let him go, then. He’ll have no stomach to try me again, glad enough to have slipped death this once. No — I’ll call to the Greeks who love war and go test some other Trojans.”

And he sprang back along the ranks, calling each man on: “Greeks, don’t hang back now, far off from the Trojans. Go man against man — set your hearts on the fight. It’s hard for me, strong as I am, to chase down so many and take them all on. Not even Ares, an immortal god, nor Athena, could fight along the whole jaws of a battle this size and never tire. But what I can do — what these hands and feet and this strength can reach — I’ll hold nothing back, not for a moment. Straight through their line I’ll go, and I don’t think any Trojan will be glad who comes within reach of my spear.”

So he urged them on. And on the other side glorious Hector called to the Trojans, and said he would stand against Achilles: “Proud Trojans, don’t be afraid of the son of Peleus. I could fight even the gods with words, if the war were one of talk — but with a spear it’s harder; they’re far stronger than we are. Even Achilles won’t make good on everything he says; some of it he’ll do, and the rest he’ll leave half-done. I’ll go against him, though his hands are like fire — though his hands are fire and his fury is bright iron.”

So he urged them, and the Trojans lifted their spears against the Greeks. The two sides crashed together, the war-cry went up — and Phoebus Apollo came and stood beside Hector and said: “Hector, you must not go out and fight Achilles alone. Wait for him in among the crowd, out of the thick of the killing — or he’ll cut you down with a spear-throw, or strike you down close in with his sword.”

So Apollo spoke, and Hector drew back into the crowd of men, shaken with fear at hearing the god’s voice. But Achilles sprang on the Trojans with a terrible scream, his heart clothed in fury. First he killed Iphition, son of Otrynteus, a captain of many men, whom a water-nymph had borne to Otrynteus the sacker of cities, in the land of Hyde under the snowy peak of Tmolus. As Iphition charged straight at him, Achilles caught him full on the head with his spear and split it clean in two, and he crashed down to the ground; and Achilles boasted over him: “Lie there, son of Otrynteus, most fearsome of men. Here is your death, far from the Gygaean lake where your father’s lands lie, by fish-filled Hyllus and the eddying Hermus.”

So he boasted, and darkness came over the other’s eyes, and the Greek chariots tore his body up under their wheels in the front of the battle. After him Achilles killed Demoleon, a brave fighter, son of Antenor, with a spear through the temple, through the bronze-cheeked helmet; the bronze did not stop it but smashed on through, and the bone broke and the brain inside was all churned to ruin, and that ended his hunger for the fight. Next he struck Hippodamas in the back as he leaped from his chariot to run, and the man breathed his life out bellowing — the way a bull bellows when young men drag it for the lord of Helice, and the heart of the earth-shaker is glad — just so Hippodamas bellowed as his proud spirit left the bones.

Then Achilles went after godlike Polydorus, Priam’s son. His father had always forbidden him to fight, because he was the youngest of all his children and the dearest, and he could outrun them all. Now, in his folly, showing off the speed of his feet, he came racing through the front ranks — until he threw his life away. As he flashed past, swift Achilles caught him with a spear in the middle of the back, where the golden clasps of his belt came together and the breastplate doubled over. The point drove straight through and out by the navel, and he dropped to his knees with a scream, and a dark cloud closed over him as he sank, clutching his own spilled guts in his hands.

When Hector saw his brother Polydorus sinking to the ground with his life in his hands, a mist of grief came down over his eyes, and he could no longer bear to circle far off. He came at Achilles, swinging his sharp spear like a tongue of fire. And Achilles, seeing him, leaped forward, glad, and said: “Here he is — the man who cut deepest into my heart, who killed the friend I loved. We won’t be shrinking from each other long now on the paths of war.” And glaring at him he said: “Come closer, then, and meet your death the sooner.”

But Hector was not afraid. He answered: “Son of Peleus, don’t think you can scare me with words like a child. I can throw insults too, and ugly ones, if I choose. I know you are a great fighter, far greater than I am. But these things rest in the lap of the gods — whether, worse man though I am, I may take your life with a cast of my spear. My spear too has been found sharp before now.”

He drew back and threw the spear; but Athena breathed it away from glorious Achilles — only a light breath — and it came back to Hector and dropped at his feet. Then Achilles sprang at him in a fury, screaming for the kill — and Apollo snatched Hector up, easily, the way a god can, and hid him in thick mist. Three times swift Achilles charged at him, spear in hand, and three times he struck nothing but deep mist. And when he came on the fourth time like something more than a man, he gave a terrible shout: “Dog — once more you’ve slipped your death, and it came very close. Phoebus Apollo has saved you again, the god you must pray to before you walk into the spears. But I’ll finish you off yet, when we meet next time, if there’s any god on my side too. For now, I’ll go after the rest, whoever I can run down.”

And he struck Dryops with his spear through the middle of the neck, and left him dropped at his feet; then he stopped Demuchus, Philetor’s son, a big strong man, with a spear in the knee, and finished him with his sword. Then he leaped on Laogonus and Dardanus, the two sons of Bias, and threw them both from their chariot, one with a thrown spear, the other cut down close at hand with the sword. And Tros, son of Alastor, came and caught Achilles by the knees, hoping he would take him alive and spare him, not kill him, out of pity for a man his own age. The fool — he should have known there was no swaying him; this was no soft-hearted man, no gentle one, but a man in a killing rage. As Tros reached for his knees, begging, Achilles drove the sword into his liver, and the liver slid out, and the dark blood welled from the wound and soaked his chest, and the darkness took his eyes as his life ran out.

Then Achilles came up to Mulius and speared him through the ear, so the bronze point came out the other ear. He struck Echeclus, Agenor’s son, full on the head with his hilted sword, and the whole blade went warm with blood, and red death and hard fate closed his eyes. Next the bronze point of his spear pierced Deucalion in the arm, where the tendons of the elbow join; Deucalion stood waiting for him, his arm hanging dead, with death in front of his eyes — and Achilles took his head off with a sword-stroke and flung it away, helmet and all, and the marrow ran from the broken neck-bones as he lay along the ground. Then he went after Rhigmus, the brave son of Peires, who had come from rich Thrace, and drove the spear into his belly so that he crashed headlong from his chariot; and as Areithous, the man’s driver, wheeled the horses to run, Achilles speared him in the back and pitched him from the chariot, and the horses bolted in terror.

As a wildfire rages through the deep glens of a mountain after a long drought, and the thick forest burns, and the wind drives the flame rolling every way — so Achilles raged everywhere with his spear, like some god, hunting down the men he meant to kill, until the dark earth ran with blood. As a farmer yokes his broad-browed oxen to tread the white barley on the threshing-floor, and the grain is quickly stripped under the feet of the bellowing cattle — so the strong-footed horses of Achilles trampled the dead and the shields together. The axle below was spattered with blood, and the rails around the chariot, struck by the spray flung up from the horses’ hooves and the rims of the wheels. And the son of Peleus pressed on, hungry to win glory, and his unstoppable hands were splattered with gore.
