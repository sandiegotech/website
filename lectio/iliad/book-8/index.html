The Iliad, Book VIII

The Tide of Battle Turns

Book VIII8 of 24

When Dawn in her saffron robe had spread her light across the earth, Zeus who loves the thunder called the gods to council on the highest peak of jagged Olympus. He spoke, and all of them listened. “Hear me,” he said, “gods and goddesses, while I tell you what is in my mind. Let none of you, female or male, try to cross my will. Obey me, every one, so that I can bring this to its end. If I catch any god slipping off to help the Trojans or the Greeks, he’ll come back to Olympus beaten and ashamed — or I’ll seize him and fling him down into the murk of Tartarus, far off, into the deepest pit under the earth, where the gates are iron and the floor is bronze, as far below Hades as the sky is high above the ground. Then he’ll learn how much the strongest of all I am.

“Test me, if you like, and find out for yourselves. Hang a golden chain from heaven and lay hold of it, all of you, gods and goddesses together — you will never drag Zeus, the high lord of counsel, down from the sky to the earth, pull as you may. But if I cared to haul on it myself, I’d drag you all up, and the earth and the sea along with you. Then I’d tie the chain to a horn of Olympus and leave the whole world dangling in the air. That is how far I am above all gods and men.”

They went silent, all of them, struck by the force of his words. At last bright-eyed Athena spoke up. “Father, son of Cronus, highest of kings — we know your strength is not to be denied. But it grieves us to watch the Greek warriors die, meeting so hard an end. We’ll keep clear of the fighting, since you command it. But let us at least drop a word of good counsel to the Greeks, so they don’t all perish under your anger.”

Zeus who gathers the clouds smiled at her. “Take heart, my child. I don’t mean it as harshly as it sounds; I want to be kind to you.”

With that he yoked his swift horses, their hooves bronze, their manes streaming gold. He wrapped his own body in gold, took up his golden whip, and stepped into the chariot. He cracked the lash, and the horses sprang forward willingly, midway between earth and the starry sky. He came to Ida of the many springs, mother of wild beasts, to Gargarus, where his sacred grove and his fragrant altar stand. There the father of gods and men reined in his horses, freed them from the chariot, and folded a thick cloud around them; and he sat down on the heights in his glory, looking out over the city of Troy and the ships of the Greeks.

The Greeks ate a quick meal among the ships and buckled on their armor. The Trojans armed as well, all through the city — fewer in number, but driven on by need, ready to fight for their wives and their children. The gates were thrown open, and out poured the army, foot and horse, with the tramp of a great host.

When the two sides came together, shield struck shield, spear struck spear, and the fury of armored men met head-on. The bossed shields ground against each other, and the air filled with a great roar — the screams of the dying and the shouts of those who killed them — and the earth ran with blood.

All through the morning, while the day climbed, the weapons bit on both sides and men kept falling. But when the sun stood at the top of the sky, the Father lifted his golden scales. In them he set two fates of death, two heavy dooms — one for the Trojans, one for the Greeks — and he took the balance by the middle and raised it high. The Greeks’ day sank. Their doom settled down to the ground that feeds us all, while the Trojans’ fate rose toward the sky. Then he thundered loud from Ida and hurled the flash of his lightning down on the Greeks; and when they saw it, a pale fear took them, and they were afraid.

Idomeneus did not dare to hold his ground, nor did Agamemnon, nor either of the two named Ajax. Only Nestor, the old horseman of Gerene, stood firm, the bulwark of the Greeks — and not by choice, for one of his horses was crippled. Paris, the husband of lovely Helen, had hit it with an arrow on the crown of the head, where the mane first springs from the skull, the deadliest place. The horse reared in agony as the point drove into its brain and threw the team into chaos. The old man set to slashing the traces with his sword, but Hector’s fast horses came bearing down on him through the rout, with their bold driver — Hector himself. The old man would have died there and then, had Diomedes not seen it in time. He called out to Odysseus with a great shout.

“Odysseus,” he cried, “son of Laertes, where are you running to, with your back turned like a coward? Watch you don’t take a spear between the shoulders in flight. Stay and help me hold off this man from the old one.”

But Odysseus would not hear him; he sped past, on toward the Greek ships. So the son of Tydeus drove in alone, into the thick of it, and pulled up before the horses of old Nestor. “Sir,” he said, “these young fighters are pressing you hard. Your strength is spent, and age weighs on you; your driver is no use to you, and your horses are slow. Up into my chariot — see what the horses of Tros can do, how they fly across the plain in chase or in flight. I took them from Aeneas. Let our squires look after your team, and we’ll drive these straight at the Trojans, so Hector finds out how my spear can rage too.”

Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, did as he said. The two sturdy squires, Sthenelus and kind Eurymedon, took charge of Nestor’s horses, and the two heroes mounted Diomedes’ chariot. Nestor caught up the reins and lashed the horses on, and they closed fast on Hector. The son of Tydeus let fly a spear at him as he charged straight at them. He missed Hector, but struck his driver and squire, Eniopeus son of brave Thebaeus, in the chest by the nipple, the reins still in his hands; and he died on the spot, and the horses shied as he pitched headlong from the chariot. Grief took Hector for the loss of his driver, but he let him lie for all his sorrow and went looking for another, and his horses did not lack a hand for long: he soon found brave Archeptolemus, son of Iphitus, and set him up behind the team and put the reins in his grip.

Now disaster would have come, past all undoing — the Trojans would have been penned in Troy like sheep — had the father of gods and men not seen it quickly. He thundered and hurled a blazing thunderbolt; it struck the ground just in front of Diomedes’ horses, and a terrible flame of burning sulfur leapt up. The horses cringed and backed under the chariot, and the reins dropped from Nestor’s hands. He was afraid, and said to Diomedes, “Son of Tydeus, turn the horses and run. Don’t you see that the hand of Zeus is against you? Today he gives the glory to Hector. Tomorrow, if he pleases, he’ll give it back to us. No man, however brave, can turn aside the will of Zeus, for he is far the stronger.”

Diomedes answered, “All of that is true, old man. But here is the grief that cuts me to the heart: someday Hector will say among the Trojans, ‘The son of Tydeus ran from me to the ships.’ That is the boast he’ll make — and on that day, let the earth open and swallow me.”

“Son of Tydeus,” Nestor said, “what are you saying? Even if Hector calls you a coward, the Trojans and the Dardanians won’t believe it, nor the wives of all the strong men you have laid in the dust.”

With that he wheeled the horses back through the press of battle, and the Trojans, with Hector at their head, sent their spears raining after them, screaming in triumph. Hector shouted at him: “Son of Tydeus, the Greeks used to give you the best seat at the table, the choicest meat, your cup filled to the brim. Now they’ll have nothing but contempt for you, for you’ve turned out no better than a woman. Off with you, you trembling girl! You’ll never climb our walls while I flinch aside, never carry our wives off in your ships. Before that, I’ll kill you with my own hand.”

The son of Tydeus was torn — should he turn the horses around and fight him? Three times he was on the point of it, and three times Zeus thundered from the heights of Ida, a sign to the Trojans that he was turning the battle their way. Then Hector called out to his men: “Trojans, Lycians, Dardanians who fight up close — be men, my friends, and fight with all your strength. I can see that Zeus means to grant me the victory and the great glory, and to deal ruin to the Greeks. Fools — to think this flimsy, worthless wall could stop me. My horses will clear that ditch at a bound, and when I’m in among the ships, don’t forget to bring me fire, so I can burn them, and cut the Greeks down beside them, blind and choking in the smoke.”

Then he called to his horses: “Xanthus, Podargus, Aethon, and noble Lampus — pay me back now for your keep, for all the honey-sweet grain Andromache, daughter of great Eetion, has fed you, mixing wine for you to drink whenever you wished, sooner than for me, her own husband. After them, fast — let us take the shield of Nestor, whose fame goes up to heaven, all of it solid gold, the arm-bars and all; and strip from the shoulders of Diomedes the breastplate Hephaestus forged for him. If we can take those two things, the Greeks will be aboard their ships and gone this very night.”

So he boasted; and queen Hera, shaking with anger on her throne, made high Olympus quake. She turned to mighty Poseidon and said, “What now, wide-ruling lord of the earthquake? Doesn’t your heart pity the dying Greeks, who bring you so many welcome gifts at Helice and at Aegae? Wish them the victory, then. If all of us who side with the Greeks were to drive the Trojans back and hold off Zeus, he could sit and sulk by himself up there on Ida.”

Lord Poseidon was troubled, and answered, “Hera, reckless tongue, what are you saying? I will not have the rest of us set ourselves against Zeus. He is far the stronger.”

While they talked, the whole space the ditch enclosed, from the ships out to the wall, was packed with horses and shield-bearers — penned there by Hector, son of Priam, now that Zeus was with him. He would have set the ships ablaze, had not queen Hera put it in Agamemnon’s mind to rouse himself and rally the Greeks. So he went along the ships and the huts, holding a great purple cloak in his hand, and took his stand by the huge black hull of Odysseus’ ship, which lay in the middle of the line, so his voice would carry both ways — to the huts of Ajax, son of Telamon, on the one side, and to those of Achilles on the other; for these two, sure of their own strength, had drawn their ships up at the two ends of the camp. From there he shouted to the Greeks in a voice that reached them all: “Shame on you, Greeks — men in name only, splendid only to look at. Where are all our boasts now, when we swore we were the best, the brave words we threw around on Lemnos, gorging on the meat of cattle and filling the wine bowls to the brim? Each of us vowed he’d stand against a hundred Trojans, two hundred — and now we’re no match for one, for Hector, who’ll soon have our ships in flames. Father Zeus, did you ever so ruin a great king and strip him of his glory? And yet, on this cursed voyage, I never let my ship pass one of your altars without burning the fat and the thigh-bones of oxen on it, so set was I on sacking the strong city of Troy. Grant me this much, Zeus: let us at least escape with our lives, and don’t let the Greeks be broken so utterly by the Trojans.”

So he prayed, and the Father pitied his tears and granted that his people would live and not die. At once he sent an eagle, the surest of all his omens, with a fawn in its talons; and it dropped the fawn beside the splendid altar where the Greeks sacrificed to Zeus, lord of omens. When the men saw that the bird had come from Zeus, they fell on the Trojans with fresh fury and threw themselves back into the fight.

Then, of all the Greeks — and they were many — not one could claim he drove his horses across the ditch and out to the fighting ahead of the son of Tydeus. He was the first by far to kill an armed Trojan, Agelaus son of Phradmon, who had turned his horses to flee; the spear caught him in the back, between the shoulders, and drove on through his chest, and his armor rang around him as he fell forward from the chariot.

After Diomedes came Agamemnon and Menelaus, the sons of Atreus; then the two named Ajax, clothed in fighting courage; then Idomeneus and his comrade Meriones, a match for the murderous war-god; then Eurypylus, the brave son of Euaemon. Ninth came Teucer with his bow, bent and taking cover behind the shield of Ajax, son of Telamon. Ajax would lift the shield aside, and Teucer would peer out, pick a man in the crowd, and shoot him dead; then he’d duck back to Ajax like a child to its mother, and Ajax would hide him again under the shield.

Which of the Trojans did Teucer bring down first? Orsilochus, then Ormenus and Ophelestes, Daetor and Chromius and godlike Lycophontes, Amopaon son of Polyaemon, and Melanippus. One after another he laid them on the earth, and Agamemnon, lord of men, was glad to see him cutting the Trojans down with his great bow. He came and stood by him and said, “Teucer, my friend, son of Telamon, captain of the host — shoot on. Be the saving of the Greeks and the glory of your father Telamon, who raised you in his own house and cared for you, child though you were born outside his marriage. Win him glory now, far away as he is. And I tell you, and I’ll see it done: if Zeus who wields the storm-shield, and Athena, ever let me sack the strong city of Troy, you’ll have the first prize of honor after my own — a tripod, or a chariot and two horses, or a woman to share your bed.”

Teucer answered, “Most noble son of Atreus, why urge me on when I’m already willing? From the moment we began driving them back toward Troy, I have never stopped watching for men to bring down with my arrows. Eight barbed shafts I’ve let fly, and every one has buried itself in the flesh of some young fighter — but this mad dog, I can’t hit.”

As he spoke he loosed another arrow straight at Hector, set on killing him. He missed again, but the shaft struck Priam’s brave son Gorgythion in the chest — whose mother, lovely Castianeira, fair as a goddess, had come as a bride from Aesyme. His head sank to one side, like a garden poppy heavy with seed and the rains of spring; just so his head drooped under the weight of his helmet.

Again Teucer aimed at Hector, longing to hit him, and again the arrow missed, for Apollo turned it aside. But it struck Hector’s brave driver Archeptolemus in the chest by the nipple as he drove furiously into the fight. The horses shied as he pitched headlong from the chariot, and the life went out of him. Grief took Hector for the loss of his driver, but he let him lie for all his sorrow and called to his brother Cebriones, who was near, to take the reins. Cebriones did as he was told. And Hector, with a great shout, leapt down from the chariot to the ground, snatched up a great stone, and made straight for Teucer, meaning to kill him. Teucer had just drawn an arrow from his quiver and laid it on the string. But as he drew the string to his shoulder and took aim, Hector struck him with the jagged stone, right where the collarbone joins the neck to the chest, the deadliest place. It snapped the bowstring against his arm; his wrist went numb, and he dropped to his knees, the bow falling from his hand. Ajax saw his brother fall and ran to him, stood astride him, and sheltered him with his shield, while two loyal squires, Mecisteus son of Echius and Alastor, lifted him and carried him to the ships, groaning in his pain.

Now Zeus put fresh heart into the Trojans, and they drove the Greeks back to their deep ditch, Hector in all his glory at their head. As a hound runs down a wild boar or a lion, snapping at its flank or its haunch and watching for the turn, so Hector hung on the heels of the Greeks, always killing the hindmost as they fled in panic. And when they had run back through the stakes and the ditch — leaving many of their own dead at the hands of the Trojans — they halted at the ships and called to one another, and every man lifted his hands and prayed aloud to the gods; while Hector wheeled his horses this way and that, his eyes glaring like the Gorgon’s, or the murderous war-god’s.

Hera saw them and pitied them, and at once she said to Athena, “Ah, child of Zeus who wields the storm-shield — will you and I take no more thought for the dying Greeks, even this last time? Look how they perish, broken by the onset of a single man. Hector, son of Priam, rages past all bearing, and already he’s done terrible harm.”

Athena answered, “I’d gladly see him dead, killed on his own soil by the hands of the Greeks. But my father rages with a black temper — always crossing me, always headstrong and unjust. He’s forgotten how often I saved his son Heracles, worn out under the labors Eurystheus laid on him. He’d cry up to heaven, and Zeus would send me down to help him. If I’d had the sense to foresee all this when Eurystheus sent him down to the house of Hades to fetch the hound of hateful Hades out of Erebus, he’d never have come back alive across the deep waters of the Styx. And now Zeus hates me, and gives Thetis her way, because she kissed his knees and took hold of his chin, begging him to honor Achilles. The day will come when he calls me his gray-eyed darling again. Harness our horses for me now, while I go into the house of Zeus and put on my armor. Then we’ll see whether Hector, Priam’s son, is glad to meet us in the lanes of battle — or whether some Trojan will glut the dogs and the vultures with his fat and his flesh, dead by the ships of the Greeks.”

White-armed Hera did as she said, and set about harnessing the horses with their golden gear. And Athena, daughter of Zeus who wields the storm-shield, let her soft embroidered robe fall to her father’s threshold — the robe she had made with her own hands — pulled on the tunic of Zeus, and armed herself for the war that brings tears. She stepped into the flaming chariot and took up the spear, heavy and huge and strong, with which she beats down the ranks of the men who have crossed her. Hera cracked the whip, and the gates of heaven groaned open of their own accord — the gates the Hours keep, who hold the sky and Olympus in their charge, to roll the thick cloud aside or close it again. Through them the two goddesses drove their willing horses.

But Zeus, looking down from Ida, saw them and was filled with rage. He sent golden-winged Iris with a message. “Go, swift Iris, turn them back. Don’t let them come near me — it will be an ugly thing if we come to blows. I tell you plainly what I will do: I’ll cripple the horses under their chariot, throw the two of them out, and smash the chariot to pieces. Ten full years they’ll not heal the wounds my lightning leaves on them. Then my gray-eyed daughter will learn what it means to fight her own father. With Hera I’m less surprised, less angry — whatever I say, she always contradicts it.”

So Iris went, fast as the wind, from the heights of Ida up to the summits of Olympus. She met the goddesses at the outer gates of its many folded valleys and gave them the message. “Where are you going? What madness is in you? The son of Cronus forbids you to help the Greeks. Here is his threat, and he means to do it: he’ll cripple the horses under your chariot, throw the two of you out, and smash the chariot to pieces. Ten full years you’ll not heal the wounds his lightning leaves on you — so you’ll learn, gray-eyed goddess, what it means to fight your own father. With Hera he’s less angry; whatever he says, she always contradicts it. But you — you shameless bitch — will you really dare lift that great spear against Zeus?”

With that she left them, and Hera said to Athena, “No, child of Zeus who wields the storm-shield — I’ll not have the two of us fight Zeus over mortal men. Let them live or die as their luck falls, and let him judge between Trojans and Greeks as his own mind tells him; that is his right.”

She turned the horses back. The Hours unyoked them, tied them at their fragrant mangers, and leaned the chariot against the bright wall of the court. And the two goddesses sat down on their golden thrones among the rest of the gods — sick at heart.

Then Zeus drove his chariot up from Ida to Olympus and came among the gathered gods. The great lord of the earthquake unyoked his horses for him, set the chariot on its stand, and threw a cloth over it; and Zeus sat down on his golden throne, and great Olympus trembled under him. Only Athena and Hera sat apart from Zeus, and neither spoke a word to him or asked him anything. But he knew their minds, and said, “Why so downcast, Athena, Hera? Surely you didn’t tire yourselves killing Trojans, those dear friends you hate so well? Know this: not all the gods on Olympus together could turn me, such is the strength of these hands. You two were shaking before you ever set eyes on the war and its grim work. I tell you, and it would have come true — I’d have struck you with lightning, and your chariot would never have carried you home to Olympus.”

Athena and Hera heard him and groaned, sitting side by side, scheming trouble for the Trojans. Athena held her tongue, though she was boiling with anger at her father and said nothing. But Hera could not contain it. “Dread son of Cronus, what a thing to say. We know your power well enough. Still, we pity the Greek warriors, dying and meeting so hard an end. We’ll keep clear of the fighting, since you command it — but we’ll drop a word of good counsel to the Greeks, so they don’t all perish under your anger.”

Zeus answered, “Wait for the morning, Hera, if you care to watch it: you’ll see the son of Cronus killing Greeks by the thousands. For fierce Hector will not stop fighting until he has roused the son of Peleus — on the day they fight in the worst of straits, by the ships’ sterns, over the body of Patroclus. That is how it is decreed; like it or not, that is how it will be. As for you — go down, if you wish, to the lowest pit beneath the earth and the sea, where Iapetus and Cronus sit with neither a ray of sunlight nor a breath of wind to comfort them, deep in Tartarus; go all the way down, and I won’t care in the least how angry you are. There is no creature on earth more shameless than you.”

Hera said nothing back. And now the sun’s bright disk dropped into the Ocean stream and drew the dark night down over the grain-giving earth. The Trojans were sorry to lose the light — but for the Greeks the darkness came welcome, three times prayed for.

Then Hector led the Trojans back from the ships and held a council on the open ground by the river, where the dead did not lie. They climbed down from their chariots and sat on the bare earth to hear him. In his hand he held a spear eleven cubits long, its bronze point gleaming, ringed at the socket with gold. Leaning on it, he spoke. “Hear me, Trojans, Dardanians, and allies. I thought, just now, I would destroy the ships and all the Greeks with them before I went home to Troy. But darkness came on too soon, and that alone has saved them and their ships on the shore. So now let us give in to the night and see to our supper. Free the horses from the chariots and feed them; then hurry and bring sheep and cattle from the city, and wine, and grain for the horses, and gather plenty of wood, so that all night until the dawn we can keep watchfires burning whose glare reaches the sky. For the Greeks may try to slip away over the sea in the dark, and they must not embark untouched and at their ease. Many a man among them must carry home a wound to nurse — hit with spear or arrow as he leaps for his ship — so that the rest learn to dread bringing war and weeping to the Trojans. And let the heralds cry it through the city: the boys not yet grown and the gray-bearded old men are to camp on the god-built walls; and let every woman light a great fire in her house, and keep a sure watch, so the town isn’t taken by surprise while our army is out on the plain. See to it, brave Trojans — that is enough for now; at daybreak I’ll have more to say. I pray, in hope, to Zeus and the other gods that we may drive these fate-driven dogs from our land, for it is fate that has carried them and their ships here. Tonight we keep watch; with the first light let us arm and rouse hard war at the ships of the Greeks. Then I’ll know whether brave Diomedes, son of Tydeus, will drive me from the ships back to the wall — or whether I’ll cut him down and carry off his bloodied armor. Tomorrow he’ll show his courage, if he dares to stand and face my spear. I think that when the day breaks he’ll be among the first to fall, and many of his men around him. If only I were as certain of being deathless, ageless forever, honored like Athena and Apollo, as I am that this coming day brings ruin to the Greeks.”

So Hector spoke, and the Trojans roared their approval. They loosed the sweating horses from the yokes and tethered each one by its own chariot. They hurried to bring sheep and cattle from the city, and wine, and grain from their houses, and gathered plenty of wood. Then they offered unblemished sacrifices to the immortals, and the wind carried the sweet savor up to heaven — but the blessed gods would not share in it, for they hated Troy, and Priam, and Priam’s people. And so, high in hope, they sat all night long in the lanes of war, and their watchfires burned past counting. As when the stars stand out bright around the moon in a windless sky, and every peak and headland and clearing shows clear, and the high air splits open into endless light, and all the stars can be counted, and the shepherd’s heart is glad — so many were the Trojan fires burning before Troy, between the ships and the streams of the Xanthus. A thousand fires blazed on the plain, and at each one sat fifty men in the glow, while the horses stood by the chariots, champing their barley and grain, and waited for Dawn on her bright throne.
