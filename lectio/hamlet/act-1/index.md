# Hamlet, Act I

SCENE I. Elsinore. A platform before the Castle.

Enter Francisco and Barnardo, two sentinels.

BARNARDO  
Who's there?

FRANCISCO  
No—you answer me. Stand, and reveal yourself.

BARNARDO  
Long live the King!

FRANCISCO  
Barnardo?

BARNARDO  
The same.

FRANCISCO  
You come right on the stroke of your hour.

BARNARDO  
It's just struck twelve. Get to bed, Francisco.

FRANCISCO  
For this relief, many thanks. It's bitter cold,  
and I am sick at heart.

BARNARDO  
Have you had a quiet watch?

FRANCISCO  
Not a mouse stirring.

BARNARDO  
Well, good night.  
If you do meet Horatio and Marcellus,  
the partners of my watch, bid them make haste.

Enter Horatio and Marcellus.

FRANCISCO  
I think I hear them. Stand, there! Who's that?

HORATIO  
Friends to this country.

MARCELLUS  
And loyal men to the Dane.

FRANCISCO  
Good night to you.

MARCELLUS  
Oh, farewell, honest soldier. Who has relieved you?

FRANCISCO  
Barnardo has my place. Good night to you.

[*Exit.*]

MARCELLUS  
Hey there, Barnardo!

BARNARDO  
What—is Horatio there?

HORATIO  
A piece of him.

BARNARDO  
Welcome, Horatio. Welcome, good Marcellus.

MARCELLUS  
So, has this thing appeared again tonight?

BARNARDO  
I have seen nothing.

MARCELLUS  
Horatio says it's only our imagination,  
and won't let belief take hold of him  
about this dreaded sight we've seen twice.  
So I've begged him to come along  
and keep watch with us through the minutes of this night,  
so that if this apparition comes again  
he can confirm our eyes, and speak to it.

HORATIO  
Tut, tut, it won't appear.

BARNARDO  
Sit down awhile,  
and let us once again assail your ears,  
so fortified against our story,  
with what we two nights have seen.

HORATIO  
Well, let's sit down,  
and let us hear Barnardo speak of this.

BARNARDO  
Just last night,  
when that same star, westward from the pole,  
had traveled its course to light that part of heaven  
where now it burns—Marcellus and myself,  
the bell just then striking one—

MARCELLUS  
Hush, stop—look where it comes again.

Enter Ghost.

BARNARDO  
In the same shape, like the King who's dead.

MARCELLUS  
You're a scholar—speak to it, Horatio.

BARNARDO  
Doesn't it look like the King? Look at it, Horatio.

HORATIO  
Most like. It harrows me with fear and wonder.

BARNARDO  
It wants to be spoken to.

MARCELLUS  
Question it, Horatio.

HORATIO  
What are you, that lay claim to this hour of night,  
together with that fair and warlike form  
in which the majesty of buried Denmark  
once marched? By heaven, I command you, speak.

MARCELLUS  
It is offended.

BARNARDO  
See, it stalks away.

HORATIO  
Stay! Speak, speak! I command you, speak!

[*Exit Ghost.*]

MARCELLUS  
It's gone, and will not answer.

BARNARDO  
How now, Horatio! You tremble and look pale.  
Is this not something more than imagination?  
What do you make of it?

HORATIO  
Before my God, I could not have believed this  
without the solid, true witness  
of my own eyes.

MARCELLUS  
Is it not like the King?

HORATIO  
As you are like yourself.  
That was the very armor he had on  
when he fought the ambitious King of Norway;  
so he frowned once, when in an angry parley  
he struck the sledded Poles down on the ice.  
It's strange.

MARCELLUS  
Twice before, and exactly at this dead hour,  
with soldier's stride he has passed by our watch.

HORATIO  
What to make of it exactly, I don't know;  
but in the broad sweep of my opinion,  
this forebodes some strange upheaval in our state.

MARCELLUS  
All right, sit down, and tell me—whoever knows—  
why this strict and watchful guard  
works the people of this land so hard each night;  
and why this daily casting of bronze cannon,  
and foreign trade for tools of war;  
why this conscription of shipwrights, whose harsh labor  
doesn't even spare the Sunday from the week.  
What could be coming, that this sweating haste  
makes night a fellow-laborer with the day?  
Who can inform me?

HORATIO  
That I can do—  
at least, the rumor goes like this. Our late King,  
whose image just now appeared to us,  
was, as you know, by Fortinbras of Norway—  
spurred on by a most jealous pride—  
dared to single combat; and our valiant Hamlet  
(for so this side of the known world esteemed him)  
slew this Fortinbras; who by a sealed agreement,  
well ratified by law and heraldry,  
forfeited, with his life, all those his lands  
that he possessed, to the conqueror.  
Against this, an equal portion  
was staked by our King; which would have passed  
to the inheritance of Fortinbras  
had he been the victor—just as by that same compact,  
and the terms of the article drawn up,  
his lands fell to Hamlet. Now, sir, young Fortinbras,  
of untested mettle, hot and full,  
has, in the outskirts of Norway, here and there,  
scraped up a band of lawless desperadoes,  
paid in food and rations, for some venture  
that takes some nerve; which is no other,  
as it appears clearly to our state,  
than to recover from us, by strong hand  
and forced terms, those same lands  
so lost by his father. And this, I take it,  
is the main reason for our preparations,  
the source of this our watch, and the chief cause  
of this headlong haste and bustle in the land.

BARNARDO  
I think it's nothing other than just that.  
It may well fit, that this ominous figure  
comes armed through our watch so like the King  
who was, and is, the very point of these wars.

HORATIO  
It's a speck to trouble the mind's eye.  
In the highest and most flourishing days of Rome,  
a little before the mightiest Julius fell,  
the graves stood empty, and the shrouded dead  
did squeak and gibber in the Roman streets;  
stars trailing fire, and dews of blood,  
blights upon the sun; and the moist star,  
upon whose influence Neptune's empire stands,  
was sick almost to doomsday with eclipse.  
And the very same omens of fierce events—  
as heralds always coming before the fates,  
and prologue to the doom on its way—  
heaven and earth together have shown  
to our own region and countrymen.

Re-enter Ghost.

HORATIO  
But wait, look! See, where it comes again!  
I'll cross its path, though it strike me dead. Stay, illusion!  
If you have any sound, or use of voice,  
speak to me.  
If there is any good thing to be done  
that may bring you ease, and grace to me,  
speak to me.  
If you are privy to your country's fate,  
which, perhaps, foreknowing might avoid—  
oh, speak!  
Or if you have hoarded up, in your life,  
plundered treasure in the womb of earth  
(for which, they say, you spirits often walk in death),  
speak of it. Stay, and speak!

[*The cock crows.*]

HORATIO  
Stop it, Marcellus!

MARCELLUS  
Shall I strike at it with my spear?

HORATIO  
Do, if it won't stand still.

BARNARDO  
It's here!

HORATIO  
It's here!

[*Exit Ghost.*]

MARCELLUS  
It's gone!  
We do it wrong, it being so majestic,  
to offer it this show of violence,  
for it is like the air, beyond all wounding,  
and our useless blows are spiteful mockery.

BARNARDO  
It was about to speak, when the cock crowed.

HORATIO  
And then it flinched, like a guilty thing  
at a dreaded summons. I have heard  
that the cock, the trumpet of the morning,  
with his high and shrill-sounding throat  
wakes the god of day; and at his warning,  
whether in sea or fire, in earth or air,  
the wandering and straying spirit hurries  
back to its confine. And of the truth of this,  
this very thing just gave the proof.

MARCELLUS  
It faded at the crowing of the cock.  
Some say that always, just before the season comes  
in which our Savior's birth is celebrated,  
the bird of dawn sings all night long;  
and then, they say, no spirit dares stir abroad,  
the nights are wholesome, then no planets blast,  
no fairy steals, no witch has power to charm—  
so holy and so gracious is the time.

HORATIO  
So I have heard, and partly believe it.  
But look, the morning, robed in russet mantle,  
walks over the dew of that high eastern hill.  
Let's break off our watch; and, by my advice,  
let us tell what we have seen tonight  
to young Hamlet—for, upon my life,  
this spirit, silent to us, will speak to him.  
Do you agree we should inform him of it,  
as our love requires, and our duty fits?

MARCELLUS  
Let's do it, I beg you; and I know this morning  
where we shall find him most conveniently.

[*Exeunt.*]

SCENE II. Elsinore. A room of state in the Castle.

Enter Claudius King of Denmark, Gertrude the Queen, Hamlet, Polonius, Laertes, Voltemand, Cornelius, Lords and Attendant.

KING  
Though the memory of our dear brother Hamlet's  
death is still fresh, and though it suited us  
to hold our hearts in grief, and our whole kingdom  
to be drawn together in one brow of woe;  
yet good sense has so far wrestled with nature  
that we now think of him with wisest sorrow,  
while remembering ourselves as well.  
Therefore our former sister, now our queen,  
the imperial partner in this warlike state,  
we have—as if with a defeated joy,  
with one eye smiling and one eye dropping tears,  
with mirth at the funeral and a dirge at the wedding,  
weighing delight and grief in equal scale—  
taken to wife; nor in this have we ignored  
your better wisdoms, which have freely gone  
along with this affair. For all, our thanks.  
Now, you should know that young Fortinbras,  
holding a low opinion of our worth,  
or thinking that, by our late dear brother's death,  
our state is disjointed and out of frame,  
allied with this dream of his advantage,  
has not failed to pester us with messages  
demanding the surrender of those lands  
lost by his father, with all bonds of law,  
to our most valiant brother. So much for him.  
Now for ourself, and for this present meeting:  
here is the business. We have written  
to Norway, uncle of young Fortinbras—  
who, sick and bedridden, scarcely hears  
of this his nephew's plans—to put a stop  
to his advance; since the levies,  
the musters, and the full forces are all drawn  
from Norway's subjects. And we now dispatch  
you, good Cornelius, and you, Voltemand,  
to carry this greeting to old Norway,  
giving you no further personal power  
to deal with the King than the scope  
of these detailed articles allows.  
Farewell; and let your haste reflect your duty.

CORNELIUS and VOLTEMAND  
In this, and all things, we will show our duty.

KING  
We doubt it not. Heartily, farewell.

[*Exeunt Voltemand and Cornelius.*]

KING  
And now, Laertes, what's the news with you?  
You told us of some request. What is it, Laertes?  
You cannot speak of anything reasonable to the Dane  
and waste your breath. What could you ask, Laertes,  
that would not be my offer before your asking?  
The head is no more natural to the heart,  
the hand no more useful to the mouth,  
than the throne of Denmark is to your father.  
What would you have, Laertes?

LAERTES  
My dread lord,  
your leave and favor to return to France—  
from which, though I came willingly to Denmark  
to do my duty at your coronation;  
yet now, that duty done, I must confess  
my thoughts and wishes bend again toward France,  
and bow before your gracious leave and pardon.

KING  
Have you your father's leave? What says Polonius?

POLONIUS  
He has, my lord, wrung from me my reluctant leave  
by tireless petition; and at last  
I sealed my hard consent upon his will.  
I beg you, give him leave to go.

KING  
Take your good hour, Laertes; the time is yours,  
and spend it as your best graces will!  
But now, my nephew Hamlet, and my son—

HAMLET  
[*Aside.*] A little more than kin, and less than kind.

KING  
How is it that the clouds still hang on you?

HAMLET  
Not so, my lord. I am too much in the sun.

QUEEN  
Good Hamlet, cast off that nighttime color,  
and let your eye look on Denmark like a friend.  
Don't forever, with your lowered lids,  
search for your noble father in the dust.  
You know it's common—all that lives must die,  
passing through nature on to eternity.

HAMLET  
Ay, madam, it is common.

QUEEN  
If it is,  
why does it seem so particular with you?

HAMLET  
Seems, madam? No, it is. I know not "seems."  
It's not just my black cloak, good mother,  
nor the customary suits of solemn black,  
nor gusty sighing of forced breath,  
no, nor the flowing river in the eye,  
nor the downcast look upon the face,  
together with all forms, moods, shows of grief,  
that can express me truly. These indeed seem,  
for they are actions that a man might play;  
but I have that within which passes show;  
these are merely the trappings and the suits of woe.

KING  
It's sweet and admirable in your nature, Hamlet,  
to pay these mourning duties to your father;  
but you must know, your father lost a father;  
that father lost, lost his; and the survivor, bound  
in a son's obligation, for some time  
to observe a dutiful grief. But to persist  
in stubborn mourning is a course  
of ungodly obstinacy. It's unmanly grief.  
It shows a will most disobedient to heaven,  
a heart unfortified, a restless mind,  
an understanding simple and untaught.  
For what we know must happen, and is as common  
as the most ordinary thing to the senses—  
why should we, in our peevish resistance,  
take it to heart? Shame—it's a fault to heaven,  
a fault against the dead, a fault to nature,  
most absurd to reason, whose constant theme  
is the death of fathers, and which has always cried,  
from the first corpse to the man who died today,  
"This must be so." We beg you, throw to earth  
this useless grief, and think of us  
as of a father; for let the world take note,  
you are the closest in line to our throne,  
and with no less nobility of love  
than that which the dearest father bears his son  
I hold toward you. As for your intention  
to go back to school in Wittenberg,  
it runs entirely against our wish;  
and we beg you, bring yourself to stay  
here in the cheer and comfort of our eye,  
our foremost courtier, nephew, and our son.

QUEEN  
Let not your mother lose her prayers, Hamlet.  
I beg you, stay with us; do not go to Wittenberg.

HAMLET  
I shall, as best I can, obey you, madam.

KING  
Why, that's a loving and a fair reply.  
Be like ourself in Denmark. Madam, come;  
this gentle, unforced agreement of Hamlet's  
sits smiling at my heart; and in its honor,  
no merry toast that Denmark drinks today  
but the great cannon shall announce it to the clouds,  
and the King's carousing the heavens shall echo back,  
resounding earthly thunder. Come away.

[*Exeunt all but Hamlet.*]

HAMLET  
Oh, that this too, too solid flesh would melt,  
thaw, and resolve itself into a dew!  
Or that the Everlasting had not fixed  
his law against self-slaughter. O God! O God!  
How weary, stale, flat, and unprofitable  
seem to me all the uses of this world!  
Shame on it! Oh, shame! It's an unweeded garden  
gone to seed; things rank and gross in nature  
possess it utterly. That it should come to this!  
But two months dead—no, not so much, not two:  
so excellent a king, who was to this one  
Hyperion to a satyr; so loving to my mother  
that he would not let the winds of heaven  
touch her face too roughly. Heaven and earth!  
Must I remember? Why, she would cling to him  
as if her appetite had only grown  
by what it fed on; and yet, within a month—  
let me not think on it—Frailty, thy name is woman!—  
a little month, before the very shoes were old  
in which she followed my poor father's body,  
like Niobe, all tears—why she, even she—  
O God! A beast that has no power of reason  
would have mourned longer—married my uncle,  
my father's brother; but no more like my father  
than I am like Hercules. Within a month,  
before the salt of her most unrighteous tears  
had left the redness in her chafed eyes,  
she married. Oh, most wicked speed, to rush  
with such dexterity to incestuous sheets!  
It is not, nor can it ever come to good.  
But break, my heart, for I must hold my tongue.

Enter Horatio, Marcellus and Barnardo.

HORATIO  
Hail to your lordship!

HAMLET  
I am glad to see you well.  
Horatio—or I've forgotten myself.

HORATIO  
The same, my lord,  
and your poor servant always.

HAMLET  
Sir, my good friend—  
I'll trade that name with you.  
And what brings you from Wittenberg, Horatio?—  
Marcellus?

MARCELLUS  
My good lord.

HAMLET  
I'm very glad to see you.—Good evening, sir.—  
But really, what brings you from Wittenberg?

HORATIO  
A truant's mood, my good lord.

HAMLET  
I wouldn't let your enemy say that;  
nor shall you do my ear that violence  
of making it believe your own report  
against yourself. I know you are no truant.  
But what's your business here in Elsinore?  
We'll teach you to drink deep before you leave.

HORATIO  
My lord, I came to see your father's funeral.

HAMLET  
Please don't mock me, fellow student.  
I think it was to see my mother's wedding.

HORATIO  
Indeed, my lord, it followed close upon it.

HAMLET  
Thrift, thrift, Horatio! The funeral's baked meats  
were served up cold for the marriage tables.  
I'd rather have met my worst enemy in heaven  
than ever have seen that day, Horatio.  
My father—I think I see my father.

HORATIO  
Where, my lord?

HAMLET  
In my mind's eye, Horatio.

HORATIO  
I saw him once; he was a fine king.

HAMLET  
He was a man, take him for all in all,  
I shall not look upon his like again.

HORATIO  
My lord, I think I saw him last night.

HAMLET  
Saw? Who?

HORATIO  
My lord, the King your father.

HAMLET  
The King my father!

HORATIO  
Calm your astonishment a while,  
with an attentive ear, until I can deliver,  
on the witness of these gentlemen,  
this marvel to you.

HAMLET  
For God's love, let me hear.

HORATIO  
For two nights running, these gentlemen,  
Marcellus and Barnardo, on their watch,  
in the dead, vacant middle of the night,  
had this encounter. A figure like your father,  
armed at every point exactly, head to foot,  
appears before them, and with solemn march  
goes slow and stately by them. Three times he walked  
before their oppressed and fear-struck eyes,  
within his baton's length, while they, dissolved  
almost to jelly by the act of fear,  
stand dumb, and do not speak to him. This, to me,  
in dreadful secrecy they confided;  
and I, with them, the third night kept the watch,  
where, just as they'd reported—both in time,  
and in the form of the thing, each word made true—  
the apparition comes. I knew your father:  
these hands are not more alike.

HAMLET  
But where was this?

MARCELLUS  
My lord, upon the platform where we watch.

HAMLET  
Did you not speak to it?

HORATIO  
My lord, I did;  
but it made no answer. Yet once I thought  
it lifted up its head, and began  
to move, as if it would speak.  
But just then the morning cock crowed loud,  
and at the sound it shrank away in haste  
and vanished from our sight.

HAMLET  
It's very strange.

HORATIO  
As I live, my honored lord, it's true;  
and we thought it set down in our duty  
to let you know of it.

HAMLET  
Indeed, indeed, sirs, but this troubles me.  
Do you hold the watch tonight?

MARCELLUS and BARNARDO  
We do, my lord.

HAMLET  
Armed, you say?

BOTH  
Armed, my lord.

HAMLET  
From top to toe?

BOTH  
My lord, from head to foot.

HAMLET  
Then you didn't see his face?

HORATIO  
Oh yes, my lord. He wore his visor up.

HAMLET  
What, did he look angry?

HORATIO  
A countenance more in sorrow than in anger.

HAMLET  
Pale, or red?

HORATIO  
No, very pale.

HAMLET  
And fixed his eyes on you?

HORATIO  
Most constantly.

HAMLET  
I wish I had been there.

HORATIO  
It would have amazed you.

HAMLET  
Very likely, very likely. Did it stay long?

HORATIO  
Long as one might count to a hundred, at a moderate pace.

MARCELLUS and BARNARDO  
Longer, longer.

HORATIO  
Not when I saw it.

HAMLET  
His beard was grizzled, was it not?

HORATIO  
It was, as I have seen it in his life,  
a sable touched with silver.

HAMLET  
I will watch tonight.  
Perhaps it will walk again.

HORATIO  
I guarantee it will.

HAMLET  
If it takes on my noble father's form,  
I'll speak to it, though hell itself should gape  
and bid me hold my peace. I beg you all—  
if you have until now concealed this sight,  
keep it locked still in your silence;  
and whatever else may happen tonight,  
give it understanding, but no tongue.  
I will repay your loves. So, fare you well.  
Upon the platform, between eleven and twelve,  
I'll visit you.

ALL  
Our duty to your honor.

HAMLET  
Your love, as mine to you. Farewell.

[*Exeunt Horatio, Marcellus and Barnardo.*]

HAMLET  
My father's spirit in arms! All is not well.  
I suspect some foul play. Would the night were here!  
Till then sit still, my soul. Foul deeds will rise,  
though all the earth should bury them, to men's eyes.

[*Exit.*]

SCENE III. A room in Polonius's house.

Enter Laertes and Ophelia.

LAERTES  
My gear is aboard. Farewell.  
And, sister, whenever the winds are favorable  
and a ship is bound your way, do not sleep,  
but let me hear from you.

OPHELIA  
Do you doubt that?

LAERTES  
As for Hamlet, and the trifle of his attention,  
treat it as a passing fancy, a whim of the blood—  
a violet in the springtime of youth,  
early, not lasting; sweet, but not enduring;  
the fragrance and diversion of a minute;  
no more.

OPHELIA  
No more than that?

LAERTES  
Think it no more.  
For as nature grows, it grows not only  
in muscle and bulk; but as this temple grows,  
the inward work of the mind and soul  
grows broad with it. Perhaps he loves you now,  
and no stain or deceit now sullies  
the purity of his intent; but you must fear—  
weighing his rank—his will is not his own,  
for he himself is subject to his birth.  
He may not, as common persons do,  
carve his own portion; for on his choice depends  
the safety and the health of this whole state;  
and therefore his choice must be confined  
to the voice and consent of that body  
whose head he is. So if he says he loves you,  
it suits your wisdom to believe it only  
so far as he, in his particular rank and place,  
can match his words with deeds; which is no further  
than the main voice of Denmark will allow.  
Then weigh what your honor stands to lose  
if, with too trusting an ear, you take his songs,  
or lose your heart, or open your chaste treasure  
to his uncurbed insistence.  
Fear it, Ophelia; fear it, my dear sister;  
and keep yourself behind your affection,  
out of the reach and danger of desire.  
The most careful girl is reckless enough  
if she unveils her beauty to the moon.  
Virtue itself does not escape slander's blows.  
The cankerworm bites the buds of spring  
too often before they even open;  
and in the morning, the fresh dew of youth,  
contagious blights are most threatening.  
Be wary, then: the best safety lies in fear.  
Youth rebels against itself, with no one near.

OPHELIA  
I'll keep the meaning of this good lesson  
as a guard upon my heart. But, good my brother,  
don't, like some ungodly preachers do,  
show me the steep and thorny way to heaven  
while you yourself, a reckless, swollen libertine,  
tread the primrose path of pleasure  
and pay no heed to your own advice.

LAERTES  
Oh, fear not for me.  
I stay too long. But here my father comes.

Enter Polonius.

LAERTES  
A double blessing is a double grace;  
chance smiles upon a second leave-taking.

POLONIUS  
Still here, Laertes? Aboard, aboard, for shame!  
The wind sits in the shoulder of your sail,  
and they are waiting for you. There—my blessing on you.

[*Laying his hand on Laertes's head.*]

POLONIUS  
And these few precepts—in your memory  
see that you write them. Give your thoughts no tongue,  
nor any rash thought any action.  
Be friendly, but by no means common.  
Those friends you have, and whose loyalty you've tested,  
bind them to your soul with hoops of steel;  
but don't wear out your palm with greeting  
every new-hatched, half-grown comrade. Beware  
of getting into a quarrel; but once in,  
bear it so that your opponent learns to fear you.  
Give every man your ear, but few your voice;  
take each man's opinion, but reserve your judgment.  
Let your clothes be costly as your purse can buy,  
but not showy; rich, not gaudy—  
for the apparel oft proclaims the man,  
and those in France of the best rank and station  
are most refined and generous in just that.  
Neither a borrower nor a lender be,  
for a loan often loses both itself and the friend,  
and borrowing dulls the edge of thrift.  
This above all: to thine own self be true,  
and it must follow, as the night the day,  
you cannot then be false to any man.  
Farewell; may my blessing ripen this in you.

LAERTES  
Most humbly do I take my leave, my lord.

POLONIUS  
The time invites you; go, your servants wait.

LAERTES  
Farewell, Ophelia, and remember well  
what I have said to you.

OPHELIA  
It's locked within my memory,  
and you yourself shall keep the key of it.

LAERTES  
Farewell.

[*Exit.*]

POLONIUS  
What is it, Ophelia, that he said to you?

OPHELIA  
If you please—something concerning the Lord Hamlet.

POLONIUS  
Well thought of, indeed.  
I'm told that he has, very often lately,  
given private time to you; and you yourself  
have been most free and generous with your company.  
If this is so—as it's been reported to me,  
and that by way of warning—I must tell you,  
you don't understand yourself as clearly  
as befits my daughter and your honor.  
What is between you? Give me up the truth.

OPHELIA  
He has, my lord, of late made many tenders  
of his affection to me.

POLONIUS  
Affection! Pah! You talk like a green girl,  
untested in such perilous circumstances.  
Do you believe his tenders, as you call them?

OPHELIA  
I do not know, my lord, what I should think.

POLONIUS  
Well, I'll teach you. Think yourself a baby,  
that you have taken these tenders for true coin,  
which are not sterling. Tender yourself more dearly—  
or (not to wear out the poor word,  
running it like this) you'll tender me a fool.

OPHELIA  
My lord, he has pressed his love on me  
in honorable fashion.

POLONIUS  
Ay, fashion, you may call it. Come, come.

OPHELIA  
And has backed up his words, my lord,  
with almost all the holy vows of heaven.

POLONIUS  
Ay—snares to catch foolish birds. I know well,  
when the blood is burning, how freely the soul  
lends the tongue its vows. These flares, daughter,  
giving more light than heat, going out in both,  
even as they're promised, in the very making,  
you must not mistake for fire. From this time on,  
be somewhat more sparing of your company.  
Set your conversation at a higher price  
than a mere command to talk. As for Lord Hamlet,  
believe only this much in him: that he is young,  
and may walk on a longer leash  
than can be granted you. In short, Ophelia,  
don't believe his vows; for they are go-betweens,  
not of the color that their garments show,  
but mere petitioners for unholy ends,  
breathing like holy and pious pimps,  
the better to deceive. This is the sum:  
in plain terms, from this time forward  
I'll not have you so slander any moment's leisure  
as to give words or talk with the Lord Hamlet.  
See to it, I command you. Come along.

OPHELIA  
I shall obey, my lord.

[*Exeunt.*]

SCENE IV. The platform.

Enter Hamlet, Horatio and Marcellus.

HAMLET  
The air bites sharply; it is very cold.

HORATIO  
It is a nipping and a biting air.

HAMLET  
What hour now?

HORATIO  
Not quite twelve, I think.

MARCELLUS  
No, it has struck.

HORATIO  
Really? I didn't hear it. Then it draws near the time  
when the spirit usually walks.

[*A flourish of trumpets, and ordnance shot off within.*]

HORATIO  
What does this mean, my lord?

HAMLET  
The King is up tonight, and carousing,  
holding his revels, the staggering dance reeling;  
and as he drains his cups of Rhine wine down,  
the kettle-drum and trumpet bray out  
the triumph of his toast.

HORATIO  
Is it a custom?

HAMLET  
Ay, indeed it is;  
and to my mind, though I'm a native here  
and to the manner born, it is a custom  
more honored in the breach than the observance.  
This heavy-headed drinking, east and west,  
makes us slandered and condemned by other nations.  
They call us drunkards, and with filthy words  
stain our reputation; and indeed it strips  
from our achievements—even at their best—  
the very heart and substance of our good name.  
So it often happens with particular men  
that, for some single flaw of nature in them—  
as in their birth, in which they have no guilt,  
since nature cannot choose its own origin—  
by the overgrowth of some one temperament,  
often breaking down the fences and forts of reason,  
or by some habit that too much corrupts  
the form of pleasing manners—that these men,  
carrying, I say, the stamp of one defect,  
whether nature's marking or fortune's mark,  
their other virtues, be they pure as grace,  
as infinite as any man may have,  
shall, in the general judgment, take corruption  
from that one particular fault. The dram of evil  
does drag down all the noble substance  
to its own disgrace.

HORATIO  
Look, my lord, it comes!

Enter Ghost.

HAMLET  
Angels and ministers of grace defend us!  
Whether you are a spirit of health or a goblin damned,  
whether you bring airs from heaven or blasts from hell,  
whether your intentions are wicked or kind,  
you come in such a questionable shape  
that I will speak to you. I'll call you Hamlet,  
King, father, royal Dane. Oh, answer me!  
Let me not burst with ignorance; but tell me  
why your sanctified bones, sealed up in death,  
have burst their burial shroud; why the tomb,  
in which we saw you quietly laid to rest,  
has opened its heavy marble jaws  
to cast you up again. What can it mean,  
that you, dead corpse, again in full armor,  
revisit thus the glimpses of the moon,  
making the night hideous, and we, fools of nature,  
so horribly shake our composure  
with thoughts beyond the reach of our souls?  
Say, why is this? Why? What should we do?

[*Ghost beckons Hamlet.*]

HORATIO  
It beckons you to go away with it,  
as if it wished to make some communication  
to you alone.

MARCELLUS  
Look with what courteous action  
it waves you to a more secluded ground.  
But do not go with it.

HORATIO  
No, by no means.

HAMLET  
It will not speak; then I will follow it.

HORATIO  
Do not, my lord.

HAMLET  
Why, what is there to fear?  
I do not value my life at a pin's worth;  
and as for my soul, what can it do to that,  
being a thing immortal as itself?  
It waves me forward again. I'll follow it.

HORATIO  
What if it tempts you toward the flood, my lord,  
or to the dreadful summit of the cliff  
that overhangs its base into the sea,  
and there takes on some other horrible form  
that might strip your reason of its rule  
and draw you into madness? Think of it.  
The very place breeds desperate fancies  
in every brain, with no other cause,  
that looks down so many fathoms to the sea  
and hears it roar below.

HAMLET  
It waves me still.  
Go on, I'll follow you.

MARCELLUS  
You shall not go, my lord.

HAMLET  
Hold off your hands.

HORATIO  
Be ruled; you shall not go.

HAMLET  
My fate cries out,  
and makes each tiny artery in this body  
as tough as the Nemean lion's sinew.

[*Ghost beckons.*]

HAMLET  
Still it calls me. Let go of me, gentlemen.

[*Breaking free from them.*]

HAMLET  
By heaven, I'll make a ghost of anyone who stops me.  
I say, away!—Go on, I'll follow you.

[*Exeunt Ghost and Hamlet.*]

HORATIO  
He grows desperate with imagination.

MARCELLUS  
Let's follow. It's not right to obey him in this.

HORATIO  
Let's go after him. What will this come to?

MARCELLUS  
Something is rotten in the state of Denmark.

HORATIO  
Heaven will direct it.

MARCELLUS  
No, let's follow him.

[*Exeunt.*]

SCENE V. A more remote part of the Castle.

Enter Ghost and Hamlet.

HAMLET  
Where will you lead me? Speak. I'll go no further.

GHOST  
Mark me.

HAMLET  
I will.

GHOST  
My hour is almost come,  
when I to sulfurous and tormenting flames  
must render up myself.

HAMLET  
Alas, poor ghost!

GHOST  
Do not pity me, but lend your serious hearing  
to what I shall reveal.

HAMLET  
Speak. I am bound to hear.

GHOST  
So are you bound to revenge, when you shall hear.

HAMLET  
What?

GHOST  
I am your father's spirit,  
doomed for a certain term to walk the night,  
and by day confined to fast in fires,  
till the foul crimes done in my lifetime  
are burnt and purged away. But that I am forbidden  
to tell the secrets of my prison house,  
I could unfold a tale whose lightest word  
would harrow up your soul, freeze your young blood,  
make your two eyes, like stars, start from their orbits,  
your knotted and combined locks part,  
and each particular hair to stand on end  
like quills upon the bristling porcupine.  
But this revelation of eternity must not be  
for ears of flesh and blood. Listen, listen, oh, listen!  
If you did ever love your dear father—

HAMLET  
O God!

GHOST  
Revenge his foul and most unnatural murder.

HAMLET  
Murder!

GHOST  
Murder most foul, as even the best of murders are;  
but this one most foul, strange, and unnatural.

HAMLET  
Haste me to know it, so that I, with wings as swift  
as meditation or the thoughts of love,  
may sweep to my revenge.

GHOST  
I find you ready;  
and you would be duller than the bloated weed  
that rots itself in ease on Lethe's shore  
if you would not stir at this. Now, Hamlet, hear.  
The story given out is that, sleeping in my orchard,  
a serpent stung me; so the whole ear of Denmark  
is, by a faked account of my death,  
grossly deceived. But know, you noble youth,  
the serpent that did sting your father's life  
now wears his crown.

HAMLET  
O my prophetic soul!  
My uncle!

GHOST  
Ay, that incestuous, that adulterous beast,  
with the witchcraft of his wit, with traitorous gifts—  
oh, wicked wit, and gifts, that have the power  
so to seduce!—won to his shameful lust  
the will of my most seemingly virtuous queen.  
O Hamlet, what a falling-off was there!  
From me, whose love was of that dignity  
that it went hand in hand even with the vow  
I made to her in marriage; and to sink down  
to a wretch whose natural gifts were poor  
beside my own. But just as virtue is never moved,  
though lewdness courts it in the shape of heaven,  
so lust, though linked to a radiant angel,  
will gorge itself in a heavenly bed  
and feed on garbage.  
But wait! I think I scent the morning air.  
Let me be brief. Sleeping within my orchard,  
as was always my custom in the afternoon,  
in my unguarded hour your uncle stole upon me  
with the juice of cursed henbane in a vial,  
and in the porches of my ears he poured  
the leprous distillation, whose effect  
holds such an enmity with the blood of man  
that, swift as quicksilver, it courses through  
the natural gates and alleys of the body,  
and with a sudden force it clots and curdles,  
like sour drops dripped into milk,  
the thin and wholesome blood. So it did mine;  
and instantly a scabbing crust spread over,  
most leper-like, with vile and loathsome scab,  
all my smooth body.  
Thus was I, sleeping, by a brother's hand,  
of life, of crown, of queen at once cut off;  
struck down in the full blossom of my sins,  
with no last rites, no sacrament, no anointing;  
no final reckoning made, but sent to my account  
with all my imperfections on my head.  
O horrible! O horrible! most horrible!  
If you have any natural feeling in you, do not bear it;  
let not the royal bed of Denmark be  
a couch for lust and damnèd incest.  
But however you pursue this act,  
do not taint your mind, nor let your soul plot  
anything against your mother; leave her to heaven,  
and to those thorns that lodge within her breast  
to prick and sting her. Fare you well at once!  
The glow-worm shows the morning to be near,  
and begins to dim his feeble fire.  
Adieu, adieu, adieu. Remember me.

[*Exit.*]

HAMLET  
O all you host of heaven! O earth! What else?  
And shall I add hell too? Oh, no! Hold, my heart;  
and you, my sinews, do not grow old in an instant,  
but bear me stiffly up. Remember you?  
Ay, you poor ghost, while memory holds a seat  
in this distracted globe. Remember you?  
Yes—from the tablet of my memory  
I'll wipe away all trivial, foolish records,  
all sayings from books, all forms, all impressions past  
that youth and observation copied there;  
and your commandment, all alone, shall live  
within the book and volume of my brain,  
unmixed with baser matter. Yes, by heaven!  
O most pernicious woman!  
O villain, villain, smiling, damnèd villain!  
My notebook. It's only right that I set it down,  
that a man may smile, and smile, and be a villain—  
at least I'm sure it may be so in Denmark.

[*Writing.*]

HAMLET  
So, uncle, there you are. Now to my watchword:  
it is "Adieu, adieu, remember me."  
I have sworn it.

HORATIO and MARCELLUS  
[*Within.*] My lord, my lord!

MARCELLUS  
[*Within.*] Lord Hamlet!

HORATIO  
[*Within.*] Heaven keep him safe.

HAMLET  
So be it!

MARCELLUS  
[*Within.*] Hey, ho, ho, my lord!

HAMLET  
Hey, ho, ho, boy! Come, bird, come.

Enter Horatio and Marcellus.

MARCELLUS  
How is it, my noble lord?

HORATIO  
What news, my lord?

HAMLET  
Oh, wonderful!

HORATIO  
Good my lord, tell it.

HAMLET  
No, you'll reveal it.

HORATIO  
Not I, my lord, by heaven.

MARCELLUS  
Nor I, my lord.

HAMLET  
How say you, then—would the heart of man ever think it?—  
but you'll keep it secret?

HORATIO and MARCELLUS  
Ay, by heaven, my lord.

HAMLET  
There's not a villain living in all Denmark  
but he's a thorough scoundrel.

HORATIO  
There's no need for a ghost, my lord, to come from the grave  
to tell us this.

HAMLET  
Why, right; you're in the right;  
and so, without any more ado,  
I think it fitting that we shake hands and part:  
you, where your business and desire shall point you—  
for every man has business and desire,  
such as it is—and for my own poor part,  
look you, I'll go pray.

HORATIO  
These are but wild and whirling words, my lord.

HAMLET  
I'm sorry they offend you, heartily;  
yes, truly, heartily.

HORATIO  
There's no offense, my lord.

HAMLET  
Yes, by Saint Patrick, but there is, Horatio,  
and much offense too. As for this vision here—  
it is an honest ghost, that I can tell you.  
As for your desire to know what's between us,  
master it as best you can. And now, good friends,  
as you are friends, scholars, and soldiers,  
grant me one poor request.

HORATIO  
What is it, my lord? We will.

HAMLET  
Never make known what you have seen tonight.

HORATIO and MARCELLUS  
My lord, we will not.

HAMLET  
No, but swear it.

HORATIO  
In faith, my lord, not I.

MARCELLUS  
Nor I, my lord, in faith.

HAMLET  
Upon my sword.

MARCELLUS  
We have sworn already, my lord.

HAMLET  
Indeed, upon my sword, indeed.

GHOST  
[*Cries under the stage.*] Swear.

HAMLET  
Ha, ha, boy! Is that what you say? Are you there, trusty fellow?  
Come on—you hear this fellow in the cellar.  
Consent to swear.

HORATIO  
Propose the oath, my lord.

HAMLET  
Never to speak of what you have seen.  
Swear by my sword.

GHOST  
[*Beneath.*] Swear.

HAMLET  
*Hic et ubique?* Then we'll shift our ground.  
Come over here, gentlemen,  
and lay your hands again upon my sword.  
Never to speak of what you have heard.  
Swear by my sword.

GHOST  
[*Beneath.*] Swear.

HAMLET  
Well said, old mole! Can you work in the earth so fast?  
A fine sapper! Once more move, good friends.

HORATIO  
O day and night, but this is wondrous strange.

HAMLET  
And therefore, as a stranger, give it welcome.  
There are more things in heaven and earth, Horatio,  
than are dreamt of in your philosophy. But come—  
here, as before, swear: never, so help you mercy,  
however strange or odd I bear myself  
(since I may think it fitting, hereafter,  
to put an antic disposition on),  
that you, at such times, seeing me, shall never,  
with arms folded thus, or with this shake of the head,  
or by the uttering of some doubtful phrase  
like "Well, we know," or "We could, if we would,"  
or "If we cared to speak," or "There are those who could, if they might"—  
or any such ambiguous hint—to show  
that you know anything of me. This you must not do—  
so grace and mercy at your hour of greatest need help you.  
Swear.

GHOST  
[*Beneath.*] Swear.

HAMLET  
Rest, rest, perturbed spirit. So, gentlemen,  
with all my love I commend myself to you;  
and whatever so poor a man as Hamlet is  
may do to express his love and friendship to you,  
God willing, shall not be lacking. Let us go in together;  
and keep your fingers on your lips, I beg you.  
The time is out of joint. O cursèd spite,  
that ever I was born to set it right!  
No, come, let's go together.

[*Exeunt.*]
