# Hamlet, Act V

SCENE I. A churchyard.

Enter two Clowns with spades, etc.

FIRST CLOWN  
Is she to be given Christian burial, when she willfully seeks her own salvation?

SECOND CLOWN  
I tell you she is, so dig her grave at once. The coroner has sat on her case and ruled it Christian burial.

FIRST CLOWN  
How can that be, unless she drowned herself in self-defense?

SECOND CLOWN  
Why, that's how it's been ruled.

FIRST CLOWN  
It must be *se offendendo*, it can't be otherwise. For here's the point: if I drown myself on purpose, that proves an act; and an act has three branches. It is to act, to do, and to perform. Argal, she drowned herself on purpose.

SECOND CLOWN  
No, but listen here, goodman digger—

FIRST CLOWN  
Let me finish. Here lies the water—good. Here stands the man—good. If the man goes to the water and drowns himself, then, like it or not, he goes—mark that. But if the water comes to him and drowns him, he does not drown himself. Argal, he that is not guilty of his own death does not shorten his own life.

SECOND CLOWN  
But is this the law?

FIRST CLOWN  
Ay, indeed it is—coroner's-inquest law.

SECOND CLOWN  
You want the truth of it? If she hadn't been a gentlewoman, she'd have been buried outside Christian ground.

FIRST CLOWN  
Why, now you've said it. And the more pity that great folk should have the backing in this world to drown or hang themselves more freely than the rest of us Christians. Come, my spade. There's no older line of gentlemen than gardeners, ditchers, and grave-makers—they carry on Adam's trade.

SECOND CLOWN  
Was he a gentleman?

FIRST CLOWN  
He was the first that ever bore arms.

SECOND CLOWN  
Why, he had none.

FIRST CLOWN  
What, are you a heathen? How do you read your Scripture? The Scripture says Adam dug. Could he dig without arms? I'll put another question to you. If you don't answer me to the point, confess yourself—

SECOND CLOWN  
Go on.

FIRST CLOWN  
What is he that builds stronger than either the mason, the shipwright, or the carpenter?

SECOND CLOWN  
The gallows-maker; for that frame outlasts a thousand tenants.

FIRST CLOWN  
I like your wit well, in good faith. The gallows does well. But how does it do well? It does well for those who do ill. Now, you do ill to say the gallows is built stronger than the church; argal, the gallows may do well for you. Have at it again, come.

SECOND CLOWN  
Who builds stronger than a mason, a shipwright, or a carpenter?

FIRST CLOWN  
Ay, tell me that, and knock off for the day.

SECOND CLOWN  
Marry, now I can tell.

FIRST CLOWN  
Out with it.

SECOND CLOWN  
Mass, I can't tell.

Enter Hamlet and Horatio, at a distance.

FIRST CLOWN  
Beat your brains over it no more, for your dull ass won't mend his pace for a beating; and when you're asked this question next, say "a grave-maker." The houses he makes last till doomsday. Go, get yourself to Yaughan, and fetch me a tankard of liquor.

[*Exit Second Clown.*]

[*Digs and sings.*]

In youth, when I did love, did love,  
I thought it was so sweet;  
to pass the time—ah—to suit my turn,  
Oh, I thought there was nothing so meet.

HAMLET  
Has this fellow no feeling for what he's about, that he sings at grave-making?

HORATIO  
Habit has made it easy work to him.

HAMLET  
That's just it—the hand that does little has the more delicate touch.

FIRST CLOWN  
[*Sings.*]  
But age, with his stealing steps,  
has clawed me in his clutch,  
and shipped me off into the ground,  
as if I had never been such.

[*Throws up a skull.*]

HAMLET  
That skull had a tongue in it, and could sing once. How the rascal dashes it to the ground, as if it were Cain's jawbone, that did the first murder! This might be the head of a politician, which this ass now lords it over—one that would outwit God himself, might it not?

HORATIO  
It might, my lord.

HAMLET  
Or of a courtier, who could say, "Good morning, sweet lord! How are you, good lord?" This might be my lord So-and-so, who praised my lord So-and-so's horse when he meant to beg it off him, might it not?

HORATIO  
Ay, my lord.

HAMLET  
Why, just so. And now it's my Lady Worm's—jawless, and knocked about the skull with a sexton's spade. Here's a fine overturning, if only we had the knack to see it. Did these bones cost no more in the breeding than to be played at skittles with? Mine ache to think of it.

FIRST CLOWN  
[*Sings.*]  
A pickaxe and a spade, a spade,  
and also a shrouding-sheet;  
Oh, a pit of clay to be made  
for such a guest is meet.

[*Throws up another skull.*]

HAMLET  
There's another. Why may that not be the skull of a lawyer? Where are his fine distinctions now, his quibbles, his cases, his titles, and his tricks? Why does he let this rude knave knock him about the head with a dirty shovel, and not charge him with assault and battery? Hmph. This fellow might have been, in his time, a great buyer of land, with his bonds, his recognizances, his fines, his double vouchers, his recoveries. Is this the end of his fines, and the recovery of his recoveries—to have his fine head full of fine dirt? Will his vouchers vouch him no more of his purchases, double ones and all, than the length and breadth of a pair of deeds? The very title-deeds to his lands will scarcely fit in this box; and must the inheritor himself have no more, ha?

HORATIO  
Not a jot more, my lord.

HAMLET  
Isn't parchment made of sheepskins?

HORATIO  
Ay, my lord, and of calfskins too.

HAMLET  
They are sheep and calves who look for their security in that. I'll speak to this fellow.—Whose grave is this, sir?

FIRST CLOWN  
Mine, sir.  
[*Sings.*]  
Oh, a pit of clay to be made  
for such a guest is meet.

HAMLET  
I think it is yours, indeed, for you lie in it.

FIRST CLOWN  
You lie outside it, sir, and so it's not yours. For my part, I do not lie in it, yet it is mine.

HAMLET  
You do lie in it, to be in it and say it's yours. It's for the dead, not the living; therefore you lie.

FIRST CLOWN  
It's a lively lie, sir; it'll jump back from me to you.

HAMLET  
What man do you dig it for?

FIRST CLOWN  
For no man, sir.

HAMLET  
What woman, then?

FIRST CLOWN  
For no woman either.

HAMLET  
Who is to be buried in it?

FIRST CLOWN  
One that was a woman, sir; but, rest her soul, she's dead.

HAMLET  
How precise the knave is! We must speak by the book, or wordplay will be the ruin of us. By the Lord, Horatio, these three years I have noticed it: the age has grown so refined that the toe of the peasant comes so close to the heel of the courtier, it chafes his blister.—How long have you been a grave-maker?

FIRST CLOWN  
Of all the days in the year, I came to it the day our late King Hamlet overcame Fortinbras.

HAMLET  
How long ago is that?

FIRST CLOWN  
Can't you tell? Every fool can tell that. It was the very day young Hamlet was born—he that's mad, and sent to England.

HAMLET  
Ay, marry, why was he sent to England?

FIRST CLOWN  
Why, because he was mad. He'll recover his wits there; or if he doesn't, it's no great matter there.

HAMLET  
Why?

FIRST CLOWN  
It won't show in him there; there the men are as mad as he is.

HAMLET  
How did he go mad?

FIRST CLOWN  
Very strangely, they say.

HAMLET  
How strangely?

FIRST CLOWN  
Faith, by losing his wits.

HAMLET  
On what grounds?

FIRST CLOWN  
Why, here in Denmark. I have been sexton here, man and boy, thirty years.

HAMLET  
How long will a man lie in the earth before he rots?

FIRST CLOWN  
Faith, if he isn't rotten before he dies—as we have many poxed corpses these days that will scarcely hold together long enough to lay them in—he'll last you some eight or nine years. A tanner will last you nine years.

HAMLET  
Why he longer than another?

FIRST CLOWN  
Why, sir, his hide is so tanned by his trade that he'll keep out water a good while; and water is a sore decayer of your damned dead body. Here's a skull now; this skull has lain in the earth three-and-twenty years.

HAMLET  
Whose was it?

FIRST CLOWN  
A mad rascal's it was. Whose do you think it was?

HAMLET  
No, I don't know.

FIRST CLOWN  
A plague on him for a mad rogue! He poured a flagon of Rhine wine on my head once. This same skull, sir, was Yorick's skull, the King's jester.

HAMLET  
This one?

FIRST CLOWN  
That very one.

HAMLET  
Let me see. [*Takes the skull.*] Alas, poor Yorick! I knew him, Horatio—a fellow of infinite jest, of most excellent imagination. He has carried me on his back a thousand times; and now—how loathsome it is to my imagination! My stomach turns at it. Here hung those lips that I have kissed I don't know how often. Where are your jibes now? Your gambols? Your songs? Your flashes of merriment, that used to set the whole table roaring? Not one now, to mock your own grinning? Quite chapfallen? Now get you to my lady's chamber, and tell her—though she paint herself an inch thick, to this state she must come at last. Make her laugh at that.—Please, Horatio, tell me one thing.

HORATIO  
What's that, my lord?

HAMLET  
Do you think Alexander looked like this in the earth?

HORATIO  
Just so.

HAMLET  
And smelled so? Pah!

[*Throws down the skull.*]

HORATIO  
Just so, my lord.

HAMLET  
To what base uses we may return, Horatio! Why may not imagination trace the noble dust of Alexander, till it finds it stopping a bunghole?

HORATIO  
That would be to think too curiously, to think so.

HAMLET  
No, faith, not a jot. But to follow him there with restraint enough, and likelihood to lead the way—like this. Alexander died, Alexander was buried, Alexander returns to dust; the dust is earth; of earth we make clay; and why of that clay, into which he was turned, might they not stop a beer-barrel?  
Imperious Caesar, dead and turned to clay,  
might stop a hole to keep the wind away.  
Oh, that the earth which kept the world in awe  
should patch a wall to expel the winter's flaw.  
But soft! But soft! Aside! Here comes the King.

Enter priests, etc., in procession; the corpse of Ophelia, Laertes and Mourners following; King, Queen, their Trains, etc.

The Queen, the courtiers. Who is it they follow?  
And with such maimed rites? This shows  
the corpse they follow, with a desperate hand,  
undid its own life. It was someone of rank.  
Let's hide a while and watch.

[*Retiring with Horatio.*]

LAERTES  
What other ceremony?

HAMLET  
That is Laertes, a very noble youth. Watch.

LAERTES  
What other ceremony?

PRIEST  
Her funeral rites have been stretched as far  
as we are warranted. Her death was in doubt;  
and but that a great command overrules the rule,  
she should have been lodged in unhallowed ground  
till the last trumpet. Instead of charitable prayers,  
shards, flints, and pebbles should be thrown on her.  
Yet here she is allowed her virgin rites,  
her maiden garlands strewn, and the bringing home  
with bell and burial.

LAERTES  
Must no more be done?

PRIEST  
No more be done.  
We should profane the service of the dead  
to sing a solemn requiem and grant such rest to her  
as to souls departed in peace.

LAERTES  
Lay her in the earth,  
and from her fair and undefiled flesh  
may violets spring. I tell you, churlish priest,  
a ministering angel shall my sister be  
when you lie howling.

HAMLET  
What, the fair Ophelia?

QUEEN  
[*Scattering flowers.*] Sweets to the sweet. Farewell.  
I hoped you would have been my Hamlet's wife;  
I thought to deck your bridal bed, sweet maid,  
and not to strew your grave.

LAERTES  
Oh, may threefold woe  
fall ten times threefold on that cursed head  
whose wicked deed robbed you of your fine mind.  
Hold off the earth a while,  
till I have caught her once more in my arms.  
[*Leaps into the grave.*]  
Now pile your dust upon the living and the dead,  
till of this flat ground you have made a mountain  
to overtop old Pelion, or the sky-high crown  
of blue Olympus.

HAMLET  
[*Advancing.*]  
What is he whose grief  
bears such a weight? whose words of sorrow  
call a halt to the wandering stars, and make them stand  
like wonder-stricken listeners? This is I,  
Hamlet the Dane.  
[*Leaps into the grave.*]

LAERTES  
[*Grappling with him.*] The devil take your soul!

HAMLET  
You pray badly.  
I beg you, take your fingers from my throat;  
for though I am not hot-tempered and rash,  
yet I have something dangerous in me  
that your wisdom should fear. Off with your hand!

KING  
Pull them apart.

QUEEN  
Hamlet! Hamlet!

All.  
Gentlemen!

HORATIO  
Good my lord, be calm.

[*The Attendants part them, and they come out of the grave.*]

HAMLET  
Why, I will fight with him over this  
till my eyelids can no longer stir.

QUEEN  
O my son, over what?

HAMLET  
I loved Ophelia. Forty thousand brothers  
could not, with all their quantity of love,  
make up my sum. What will you do for her?

KING  
Oh, he is mad, Laertes.

QUEEN  
For the love of God, leave him be!

HAMLET  
God's wounds, show me what you'll do.  
Will you weep? fight? fast? tear yourself apart?  
Will you drink down vinegar? eat a crocodile?  
I'll do it. Did you come here to whine?  
To show me up by leaping in her grave?  
Be buried alive with her, and so will I.  
And if you babble of mountains, let them throw  
millions of acres on us, till our mound,  
scorching its crown against the burning sun,  
makes Ossa look like a wart. No—if you'll rant,  
I'll rant as well as you.

QUEEN  
This is sheer madness;  
and so for a while the fit will work on him.  
Then, as patient as the mother dove  
when her pair of golden chicks have hatched,  
he'll sit drooping in silence.

HAMLET  
Listen, sir;  
what is the reason you treat me like this?  
I always loved you. But it is no matter.  
Let Hercules himself do what he will,  
the cat will mew, and the dog will have his day.

[*Exit.*]

KING  
I beg you, good Horatio, keep an eye on him.

[*Exit Horatio.*]

[*To Laertes*]  
Strengthen your patience, mindful of last night's talk;  
we'll bring the matter to its head right now.—  
Good Gertrude, set some watch over your son.  
This grave shall have a living monument.  
An hour of quiet we shall shortly see;  
till then, let our proceeding patient be.

[*Exeunt.*]

SCENE II. A hall in the Castle.

Enter Hamlet and Horatio.

HAMLET  
So much for this, sir. Now let me tell you the other;  
you remember all the circumstances?

HORATIO  
Remember them, my lord!

HAMLET  
Sir, in my heart there was a kind of fighting  
that would not let me sleep. I thought I lay  
worse off than mutineers in chains. Rashly—  
and praised be rashness for it—let us grant  
that our impulsiveness sometimes serves us well  
when our deep plans fall through; and that should teach us  
there's a divinity that shapes our ends,  
rough-hew them how we will.

HORATIO  
That is most certain.

HAMLET  
Up from my cabin,  
my sea-cloak wrapped about me, in the dark  
I groped to find them; got my wish,  
fingered their packet, and in the end withdrew  
to my own room again—making so bold,  
my fears forgetting manners, as to break the seal  
on their grand commission. There I found, Horatio—  
Oh, royal villainy!—an exact command,  
larded with all sorts of reasons,  
touching on Denmark's safety, and England's too—  
with, ho!—such bogeys and goblins made of my life  
that, the instant it was read, with no delay allowed,  
no, not even to wait while the axe was sharpened,  
my head should be struck off.

HORATIO  
Is it possible?

HAMLET  
Here's the commission; read it at more leisure.  
But will you hear how I went on from there?

HORATIO  
I beg you to.

HAMLET  
Being thus hemmed round with villainies—  
before I could so much as set my brains a prologue,  
they had begun the play—I sat me down,  
devised a new commission, wrote it fair.  
I once held, as our statesmen do,  
that to write a fair hand was beneath me, and worked hard  
to forget the skill; but, sir, now  
it did me yeoman's service. Will you hear  
the gist of what I wrote?

HORATIO  
Ay, good my lord.

HAMLET  
An earnest plea from the King—  
that as England was his faithful tributary,  
as love between them should flourish like the palm,  
as peace should still wear her crown of wheat  
and stand as a link between their friendships,  
and many such weighty "as"es of great import—  
that upon the reading and the understanding of these contents,  
without debate, no more, no less,  
he should put the bearers to sudden death,  
with no time allowed for confession.

HORATIO  
How was this sealed?

HAMLET  
Why, even in that, heaven was guiding me.  
I had my father's signet in my purse,  
which was the model of that Danish seal.  
I folded the writ in the form of the other,  
signed it, gave it the imprint, set it safely back,  
the swap never known. Now, the next day  
was our sea-fight; and what followed from that  
you already know.

HORATIO  
So Guildenstern and Rosencrantz go to it.

HAMLET  
Why, man, they courted this assignment.  
They are not on my conscience; their downfall  
grows from their own meddling.  
It's dangerous when the lesser sort come in  
between the thrusts and fierce, enraged points  
of mighty opposites.

HORATIO  
Why, what a king this is!

HAMLET  
Doesn't it now, don't you think, fall to me—  
he that has killed my king, and whored my mother,  
slipped in between the election and my hopes,  
cast his hook for my very life,  
and with such trickery—is it not perfect conscience  
to pay him back with this arm? And is it not to be damned  
to let this canker of our nature grow  
to further evil?

HORATIO  
It must soon be made known to him from England  
what the outcome of the business there has been.

HAMLET  
It will be soon. The interval is mine;  
and a man's life is no more than to say "one."  
But I am very sorry, good Horatio,  
that I forgot myself with Laertes;  
for in the likeness of my own cause I see  
the very portrait of his. I'll court his favor.  
But surely the showy display of his grief threw me  
into a towering rage.

HORATIO  
Quiet, who comes here?

Enter Osric.

OSRIC  
Your lordship is right welcome back to Denmark.

HAMLET  
I humbly thank you, sir. Do you know this water-fly?

HORATIO  
No, my good lord.

HAMLET  
You're the more blessed for it, for it's a vice to know him. He has much land, and fertile; let a beast be lord of beasts, and his trough shall stand at the king's table. He's a jackdaw, but, as I say, vast in his holdings of dirt.

OSRIC  
Sweet lord, if your lordship were at leisure, I should impart a thing to you from his Majesty.

HAMLET  
I will receive it with all diligence of spirit. Put your hat to its right use; it's for the head.

OSRIC  
I thank your lordship; it's very hot.

HAMLET  
No, believe me, it's very cold; the wind is northerly.

OSRIC  
It is rather cold, my lord, indeed.

HAMLET  
And yet it seems to me very sultry and hot for my constitution.

OSRIC  
Exceedingly, my lord; it is very sultry—as it were—I can't say how. But, my lord, his Majesty bade me tell you that he has laid a great wager on your head. Sir, here is the matter—

HAMLET  
I beg you, remember—

[*Hamlet moves him to put on his hat.*]

OSRIC  
No, in good faith; for my own comfort, in good faith. Sir, here is newly come to court Laertes—believe me, an absolute gentleman, full of the most excellent qualities, of very gentle manner and fine presence. Indeed, to speak truly of him, he is the very model and calendar of gentility; for you'll find in him the whole continent of every quality a gentleman would wish to see.

HAMLET  
Sir, his definement suffers no perdition in you—though I know that to divide him inventorially would dizzy the arithmetic of memory, and yet but yaw neither, in respect of his quick sail. But, in the verity of extolment, I take him to be a soul of great article, and his infusion of such dearth and rareness that, to make true diction of him, his semblable is his mirror, and whoever else would trace him, his umbrage, nothing more.

OSRIC  
Your lordship speaks most unerringly of him.

HAMLET  
The concernancy, sir? Why do we wrap the gentleman in our cruder breath?

OSRIC  
Sir?

HORATIO  
Is it not possible to make oneself understood in plainer speech? You'll manage it, sir, truly.

HAMLET  
What is the point of naming this gentleman?

OSRIC  
Of Laertes?

HORATIO  
His purse is empty already; all his golden words are spent.

HAMLET  
Of him, sir.

OSRIC  
I know you are not ignorant—

HAMLET  
I wish you did know, sir; though, in faith, even if you did, it would not do much for my credit. Well, sir?

OSRIC  
You are not ignorant of what excellence Laertes is—

HAMLET  
I dare not confess that, lest I should set myself up as his equal in excellence; but to know another man well, one would have to know oneself.

OSRIC  
I mean, sir, in his swordsmanship; but in the reputation given him by those in his pay, he has no equal.

HAMLET  
What's his weapon?

OSRIC  
Rapier and dagger.

HAMLET  
That's two of his weapons. But, very well.

OSRIC  
The King, sir, has wagered six Barbary horses, against which Laertes has imponed, as I take it, six French rapiers and daggers, with their fittings—belt, hangers, and so on. Three of the carriages, in faith, are very pleasing to the eye, very well matched to the hilts, most delicate carriages, and of very elegant design.

HAMLET  
What do you call the carriages?

HORATIO  
I knew you'd need a note in the margin before you were through.

OSRIC  
The carriages, sir, are the hangers.

HAMLET  
The word would suit the matter better if we could carry cannon at our sides. Let it be hangers till then. But on. Six Barbary horses against six French swords, their fittings, and three elegantly designed carriages—that's the French bet against the Danish. Why is all this imponed, as you call it?

OSRIC  
The King, sir, has wagered that in a dozen passes between you and him, Laertes shall not outscore you by three hits. He has wagered twelve hits against nine; and it would come to immediate trial, if your lordship would grant your answer.

HAMLET  
What if I answer no?

OSRIC  
I mean, my lord, the matching of your person in the trial.

HAMLET  
Sir, I will walk here in the hall. If it please his Majesty, this is my hour of exercise. Let the foils be brought, the gentleman willing, and the King holding to his purpose, I will win for him if I can; if not, I'll gain nothing but my shame and the extra hits.

OSRIC  
Shall I report you just so?

HAMLET  
To that effect, sir; with whatever flourishes suit your nature.

OSRIC  
I commend my duty to your lordship.

HAMLET  
Yours, yours.

[*Exit Osric.*]

He does well to commend it himself; there are no other tongues that would.

HORATIO  
This lapwing runs off with the shell still on his head.

HAMLET  
He paid court to his nurse's breast before he sucked it. Thus he—and many more of the same flock that I know this shallow age dotes on—have only caught the tune of the times and the outward fashion of manners; a kind of frothy mixture that carries them through the most refined and well-sifted opinions; and yet just blow on them for a test, and the bubbles burst.

Enter a Lord.

LORD  
My lord, his Majesty sent his compliments to you by young Osric, who brings back word that you are waiting for him in the hall. He sends to ask whether it is still your pleasure to play with Laertes, or whether you'll take more time.

HAMLET  
I am steady in my purposes; they follow the King's pleasure. If he is ready, I am ready—now or whenever, provided I am as fit as I am now.

LORD  
The King and Queen and all are coming down.

HAMLET  
In good time.

LORD  
The Queen wishes you to show some courtesy to Laertes before you begin to play.

HAMLET  
She advises me well.

[*Exit Lord.*]

HORATIO  
You will lose this wager, my lord.

HAMLET  
I don't think so. Since he went into France, I have practiced continually. I shall win at the odds. But you wouldn't believe how ill I feel here, about my heart. But it's no matter.

HORATIO  
Nay, good my lord—

HAMLET  
It's only foolishness; but it's the kind of misgiving that would perhaps trouble a woman.

HORATIO  
If your mind dislikes anything, obey it. I'll head them off from coming here, and say you are not fit.

HAMLET  
Not at all; we defy omens. There's a special providence in the fall of a sparrow. If it is now, it is not to come; if it is not to come, it will be now; if it is not now, yet it will come. The readiness is all. Since no man owns any of what he leaves, what does it matter to leave early?

Enter King, Queen, Laertes, Lords, Osric and Attendants with foils, etc.

KING  
Come, Hamlet, come, and take this hand from me.

[*The King puts Laertes's hand into Hamlet's.*]

HAMLET  
Give me your pardon, sir. I have wronged you;  
but pardon it, as you are a gentleman.  
This company knows, and you must have heard,  
how I am afflicted with a sore derangement.  
What I have done  
that might roughly wake your nature, your honor,  
and your resentment, I here declare was madness.  
Was it Hamlet wronged Laertes? Never Hamlet.  
If Hamlet is taken away from himself,  
and, not being himself, does Laertes wrong,  
then Hamlet does not do it; Hamlet denies it.  
Who does it, then? His madness. If that's so,  
Hamlet is on the side of the one wronged;  
his madness is poor Hamlet's enemy.  
Sir, before this audience,  
let my disowning of any deliberate wrong  
clear me so far in your most generous thoughts  
that I may seem to have shot my arrow over the house  
and hurt my brother.

LAERTES  
I am satisfied in my heart,  
whose feeling in this case should move me most  
to take revenge. But in matters of my honor  
I hold back, and want no reconciliation  
till some older masters of proven honor  
grant me a precedent and verdict for peace,  
to keep my name unstained. But till that time  
I receive your offered love as love,  
and will not wrong it.

HAMLET  
I embrace it freely,  
and will play this brotherly wager in good faith.—  
Give us the foils; come on.

LAERTES  
Come, one for me.

HAMLET  
I'll be your foil, Laertes; against my clumsiness  
your skill, like a star in the darkest night,  
will shine out bright indeed.

LAERTES  
You mock me, sir.

HAMLET  
No, by this hand.

KING  
Give them the foils, young Osric. Cousin Hamlet,  
you know the wager?

HAMLET  
Very well, my lord.  
Your Grace has laid the odds on the weaker side.

KING  
I do not fear it. I have seen you both;  
but since he has improved, we have the odds.

LAERTES  
This is too heavy. Let me see another.

HAMLET  
This suits me well. Are these foils all one length?

[*They prepare to play.*]

OSRIC  
Ay, my good lord.

KING  
Set the flagons of wine on that table.  
If Hamlet gives the first or second hit,  
or answers back on the third exchange,  
let all the battlements fire off their cannon.  
The King shall drink to Hamlet's better wind,  
and into the cup he'll throw a union  
richer than that which four successive kings  
have worn in Denmark's crown. Give me the cups;  
and let the kettledrum speak to the trumpet,  
the trumpet to the cannoneer outside,  
the cannons to the heavens, the heavens to earth,  
"Now the King drinks to Hamlet." Come, begin.  
And you, the judges, keep a watchful eye.

HAMLET  
Come on, sir.

LAERTES  
Come, my lord.

[*They play.*]

HAMLET  
One.

LAERTES  
No.

HAMLET  
Judgment.

OSRIC  
A hit, a very palpable hit.

LAERTES  
Well; again.

KING  
Stay, give me drink. Hamlet, this pearl is yours;  
here's to your health.

[*Trumpets sound, and cannon shot off within.*]

Give him the cup.

HAMLET  
I'll play this round first; set it by a while.

[*They play.*]

Come. Another hit; what do you say?

LAERTES  
A touch, a touch, I do confess.

KING  
Our son shall win.

QUEEN  
He's out of shape, and short of breath.  
Here, Hamlet, take my handkerchief; wipe your brow.  
The Queen drinks to your fortune, Hamlet.

HAMLET  
Good madam.

KING  
Gertrude, do not drink.

QUEEN  
I will, my lord; I pray you, pardon me.

KING  
[*Aside.*] It is the poisoned cup; it is too late.

HAMLET  
I dare not drink yet, madam. By and by.

QUEEN  
Come, let me wipe your face.

LAERTES  
My lord, I'll hit him now.

KING  
I do not think so.

LAERTES  
[*Aside.*] And yet it's almost against my conscience.

HAMLET  
Come, the third bout, Laertes. You're only toying with me.  
I beg you, thrust with your best force;  
I fear you're making a fool of me.

LAERTES  
Is that so? Come on.

[*They play.*]

OSRIC  
Nothing either way.

LAERTES  
Have at you now!

[*Laertes wounds Hamlet; then, in scuffling, they change rapiers, and Hamlet wounds Laertes.*]

KING  
Part them; they are enraged.

HAMLET  
No, come again!

[*The Queen falls.*]

OSRIC  
Look to the Queen there, ho!

HORATIO  
They bleed on both sides. How is it, my lord?

OSRIC  
How is it, Laertes?

LAERTES  
Why, like a woodcock caught in my own snare, Osric.  
I am justly killed by my own treachery.

HAMLET  
How is the Queen?

KING  
She faints to see them bleed.

QUEEN  
No, no, the drink, the drink! O my dear Hamlet!  
The drink, the drink! I am poisoned.

[*Dies.*]

HAMLET  
O villainy! Ho! Let the door be locked.  
Treachery! Hunt it out.

[*Laertes falls.*]

LAERTES  
It is here, Hamlet. Hamlet, you are slain.  
No medicine in the world can do you good.  
In you there is not half an hour of life.  
The treacherous instrument is in your hand,  
unblunted and envenomed. The foul scheme  
has turned itself on me. Look, here I lie,  
never to rise again. Your mother's poisoned.  
I can say no more. The King, the King's to blame.

HAMLET  
The point envenomed too!  
Then, venom, to your work.

[*Stabs the King.*]

OSRIC and LORDS  
Treason! treason!

KING  
Oh, still defend me, friends. I am only hurt.

HAMLET  
Here, you incestuous, murderous, damned Dane,  
drink off this potion. Is your union here?  
Follow my mother.

[*King dies.*]

LAERTES  
He is justly served.  
It is a poison he himself prepared.  
Exchange forgiveness with me, noble Hamlet.  
Let my death and my father's not fall on you,  
nor yours on me.

[*Dies.*]

HAMLET  
Heaven absolve you of it! I follow you.  
I am dead, Horatio. Wretched Queen, farewell.  
You that look pale and tremble at this turn,  
that are but silent watchers of this act—  
had I but time—since this grim officer, Death,  
is strict in making his arrest—Oh, I could tell you—  
but let it be. Horatio, I am dead;  
you live; report me and my cause aright  
to those who do not know.

HORATIO  
Never believe it.  
I am more an ancient Roman than a Dane.  
Here's still some liquor left.

HAMLET  
As you are a man,  
give me the cup. Let go. By Heaven, I'll have it.  
O good Horatio, what a wounded name—  
things standing thus unknown—I'll leave behind me.  
If you ever held me in your heart,  
absent thee from felicity awhile,  
and in this harsh world draw your breath in pain  
to tell my story.

[*March afar off, and shot within.*]

What warlike noise is this?

OSRIC  
Young Fortinbras, returned in triumph from Poland,  
gives this warlike volley to the ambassadors  
of England.

HAMLET  
Oh, I die, Horatio.  
The potent poison quite overcrows my spirit.  
I cannot live to hear the news from England;  
but I do prophesy the election lights  
on Fortinbras. He has my dying voice.  
So tell him that, with all that has befallen,  
great and small, that prompted— The rest is silence.

[*Dies.*]

HORATIO  
Now cracks a noble heart. Good night, sweet prince,  
and flights of angels sing thee to thy rest.  
Why does the drum come this way?

[*March within.*]

Enter Fortinbras, the English Ambassadors and others.

FORTINBRAS  
Where is this sight?

HORATIO  
What is it you would see?  
If it's anything of grief or wonder, end your search.

FORTINBRAS  
This heap of dead cries slaughter. O proud Death,  
what feast is in the making in your eternal cell,  
that you have struck down so many princes  
at one bloody blow?

FIRST AMBASSADOR  
The sight is grim;  
and our news from England comes too late.  
The ears are deaf that should give us hearing,  
to tell him his command is carried out,  
that Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are dead.  
Where should we look for our thanks?

HORATIO  
Not from his mouth,  
had it the power of life to thank you.  
He never gave the order for their death.  
But since, so hard upon this bloody business,  
you from the Polish wars, and you from England,  
are arrived here, give order that these bodies  
be placed high on a platform, in full view;  
and let me tell the world, still in the dark,  
how these things came about. So you shall hear  
of carnal, bloody, and unnatural acts,  
of accidental judgments, chance slaughters,  
of deaths brought on by cunning and by forced occasion,  
and, in this final reckoning, of purposes mistaken  
fallen back on their plotters' heads. All this I can  
truly relate.

FORTINBRAS  
Let us hurry to hear it,  
and call the noblest to the audience.  
For myself, with sorrow I embrace my fortune.  
I have some rights of long standing in this kingdom,  
which now my advantage invites me to claim.

HORATIO  
Of that I too shall have cause to speak,  
and from the mouth of one whose voice will draw on more.  
But let this be done at once,  
while men's minds are wild, in case more mischance  
comes of plots and errors.

FORTINBRAS  
Let four captains  
bear Hamlet, like a soldier, to the platform;  
for he was likely, had he been put to the test,  
to have proved most royal. And for his passing,  
let the soldiers' music and the rites of war  
speak loudly for him.  
Take up the bodies. A sight like this  
becomes the battlefield, but here it shows much amiss.  
Go, bid the soldiers fire.

[*A dead march.*]

[*Exeunt, bearing off the bodies, after which a peal of ordnance is shot off.*]
