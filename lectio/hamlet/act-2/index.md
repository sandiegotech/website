# Hamlet, Act II

SCENE I. A room in Polonius's house.

Enter Polonius and Reynaldo.

POLONIUS  
Give him this money and these notes, Reynaldo.

REYNALDO  
I will, my lord.

POLONIUS  
You'll do marvelously wisely, good Reynaldo,  
to make some inquiry about his behavior  
before you visit him.

REYNALDO  
My lord, I did intend to.

POLONIUS  
Well said—very well said. Look here, sir:  
first ask what Danes there are in Paris,  
and how, and who, what means they have, and where they lodge,  
what company they keep, at what expense; and finding,  
by this roundabout drift of questions,  
that they do know my son, then close in nearer  
than your direct questions would reach.  
Let on that you have some distant knowledge of him—  
as thus: "I know his father and his friends,  
and, in part, him." Do you follow this, Reynaldo?

REYNALDO  
Ay, very well, my lord.

POLONIUS  
"And, in part, him—but," you may say, "not well;  
but if it's the one I mean, he's very wild,  
addicted to this and that"—and then pin on him  
whatever lies you please. Nothing so vile, mind,  
as would dishonor him—take care of that—  
but, sir, such loose, wild, everyday slips  
as are the well-known companions  
of youth and freedom.

REYNALDO  
Like gambling, my lord?

POLONIUS  
Ay, or drinking, fencing, swearing,  
quarreling, whoring—you may go that far.

REYNALDO  
My lord, that would dishonor him.

POLONIUS  
No, honestly—not if you season the charge right.  
You must not lay another scandal on him,  
that he's given to outright debauchery;  
that's not my meaning. Breathe out his faults so deftly  
that they seem only the blemishes of freedom,  
the flash and outburst of a fiery mind,  
a wildness in unbroken blood,  
that strikes at all young men.

REYNALDO  
But, my good lord—

POLONIUS  
Why should you do this?

REYNALDO  
Ay, my lord, I'd like to know that.

POLONIUS  
Well, sir, here's my aim,  
and I believe it's a trick that works.  
When you've laid these slight stains on my son,  
as though he were a thing a little soiled in the handling—  
mark me—  
your man in conversation, the one you'd sound out,  
if he has ever seen the youth you describe  
guilty of the crimes you named, rest assured  
he'll fall in with you, like this:  
"Good sir," or so; or "friend," or "gentleman"—  
according to the phrase and the form of address  
of his rank and country.

REYNALDO  
Very good, my lord.

POLONIUS  
And then, sir, he does this—he does—  
What was I about to say? By the mass, I was about to say something. Where did I leave off?

REYNALDO  
At "he'll fall in with you,"  
at "friend or so," and "gentleman."

POLONIUS  
At "he'll fall in with you"—ay, indeed!  
He falls in with you like this: "I know the gentleman.  
I saw him yesterday, or the other day,  
or then, or then, with such and such; and, as you say,  
there he was gambling; there he was overtaken in his drink;  
there quarreling at tennis"—or perhaps,  
"I saw him go into such a house of ill repute"—  
that is, a brothel, or the like. You see now,  
your bait of falsehood catches this carp of truth;  
and so we men of wisdom and far reach,  
with roundabout casts and sidelong feints,  
by indirections find directions out.  
So, by my lesson and advice just now,  
shall you find out my son. You follow me, don't you?

REYNALDO  
My lord, I do.

POLONIUS  
God be with you. Fare you well.

REYNALDO  
Good my lord.

POLONIUS  
Study his leanings for yourself.

REYNALDO  
I shall, my lord.

POLONIUS  
And let him keep up his music.

REYNALDO  
Well, my lord.

POLONIUS  
Farewell.

[*Exit Reynaldo.*]

Enter Ophelia.

POLONIUS  
What now, Ophelia—what's the matter?

OPHELIA  
Oh, my lord, I have been so frightened.

POLONIUS  
By what, in the name of God?

OPHELIA  
My lord, as I was sewing in my room,  
Lord Hamlet—his jacket all unfastened,  
no hat upon his head, his stockings dirty,  
ungartered, and fallen down around his ankles,  
pale as his shirt, his knees knocking together,  
and with a look so pitiful in its meaning  
as if he had been let loose out of hell  
to tell of horrors—he comes before me.

POLONIUS  
Mad for love of you?

OPHELIA  
My lord, I do not know, but truly I do fear it.

POLONIUS  
What did he say?

OPHELIA  
He took me by the wrist and held me hard;  
then draws back to the full length of his arm,  
and with his other hand held thus above his brow,  
he falls to studying my face so closely  
as though he'd draw it. Long he stayed like that.  
At last—a little shaking of my arm,  
and three times nodding his head up and down—  
he raised a sigh so pitiful and deep  
it seemed to shatter his whole frame  
and end his life. That done, he lets me go,  
and, with his head turned back over his shoulder,  
he seemed to find his way without his eyes,  
for out the door he went without their help,  
and to the last kept their light fixed on me.

POLONIUS  
Come, go with me. I'll go and find the King.  
This is the very madness of love,  
whose violent nature destroys itself  
and drives the will to desperate undertakings,  
as often as any passion under heaven  
that afflicts our nature. I am sorry—  
what, have you given him any hard words lately?

OPHELIA  
No, my good lord; but, as you did command,  
I refused his letters and denied  
his access to me.

POLONIUS  
That has made him mad.  
I'm sorry that I didn't watch him  
with better care and judgment. I feared he was only toying with you,  
and meant to ruin you. But curse my suspicion!  
It seems it's as natural to us old men  
to overshoot in our suspicions  
as it is common for the younger sort  
to lack all caution. Come, let's go to the King.  
This must be known; kept quiet, it might breed  
more grief by hiding than hate by telling this love.

[*Exeunt.*]

SCENE II. A room in the Castle.

Enter King, Queen, Rosencrantz, Guildenstern and Attendants.

KING  
Welcome, dear Rosencrantz and Guildenstern.  
Besides our great longing to see you,  
the need we have to make use of you spurred  
our hasty summons. You've heard something  
of Hamlet's transformation—so I call it,  
since neither the outer nor the inner man  
resembles what it was. What it could be,  
beyond his father's death, that has so put him  
beside the understanding of himself,  
I cannot guess. I beg you both,  
since you were brought up with him from such young days,  
and are so close in age and temperament,  
to stay here at our court awhile,  
so that your company may draw him out  
to pleasures, and may gather, as occasion lets you,  
whether anything unknown to us afflicts him  
that, once revealed, lies within our cure.

QUEEN  
Good gentlemen, he has talked much of you,  
and I am sure there are not two men living  
to whom he's more attached. If it would please you  
to show us so much grace and good will  
as to spend some time with us awhile,  
for the help and furtherance of our hope,  
your visit shall receive such thanks  
as suit a king's remembering.

ROSENCRANTZ  
Both your majesties  
might, by the sovereign power you have over us,  
lay your dread wishes on us as commands  
rather than requests.

GUILDENSTERN  
We both obey,  
and here give ourselves up, to the full,  
to lay our service freely at your feet,  
to be commanded.

KING  
Thanks, Rosencrantz and gentle Guildenstern.

QUEEN  
Thanks, Guildenstern and gentle Rosencrantz.  
And I beg you, go at once and visit  
my too-much-changed son. Go, some of you,  
and bring these gentlemen to where Hamlet is.

GUILDENSTERN  
May heaven make our presence and our efforts  
pleasant and helpful to him.

QUEEN  
Ay, amen.

[*Exeunt Rosencrantz, Guildenstern and some Attendants.*]

Enter Polonius.

POLONIUS  
The ambassadors from Norway, my good lord,  
are back, and joyfully.

KING  
You have always been the father of good news.

POLONIUS  
Have I, my lord? I assure you, my good liege,  
I hold my duty, as I hold my soul,  
both to my God and to my gracious King;  
and I do think—or else this brain of mine  
no longer hunts the trail of statecraft as surely  
as it once did—that I have found  
the very cause of Hamlet's madness.

KING  
Oh, speak of that—that I long to hear.

POLONIUS  
Give the ambassadors their hearing first.  
My news shall be the dessert to that great feast.

KING  
Do them the honor yourself, and bring them in.

[*Exit Polonius.*]

KING  
He tells me, my sweet queen, that he has found  
the head and source of all your son's distress.

QUEEN  
I doubt it's anything but the obvious thing—  
his father's death and our too-hasty marriage.

KING  
Well, we shall sound him out.

Enter Polonius with Voltemand and Cornelius.

KING  
Welcome, my good friends!  
Say, Voltemand, what news from our brother of Norway?

VOLTEMAND  
The fairest return of greetings and goodwill.  
Upon our first word, he sent to suppress  
his nephew's troop levies, which he had thought  
were a preparation against Poland;  
but, looking closer, he truly found  
it was against your Highness. Grieved at this—  
that his own sickness, age, and helplessness  
had been so abused—he sends out orders  
arresting Fortinbras; who, in short, obeys,  
takes a rebuke from Norway, and in the end  
vows before his uncle never again  
to try force of arms against your Majesty.  
At which old Norway, overcome with joy,  
gives him three thousand crowns a year,  
and his commission to employ those soldiers,  
so levied as before, against the Poles;  
with a request, set out more fully here—  
[*Gives a paper.*]  
that it might please you to grant safe passage  
through your domains for this campaign,  
on such terms of safety and allowance  
as are written down inside.

KING  
It pleases us well;  
and at a more considered time we'll read,  
answer, and think upon this business.  
Meanwhile, we thank you for your good labor.  
Go, take your rest. Tonight we'll feast together.  
Most welcome home.

[*Exeunt Voltemand and Cornelius.*]

POLONIUS  
This business is well ended.  
My liege and madam, to hold forth on  
what majesty should be, what duty is,  
why day is day, night night, and time is time,  
would be only to waste night, day, and time.  
Therefore, since brevity is the soul of wit,  
and tediousness is the limbs and outward show,  
I will be brief. Your noble son is mad.  
Mad I call it; for, to define true madness,  
what is it but to be nothing else but mad?  
But let that go.

QUEEN  
More matter, with less art.

POLONIUS  
Madam, I swear I use no art at all.  
That he is mad, it's true; it's true it's a pity;  
and it's a pity it's true—a foolish turn of phrase,  
but goodbye to it, for I will use no art.  
Mad let us grant him, then. And now it remains  
that we find out the cause of this effect—  
or rather say, the cause of this defect,  
for this defective effect comes from a cause.  
Thus it stands, and the rest stands thus. Consider:  
I have a daughter—have, while she is mine—  
who, in her duty and obedience, mark,  
has given me this. Now hear it, and judge.  
[*Reads.*]  
"To the heavenly, and my soul's idol, the most beautified Ophelia"—  
that's an ugly phrase, a vile phrase; "beautified" is a vile phrase—but you shall hear. Then:  
[*Reads.*]  
"these; in her excellent white bosom, these," and so on.

QUEEN  
Did this come from Hamlet to her?

POLONIUS  
Good madam, wait awhile; I will be exact.  
[*Reads.*]  
"Doubt that the stars are fire,  
doubt that the sun does move,  
doubt truth to be a liar,  
but never doubt I love.  
O dear Ophelia, I am bad at this verse. I have no skill to count out my groans. But that I love you best, oh, best of all, believe it. Goodbye.  
Yours forever, most dear lady, as long as this body is his,  
HAMLET."  
This, in obedience, my daughter has shown me;  
and more than that, she has told me all his courting—  
as it fell out, by time, by means, and place—  
laid every word in my ear.

KING  
But how has she received his love?

POLONIUS  
What do you think of me?

KING  
As of a man faithful and honorable.

POLONIUS  
I'd gladly prove so. But what might you think,  
when I had seen this hot love taking wing—  
as I noticed it, I must tell you, before  
my daughter told me—what might you,  
or my dear Majesty, your queen here, think,  
if I had played the writing-desk or notebook,  
or winked at my own heart, gone mute and dumb,  
or looked upon this love with idle eyes—  
what might you think? No, I went straight to work,  
and to my young lady I said this:  
"Lord Hamlet is a prince, out of your sphere;  
this must not be." And then I gave her orders  
that she should shut herself off from his visits,  
admit no messengers, take in no gifts.  
Which done, she took the fruits of my advice;  
and he, rebuffed—to make the tale short—  
fell into a sadness, then into a fast,  
then to a sleeplessness, then to a weakness,  
then to a lightheadedness, and, by this decline,  
into the madness he now raves in,  
which all of us mourn.

KING  
Do you think it's this?

QUEEN  
It may be; very likely.

POLONIUS  
Has there ever been a time—I'd dearly like to know—  
when I have positively said "It is so,"  
and it proved otherwise?

KING  
Not that I know.

POLONIUS  
Take this from this, if it's otherwise.  
[*Points to his head and shoulder.*]  
If circumstances lead me, I will find  
where truth is hidden, though it were hidden  
at the center of the earth.

KING  
How can we test it further?

POLONIUS  
You know, sometimes he walks for hours together  
here in the lobby.

QUEEN  
So he does indeed.

POLONIUS  
At such a time, I'll loose my daughter to him.  
You and I will be behind a curtain then;  
watch the encounter. If he doesn't love her,  
and hasn't lost his reason over it,  
let me be no royal counselor,  
but keep a farm and farmhands.

KING  
We'll try it.

Enter Hamlet, reading.

QUEEN  
But look where the poor wretch comes, reading, so sadly.

POLONIUS  
Away, I beg you, both of you, away.  
I'll go at him right now. Oh, give me leave.

[*Exeunt King, Queen and Attendants.*]

POLONIUS  
How is my good Lord Hamlet?

HAMLET  
Well, thank God.

POLONIUS  
Do you know me, my lord?

HAMLET  
Very well. You're a fishmonger.

POLONIUS  
Not I, my lord.

HAMLET  
Then I wish you were as honest a man.

POLONIUS  
Honest, my lord?

HAMLET  
Ay, sir; to be honest, the way this world goes, is to be one man picked out of ten thousand.

POLONIUS  
That's very true, my lord.

HAMLET  
For if the sun breeds maggots in a dead dog—a carcass good enough for the sun to kiss—  
Have you a daughter?

POLONIUS  
I have, my lord.

HAMLET  
Don't let her walk in the sun. Conception is a blessing, but not the kind your daughter might come by. Friend, see to it.

POLONIUS  
[*Aside.*] What do you make of that? Still harping on my daughter. And yet he didn't know me at first; he said I was a fishmonger. He's far gone, far gone. And truly, in my youth I suffered great extremes for love—very near this. I'll speak to him again.—What are you reading, my lord?

HAMLET  
Words, words, words.

POLONIUS  
What is the matter, my lord?

HAMLET  
Between whom?

POLONIUS  
I mean the matter that you read, my lord.

HAMLET  
Slanders, sir. For this satirical rogue says here that old men have gray beards; that their faces are wrinkled; that their eyes ooze thick amber and gum from a plum tree; and that they have a plentiful lack of wit, together with very weak legs. All of which, sir, though I most powerfully and forcibly believe, still I don't think it decent to set it down like this. For you yourself, sir, would be as old as I am—if, like a crab, you could go backward.

POLONIUS  
[*Aside.*] Though this be madness, yet there is method in it.—Will you step out of the open air, my lord?

HAMLET  
Into my grave?

POLONIUS  
Indeed, that's out of the air. [*Aside.*] How telling his replies sometimes are! A luck that madness often hits on, which reason and sanity couldn't so happily give birth to. I'll leave him, and at once contrive a way to bring him and my daughter together.—My honorable lord, I'll most humbly take my leave of you.

HAMLET  
You cannot, sir, take anything from me that I'll more willingly part with—except my life, except my life, except my life.

POLONIUS  
Fare you well, my lord.

HAMLET  
These tedious old fools.

Enter Rosencrantz and Guildenstern.

POLONIUS  
You go to find Lord Hamlet; there he is.

ROSENCRANTZ  
[*To Polonius.*] God save you, sir.

[*Exit Polonius.*]

GUILDENSTERN  
My honored lord!

ROSENCRANTZ  
My most dear lord!

HAMLET  
My excellent good friends! How are you, Guildenstern? Ah, Rosencrantz. Good lads, how are you both?

ROSENCRANTZ  
Like the ordinary children of the earth.

GUILDENSTERN  
Happy in that we're not too happy;  
on Fortune's cap we're not the very button.

HAMLET  
Nor the soles of her shoe?

ROSENCRANTZ  
Neither, my lord.

HAMLET  
Then you live around her waist, or in the middle of her favors?

GUILDENSTERN  
Honestly, we're her privates.

HAMLET  
In the secret parts of Fortune? Oh, very true; she's a whore. What's the news?

ROSENCRANTZ  
None, my lord, except that the world's grown honest.

HAMLET  
Then doomsday's near. But your news isn't true. Let me question you more particularly. What have you, my good friends, done to deserve, at the hands of Fortune, that she sends you here to prison?

GUILDENSTERN  
Prison, my lord?

HAMLET  
Denmark's a prison.

ROSENCRANTZ  
Then the whole world is one.

HAMLET  
A fine one, in which there are many cells, wards, and dungeons—Denmark being one of the worst.

ROSENCRANTZ  
We don't think so, my lord.

HAMLET  
Why, then it's none to you; for there's nothing either good or bad, but thinking makes it so. To me it is a prison.

ROSENCRANTZ  
Why, then your ambition makes it one; it's too cramped for your mind.

HAMLET  
O God, I could be bounded in a nutshell and count myself a king of infinite space—were it not that I have bad dreams.

GUILDENSTERN  
Which dreams, indeed, are ambition; for the very substance of the ambitious is merely the shadow of a dream.

HAMLET  
A dream itself is only a shadow.

ROSENCRANTZ  
True, and I hold ambition to be of so airy and weightless a quality that it's only the shadow of a shadow.

HAMLET  
Then our beggars are the real bodies, and our monarchs and grand stretched-out heroes are the beggars' shadows. Shall we go to the court? For, by my faith, I cannot reason.

ROSENCRANTZ and GUILDENSTERN  
We'll attend you.

HAMLET  
No such thing. I won't lump you in with the rest of my servants; for, to speak to you like an honest man, I am most dreadfully waited on. But, on the plain road of friendship, what are you doing at Elsinore?

ROSENCRANTZ  
To visit you, my lord; no other reason.

HAMLET  
Beggar that I am, I'm even poor in thanks; but I thank you—and surely, dear friends, my thanks aren't worth a halfpenny. Weren't you sent for? Is it your own inclination? Is it a free visit? Come, deal honestly with me. Come, come—out with it.

GUILDENSTERN  
What should we say, my lord?

HAMLET  
Why, anything—but to the point. You were sent for; and there's a kind of confession in your faces, which your modesty hasn't the craft to disguise. I know the good King and Queen have sent for you.

ROSENCRANTZ  
To what end, my lord?

HAMLET  
That you must teach me. But let me appeal to you, by the bonds of our fellowship, by the harmony of our youth, by the duty of our ever-kept love, and by whatever dearer thing a better pleader could press on you—be straight and direct with me: were you sent for, or not?

ROSENCRANTZ  
[*To Guildenstern.*] What do you say?

HAMLET  
[*Aside.*] No, then I've got my eye on you.—If you love me, don't hold back.

GUILDENSTERN  
My lord, we were sent for.

HAMLET  
I'll tell you why; so my guessing will spare you the disclosure, and your secrecy to the King and Queen won't molt a feather. I have, lately—though why I don't know—lost all my mirth, given up all my usual exercises; and indeed it weighs so heavily on my spirit that this goodly frame, the earth, seems to me a sterile promontory; this most excellent canopy, the air—look you—this brave overhanging sky, this majestic roof studded with golden fire—why, it seems to me nothing but a foul and pestilent gathering of vapors. What a piece of work is man! How noble in reason, how infinite in faculties, in form and movement how precise and admirable, in action how like an angel, in understanding how like a god—the beauty of the world, the paragon of animals! And yet, to me, what is this quintessence of dust? Man delights not me—no, nor woman either, though by your smiling you seem to say so.

ROSENCRANTZ  
My lord, there was no such thought in my mind.

HAMLET  
Why did you laugh, then, when I said "Man delights not me"?

ROSENCRANTZ  
To think, my lord, that if man doesn't delight you, what a lean welcome the players will get from you. We passed them on the road; and they're coming here to offer you their service.

HAMLET  
The one who plays the king shall be welcome—his Majesty shall have his due from me; the adventurous knight shall use his foil and shield; the lover shall not sigh for free; the comic man shall finish his part in peace; the clown shall make those laugh whose lungs are easily tickled; and the lady shall speak her mind freely, or the blank verse shall limp for it. What players are they?

ROSENCRANTZ  
The very ones you used to take such delight in—the tragic actors of the city.

HAMLET  
How does it happen they're traveling? Staying put, both in reputation and profit, was better for them every way.

ROSENCRANTZ  
I think their ban from the city comes from the recent upheaval.

HAMLET  
Do they still stand in the same esteem they did when I was in the city? Are they so popular?

ROSENCRANTZ  
No, indeed, they're not.

HAMLET  
How's that? Are they growing rusty?

ROSENCRANTZ  
No, their effort keeps to its usual pace; but there is, sir, a whole nestful of children, little fledgling hawks, who shriek out their lines at the top of their range and are clapped for it most extravagantly. These are now the fashion, and they so rail at the ordinary stages—so they call them—that many a gentleman wearing a sword is afraid of the playwrights' pens and scarcely dares go there.

HAMLET  
What, are they children? Who keeps them? How are they paid? Will they follow the trade no longer than they can sing? Won't they say afterward—if they grow up to be ordinary players, which is most likely, if their means are no better—that their writers wronged them, making them rail against the very career they'll inherit?

ROSENCRANTZ  
Honestly, there's been much ado on both sides; and the nation thinks it no sin to egg them on to a quarrel. For a while there, no one would pay for a play unless the playwright and the actor came to blows in it.

HAMLET  
Is it possible?

GUILDENSTERN  
Oh, there's been a great throwing about of brains.

HAMLET  
Do the boys win the day?

ROSENCRANTZ  
Ay, that they do, my lord—Hercules and his load too.

HAMLET  
It's not so strange; for my uncle is King of Denmark, and those who would have made faces at him while my father lived now give twenty, forty, fifty, a hundred ducats apiece for his portrait in miniature. By God's blood, there is something in this beyond nature, if only philosophy could find it out.

[*Flourish of trumpets within.*]

GUILDENSTERN  
There are the players.

HAMLET  
Gentlemen, you're welcome to Elsinore. Your hands—come. The trappings of a welcome are manners and ceremony. Let me observe them with you, in this style, so that my reception of the players—which, I tell you, must look generous on the surface—won't seem warmer than yours. You're welcome. But my uncle-father and aunt-mother are deceived.

GUILDENSTERN  
In what, my dear lord?

HAMLET  
I'm only mad north-north-west. When the wind is southerly, I know a hawk from a handsaw.

Enter Polonius.

POLONIUS  
Good day to you, gentlemen.

HAMLET  
Listen, Guildenstern—and you too—a hearer at each ear: that great baby you see there isn't out of his swaddling clothes yet.

ROSENCRANTZ  
Maybe he's come round to them a second time; for they say an old man is twice a child.

HAMLET  
I'll prophesy that he comes to tell me about the players. Watch.—You're right, sir; it was indeed Monday morning.

POLONIUS  
My lord, I have news to tell you.

HAMLET  
My lord, I have news to tell you. When Roscius was an actor in Rome—

POLONIUS  
The actors have come here, my lord.

HAMLET  
Bah, bah.

POLONIUS  
Upon my honor.

HAMLET  
Then came each actor on his ass—

POLONIUS  
The best actors in the world, whether for tragedy, comedy, history, pastoral, pastoral-comical, historical-pastoral, tragical-historical, tragical-comical-historical-pastoral; for the strictly-plotted scene, or the poem without limits. Seneca can't be too heavy for them, nor Plautus too light. For the written rules and for free improvising, these are the only men.

HAMLET  
O Jephthah, judge of Israel, what a treasure you had!

POLONIUS  
What treasure did he have, my lord?

HAMLET  
Why—  
"One fair daughter, and no more,  
whom he loved exceedingly well."

POLONIUS  
[*Aside.*] Still on my daughter.

HAMLET  
Am I not right, old Jephthah?

POLONIUS  
If you call me Jephthah, my lord, I do have a daughter that I love exceedingly well.

HAMLET  
No, that's not what follows.

POLONIUS  
What follows, then, my lord?

HAMLET  
Why—  
"As by lot, God knows,"  
and then, you know,  
"it came to pass, as most likely it was—"  
The first line of the holy ballad will show you the rest. For look where my interruption comes.

Enter four or five Players.

HAMLET  
You're welcome, masters; welcome, all.—I'm glad to see you well.—Welcome, good friends.—Oh, my old friend! Your face is fringed with a beard since I saw you last. Have you come to brave me out in Denmark?—What, my young lady and mistress! By our Lady, your ladyship is nearer to heaven than when I saw you last—by the height of a high-heeled shoe. Pray God your voice, like a gold coin gone out of circulation, isn't cracked inside the ring. Masters, you're all welcome. We'll fall to it like French falconers, fly at anything we see. We'll have a speech right now. Come, give us a taste of your quality. Come, a passionate speech.

FIRST PLAYER  
What speech, my lord?

HAMLET  
I heard you once speak a speech to me—but it was never acted; or, if it was, not more than once, for the play, I remember, didn't please the masses; it was caviar to the common crowd. But it was—as I received it, and others, whose judgment in such matters outranked my own—an excellent play, well laid out in its scenes, set down with as much restraint as skill. I remember someone said there were no spicy bits in the lines to give the matter flavor, nor anything in the phrasing that could convict the author of affectation; but he called it an honest style, as wholesome as it was sweet, and far more handsome than fancy. One speech in it I especially loved—it was Aeneas's tale to Dido, and the part of it especially where he tells of Priam's killing. If it's still alive in your memory, begin at this line—let me see, let me see—  
"The rugged Pyrrhus, like the Hyrcanian beast—"  
No, that's not it; it begins with Pyrrhus—  
"The rugged Pyrrhus, he whose black armor,  
black as his purpose, was like the night  
when he lay hidden in the fateful horse,  
has now this dread and black complexion smeared  
with deadlier heraldry. From head to foot  
he's now all red, horribly painted over  
with the blood of fathers, mothers, daughters, sons,  
baked and crusted on by the burning streets,  
which lend a cruel and a damned light  
to their vile murders. Roasted in wrath and fire,  
and thus glazed thick with clotted gore,  
with eyes like burning coals, the hellish Pyrrhus  
seeks out old grandfather Priam."  
Now you go on.

POLONIUS  
Before God, my lord, well spoken—with good accent and good judgment.

FIRST PLAYER  
"Soon he finds him,  
striking too short at the Greeks. His ancient sword,  
rebelling against his arm, lies where it falls,  
deaf to command. In this unequal match,  
Pyrrhus drives at Priam, in his rage strikes wide;  
but at the mere whiff and wind of his savage sword  
the weak old father falls. Then lifeless Troy,  
seeming to feel this blow, with its flaming top  
crashes down to its base, and with a hideous roar  
takes Pyrrhus's ear prisoner. For look—his sword,  
which was coming down on the milk-white head  
of venerable Priam, seemed to stick in the air.  
So, like a tyrant in a painting, Pyrrhus stood,  
and, as if neutral between his will and his task,  
did nothing.  
But as we often see, before some storm,  
a silence in the heavens, the clouds standing still,  
the bold winds speechless, and the earth below  
as hushed as death—then suddenly the dreadful thunder  
splits the sky; so, after Pyrrhus's pause,  
roused vengeance sets him back to work,  
and never did the Cyclops' hammers fall  
on Mars's armor, forged to last forever,  
with less mercy than Pyrrhus's bleeding sword  
now falls on Priam.  
Away, away, you whore Fortune! All you gods,  
in full assembly, strip away her power;  
break all the spokes and rims from her wheel,  
and bowl the round hub down the hill of heaven,  
as far down as to hell."

POLONIUS  
This is too long.

HAMLET  
It shall go to the barber's, along with your beard.—Please, go on. He's only for a jig or a dirty story, or he falls asleep. Go on; come to Hecuba.

FIRST PLAYER  
"But who—oh, who—had seen the mobled queen—"

HAMLET  
"The mobled queen"?

POLONIUS  
That's good! "Mobled queen" is good.

FIRST PLAYER  
"running barefoot up and down, threatening the flames  
with blinding tears; a rag upon that head  
where lately the crown stood; and for a robe,  
about her thin and worn-out, much-childbearing waist,  
a blanket, snatched up in the panic of fear—  
whoever had seen this, with a tongue dipped in venom,  
would have proclaimed Fortune's reign a treason.  
But if the gods themselves had seen her then,  
when she saw Pyrrhus making cruel sport,  
hacking her husband's limbs with his sword,  
the sudden burst of outcry that she made—  
unless mortal things move them not at all—  
would have made the burning eyes of heaven weep,  
and stirred grief in the gods."

POLONIUS  
Look—hasn't he changed color, and got tears in his eyes? Please, no more.

HAMLET  
That's well. I'll have you speak the rest of this soon.—Good my lord, will you see the players properly looked after? Do you hear—let them be well used; for they are the summaries and brief chronicles of the age. You had better have a bad epitaph after your death than their ill report while you live.

POLONIUS  
My lord, I'll treat them as they deserve.

HAMLET  
God's little body, man, much better! Treat every man as he deserves, and who would escape a whipping? Use them after your own honor and dignity. The less they deserve, the more credit is in your generosity. Take them in.

POLONIUS  
Come, sirs.

HAMLET  
Follow him, friends. We'll hear a play tomorrow.

[*Exeunt Polonius with all the Players but the First.*]

HAMLET  
Do you hear me, old friend? Can you perform The Murder of Gonzago?

FIRST PLAYER  
Ay, my lord.

HAMLET  
We'll have it tomorrow night. You could, if needed, learn a speech of some dozen or sixteen lines, which I would write down and insert in it—could you not?

FIRST PLAYER  
Ay, my lord.

HAMLET  
Very well. Follow that lord—and see you don't mock him.

[*Exit First Player.*]

HAMLET  
[*To Rosencrantz and Guildenstern.*] My good friends, I'll leave you till tonight. You're welcome to Elsinore.

ROSENCRANTZ  
Good my lord.

[*Exeunt Rosencrantz and Guildenstern.*]

HAMLET  
Ay, so. God be with you.—Now I am alone.  
Oh, what a rogue and peasant slave am I!  
Isn't it monstrous that this player here,  
in a mere fiction, in a dream of passion,  
could force his soul so to his own imagining  
that, from its working, all his face went pale,  
tears in his eyes, distraction in his look,  
a broken voice, and his whole being shaped  
to fit his thought? And all for nothing!  
For Hecuba!  
What's Hecuba to him, or he to Hecuba,  
that he should weep for her? What would he do  
had he the motive and the cue for passion  
that I have? He would drown the stage with tears  
and split the general ear with dreadful speech,  
drive the guilty mad, and terrify the innocent,  
confound the ignorant, and stun, indeed,  
the very powers of eyes and ears. Yet I,  
a dull and muddy-spirited rascal, mope  
like a daydreamer, with nothing to say for my cause,  
and can say nothing—no, not for a king  
upon whose property and most dear life  
a damned defeat was worked. Am I a coward?  
Who calls me villain, breaks my skull across,  
plucks off my beard and blows it in my face,  
tweaks me by the nose, gives me the lie in the throat  
as deep as to the lungs? Who does this to me?  
Ha! By God's wounds, I'd have to take it; for it must be  
that I am pigeon-livered and lack the gall  
to make oppression bitter—or before now  
I'd have fattened all the kites of the air  
with this slave's guts. Bloody, lecherous villain!  
Remorseless, treacherous, lustful, unnatural villain!  
Oh, vengeance!  
Why, what an ass am I! This is most fine—  
that I, the son of a dear father murdered,  
prompted to my revenge by heaven and hell,  
must, like a whore, unpack my heart with words  
and fall to cursing like a common slut,  
a scullery-maid! Shame on it! Foh!  
Get to work, my brain! I have heard  
that guilty creatures, sitting at a play,  
have, by the very cunning of the scene,  
been struck so to the soul that on the spot  
they have confessed their crimes;  
for murder, though it has no tongue, will speak  
with a most miraculous voice. I'll have these players  
play something like the murder of my father  
before my uncle. I'll watch his face;  
I'll probe him to the quick. If he so much as flinches,  
I know my course. The spirit that I've seen  
may be the devil; and the devil has the power  
to take a pleasing shape; yes, and perhaps,  
out of my weakness and my melancholy—  
as he is very potent with such moods—  
he abuses me to damn me. I'll have grounds  
more solid than this. The play's the thing  
in which I'll catch the conscience of the King.

[*Exit.*]
