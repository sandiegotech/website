Confessions, Book V

Faustus and Ambrose

Book V5 of 13

Accept the sacrifice of my confessions from the hand of my tongue — you formed that tongue, and you stirred it up to confess your name. Heal all my bones, and let them say: Lord, who is like you? A man who confesses to you is not telling you anything new about himself; a closed heart does not shut out your eye, and the hardness of a human heart cannot push back your hand. You melt it whenever you choose, in mercy or in vengeance, and nothing can hide from your heat. Let my soul praise you, so that it may love you; let it confess your mercies to you, so that it may praise you. Your whole creation never stops praising you, never falls silent: every human spirit turns its voice toward you, and every animal and lifeless thing praises you through the mouths of those who contemplate them — so that our souls may rouse themselves out of weariness and rise toward you, leaning on the things you have made and passing beyond them to you, who made them so wonderfully. And there is refreshment there, and real strength.

Let the restless and the wicked run; let them flee from you. You see them anyway; you divide the shadows. And look — the whole, with them inside it, is beautiful, though they themselves are ugly. What harm have they done you? How have they disgraced your rule, which is just and whole from the heavens down to the lowest things of earth? Where did they run, when they ran from your face? Where will you fail to find them? They ran so they would not see you seeing them — ran blind, and blundered into you, because you abandon nothing you have made. The unjust blundered into you and were justly hurt: pulling away from your gentleness, striking against your straightness, falling on their own jaggedness. They do not know, evidently, that you are everywhere, that no place contains you, that you alone stay near even to those who put themselves far away. Then let them turn and look for you. You have not abandoned your creation the way they abandoned their Creator. Let them turn — and look, there you are, in their heart, in the heart of everyone who confesses to you, who throws himself on you and weeps into your breast after all his hard roads. And you gently wipe away the tears, and they weep the more, and are glad in the weeping — because it is you, Lord, not some human being of flesh and blood, but you, Lord, their maker, who remake them and console them. And where was I, when I was looking for you? You were right in front of me; but I had walked away — even from myself. I could not find myself. How much less you.

Let me lay open before my God my twenty-ninth year. A Manichee bishop named Faustus had just arrived in Carthage — a great snare of the devil, and many people were tangled in him, caught by the lure of his smooth talk. I admired the talk too, but I could tell it apart from the truth of the things I was hungry to learn: I cared less for the serving-dish of his rhetoric than for the knowledge this Faustus, so celebrated among them, was supposed to set in front of me. His reputation had reached me ahead of him: superbly trained in every honorable study, an expert in the liberal arts. And since I had read a good deal of the philosophers and kept it in memory, I compared some of their claims with the endless fables of the Manichees, and the philosophers seemed to me more probable — men with strength enough to take the measure of this world, though they never found its Lord. Because you are great, Lord, and you look kindly on the lowly, but the proud you know only from far off. You come near to the broken-hearted alone; the proud do not find you — not even if their exacting skill could count the stars and the grains of sand, survey the fields of the sky, and trace out the paths of the planets.

With the mind and the intelligence you gave them, they investigate these things, and they have found out a great deal. They predicted eclipses of the sun and the moon years in advance — the day, the hour, how much of the light would go — and the arithmetic did not fail them: it happened exactly as they foretold. They wrote down the rules they discovered, and the rules are read to this day, and from them others still predict the year, the month, the day, the hour, and what fraction of its light the moon or the sun is going to lose — and it goes dark on schedule. People who do not know the art marvel and are dumbfounded; people who know it exult and are puffed up. In their godless pride they draw back from you, and your light fails inside them; and they can see an eclipse of the sun coming years ahead, but their own eclipse, happening now, they cannot see. Because they never ask, reverently, where the intelligence came from that they use to investigate all this. And when they do discover that you made them, they do not hand themselves over to you, so that you can preserve what you made; they do not slaughter for you what they have made of themselves. They do not kill their soaring pretensions, like birds of the air; or their diving curiosities, like fish of the sea that wander the hidden paths of the deep; or their appetites, like beasts of the field — so that you, God, a devouring fire, could burn up their dead preoccupations and re-create the men themselves for immortality.

But they did not know the way: your Word, through whom you made the things they count, and the men who do the counting, and the senses by which they perceive what they count, and the mind they count with. And of your wisdom there is no counting. The Only-begotten himself was made wisdom for us, and righteousness, and sanctification; he was counted as one of us, and he paid his tax to Caesar. They did not know this way — the way down from themselves to him, and through him up to him. They did not know it; they thought themselves high and shining among the stars, and look, they crashed to the earth, and their foolish heart went dark. They say many true things about the creation, but the Truth, the master craftsman of creation, they do not seek reverently, and so they do not find him. Or if they find him, and know that he is God, they do not honor him as God or give thanks; they evaporate into their own speculations and call themselves wise, crediting to themselves what is yours — and then, with the most perverse blindness of all, they work to charge you with what is theirs, pinning lies on you, who are the Truth, and swapping the glory of the incorruptible God for an image shaped like corruptible man, like birds and four-footed animals and snakes — trading your truth for a lie, worshiping and serving the creature instead of the Creator.

Still, I held onto many true things these men said about creation, and I saw the reasoning behind them — the calculations, the ordered sequence of the seasons, the visible testimony of the stars — and I set it all against the sayings of Mani, who had written on these subjects at enormous length, raving. And in him I found no account of solstices, or equinoxes, or eclipses of the sun and moon, nothing of what I had learned in the books of ordinary philosophy. I was simply ordered to believe it; and it did not square with what calculation and my own eyes had established. It was flatly contrary.

Lord God of truth — is knowing such things enough to please you? A man who knows them all and does not know you is unhappy; a man who knows you, though he knows none of them, is happy. And whoever knows both you and them is not the happier for them; he is happy for you alone — if, knowing you, he honors you as God and gives thanks, and does not evaporate into his own thoughts. A man who owns a tree and thanks you for the use of it is better off — though he cannot say how tall it stands or how wide it spreads — than the man who can measure it and count every branch, but does not own it, and neither knows nor loves its maker. Just so, a believer has the whole world of riches for his own, and having nothing, possesses everything, by holding fast to you, whom all things serve; and even if he cannot trace the wheel of the Great Bear, it is folly to doubt that he is better off than the man who can measure the sky, count the stars, and weigh the elements — and neglects you, who arranged all things by number, weight, and measure.

But who asked this Mani to write about these subjects in the first place? Skill in them is no part of holiness. For you have said to man: Behold, reverence — that is wisdom. He could have been ignorant of all the science and still have had the wisdom; but since he knew none of it and still had the gall to teach it, he plainly could not have had the wisdom either. Parading a knowledge of worldly things is vanity even when the knowledge is real; confession to you is holiness. So this wanderer talked at length about the heavens to his own undoing: refuted by those who had truly studied them, he showed exactly what his judgment was worth on the harder, deeper matters. He would not settle for being thought a small man. He went about persuading people that the Holy Spirit — the Comforter, the Enricher of your faithful — dwelt in him personally, with full authority. So when he was caught teaching falsehoods about the sky and the stars and the motions of the sun and moon — matters that are no part of religious doctrine — the sacrilege of his presumption stood exposed clearly enough: he was delivering things he not only did not know but had gotten wrong, with a vanity so deranged by pride that he tried to have them credited to himself as to a divine person.

When I hear some Christian brother who is ignorant of these matters, or mistaken about them, I can watch him hold his opinion patiently. So long as he believes nothing unworthy of you, Lord, Creator of all things, I do not see that it hurts him to be ignorant of the layout and workings of the physical creation. It does hurt him if he decides this belongs to the very form of holy teaching, and insists too stubbornly on what he does not understand. And even that weakness, in the infancy of faith, is carried by charity, our mother, until the new man grows up to maturity and is no longer blown about by every wind of doctrine. But a man who presumed to set himself up as teacher, source, guide, and chief of everyone he could persuade — so that his followers believed they were following not a man but your Holy Spirit — who would not judge that such madness, once it was caught teaching anything false, deserved detestation and total rejection? But I had not yet settled for certain whether the lengthening and shortening of days and nights, the alternation of day and night itself, the eclipses, and everything else of the kind I had read in other books could somehow be squared with what Mani said. If they could, whether he was right would still remain an open question for me; but I could rest my belief on his authority, because of his reputation for holiness.

For almost all of those nine years in which I drifted as their hearer, I had waited for this Faustus with an eagerness that was too much. The other Manichees I happened to meet, whenever they failed against my objections, kept promising me this Faustus: once he came, and I could talk with him, these difficulties — and greater ones, if I had them — would be untangled with the greatest of ease. Then he came. I found a charming man, pleasant with words, chattering the very things the rest of them always say — only much more sweetly. But what good is the most elegant of cup-bearers to a man thirsty for a costlier drink? My ears were already stuffed with that fare. It did not seem better to me for being better said, or true for being eloquent; the soul did not seem wise because the face was attractive and the language graceful. The people who kept promising him to me were not good judges of things: to them he seemed prudent and wise because his talk delighted them. I have noticed another breed, meanwhile, that suspects even the truth, and refuses to accept it if it arrives in fluent, ample speech. But you had already taught me, my God, by wonderful and hidden ways — and I believe it was you who taught me, because it is true, and there is no other teacher of truth besides you, wherever and from whatever quarter it dawns. From you I had already learned this: a thing is not true because it is said eloquently, or false because the sounds from the lips come out awkward; and not true because it is roughly delivered, or false because the language is splendid. Wisdom and folly are like foods, one nourishing, one worthless; and ornate words or plain are like dishes, fine or homely — and either kind of food can be served on either kind of plate.

The hunger with which I had waited so long for that man was indeed fed by the energy and feeling of his performance in debate, and by the apt words that came so readily to clothe his thoughts. I was delighted; along with many others — more than most of them — I praised him and sang his praises. But it galled me that in the crowd of his listeners I was not allowed to put forward the questions that troubled me and trade them back and forth with him in real conversation. When at last I could — when my friends and I began to claim his ears at hours when a mutual discussion was not out of place, and I laid out the things that were pressing on me — I found, first of all, a man ignorant of the liberal arts except literature, and of that he knew only the ordinary amount. He had read some of Cicero's speeches, a very few books of Seneca, some scraps of the poets, and whatever volumes of his own sect happened to be written in decent Latin; add daily practice at speaking, and out of that came his eloquence — the more pleasing, and the more seductive, because a good native wit steered it and a kind of natural grace attended it. Is it as I remember it, Lord my God, judge of my conscience? My heart and my memory lie open before you. You were working on me even then, in the hidden secret of your providence, turning my shameful errors around to face me, so that I would see them and hate them.

Once it was plain enough that he was untrained in the very arts I had believed him to excel in, I began to despair of his ever opening and untying the knots that bound me. A man could be ignorant of all that, it is true, and still hold the truths of reverence — if he were not a Manichee. Their books are stuffed with interminable fables about the sky and the stars, the sun and the moon; and I no longer believed he could do what I urgently wanted — weigh the calculations I had read elsewhere against what stood in Mani's books, and show me with any precision that Mani's account was the better one, or even that it held up equally well. When I put this on the table for discussion, he — modestly enough — did not even dare to take up the load. He knew that he did not know these things, and he was not ashamed to admit it. He was not one of those talkers, of whom I had endured so many, who undertook to teach me and said nothing. This man had a heart — not right toward you, but not altogether false to himself either. He was not entirely ignorant of his own ignorance, and he would not rashly let himself be boxed into an argument with no exit and no honorable retreat. I liked him better for it. The modesty of a mind that admits its limits is a fairer thing than the knowledge I was after; and that is how I found him in every harder and subtler question.

So the enthusiasm I had flung into Mani's writings was broken. And despairing all the more of their other teachers — since the famous one had turned out this way on the many points that perplexed me — I began instead to spend time with Faustus on the studies he himself was burning for: the literature I was then teaching, as professor of rhetoric, to the young men of Carthage. We read together whatever he wanted to hear, or whatever I judged suited to a mind like his. But every plan I had made for rising in that sect collapsed completely once I knew that man. Not that I cut myself off from them altogether; it was rather that, having found nothing better than the thing I had somehow stumbled into, I decided to stay put for the time being, unless something preferable showed itself. And so this Faustus, who had been a trap of death for so many, had begun — neither willing it nor knowing it — to loosen the trap that held me. For your hands, my God, in the secrecy of your providence, did not desert my soul; and out of my mother's heart's blood, through the tears she poured out day and night, a sacrifice was being offered to you for me; and you dealt with me in astonishing ways. You did it, my God — for by the Lord a man's steps are directed, and it is he who makes the way sure. And what other securing of salvation is there than your hand, remaking what it made?

You dealt with me so that I would be persuaded to go to Rome, and to teach there instead what I was teaching at Carthage. And how I came to be persuaded I will not fail to confess to you, because in this too the deepest workings of your wisdom, and your mercy always so near to us, deserve to be pondered and declared. It was not because my friends promised higher earnings and higher standing — though those things did tug at my mind at the time. The chief reason, almost the only one, was this: I kept hearing that students in Rome were quieter, held in check by a stricter and more orderly discipline; that they did not barge insolently, whenever they pleased, into the classroom of a teacher who was not their own, and were not admitted at all without his leave. At Carthage, by contrast, the license of the students is disgraceful and past all restraint. They burst in shamelessly and, looking half-crazed, wreck whatever order a teacher has established for his pupils' good. They commit outrage after outrage, with an astonishing obtuseness — conduct the law would punish, if custom did not stand as its patron. And the custom only proves them the more pitiable: they now do, as if it were lawful, what by your eternal law will never be lawful, and they think they do it with impunity — when the very blindness with which they do it is the punishment, and they suffer something incomparably worse than what they inflict. So the habits I had refused to make my own as a student, I was forced as a teacher to endure in others; and that is why it suited me to go to a place where, everyone who knew it assured me, such things were not done. But it was you — my hope, my portion in the land of the living — who, so that I would change countries for my soul's health, set the goad at Carthage that pried me loose, and hung the lure at Rome that drew me on; and you did it through men in love with a dying life — the ones doing insane things, the others promising empty ones — and, to straighten my steps, you secretly used their crookedness and mine. Because the men wrecking my peace were blind with a squalid frenzy, and the men beckoning me elsewhere had the taste of earth in their mouths. And I, who detested a real misery here, chased a false happiness there.

But why I left the one place and went to the other — you knew, God, and you showed it neither to me nor to my mother, who grieved terribly over my going and followed me all the way to the sea. She held onto me by force, to pull me back or to sail with me; and I deceived her. I pretended I had a friend I could not leave until the wind rose and he could sail. I lied to my mother — to that mother — and slipped away. Even this you have mercifully forgiven me: you kept me, brimming as I was with unspeakable filth, safe from the waters of the sea, and brought me through to the water of your grace — the washing that would dry the rivers running from my mother's eyes, with which, day after day, she watered the ground beneath her face for my sake. She refused to go home without me, and it was all I could do to persuade her to spend that night in a place close by our ship, a shrine to the memory of blessed Cyprian. That night I stole away; she stayed behind, praying and weeping. And what was she begging of you, my God, with all those tears, but that you would not let me sail? But you, in the depth of your counsel, heard the hinge of her desire: you ignored what she was asking then, in order to make of me what she was asking always. The wind blew and filled our sails and pulled the shore out of our sight — the shore where, come morning, she stood out of her mind with grief, filling your ears with complaints and groans. And you disregarded them, because you were using my own desires to haul me toward the end of all desiring, and chastening the earthly part of her love for me with the just whip of sorrows. She loved having me with her, as mothers do — only far more than most mothers; and she did not know what joy you were going to make for her out of my absence. She did not know. So she wept and wailed, and in that agony the inheritance of Eve showed itself in her: seeking in sorrow what in sorrow she had brought into the world. And yet, when she had finished accusing my deceit and my cruelty, she turned back to pleading with you for me. She went home to her accustomed life; and I went to Rome.

And look what received me at Rome: the whip of bodily sickness. I was on my way down to hell, carrying all the evils I had committed against you and against myself and against others — many, and grave, over and above the bond of that original sin by which we all die in Adam. For you had forgiven me none of them in Christ, nor had he dissolved on his cross the enmity my sins had built up between me and you. How could he dissolve it by the crucifixion of a phantom — which is what I believed him to be? The death of my soul was exactly as real as the death of his flesh seemed to me unreal; and the life of my soul was exactly as false as his death in the flesh was true — because my soul did not believe it. The fevers were climbing; I was on my way out, and on my way to ruin. If I had gone then, where would I have gone but into fire and torments that fit my deeds, in the truth of your order? My mother knew nothing of this — and, far away, she went on praying for me. And you, present everywhere, heard her where she was, and pitied me where I was, so that I got back the health of my body — while my sacrilegious heart stayed frantic and sick. Even in all that danger, I did not want your baptism. I was better as a boy, when I begged for it from my mother's devotion, as I have already recalled and confessed. But I had grown up into my own disgrace, and in my madness I laughed at the prescriptions of your medicine — you, who would not let me, in that condition, die twice over. Had that wound gone through my mother's heart, she would never have healed. For I cannot say enough what she carried in her heart for me — with how much harder an anguish she was now in labor with me in the spirit than she had ever been in the flesh.

I do not see how she could have been healed if a death like that — my death, in that state — had run through the very core of her love. And where would they have been then, all those prayers — so many of them, so constant, offered to no one but you? Would you, God of mercies, have despised the contrite and humbled heart of a chaste and sober widow, generous with alms, dutiful in the service of your saints, letting no day pass without an offering at your altar; coming to your church twice a day, morning and evening, without fail — and not for idle gossip and old wives' tales, but to hear you in your words, and to be heard by you in her prayers? Could you have despised the tears of such a woman, and turned her away from your help — when what she begged of you was not gold or silver, not any shifting, perishable good, but the salvation of her son's soul — she, whom your own gift had made what she was? Never, Lord. No: you were there, hearing and acting, in the order you had determined long before that it should be done. Far be it from you to have deceived her in those visions and those answers of yours — the ones I have mentioned and the ones I have not — which she kept in her faithful heart, and in her constant prayer pressed upon you like bonds signed in your own hand. Because your mercy endures forever, you condescend, with those whose debts you have wholly forgiven, to make yourself a debtor in turn — by your promises.

So you brought me back from that sickness. You healed the son of your handmaid — in body, for the time being — so that he would be alive for you to give him a better and surer health. And even then, in Rome, I attached myself to those "saints" — deceived men, and deceivers; and not only to their hearers, one of whom was the man in whose house I had fallen sick and recovered, but even to the ones they call the Elect. For I still believed that it is not we who sin, but some other nature — who knows what — sinning inside us. It flattered my pride to stand outside the fault: when I had done something evil, not to confess that I had done it, so that you could heal my soul for sinning against you, but to excuse the soul and accuse — I never knew what — some other thing that was with me and was not me. But of course it was all me. My own impiety had split me against myself. And my sin was the more incurable precisely because I did not count myself a sinner; and it was a damnable perversity to prefer that you, you, God almighty, be defeated inside me to my ruin, rather than that I be defeated by you and saved. You had not yet set a guard on my mouth and a door of restraint around my lips, to keep my heart from sliding into wicked words and making excuses for sins in the company of men who deal in evil — and so I still kept company with their Elect.

But I had given up hope of getting anywhere in that false doctrine, and even the positions I had resolved to settle for — failing anything better — I now held loosely and carelessly. For a thought was half-forming in me that the shrewdest of the philosophers had been the ones they call Academics, because they taught that everything must be doubted, and ruled that no truth can be grasped by man. That, at least, is what they plainly seemed to me to hold — the common opinion of them — though I did not yet understand what they were really after. And I did not hesitate to talk my host out of the overconfidence I could see he had in the fabulous stuff that fills Mani's books. Still, I stayed on closer terms of friendship with the Manichees than with people outside that heresy. I no longer defended it with my old fire; but intimacy with the sect — Rome quietly shelters a good many of them — made me slower to look for anything else. Above all, I had no hope that the truth could be found in your Church, Lord of heaven and earth, maker of all things visible and invisible, because they had turned me away from it: it seemed to me outright shameful to believe that you had the shape of human flesh and were bounded by the bodily outline of our limbs. And since, whenever I wanted to think about my God, I could think of nothing but masses of body — nothing else seemed to me to exist at all — this was the greatest, and almost the only, cause of my error; and the error could not be escaped.

From the same root, I believed that evil too was a substance of that kind, with its own foul, misshapen bulk — either dense, the kind they call earth, or thin and fine like the body of the air, which they picture as a malignant mind crawling through that earth. And because such piety as I had forced me to believe that a good God never created any evil nature, I set up two masses facing each other, both infinite — the evil one narrower, the good one vaster — and from that diseased starting-point the rest of my sacrileges followed. When my mind tried to run back to the Catholic faith, it was thrown back, because what I took for the Catholic faith was not the Catholic faith. And I felt more devout believing you infinite on every side but one, my God — to whom your own mercies confess out of my mouth — even though on the side where the mass of evil stood against you I was forced to call you finite, than if I had imagined you bounded on all sides by the shape of a human body. And it seemed better to believe that you had created no evil — which in my ignorance I took to be not just a substance but a bodily one, since I could not think of mind except as a fine sort of body, spread out through space — better that, than to believe that the nature of evil, as I conceived it, could come from you. Even our Savior himself, your Only-begotten, I imagined as something stretched out for our rescue from the mass of your most luminous bulk — so that I believed nothing about him except what my vanity could picture. A nature like that, I reasoned, could not be born of the Virgin Mary without being mixed into flesh; and how what I had imagined could be mixed in and not be defiled, I could not see. So I was afraid to believe him born in the flesh, or I would have had to believe him dirtied by the flesh. Your spiritual ones will smile at me now, gently and with love, if they read these confessions of mine. But that is what I was.

Besides, I did not think the things the Manichees attacked in your Scriptures could be defended. Yet now and then I genuinely wanted to sit down over each disputed point with someone deeply versed in those books, and find out what he thought. Already at Carthage the words of a man named Elpidius, who spoke and argued face to face against the Manichees, had begun to work on me: he brought material out of the Scriptures that was not easy to resist, and their answer to it seemed feeble to me. They did not like giving that answer in public, but only to us in private. It was that the Scriptures of the New Testament had been tampered with by persons unknown, who wanted to graft the law of the Jews onto the Christian faith — and yet the Manichees themselves could produce no uncorrupted copies. But it was those "masses" that chiefly held me down. Thinking of nothing but bodies, I lay pinned under them, half-suffocated — and panting for the air of your truth, I could not breathe it pure and clean.

So I set to work at the thing I had come to Rome to do: teach rhetoric. First I gathered a few students at my lodgings, and with them, and through them, my name began to be known. And behold — I discovered that Rome had abuses of its own, which I had not had to swallow in Africa. The wreckings by degenerate young men did not happen there, it was true, just as I had been told. But instead, they warned me, a pack of students will suddenly conspire to dodge paying their teacher's fee, and decamp together to another master — breakers of faith, who hold justice cheap and money dear. My heart hated them too, though not with a perfect hatred: I probably hated them more for what I stood to lose by them than for the wrong they did to anyone at all. Still, such people are foul. They go whoring away from you; they love the passing mockeries of time, and dirty money, which fouls the hand that closes on it; they hug a world that is running through their fingers, and despise you — you, who remain, who call back, who pardon the prostituted human soul when it returns to you. And I hate such warped and crooked men now, too; but I love them as men who can be corrected — men who might come to prefer the learning to the money, and to the learning, you, God: the truth, the fullness of certain good, the purest peace. Back then, though, I simply did not want them to be bad, for my own sake; I had not yet learned to want them good for yours.

Then word came from Milan to Rome, to the prefect of the city, asking that a professor of rhetoric be furnished for Milan, with travel at public expense. I applied for the post — through those same men, drunk on Manichaean vanities; I was going, though neither they nor I knew it, in order to be rid of them — and Symmachus, then prefect, tested me with a set speech and sent me. And I came to Milan, to Ambrose the bishop, known across the whole world as one of the best of men, your devout servant, whose eloquence in those days was strenuously serving your people the finest of your wheat, the gladness of your oil, and the sober drunkenness of your wine. I was led to him, unknowing, by you, so that by him I might be led, knowing, to you. That man of God took me in like a father; he welcomed this stranger's coming the way a bishop should. And I began to love him — not, at first, as a teacher of the truth, which I had utterly despaired of finding in your Church, but simply as a man who was kind to me. I listened closely whenever he preached to the people — not with the intent I should have had, but sampling his eloquence, so to speak: testing whether it lived up to its fame, whether it ran fuller or thinner than report made it. I hung on his words, attentive; toward the substance I stood aloof, a scornful bystander. And I enjoyed the sweetness of his speech — more learned than Faustus's, though in delivery less sparkling, less caressing. In substance, there was no comparison: the one was straggling through Manichaean tricks; the other was teaching salvation, and teaching it soundly. But salvation is far from sinners, and that is what I was, standing there. And yet I was drawing closer, little by little, and did not know it.

For although I took no trouble to learn what he was saying, only to hear how he said it — that hollow interest was all I had left, since I despaired of any road lying open to you for man — still, along with the words I loved, the matter I meant to ignore kept arriving in my mind. I could not pry them apart. And while I was opening my heart to take in how eloquently he spoke, there entered, in the same motion, how truly he spoke — though only by degrees. First it began to dawn on me that these positions could actually be defended: the Catholic faith, for which I had thought nothing could be said against the Manichees' attacks, I now judged could be held without embarrassment — especially once I had heard one hard place in the Old Testament after another untangled, and so often "in a figure." Taken literally, those passages had been killing me. So when many texts of those books had been opened up in their spiritual sense, I began to fault that old despair of mine — the belief that the Law and the Prophets simply could not stand against those who hated and mocked them. Yet I did not therefore feel that the Catholic way was the one to take, just because it too could field learned defenders, who could answer objections at length and without absurdity; nor that what I held stood condemned, just because the defenses had come out even. The Catholic side seemed to me not beaten — but not yet the winner, either.

Then I bent my whole mind to the question of whether I could, by any decisive proof, convict the Manichees of falsehood. If I had been able to conceive of a spiritual substance, all their contraptions would have come apart at once and been thrown out of my mind. But I could not. Even so, where the physical world was concerned — the whole of nature that the senses of the flesh can reach — considering and comparing more and more, I judged that most of the philosophers had held views far more probable. So, doubting everything in the manner attributed to the Academics, wavering among all the options, I did settle this much: the Manichees had to go. Even in a season of doubt, I decided, I could not stay in a sect to which I now preferred certain of the philosophers — though to those philosophers, because they were without the saving name of Christ, I flatly refused to hand over the cure of my sick soul. So I determined to remain a catechumen in the Catholic Church — the church my parents had set before me — until some certainty should light the way I ought to steer.
