# Confessions, Book VI

More Wretched, and You Nearer

My hope from my youth — where were you all that time, and where had you gone? Wasn't it you who made me, you who set me apart from the four-footed beasts and made me wiser than the birds of the air? Yet I was walking through darkness, on slick ground, and looking for you outside myself; and I did not find the God of my own heart. I had gone down into the deep of the sea, and I had lost confidence, and despaired of ever finding truth. By now my mother had come to me — strong in her devotion, following me over land and sea, and in every danger of the road certain of you. In the storms at sea it was she who steadied the sailors, the very men who usually steady frightened passengers new to the deep: she promised them they would come through safe, because you had promised her so in a vision. She found me in a graver danger of my own — despair of ever tracking truth down. But when I told her that I was no longer a Manichee, though not yet a Catholic Christian either, she did not jump for joy as if the news were unhoped-for. About that half of my misery she was already calm. She had wept over me as over a dead man — but a dead man you would raise; she had been carrying me out to you on the bier of her thought, so that you might say to the widow's son, Young man, I say to you, get up — and he would revive and begin to speak, and you would give him back to his mother. So her heart did not shake with any wild exultation when she heard that what she begged of you daily, with tears, was already in such large part done: I had not yet reached truth, but I had been pulled clear of falsehood. Certain that you, who had promised the whole, would give what remained, she answered me very calmly, out of a heart brimming with confidence: she believed in Christ that before she left this life she would see me a faithful Catholic. That much she said to me. To you, fountain of mercies, she poured out prayers and tears redoubled, that you would hurry your help and light my darkness; and she ran the more eagerly to church and hung on Ambrose's lips, on the spring of water leaping up into everlasting life. She loved that man as an angel of God, because she knew it was he who had brought me, for the moment, to this wavering middle state — and she was fully confident that through it I would pass from sickness to health, once the fever had spiked the way it does just before recovery, at what physicians call the crisis.

In Africa my mother had been used to bringing food — a dish and bread and unmixed wine — to the memorial shrines of the saints. When she tried it here, the doorkeeper stopped her; and the moment she learned it was the bishop who had forbidden it, she took the ruling so devoutly and so obediently that I marveled at her — how readily she turned accuser of her own custom rather than critic of his ban. No thirst for wine had her spirit under siege; no love of drink goaded her into hating truth, the way it does so many, men and women both, who gag at a hymn to sobriety like drunkards at a watered cup. When she brought her basket of festival food, to taste and then give away, she never set out for herself more than one small cup, watered to the strength of her own very sober palate, and sipped from it only for courtesy. And if there were many shrines of the dead to be honored that way, it was the same single cup she carried round to all of them — not only watered thin by now but grown lukewarm from the carrying — and she shared it out in little sips to those around her. She was there for devotion, not pleasure. So as soon as she found that this famous preacher, this most devout bishop, had banned the custom even for those who kept it soberly — so that drunkards would get no chance to gorge themselves, and because these rites, like a yearly feast for the dead, looked all too close to pagan superstition — she gave it up with all her heart; and in place of a basket full of the fruits of the earth she learned to bring the shrines of the martyrs a breast full of purer offerings. She gave what she could to the poor; and so the communion of the Lord's body was celebrated rightly there — there, where the martyrs, after the pattern of his passion, had been sacrificed and crowned. And yet it seems to me, Lord my God — and this is how my heart weighs it in your sight — that she might not have bent so easily to the cutting off of that custom if it had been forbidden by some other man, one she did not love as she loved Ambrose. Him she loved above all men for the sake of my salvation; and he loved her for her deeply devout way of living — for the good works and the fervor of spirit that kept her always at church — so that when he saw me he would often break out in her praises and congratulate me on having such a mother. He did not know what a son she had: a man who doubted all of it, and did not believe the way to life could be found.

And I was not yet groaning in prayer for your help. My mind was bent on inquiry and restless for debate. Ambrose himself I counted a fortunate man as the world reckons fortune — so much honor from such important people; only his celibacy looked like a hardship to me. What hope he carried inside him, what battles he fought against the temptations that came with his very eminence, what comfort he had in adversity, what sweet joys your bread held for the hidden mouth of his heart when he chewed it over — that I could not guess, and had never tasted. Nor did he know my seethings, or the pit gaping under me. I could not ask him what I wanted the way I wanted, because crowds of people with business — men whose weaknesses he served — walled me off from his ear and his mouth. And the little time he was not with them, he was restoring either his body with necessary food or his mind with reading. When he read, his eyes traveled the pages and his heart hunted out the sense, but his voice and tongue were still. Often when we came in — no one was ever turned away, and it was not his custom to have visitors announced — we found him reading like that, silently; never any other way. We would sit a long time in silence (who would dare intrude on a man so absorbed?) and then slip out, guessing that in the small interval he was granted for restoring his mind, free for a while from the din of other men's business, he did not want to be called away — and perhaps he was wary that if his author put something obscurely, some rapt or puzzled listener would make him stop and explain it, or argue out the knottier questions, and with his time spent that way he would get through fewer books than he wished. Though a truer reason for reading silently may have been to spare his voice, which broke down easily. But whatever his reason was, in such a man it was a good one.

So there was no chance to ask what I longed to ask of that holy oracle of yours, his heart — nothing, unless it could be answered briefly. My surging troubles needed him fully at leisure before I could pour them out, and they never found him so. But every Sunday I heard him expounding the word of truth rightly among the people, and I grew surer and surer that every knot of cunning slander our deceivers had tied around the divine books could be untied. And I discovered that your spiritual children — the ones you have brought to second birth by grace, out of our Catholic mother — did not take "man made by you in your own image" to mean that you are penned inside the shape of a human body. What a spiritual substance might be I still could not conceive, even faintly, even in shadow; but I blushed, and gladly: all those years I had been barking not at the Catholic faith but at the figments of a mind that could think only in flesh. I had been reckless and impious: things I ought to have asked about and learned, I had pronounced on and condemned. For you — highest and nearest, most hidden and most present — have no limbs, some larger and some smaller; you are whole everywhere, and nowhere confined in space; you are certainly not this bodily shape of ours. Yet you made man in your own image — and look at him: head to foot, he fits inside space.

Not knowing how this image of yours could be, I should have knocked and put the question — how is this to be believed? — instead of jeering at it and fighting it, as if it were believed the crude way I imagined. So the anxiety over what to hold on to gnawed my heart all the harder for the shame: that I had been strung along so many years by the promise of certainties, and had prattled out so many uncertainties as if I knew them, with a child's error and a child's heat. That they were flat falsehoods only became clear to me later. What was already clear was that they were uncertain — and that I had once held them for certain, in the days when I attacked your Catholic Church with a blind pugnacity. I had not yet discovered that she taught the truth; but I had discovered that she did not teach the things I had so bitterly charged her with. So I was confounded, and I was turning; and I rejoiced, my God, that your one and only Church, the body of your only Son — the Church in which the name of Christ was laid on me as an infant — had no taste for infantile nonsense, and nowhere in her sound teaching would cram you, the creator of all, into any space, however vast, however wide, that was still bounded on every side by the outline of a human body.

I rejoiced too that the old scriptures, the law and the prophets, were now set before me to be read with a different eye than before, when they had looked absurd to me and I would attack your saints for holding opinions they never held. And it gladdened me to hear Ambrose, in his sermons to the people, commend this text again and again, most diligently, as a rule: The letter kills, but the spirit gives life. And when he drew back the veil of mystery and laid open the spiritual sense of passages that, taken by the letter, seemed to teach something warped, nothing he said offended me — though whether what he said was true, I did not yet know. I was holding my heart back from assenting to anything, afraid of a headlong fall — and the hanging was killing me instead. I wanted to be as certain about things I could not see as I was certain that seven and three are ten. I was not so far gone as to think even that was beyond knowing; but I wanted everything else exactly that clear — bodily things not present to my senses, and spiritual things, which I had no way of conceiving except as bodies. Believing could have healed me: the eyesight of my mind, cleared, might then have been turned toward your truth, which abides forever and fails in nothing. But it was with me as with a man who has known a bad doctor and is afraid afterward to trust himself to a good one. The health of my soul could not be healed except by believing — and it refused to be healed, for fear of believing falsehoods; and it fought your hands, though you are the one who mixed the medicines of faith, and spread them over the diseases of the whole world, and vested them with such authority.

From this point I began to prefer the Catholic teaching. Here, I felt, there was more modesty and no fraud in her asking belief for things not demonstrated — whether they were demonstrable in themselves though not to everyone, or not demonstrable at all — while over there a rash promise of knowledge first mocked our credulity, and then a mountain of the most fabulous and absurd things was ordered to be believed, precisely because they could not be demonstrated. Then you, Lord, little by little, with your most tender and most merciful hand touching my heart and settling it, persuaded me. I considered how innumerable were the things I believed without ever seeing them or being there when they happened — so much in the history of nations, so much about places and cities I had never laid eyes on, so much taken on trust from friends, from doctors, from this man and that — things we could do nothing at all in this life without believing; and last, how unshakably fixed I held it which two parents I came from, which I could not possibly have known without believing what I was told. Considering all this, you persuaded me that the blame lay not with the people who believed your books — books you have established with such authority through nearly all the nations — but with the people who refused to believe them; and that I need not listen to anyone who said, "How do you know those books were given to the human race by the spirit of the one true and truthful God?" That was the very thing that deserved belief most of all. For no siege of quibbling questions, out of the whole crowd of philosophers I had read contradicting one another, had ever been able to wring from me the belief that you are — whatever you are, and I did not know what — and that the government of human affairs is yours.

This I believed sometimes more strongly, sometimes more faintly; but I always believed both that you are and that you care for us, even though I did not know what to think about your substance, or which road leads to you and back to you. And so, since we were too weak to find truth by pure reasoning, and for that very reason needed the authority of the holy writings, I now began to believe that you would never have conferred such towering authority on that scripture through every land unless you had willed to be believed through it, and sought through it. The strange-sounding passages that used to trip me in those pages — now that I had heard so many of them explained, and explained credibly — I referred to the depth of the mysteries. And its authority seemed to me the more venerable, the more worthy of sacred trust, for this: that it lay open for everyone to read, yet kept the dignity of its secret for a deeper understanding — offering itself to all in the plainest words and the humblest style, while stretching to the utmost the attention of those who are not light of heart — so that it might gather everyone into its wide-open lap, and through narrow passages bring a few through to you: a few, but far more than if it did not stand on such a peak of authority, or draw the crowds into the lap of its holy lowliness. These were my thoughts, and you were beside me; I sighed, and you heard me; I wavered, and you steered me; I drifted down the broad way of the world, and you did not abandon me.

I was gaping after honors, after money, after marriage — and you were laughing. In those cravings I suffered the bitterest reversals, and in this you were the kinder to me, the less you allowed anything to grow sweet that was not you. Look at my heart, Lord — you willed that I remember this and confess it to you. Let my soul cleave to you now, now that you have pulled it free of that clinging birdlime of death. How wretched it was! And you kept needling the raw place in its wound, so that it would leave everything else and turn to you — you who are above all things, and without whom all things would be nothing — turn, and be healed. How wretched I was, then, and how you worked on me, that one day, to make me feel my wretchedness. I was preparing to deliver a speech in praise of the emperor — a speech in which I would tell plenty of lies, and be applauded for the lying by men who knew they were lies — and my heart was panting under those anxieties, boiling with the fever of thoughts that were eating me alive, when, crossing one of the streets of Milan, I noticed a beggar — his belly full by then, I imagine — joking and glad. And I groaned, and said to the friends who were with me: look at the sorrows our madness buys us. All this straining of ours — like the load I was dragging that day, hauling my own unhappiness along under the whip of desire, and making it heavier by the hauling — and the whole of what we wanted was only to arrive at some carefree gladness. That beggar had beaten us to it, and we might never arrive at all. What he had gotten with a few begged coins, I was scheming toward through wretched twists and turnings: the joy of a happiness that passes. He did not have true joy — granted; but what I was hunting with those ambitions of mine was far falser still. And there was no arguing with this much: he was merry, I was anxious; he was carefree, I was afraid. If anyone had asked me whether I would rather be glad or afraid, I would have answered, glad. But if he had asked next whether I would rather be that beggar or myself, I would have chosen myself, worn out with cares and fears as I was — and chosen out of sheer perversity. For where was the truth in it? I had no right to set myself above him because I was more educated, since the education gave me no joy: I used it to court men's approval — not even to teach them, only to please. Which is why you kept breaking my bones with the rod of your discipline.

So away from my soul with those who tell it, "It matters where a man's joy comes from. That beggar rejoiced in drunkenness; you wanted to rejoice in glory." What glory, Lord? A glory not in you. For just as his was no true joy, mine was no true glory — and it did the deeper damage to my mind. He would sleep off his wine that very night. I had gone to bed with mine and gotten up with it, and I would go to bed with it and get up with it again — how many days more, you, God, knew. Yes: it does matter where a man's joy comes from. I know it; and the joy of a faithful hope stands incomparably far from all that emptiness. But even then the beggar stood ahead of me. He was plainly the happier man — not only because he was drenched in cheerfulness while I was being gutted by worry, but because he had earned his wine by wishing people well, and I was chasing a swollen name by lying. I said much along these lines to my dear friends at the time, and through them I often took my own measure; and I found that it went badly with me, and I grieved, and doubled the badness by grieving; and if some piece of fortune smiled on me, I could hardly rouse myself to catch it, because it flew off almost before it could be held.

All this we mourned over together, those of us who lived as friends, but I talked it through most, and most intimately, with Alypius and Nebridius. Alypius was born in the same town I was, to parents of the first rank there, and was younger than I. He had studied under me, both when I first taught in our town and later at Carthage, and he was very attached to me, because I seemed to him good and learned; and I to him, because of the great bent toward virtue that showed in him so young. And yet the whirlpool of Carthaginian morals — where the frivolous shows run at full boil — had sucked him into the madness of the circus races. While he was being tumbled about in that misery, I had a public school there and was teaching rhetoric; but he was not yet coming to me, because of a quarrel that had sprung up between his father and me. I had found out how ruinously he loved the circus, and it grieved me deeply that so much promise seemed about to be thrown away — if it had not been thrown away already. But I had no means of warning him or hauling him back, no claim on him through a friend's goodwill or a teacher's authority; I assumed he felt about me the way his father did. He was not like that. So, setting his father's feelings aside in the matter, he began to greet me, and to drop into my lecture room — listen a little, and leave.

Still, it had slipped my mind to deal with him at all — to keep a mind that good from being wrecked by a blind, headlong passion for empty games. But you, Lord, whose hand is on the tiller of everything you have created, had not forgotten him — a man who would one day stand among your sons as priest and steward of your sacrament. And so that his correction would be credited plainly to you, you worked it through me, and I knew nothing. One day, as I sat in my usual place with my students before me, he came in, greeted me, sat down, and gave his mind to what I was handling. I happened to have a text in hand, and as I expounded it a comparison from the circus races occurred to me — a way to make the point sweeter and plainer, salted with biting mockery for the men that madness had enslaved. You know, our God, that curing Alypius of that plague was the last thing on my mind. But he took it wholly to himself, and believed I had said it purely for his sake. What another man would have taken as grounds for anger at me, that honest young man took as grounds for anger at himself — and for loving me more warmly. You said it long ago, and wove it into your book: Rebuke a wise man, and he will love you. It was not I who had rebuked him. You use us all, knowing and unknowing, in the order you know — and that order is just — and out of my heart and my tongue you made burning coals, to sear a promising mind that was wasting away, and heal it. Let the man who does not weigh your mercies keep silent about your praise — those mercies confess to you out of my very marrow. For at those words he wrenched himself up out of the pit he had chosen to sink into, where the pleasure kept him in the dark; he shook his mind with a hard grip of self-command, and all the filth of the circus fell away from him, and he never went back. Then he wore down his reluctant father until he was allowed to be my student: the father gave way, and gave in. But once he began to hear me again, he got tangled with me in the same superstition. What he loved in the Manichees was their show of self-denial, which he took to be true and unfeigned. In fact it was a senseless, seducing self-denial — a snare for precious souls not yet able to touch the depth of virtue, and easily taken in by the surface of what was only a shadowed, counterfeit virtue.

He had gone ahead of me to Rome to study law — not dropping the worldly career his parents had sung into his ears — and there, unbelievably, an unbelievable appetite for the gladiator shows carried him away. He had been utterly opposed to such things; he detested them. But one day a band of his friends and fellow students, running into him on their way back from dinner, dragged him — refusing hard, resisting — with that friendly violence of theirs into the amphitheater, on a day of the cruel and lethal games. He kept telling them: "You can drag my body into that place and sit it down, but can you point my mind and my eyes at the show? I will be there and be absent — and beat both you and it." They heard him out and took him along anyway, curious, perhaps, to see whether he could actually do it. They arrived; they found what seats they could; and the whole place was seething with its monstrous pleasures. He shut the doors of his eyes and forbade his mind to walk out into all that evil. If only he had stopped his ears too! For at some turn in the fight a man went down, and a huge roar from the whole crowd struck him so hard that curiosity got the better of him. Ready, he thought, to despise the sight whatever it was — to see it and stay above it — he opened his eyes. And he took a worse wound in the soul than the fighter he had itched to look at took in the body; he fell more pitiably than the man whose fall had raised the roar. The noise came in through his ears and unlocked his eyes, and there was the way in — the opening for striking down and toppling a mind that was still bold rather than strong, and all the weaker because it had counted on itself when it ought to have counted on you. The instant he saw the blood, he drank down savagery with it. He did not turn away; he fixed his stare; he was gulping the madness and did not know it; he relished the crime of the combat and got drunk on its bloody pleasure. And he was no longer the man who had come, but one of the crowd he had come to — a true accomplice of the men who had brought him. What more is there to say? He watched, he shouted, he caught fire; he carried away with him a madness that goaded him to come back — not only with the friends who had first hauled him in, but ahead of them, hauling in others. And yet from all this too you plucked him out, with a hand most strong and most merciful, and taught him to put his confidence not in himself but in you. But that came long afterward.

That day, though, was already being laid up in his memory as medicine for the future. So was another: while he was still my student at Carthage, he was in the forum at midday, turning over in his head a speech he had to give from memory — the usual school drill — when you let him be seized by the officers of the market as a thief. And I think you let it happen, our God, for one reason only: that a man who was to become so great should begin learning early how rashly a man can be condemned by a man on credulous suspicion. He was walking by himself in front of the tribunal with his tablets and pen when a young man — a lawyer, and the real thief — carrying a hidden hatchet, slipped in without Alypius noticing, got as far as the lead gratings that fence off the silversmiths' row, and began chopping out the lead. At the ring of the hatchet the silversmiths below began to stir, and sent men up to seize whomever they found. The thief heard their voices, dropped the hatchet, and ran, terrified of being caught holding it. Alypius, who had not seen him come in, did see him leave, and saw how fast he went; and wanting to know why, he stepped into the place, found the hatchet, and was standing there wondering at it, turning it over, when the men who had been sent found him — alone, iron in hand, the very iron whose ringing had startled them into coming. They seize him; they haul him off; and gathering the market crowd around them, they brag that they have caught a thief in the act. And from there he was led away, to be handed to the judge.

But this far, and no farther, was the lesson to go. For at once, Lord, you came to the rescue of his innocence — you, its only witness. As he was being marched off to prison or punishment, they were met by a certain architect, the man with chief charge of the public buildings. The crowd was especially glad it was he, because he had a way of suspecting them of the goods that kept vanishing from the forum: now at last, they thought, he could see who had really been doing the stealing. But this man had often seen Alypius at the house of a certain senator whose receptions he used to attend. Recognizing him at once, he took him by the hand, drew him out of the mob, asked how so great a calamity had come about, and heard the story; then he ordered the whole rabble — churning around him, shouting threats — to come with him. They came to the house of the young man who had done it. There in front of the door stood a slave boy, young enough to blurt the whole thing out with no thought of what trouble it might bring his master — for he had gone with his master to the forum. Alypius remembered him and told the architect; the architect showed the hatchet to the boy and asked whose it was. "Ours," he said at once — and, questioned further, he gave up everything. So the charge crossed over to that house; the mob that had already begun its victory strut over Alypius was put to shame; and the future steward of your word, the future examiner of so many cases in your church, went away more experienced, and better instructed.

This was the man I had found again at Rome. He fastened himself to me with the strongest of ties and went with me to Milan — partly so as not to leave me, partly to make some use of the law he had studied, more to please his parents than himself. Three times already he had sat as an assessor, with an incorruptibility that amazed everyone else — while he was more amazed at men who could set gold above honesty. And his character was tested with more than the bait of greed; it was tested with the goad of fear. At Rome he was assessor to the count of the Italian treasury. There was at that time a certain senator of enormous power, a man who had half the city bound to him by favors and the other half afraid of him. He wanted, in the usual style of such power, something the law did not allow. Alypius refused. A bribe was offered: he laughed at it from the bottom of his heart. Threats were raised: he trampled them. And everyone marveled at so rare a spirit, which neither wanted so great a man for a friend nor feared him as an enemy — a man renowned for his countless ways of helping or hurting. The judge Alypius counseled did not want to grant the thing either, but he would not refuse it to the man's face: he shifted the whole business onto Alypius, claiming that Alypius would not permit him — and truly, if the judge had done it, Alypius would have walked out. In one thing only, and that through his love of learning, was he nearly seduced: he could have had books copied for himself at the cut rates court officials paid. But he took counsel with justice and turned his thought the better way, judging the fairness that forbade it worth more than the privilege that allowed it. Small things, all this — but he who is faithful in a little is faithful also in much; and what came from the mouth of your Truth can never stand void: If you were not faithful with dishonest wealth, who will trust you with true riches? And if you were not faithful with what belongs to another, who will give you what is your own? Such was the man who held close to me then, and wavered along with me over what course of life to take.

Nebridius too. He had left his birthplace near Carthage, and Carthage itself, where he had mostly lived; left behind a fine family estate, and the house, and a mother who would not follow — and had come to Milan for no other reason on earth than to live with me in a burning search for truth and for wisdom. He sighed as I sighed; he wavered as I wavered — an ardent seeker of the happy life, and the sharpest of examiners of the hardest questions. So there were three hungry mouths, sighing out their want to one another, and waiting on you, that you might give them their food in due season. And in all the bitterness that, by your mercy, trailed our worldly doings, whenever we looked toward the end of it — asked why we should suffer all this — darkness met us; and we turned away groaning, and saying, "How long shall it be like this?" We said it constantly — and saying it, we did not quit those doings, because nothing certain had yet shown itself that we could grasp once we let them go.

And I, turning it all over and over, was amazed above everything at the stretch of time since that nineteenth year of mine, when I first caught fire with a passion for wisdom and resolved that once I found her I would abandon all the empty hopes and lying lunacies of vain desire. And here I was going on thirty, stuck fast in the same mud, greedy for the enjoyment of present things that slipped away and scattered me, all the while saying: "Tomorrow I will find it. It will show itself plainly, and I will hold it fast. Faustus will come and explain everything. — You great men, you Academics! So nothing certain can be grasped for the conduct of life? — No: search harder, and do not despair. Look, the things in the church's books that used to seem absurd are not absurd now; they can be read another way, and honorably. I will plant my feet on the step where my parents set me as a boy, until the clear truth is found. But where is it to be looked for? When is it to be looked for? Ambrose has no spare time; there is no spare time to read. And where do we even hunt for the books? Where do we get them, and when? Who lends them? No — appoint set times; portion out hours for the health of the soul. A great hope has dawned: the Catholic faith does not teach what we thought it taught; our accusations were empty. Her learned men hold it blasphemy to believe God bounded by the shape of a human body. And we hesitate to knock, so that the rest may be opened? Our students take the mornings; what do we do with the hours that are left? Why not this? — But then when do we wait on our great friends, whose backing we need? When do we write what the students will pay for? When do we recover ourselves, and let the mind go slack from its strain of cares?"

"Let it all perish! Dismiss this hollow emptiness, and turn wholly to the one search — for truth. Life is wretched; death may come any hour. If it creeps up suddenly, in what state will we leave this world? And where will we learn what we neglected here — or rather, won't we pay the penalty of the neglect? But what if death itself cuts off every care, and ends it along with the feeling that feels it? Then that must be looked into too. — No; God forbid it should be so. It is not for nothing, it is not empty, that the towering authority of the Christian faith stands spread across the whole world. Such great things would never have been worked for us by God if the soul's life ended with the body's death. Why then do we delay to drop our worldly hope and give ourselves wholly to seeking God and the blessed life? — But wait: these things are pleasant too; they carry a sweetness of their own, and not a small one. We must not cut ourselves off from them lightly, because it would be shameful to come crawling back. And look how little it would take now to land some post of honor — and what more, in this line, could a man wish for? We have a crowd of powerful friends; if nothing else offers and we press hard, at least a governorship might be handed us. And a wife should be taken, with a little money of her own, so she does not swell the expenses; and that will be the boundary of desire. Many great men, well worth our imitation, have given themselves to the study of wisdom with a wife at their side."

While I said all this, and the winds shifted and shoved my heart now this way, now that, time was passing, and I delayed turning to the Lord. From day to day I put off living in you; I did not put off dying, daily, in myself. In love with the happy life, I feared it in the very place where it lives, and hunted for it by running away from it. I thought I would be miserable beyond bearing without a woman's arms around me; and of your mercy's medicine for curing that weakness I never thought at all, because I had never tried it. Continence, I assumed, was a matter of a man's own strength — and I knew no such strength in myself. I was fool enough not to know what is written: that no one can be continent unless you grant it. And grant it you would have, if with inward groaning I had knocked at your ears, and with settled faith had cast my care upon you.

Alypius, it is true, kept me back from taking a wife. His refrain was that if I did, there was no chance of our living together at undistracted leisure in the love of wisdom, the way we had long wanted. In this matter he was even then astonishingly pure. At the very door of his youth he had gone through that experience — and had not stuck in it; he had grieved over it instead, and scorned it, and lived from then on in complete continence. I countered him with the examples of men who had cultivated wisdom inside marriage, and served God well, and kept their friends and loved them faithfully. I was a long way short of their greatness of soul. Bound by the disease of the flesh and its deadly sweetness, I dragged my chain, dreading to have it struck off; and as if he were jarring the wound, I pushed away his good words like the hand of a man come to unchain me. Worse than that: the serpent used me to speak to Alypius himself. My tongue wove sweet snares and strewed them along his path, to tangle those honest, unbound feet of his.

For he could not understand it: I, a man he did not weigh lightly, stuck so fast in the glue of that pleasure that whenever we discussed it I swore I could not possibly live unmarried. And when I saw him wondering, I defended myself: what he had tasted was a snatched and furtive thing, which he could barely remember and so could despise without a struggle — nothing like the settled delight of my own habit; and if the honorable name of marriage were added to that habit, he had no business being surprised that I could not throw such a life away. Hearing all this, he began to want marriage himself — not overcome by any appetite for the pleasure, but out of curiosity. He wanted to know, he said, what this thing could be, without which my life — a life that pleased him so — seemed to me not a life but a punishment. His mind, free of that chain, was astounded at my slavery; and from astonishment he was moving toward a desire to try it, and from there he might have come to the trial itself, and from the trial, perhaps, fallen into the very slavery that astounded him — since he was willing to strike a covenant with death; and the man who loves danger will fall into it. Whatever honor there is in the office of marriage — in the ordering of a household and the raising of children — moved us both only faintly. What tortured me, mostly, and held me captive, was the habit of glutting an appetite that could never be filled; what was leading him toward captivity was wonder. So we stood — until you, Most High, who do not abandon our clay, in pity for the pitiful came to our help, by hidden and wonderful ways.

The push to get me married never slackened. I was already asking; the promise was already made. My mother worked at it hardest, hoping that once I was married, the baptism that saves would wash me clean; she rejoiced that I was daily growing fitter for it, and saw in my faith the answering of her prayers and the keeping of your promises. All that while — at my asking, and out of her own longing — she begged you every day, with a great cry of the heart, to show her in a vision something of my future marriage. You never would. She saw certain vain, fantastic things instead, the kind the human spirit's own straining sweeps together when it is bent hard on a question; and she told me of them, but dismissively, without the confidence she had when it was you who showed her. For she could tell, she said, by a certain savor she could not put into words, the difference between your revealing and the dreaming of her own soul. Still the matter was pressed, and a girl was asked for — about two years short of the age for marrying; and since she pleased, she was waited for.

And a whole company of us friends, talking together and detesting the storm and grind of human life, had considered — had all but firmly resolved — to withdraw from the crowds and live in quiet. The quiet was to be arranged like this: whatever each of us could bring, we would pool into one household fund, so that in the honesty of friendship nothing would be this man's or that man's, but one purse made out of everything — the whole of it belonging to each of us, and all of it to all. It seemed there might be some ten of us in this fellowship, and some very rich men among us — above all Romanianus, from our own town, an intimate friend of mine from childhood, whom the grave tangles of his affairs had brought up to court. He was the hottest of all for the plan, and his word carried the greatest weight, because his ample fortune towered over everyone else's. We had settled, too, that two of us at a time, like annual magistrates, would see to all the practical needs, leaving the rest in peace. But then the question arose whether the wives would put up with it — the wives some of us already had, and the ones we meant to get — and the whole plan, so beautifully shaped, burst apart in our hands, and was broken, and thrown away. Back we went to our sighing and groaning, and our steps to the broad and beaten roads of the world; for many plans were in our heart, but your counsel stands forever. And out of that counsel you laughed at ours, and made ready your own — meaning to give us food in due season, to open your hand, and to fill our souls with blessing.

Meanwhile my sins were multiplying. The woman I had shared my bed with for so long was torn from my side as an obstacle to the marriage; and my heart, which had grown into her, was cut, and wounded, and it trailed blood. She went back to Africa, vowing to you that she would never know another man, and leaving with me the natural son I had by her. But I, in my misery, could not manage even a woman's example. Impatient of the delay — it would be two years before I could have the girl I was promised — and being no lover of marriage but a slave of lust, I procured another woman, no wife, of course: someone to keep the disease of my soul alive, whole or even fattened, and to escort it, under the guard of unbroken habit, into the kingdom of a wife. And the wound where the first was cut away did not heal. After the raging heat of the pain, it began to fester; and the pain went colder — and more desperate.

To you be praise, to you be glory, fountain of mercies! The more wretched I became, the nearer you came. Your right hand was ready — again and again it was ready — to pull me out of the mud and wash me clean, and I knew nothing of it. And nothing called me back from the still deeper pit of the flesh's pleasures except the fear of death and of your judgment to come, which, through every change of my opinions, never once left my breast. I used to argue with my friends Alypius and Nebridius about the ends of good and evil, and in my private judgment Epicurus would have carried the palm, had I not believed what Epicurus refused to believe: that after death a life remains for the soul, and a reckoning by deserts. And I would ask: suppose we were immortal, and lived in unbroken bodily pleasure with no fear of ever losing it — why shouldn't we be happy? What else would there be to want? I did not know that exactly this was part of my great misery: that I was sunk so low, so blind, that I could not even conceive the light of an excellence and a beauty to be embraced for its own sake — a light the eye of the flesh cannot see; it is seen from within. Nor did I stop to ask, wretch that I was, out of what spring it flowed that even these discussions, foul as they were, were sweet to me only in the company of friends; or that not even by my own idea of happiness could I be happy without friends, however deep the flood of the flesh's pleasures ran. For those friends I loved for themselves alone, for nothing — and I felt that they loved me the same way, for nothing back.

Crooked, crooked roads! Woe to the reckless soul that hoped, by pulling away from you, to find something better. It has turned and turned again — onto its back, onto its sides, onto its belly — and every surface is hard. You alone are rest. And look: you are here. You free us from our miserable wanderings, and you set us on your way, and you comfort us, and you say: "Run — I will carry you. I will bring you all the way through; and there, at the end, I will still be carrying you."
