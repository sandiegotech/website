Confessions, Book XII

The Heaven of Heavens

Book XII12 of 13

My heart is busy with many things, Lord — prodded by the words of your holy Scripture in the middle of this poor life of mine. That is why the poverty of human understanding usually runs long in words: searching has more to say than finding, asking takes longer than getting, and the hand that knocks works harder than the hand that receives. But we hold the promise — who can break it? If God is for us, who can be against us? Ask, and you will receive; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you. Everyone who asks receives; whoever seeks finds; to the one who knocks, it will be opened. These are your own promises — and who fears being deceived when Truth is the one who promises?

The lowliness of my tongue confesses to your highness: you made heaven and earth — this heaven I see, this earth I walk on, the earth I carry about in this body. You made them. But where, Lord, is that heaven of heavens we hear of in the words of the Psalm: The heaven of heavens is the Lord's, but the earth he has given to the children of men? Where is the heaven we cannot see, beside which everything we can see is earth? For this bodily universe, which is nowhere whole all at once, has received its portion of beauty in these lower parts, and the lowest of them is our earth; but next to that heaven of heavens, even the heaven over our earth is only earth. Yes, both of these great bodies may fairly be called earth beside that unknown heaven, which is the Lord's and not the sons of men's.

And this earth, surely, was invisible and formless — some unfathomable deep of abyss with no light above it, because it had no shape. So you commanded it to be written that darkness was upon the face of the deep — and what is that but the absence of light? If light had been there, where would it have been but overhead, above everything, filling it all with light? So where light was not yet, what was the presence of darkness but the absence of light? Darkness lay over it because light did not lie over it — just as silence is wherever sound is not. And what is it for silence to be there, except for sound not to be there? Haven't you, Lord, taught this soul what it confesses to you? Haven't you taught me, Lord, that before you formed this formless matter and sorted it into distinct things, there was nothing at all — no color, no shape, no body, no spirit? And yet not absolutely nothing: there was a certain formlessness, with no beauty in it.

Then what should it be called, so that even slower minds could take some hold of it, if not by a familiar word? And what can be found anywhere in the world closer to utter formlessness than earth and the deep? They occupy the lowest rung, and they are less beautiful than the higher regions, which are translucent and shining, all of them. So why shouldn't I take earth invisible and formless as the fitting way to suggest to human beings that formlessness of matter — the matter you had created without beauty, in order to make from it this beautiful world?

So when thought looks for what the senses might find underneath that name, it says to itself: "This is no intelligible form, like life or like justice, because it is the stuff of bodies; and it is nothing the senses can perceive, because in the invisible and the formless there is nothing to see and nothing to sense." And while human thought talks this way to itself, let it attempt one of two things: to know it by not knowing it, or not to know it by knowing it.

But I, Lord — if I am to confess to you, with tongue and pen, everything you have taught me about this matter — the truth is that I heard its very name before I understood it, from people who spoke of it without understanding it themselves; and I pictured it with countless forms of every kind, which is to say I did not picture it at all. My mind churned up foul and hideous forms, everything out of order — but forms still — and I called what I saw "formless" not because it lacked form, but because its form was such that, had it actually appeared, my senses would have recoiled from it as something freakish and wrong, and my human weakness would have been shaken. Even then, what I was imagining was formless not because every form had been stripped from it, but only in comparison with lovelier forms. And true reason kept persuading me that if I meant to conceive the truly formless, I must peel away every last remnant of form whatsoever — and I could not do it. I found it easier to suppose that a thing stripped of all form simply was not, than to conceive of something between form and nothing — neither formed nor nothing: formless, almost nothing. So my mind gave up interrogating my own spirit about it, stuffed as that spirit was with images of formed bodies, which it shuffled and varied at will; and I turned to bodies themselves, and looked more deeply into their changeableness — the way they cease to be what they were and begin to be what they were not. And this crossing from form to form, I began to suspect, passes through something formless, not through sheer nothing. But I wanted to know it, not just suspect it. — Now, if my voice and pen were to confess to you the whole of it, every knot you untied for me in this question, which of my readers would hold out long enough to take it all in? But my heart will not, for that, stop giving you honor, and a song of praise for the things it has no way to say. For the changeableness of changeable things is itself the capacity for all the forms into which changeable things change. And this changeableness — what is it? Soul? Body? Some mode of soul or body? If one could say "a nothing-something," or "an is that isn't," that is what I would call it. And yet even then it in some way was, since it could receive these visible, composite appearances.

And where did it get even that degree of being, if not from you, from whom all things are, insofar as they are? But a thing stands the further from you the less like you it is — and I am not speaking of distance in space. So you, Lord — you who are not one thing in one place and another thing in another, but the Selfsame, and the Selfsame, and the Selfsame: Holy, Holy, Holy, Lord God Almighty — you, in the Beginning, which is from you, in your Wisdom, which was born of your substance, made something, and made it from nothing. For you made heaven and earth, and not out of yourself: had they been out of yourself, they would have been equal to your only-begotten Son, and through him to you; and nothing could rightly be your equal that was not of you. And there was nothing else besides you from which you might make them, God — one Trinity, threefold Unity — and therefore you made heaven and earth out of nothing: a great thing and a small thing, since you are almighty and good, and make all things good — the great heaven and the little earth. You were; nothing else was; and out of nothing you made heaven and earth: two things, one near to you, the other near to nothing; one with only you above it, the other with nothing at all below it.

But that heaven of heavens is yours, Lord; while the earth you gave to the sons of men to see and touch was not yet the earth we now see and touch. It was invisible, formless; there was a deep, and no light over it — or rather, darkness was over the deep, meaning more darkness than there was in the deep. For this deep of waters we see now has, even down in its depths, a light proper to its kind, sensed in some fashion by the fish and the creatures that crawl on the bottom. But that whole deep was almost nothing, because it was still altogether formless — and yet already there was something there that could be formed. For you, Lord, made the world out of formless matter, and that matter you made out of nothing: you made it next to nothing, in order to make from it the great things that we sons of men wonder at. Truly wonderful is this bodily heaven, the firmament between water and water, which you made on the second day, after the creation of light, saying, Let it be made — and it was made. That firmament you called heaven: heaven, that is, relative to this earth and sea, which you made on the third day, when you gave visible shape to the formless matter you had made before there was any day. For you had already made a heaven before any day — but that was the heaven of this heaven, because in the beginning you had made heaven and earth. And the earth you had made then was this formless matter, because it was invisible and formless, and darkness was over the deep. Out of that invisible, formless earth — out of that formlessness, that almost-nothing — you were to make all the things this changeable world consists of, consists and never holds still; the world whose very changeableness shows itself in this, that times can be noticed in it and counted. For times are made by the changing of things, as the shapes whose matter is that invisible earth are varied and turned.

That is why the Spirit, the teacher of your servant, in recounting that in the Beginning you made heaven and earth, says nothing about times and nothing about days. For that heaven of heavens which you made in the Beginning is some intellectual creature which, though in no way coeternal with you, the Trinity, still shares in your eternity: through the sweetness of gazing on you in perfect happiness, it holds its own changeableness firmly in check; and clinging to you without one lapse since the moment it was made, it stands beyond the whole rolling turn of times. Nor is that formlessness, the earth invisible and formless, counted among the days either. Where there is no shape and no order, nothing comes and nothing goes; and where nothing comes or goes, there are plainly no days, and no succession of one stretch of time after another.

O let Truth, the light of my heart, speak to me — not my own darkness! I slid down into that darkness and was darkened; but even from there, even from there, I loved you. I lost my way, and I remembered you. I heard your voice behind me calling me back, and I could barely hear it through the uproar of those who have no peace. And now, look — I come back, burning and gasping, to your fountain. Let no one bar my way: I will drink from this, and I will live from this. Let me not be my own life. Out of myself I lived badly — I was death to myself; in you I come alive again. Speak to me; talk with me. I have believed your books, and their words are deep mystery.

You have already told me, Lord, with a strong voice in my inner ear, that you are eternal — you who alone have immortality — since no shape and no motion changes you, and your will does not vary with the times; for no will that shifts is immortal. In your sight this is clear to me; let it grow clearer and clearer, I beg you, and in that light let me stay sober under your wings. You have told me too, Lord, with a strong voice in my inner ear, that you made all natures and all substances — the things that are not what you are, and yet are. Only what is not comes not from you: that, and the movement of the will away from you, who are, toward what is in a lesser degree — for that movement is transgression and sin. And you have told me that no one's sin harms you, or disturbs the order of your rule, at the highest point or the lowest. In your sight this is clear to me; let it grow clearer and clearer, I beg you, and in that light let me stay sober under your wings.

You have told me too, with a strong voice in my inner ear, that not even that creature is coeternal with you whose only joy is you — the creature that drinks from you with the most persevering purity, and nowhere and never lets go into its own changeableness; with you present to it always, holding itself to you with its whole affection, having no future to wait for and nothing to carry over into a past it remembers, it is neither altered by any change nor stretched out into time. O happy creature, if such a creature exists — happy in clinging to your happiness; happy in you, its everlasting inhabitant, its light! I cannot find a better name for the heaven of heavens that is the Lord's than your house — the house that contemplates your delight without ever tiring of it, without ever leaving it for anything else: one pure mind, made harmoniously one by the settled peace of holy spirits, the citizens of your city in the heavens above these heavens we see.

From this the soul whose pilgrimage has carried her far away may understand — if she now thirsts for you, if her tears have become her bread while day after day they say to her, Where is your God?, if she now asks one thing of you and seeks only that: to dwell in your house all the days of her life (and what is her life, if not you? and what are your days, if not your eternity — like your years, which do not fail, because you are always the same?) — from this, then, let the soul that is able understand how far above all times you are, eternal: since your house, which has never gone abroad to a far country, though it is not coeternal with you, suffers no vicissitude of time, because it clings to you continually and without failing. In your sight this is clear to me; let it grow clearer and clearer, I beg you, and in that light let me stay sober under your wings.

And look — there is some formlessness or other in the changes of these last and lowest things. Who will tell me — unless it is a man wandering and tossing among his own fantasies out of the emptiness of his heart — who but such a man would tell me that if all shape were so worn down and eaten away that only formlessness remained, the formlessness through which a thing was changed and turned from one shape to another, that formlessness could by itself exhibit the changes of time? Plainly it could not. Without a variety of motions there are no times; and there is no variety where there is no shape.

Weighing all this — as much as you grant, my God, as much as you rouse me to knock, and as much as you open to me when I knock — I find two things you have made that stand outside time, though neither is coeternal with you. One is so formed that without any lapse in its contemplation, without any interval of change — changeable, yet never changed — it enjoys your eternity and your unchangeableness to the full. The other was so formless that it had nothing in it that could be changed from one form to another, in motion or at rest, and so nothing by which it could become subject to time. But you did not leave it formless: before there was any day, in the Beginning you created heaven and earth — the two things I have been speaking of. And the earth was invisible and formless, and darkness was over the deep. Those words convey the formlessness to us — so that minds may be drawn along by degrees which cannot yet conceive a total absence of form that still stops short of nothing — the formlessness out of which another heaven was to be created, and an earth visible and well formed, and waters in their beautiful order, and whatever else the making of this world records as created not without days; because such things are of a kind in which the successions of time can happen, through the ordered alterations of their motions and their forms.

This, then, is what I understand, my God, when I hear your Scripture say, In the beginning God made heaven and earth; and the earth was invisible and formless, and darkness was over the deep — and does not say on what day you made them. I understand it this way because of the heaven of heavens — that intellectual heaven, where it belongs to intellect to know all at once: not in part, not in a riddle, not through a mirror, but whole, out in the open, face to face; not this thing now and that thing later, but — as I said — all at once, with no succession of times. And because of the earth invisible and formless, which likewise has no succession of times, no "this now and that later," since where there is no form there is no distinguishing this from that. Because of these two, then — one formed from the very first, the other utterly formless; the one heaven, but the heaven of heavens, the other earth, but the earth invisible and formless — because of these two, as I understand it, your Scripture said, without mention of days, In the beginning God made heaven and earth. For it added at once which earth it meant. And when the firmament is recorded as made on the second day and called heaven, it points us back to which heaven was meant before, when no days were yet named.

The wondrous depth of your words! Look — their surface lies before us, smiling on little ones; but the depth is wondrous, my God, a wondrous depth. To look into it makes one shudder — a shudder of reverence, a trembling of love. Its enemies I hate with a vehement hatred: oh, that you would slay them with your two-edged sword, so that they would be its enemies no longer! For this is how I love them slain: slain to themselves, so that they may live to you. But look — here are others, not fault-finders but praisers of the book of Genesis, who say: "The Spirit of God, who wrote these things through his servant Moses, did not want these words understood the way you read them. He did not mean what you say; he meant what we say." To them, God of us all, with you as judge, I make this answer.

"Will you call it false, what Truth tells me with a strong voice in my inner ear about the real eternity of the Creator — that his substance changes in no way through time, and that his will is nothing apart from his substance? Which is why he does not will one thing now and another thing later: whatever he wills, he wills once, and all at once, and always. Not again and again, not now this and now that; he does not later will what he did not will before, or unwill what he willed — because a will like that is changeable, and nothing changeable is eternal; but our God is eternal. And this too, which he tells me in my inner ear: the expectation of things to come turns into sight when they come, and sight turns into memory when they have passed. All attention that varies like that is changeable — and nothing changeable is eternal; but our God is eternal." These things I gather up and put together, and I find that my God, the eternal God, did not found creation by some new act of will, and that his knowledge admits nothing that merely passes. "What will you say to that, you objectors? Are these things false?" "No," they say. "What then? Is it false that every nature already formed, and every matter capable of form, is only from him who is supremely good, because he supremely is?" "We do not deny that either," they say. "What then — do you deny that there is a certain sublime creature which clings with so chaste a love to the true and truly eternal God that, although it is not coeternal with him, it never detaches itself from him, never spills away into the variety and vicissitude of times, but rests in the utterly true contemplation of him alone?" For you, God, show yourself to whoever loves you as much as you command, and you are enough for him; and this is why he does not swerve from you — not even toward himself. This is the house of God: not earthly, not made of any heavenly mass of body, but spiritual, and a sharer in your eternity, because it is without blemish forever. You have set it fast for ever and ever; you have given it a law, and that law shall not pass away. And yet it is not coeternal with you, God, because it is not without a beginning: it was made.

For though we find no time before it — since wisdom was created before all things — this is certainly not that Wisdom fully coeternal and equal with you, our God, its Father, the Wisdom through whom all things were created, the Beginning in whom you made heaven and earth. It is the wisdom that is created: the intellectual nature, which is light because it contemplates the light. For this too, though created, is called wisdom. But whatever the difference between the light that gives light and the light that receives it, that is the difference between the Wisdom that creates and the wisdom that is created — as between the Righteousness that justifies and the righteousness that justification makes. For we too are called your righteousness; as a certain servant of yours says, that we might become the righteousness of God in him. So a certain created wisdom was created before all things: the rational and intellectual mind of your chaste city, our mother, which is above, and is free, and is eternal in the heavens — and in what heavens, if not the heavens that praise you, the heaven of heavens? for this too is the heaven of heavens for the Lord. And though we find no time before it — because what was created before all things precedes even the creature called time — still, before it stands the eternity of the Creator himself, from whom, when it was made, it took its beginning: not a beginning in time, for time did not yet exist, but the beginning of its own creation.

So it comes from you, our God, but in such a way as to be wholly other than you — not the Selfsame. Though we find time neither before it nor within it — because it is fitted to see your face forever and is never drawn away from it, and therefore is never varied by any change — still the possibility of change is in it; and it would grow dark and cold, if it did not cling to you with a great love and shine and glow from you like perpetual noon. O house full of light and beauty! I have loved your loveliness, and the place where the glory of my Lord dwells — my Lord, your builder and your possessor. Let my wandering sigh for you. I say to him who made you: let him possess me too, in you, since he made me too. I have strayed like a lost sheep; but on the shoulders of my shepherd, your builder, I hope to be carried home to you.

What do you say to me now, you objectors I was addressing — you who still believe that Moses was the devoted servant of God and that his books are oracles of the Holy Spirit? Is this house of God not — though not coeternal with God — in its own measure eternal in the heavens, where you look for changes of time in vain, because you will not find them? For it holds fast to God, as it is always good for it to do, and so it outstrips every stretching-out and every rolling span of time. "It is," they say. "Then which part of what my heart cried out to my God, when inwardly it heard the voice of his praise — which part of that do you call false? That the matter was formless, and that where there is no form there is no order? But where there was no order, there could be no vicissitude of times; and yet this almost-nothing, since it was not absolutely nothing, was certainly from him from whom is whatever is, in whatever degree it is." "That too," they say, "we do not deny."

With these people I want to talk a little now in your presence, my God — with those who grant that everything your Truth whispers within my soul is true. As for those who deny it — let them bark as loud as they like, and deafen themselves. I will keep trying to calm them, to open in them a way for your word. But if they refuse and push me off, I beg you, my God, do not you be silent to me. Speak truly in my heart — only you speak that way — and I will leave them outside, blowing on the dust and kicking the dirt up into their own eyes, and I will go into my inner room and sing you love songs there: groaning, on my pilgrimage, with groans that words cannot carry; remembering Jerusalem, my heart stretched upward toward her — Jerusalem my homeland, Jerusalem my mother — and remembering you who rule over her: her light, her father, her guardian, her husband; her delight, pure and strong; her solid joy and all her good things past telling, all of them at once, because you are the one supreme and true Good. And I will not be turned aside until you gather everything that I am out of this scattered and broken condition into the peace of that dearest mother — where the firstfruits of my spirit already are, and from which all this is certain to me — and shape it, and set it firm forever, my God, my Mercy. But to those who do not call all these truths false — who honor your holy Scripture given through holy Moses, and set it, as we do, at the summit of authority to be followed — and yet contradict me on some point, I answer this: judge for yourself, our God, between my confessions and their contradictions.

For they say: "All this may be true; but Moses did not have those two things in view when, by the Spirit's revelation, he said, In the beginning God created heaven and earth. Under the name of heaven he did not signify that spiritual or intellectual creature that beholds God's face forever; nor, under the name of earth, that formless matter." What then? "That man of God," they say, "meant what we say. That is what he declared by those words." And what is that? "By the words heaven and earth," they say, "he wished first to signify, universally and compactly, this whole visible world — so that afterward, through the numbering of the days, he could lay everything out in detail, piece by piece as it were, everything the Holy Spirit chose to declare in this way. For the people he spoke to were so rough and carnal that he judged only the visible works of God fit to be entrusted to them." They agree, though, that the earth invisible and formless, and that darksome deep — out of which, as the sequel shows, all these visible things known to everyone were made and set in order through those days — may without any strain be understood as this formless first matter.

And now what if another man says that this same formless, jumbled matter was put first under the name of heaven and earth because out of it was created and completed this visible world, with all the natures that appear so plainly in it — this whole which is so often called by the name of heaven and earth? And what if yet another says that invisible and visible nature together are not unfittingly called heaven and earth, so that the whole creation which God made in his Wisdom — that is, in the Beginning — is comprehended under those two words? But since all things were made not from God's substance but out of nothing — because they are not the same as God, and a certain changeableness is in them all, whether they abide, like the eternal house of God, or change, like the soul and body of man — the common matter of all things invisible and visible, still formless but certainly formable, out of which heaven and earth were to be created (that is, the invisible and the visible creature, each now formed) was called by the same names given to the earth invisible and formless and the darkness over the deep; with this distinction, that the earth invisible and formless means corporeal matter before it had any quality of form, and the darkness over the deep means spiritual matter before its unbounded fluidness was reined in, and before Wisdom gave it any light.

There is still room for a man to say, if he likes, that the already finished and formed natures, visible and invisible, are not what the words heaven and earth signify when we read, In the beginning God made heaven and earth; rather, the still unformed beginning of things, the stuff apt for forming and making, was called by those names — because in it, jumbled together, not yet distinguished by their qualities and forms, lay all the things that now, arranged each in its own order, are called heaven and earth: the one the spiritual creation, the other the corporeal.

Having heard and weighed all of this, I will not fight over words: that is good for nothing but the ruin of the listeners. But the law is good for building up, if a man uses it lawfully; for its end is love out of a pure heart and a good conscience and unfeigned faith. And our Master knew well which two commandments he hung all the Law and the Prophets on. And if I confess all this with burning zeal, my God, light of my eyes in secret, what harm does it do me that these words can be understood in several ways — all of them true? What harm does it do me if I take the writer to have thought something different from what someone else takes him to have thought? All of us who read are certainly straining to track down and grasp the meaning of the man we are reading; and since we believe he speaks the truth, we do not dare suppose he said anything we know or think to be false. So while each of us is trying to understand, in the holy Scriptures, the same thing the writer understood, what is the harm if a man understands what you — the light of every truthful mind — show him to be true, even if the author he is reading did not understand this, since the author too understood a truth, though not this one?

For it is true, Lord, that you made heaven and earth. And it is true that the Beginning is your Wisdom, in which you made all things. And it is true, again, that this visible world has as its greatest parts the heaven and the earth — a short way of comprehending the whole of made and established nature. And it is true that whatever changes points us to a certain formlessness in it, by which it receives form, or is changed, or turned. It is true that what clings so fast to the unchangeable Form that, although changeable, it is never changed, undergoes no times. It is true that the formlessness which is almost nothing cannot have alternations of time. It is true that what a thing is made of can, by a certain manner of speech, be called by the name of the thing made from it — so the formlessness out of which heaven and earth were made could itself be called "heaven and earth." It is true that of all things having form, nothing is nearer to the formless than earth and the deep. It is true that not only everything created and formed, but everything capable of being created and formed, was made by you, from whom all things are. It is true that whatever is formed out of the formless was formless before it was formed.

Out of these truths — which none of them doubt whose inner eye you have opened to see such things, and who believe unshakably that your servant Moses spoke in the Spirit of truth — out of all these, one man takes one for himself, and says: In the beginning God made heaven and earth means "in his Word, coeternal with himself, God made the intelligible and the sensible creation — the spiritual and the corporeal." Another takes another: In the beginning God made heaven and earth means "in his Word, coeternal with himself, God made the whole mass of this corporeal world, with all the evident and familiar natures it contains." Another: "in his Word, coeternal with himself, God made the formless matter of creation, spiritual and corporeal alike." Another: "in his Word, coeternal with himself, God created the formless matter of corporeal creation, in which heaven and earth still lay mingled together — the heaven and earth we now see, distinguished and formed, in the mass of this world." And another: "at the very outset of making and working, God made a formless matter that contained heaven and earth confusedly within itself; and out of it, once they were formed, they now stand forth, plain to see, with everything that is in them."

And so too with the understanding of the words that follow. Out of all those truths, one man chooses one for himself, and says: But the earth was invisible and formless, and darkness was over the deep — meaning, "the corporeal thing God made was still only the formless matter of corporeal things, without order, without light." Another says: The earth was invisible and formless, and darkness was over the deep — meaning, "this whole, which is called heaven and earth, was still formless, dark matter, out of which were to be made the corporeal heaven and the corporeal earth, with everything in them that our bodily senses know." Another: "this whole called heaven and earth was still formless, dark matter, out of which were to be made both the intelligible heaven — elsewhere called the heaven of heavens — and the earth, meaning the whole of corporeal nature, a name that takes in this bodily sky as well: in short, the matter out of which every creature, visible and invisible, was to be created." Another: "Scripture did not call that formlessness by the name of heaven and earth. The formlessness already existed, and it is what Scripture called the earth invisible and formless and the darkness over the deep; and Scripture had already said of it that God made heaven and earth — namely, the spiritual and the corporeal creature." And another: "a certain formlessness already existed as matter, and of it Scripture had already said that God made heaven and earth — namely, the whole corporeal bulk of the world, divided into its two greatest parts, upper and lower, with all the common and familiar creatures in them."

Suppose someone tried to argue against these last two opinions like this: "If you will not allow that this formlessness of matter is what the name of heaven and earth signifies, then there was something God had not made, out of which he made heaven and earth. For Scripture nowhere tells us that God made this matter — unless we understand it to be signified by the phrase heaven and earth, or by earth alone, when it is said, In the beginning God made heaven and earth. So in what follows — and the earth was invisible and formless, even though it pleased him to name the formless matter that way — we may understand no other matter than the very matter of which it is written above, God made heaven and earth." The holders of those two last opinions, hearing this, will answer: "We do not at all deny that this formless matter was made by God — the God from whom all things are, and are very good; for as we call what is created and formed a greater good, so we grant that what is made capable of creation and form is a lesser good — but still a good. Scripture simply did not record that God made this formlessness, just as it has not recorded many other things — the Cherubim, and the Seraphim, and those the Apostle names one by one: Thrones, Dominions, Principalities, Powers. That God made all of these is perfectly plain. Or if everything is included in the words He made heaven and earth, what shall we say of the waters, over which the Spirit of God moved? If they are included under the word earth, how can that word mean formless matter, when we see the waters, and they are so beautiful? Or if we do take it so, why is it written that out of this formlessness the firmament was made, and called heaven — and not written that the waters were made? For the waters have not stayed formless and invisible: we watch them flow, and how lovely their flowing is. And if they received that beauty when God said, Let the waters under the firmament be gathered together — the gathering itself being their forming — what will be said of the waters above the firmament? If they were formless, they would hardly have deserved so honorable a seat; and yet no word is written by which they were formed. So if Genesis is silent about God making something which neither sound faith nor sure understanding doubts that God made — and no sober teaching will dare to say that those waters are coeternal with God merely because we hear them mentioned in the book of Genesis and never find when they were created — then why, with truth to teach us, should we not understand that this formless matter too, which this Scripture calls the earth invisible and formless and the darksome deep, was made by God out of nothing, and therefore is not coeternal with him, even though the narrative leaves out when it was made?"

Having heard all this and taken it in, as far as the weakness of my capacity allows — a weakness I confess to you, Lord, though you know it already — I see that two kinds of disagreement can arise when honest reporters tell of something in words: one about the truth of the things themselves, the other about the intention of the one who tells them. It is one question to ask what is true about the making of the creation, and another to ask what Moses, that excellent servant of your faith, wanted his reader and hearer to understand by those words. As for the first kind — away from me, all who imagine they know as truth what is in fact false. As for the second — away from me too, all who imagine Moses wrote what is false. But let me be joined in you, Lord, with the others, and take my delight in you with them — with those who feed on your truth in the wide field of charity. Let us come together to the words of your book, and seek in them your meaning through the meaning of your servant, by whose pen you dispensed them.

But among so many truths that offer themselves to inquirers in those words, understood now one way and now another — which of us can single out the one meaning and say, "This is what Moses thought; this is what he wanted understood in that account," with the same confidence he would say, "This is true" — whether Moses thought it or something else? Look at me, my God — I, your servant, who have vowed in this book a sacrifice of confession to you, and who pray that by your mercy I may pay my vow. Look: can I say, with the same confidence with which I say that in your unchangeable Word you created all things, visible and invisible, that Moses meant nothing else than this when he wrote, In the beginning God made heaven and earth? No. For I do not see into his mind and see that he was thinking this when he wrote it — the way I do see, in your Truth, that the thing itself is certain. He might have had his thought on the mere start of creating, when he said in the beginning; he might have wanted heaven and earth in this place to mean no formed and finished nature, whether spiritual or corporeal, but both of them begun, and still formless. I can see that whichever of these had been said, it could have been said truly; but which of them he had in mind in these words, I do not see in the same way. Yet whether it was one of these, or some other meaning I have not mentioned here, that this great man saw in his mind when he brought out these words, I have no doubt that he saw it truly, and expressed it aptly.

So let no one badger me now by saying, "Moses did not think what you say; he thought what I say." If he asked me, "How do you know Moses thought what you draw out of his words?" — I ought to take that calmly, and I would answer, perhaps, what I have answered above, or somewhat more fully if he were stubborn. But when he says, "Moses did not mean what you say but what I say," while not denying that what each of us says is true — then, O my God, life of the poor, in whose breast there is no contradiction, rain a softness down into my heart, so that I can bear such people patiently. They say this to me not because they are seers who have looked into the heart of your servant and found what they assert; they say it because they are proud. They do not know Moses' opinion; they love their own — not because it is true, but because it is theirs. Otherwise they would love another true opinion just as much, the way I love what they say when they say what is true: not because it is theirs, but because it is true — and precisely because it is true, it is by that fact not theirs. But if they love it because it is true, then it is theirs and mine alike, the common property of all lovers of truth. Their insistence that Moses meant not what I say but what they say — that I refuse; that I do not love. Even if it were so, their boldness belongs not to knowledge but to recklessness: not sight but swollen pride gave it birth. This, Lord, is why your judgments must make us tremble: because your truth is not mine, not his, not anyone's, but belongs to all of us whom you call openly to share in it — warning us, in words of terror, not to hold it as private property, or we will lose it. For whoever claims as his own what you offer to all for their enjoyment, and wants what belongs to everyone to be his alone, is driven from what is common to what is merely his own — that is, from truth to a lie. For he who speaks a lie speaks out of what is his own.

Listen, God, best of judges — Truth itself — listen to what I will say to this objector. Listen: I say it before you, and before my brothers who use the law lawfully, aiming it at the end of charity. Listen and see, if it pleases you, what I say to him. For this is the brotherly, peace-making word I return to him: if we both see that what you say is true, and we both see that what I say is true — where, I ask, do we see it? Not I in you, nor you in me: both of us see it in the unchangeable Truth itself, which stands above our minds. Since we do not quarrel, then, about the very light of the Lord our God, why do we quarrel about the thought of our neighbor, which we cannot see the way unchangeable Truth is seen? If Moses himself appeared to us and said, "This is what I meant," even then we would not see his meaning — we would believe him. So let us not be puffed up, one on behalf of one man against another, beyond what is written. Let us love the Lord our God with all our heart, with all our soul, and with all our mind, and our neighbor as ourselves. Unless we believe that whatever Moses meant in those books, he meant it with a view to those two commandments of charity, we make the Lord a liar — supposing about our fellow servant's mind something other than what he taught. See now how foolish it is, in such an abundance of utterly true meanings that can be drawn from those words, to rush to assert which of them Moses chiefly intended — and, by quarrels that destroy, to offend against charity itself, the very thing for whose sake he said every word we are laboring to expound.

And yet I, my God — you height of my lowliness and rest of my labor, you who hear my confessions and forgive my sins — since you command me to love my neighbor as myself, I cannot believe you gave Moses, your most faithful servant, a lesser gift than I would have wished and wanted from you for myself, had I been born in his time and had you set me in his place: to dispense, by the service of my heart and tongue, those books which were to profit all the nations for so long afterward, and from such a height of authority to overtop, throughout the whole world, the words of every false and arrogant teaching. Had I been Moses then — and we all come from the same lump, and what is man, that you are mindful of him? — had I been then what he was, and had you charged me with writing the book of Genesis, I would have wished to be given such a power of expression, and such a way of weaving speech, that not even those who cannot yet understand how God creates would reject the words as beyond their strength; that those who already can understand would find whatever true opinion their thought had reached not left out of your servant's few words; and that if another man, in the light of truth, had seen some further truth, that too would not fail to be found in those same words.

For as a spring in its narrow basin is more abundant, and feeds more streams across wider country than any one of those streams, however far it runs from that same source — so the account given by your steward, destined to serve so many future expounders, pours out of a small measure of words whole rivers of clear truth; and from those rivers each man draws off for himself what truth he can about these things — one man one truth, another man another — through longer windings of speech. For some people, when they read or hear these words, picture God like a man, or like some mass endowed with enormous power, who by a new and sudden decision made heaven and earth outside himself, at a certain distance as it were — two great bodies, one above and one below, with everything packed inside them. And when they hear, God said, Let it be made, and it was made, they picture words that began and ended, sounding through time and passing away — and the moment they passed, the thing commanded to exist existed; and whatever else of that sort their familiarity with the world of flesh suggests to them. In such people — little ones still, and carnal — while their weakness is carried along by this humblest style of speech as in a mother's arms, faith is being built up soundly: the faith by which they hold it certain that God made all the natures their eyes see around them in astonishing variety. But if any one of them scorns those words as cheap, and in proud weakness stretches himself out beyond the nest that reared him — alas, the wretch will fall. Have pity, Lord God: do not let the passers-by trample the unfledged chick. Send your angel to set it back in the nest, and let it live there until it can fly.

But there are others for whom these words are no longer a nest but a shaded orchard: they see the fruit hidden in the leaves, and they fly about it rejoicing, and search it out, chattering, and pluck it. For when they read or hear these words, they see that all times, past and future, are outstripped by your eternal, stable permanence — and yet that there is no creature in time that you did not make. They see that your will, because it is the very thing you are, made all things without any alteration in itself, without the rising of any will that was not there before; and that you made them not out of yourself, in your own likeness — the likeness that is the form of all things — but out of nothing: a formless unlikeness, to be formed by your likeness and drawn back to you, the One, each thing according to its appointed capacity, so far as is given to each in its kind; and that all things were made very good, whether they stay close around you or, ranged at their distances through times and places, work or undergo the beautiful changes of the universe. These things they see, and they rejoice in the light of your truth, in the small measure that is possible here.

One of them fastens on the words, In the beginning God made heaven and earth, and sees the Beginning as Wisdom — because Wisdom also speaks to us. Another fastens on the same words and takes "beginning" as the start of things created: In the beginning he made, as if to say, at first he made. Among those who take "in the Beginning" to mean "in your Wisdom you made heaven and earth," one believes "heaven and earth" names the matter out of which heaven and earth were to be created; another, natures already formed and distinct; another, one formed nature — the spiritual, under the name of heaven — and one formless nature, corporeal matter, under the name of earth. And those who take the names heaven and earth to mean matter still formless, out of which heaven and earth were to be formed, do not take even that in one way: for one, it is the matter from which both the intelligible and the sensible creation were to be completed; for another, only the matter of this sensible, bodily mass, holding in its vast lap all these visible and ready-to-hand natures. Nor do those who believe heaven and earth here means the creation already ordered and arranged read it alike: one takes it as the invisible and the visible both; another, the visible only, in which we look up at the luminous sky and down at the dark earth, and at all the things they contain.

But whoever takes In the beginning he made simply as at first he made has no true way to understand heaven and earth except as the matter of heaven and earth — that is, of the entire creation, intelligible and corporeal. For if he tries to take it as the universe already formed, he can rightly be asked: "If God made this first, what did he make afterward?" After the universe he will find nothing — and then, like it or not, he must hear: "How is this first, if there is nothing after?" But when he says that God made matter first formless and then formed, he says nothing absurd — provided he is able to tell what comes before what: what by eternity, what by time, what by value, and what by origin. By eternity, as God is before everything; by time, as the flower is before the fruit; by value, as the fruit is before the flower; by origin, as the sound is before the song. Of these four, the first and the last are grasped only with great difficulty; the middle two, very easily. For it is a rare and lofty vision, Lord, to behold your eternity making changeable things without changing, and so standing before them. And who has a mind sharp enough to make out, without hard labor, how the sound is before the song? The song is formed sound; and a thing not yet formed can exist, but what does not exist cannot be formed. In this way the matter is before what is made from it — not before because it makes it, for the matter is what is made into it rather than what makes; and not before by any interval of time. We do not first, at one moment, emit formless noises that are not yet song, and later adapt them and hammer them into the shape of a song, the way wood is worked into a chest or silver into a dish; materials of that kind do precede, in time, the forms of the things made from them. But with a song it is not so. When a song is sung, its sound is heard then: there is not first a formless sounding which is afterward formed into a song. Each sound, the moment it sounds, passes away, and you will find nothing of it to gather back and set in order by art. So the song is carried in its own sound, and its sound is its matter — and that very sound is formed so that it may be a song. This is why, as I was saying, the matter of the sounding is prior to the form of the singing: prior not by any power of making the song, for the sound is no craftsman of the song; it is supplied from the body to the singer's soul, for the soul to make a song of it. Not prior in time, either: it is poured out together with the song. Not prior in value: sound is not better than song, since a song is not merely sound but beautiful sound. But it is prior in origin, because a song is not formed in order to become sound; sound is formed in order to become a song. By this example, let anyone who can, understand how the matter of things was made first, and was called heaven and earth because heaven and earth were made out of it — and yet was not made first in time, since it is the forms of things that give rise to time. That matter was formless; now, in time, it is perceived only along with its form. And still, nothing can be said about that matter except as if it were first in time — though in worth it stands last, since formed things are certainly better than formless ones — and it is preceded by the eternity of the Creator: so that out of nothing there might be that out of which something could be made.

In this diversity of true opinions, let Truth herself bring forth concord; and may our God have mercy on us, so that we may use the law lawfully — for the end of the commandment, which is pure charity. And so, if anyone asks me which of these your servant Moses meant, this book is not my confessions unless I confess to you: I do not know. Yet I do know that those readings are true — apart from the carnal ones, about which I have said what seemed needed. And even the hopeful little ones who read that way are not frightened by the words of your Book, which speak high things humbly and say much in few words. But all of us who, I acknowledge, see and speak truth in those words — let us love one another, and together love you, our God, fountain of truth, if truth is what we are thirsty for and not vanity. And let us so honor this servant of yours, the dispenser of this Scripture, filled with your Spirit, as to believe that when he wrote these things by your revelation, his mind was fixed on whatever in them most excels — in the light of truth, and in the fruit of profit.

So when one man says, "Moses meant what I mean," and another, "No — what I mean," I think it more reverent to say: why not both, if both are true? And if there is a third truth, and a fourth, and if anyone sees any other truth in those words, why not believe that he saw them all — that great man through whom the one God tempered the sacred writings to the minds of many readers, who would see in them things true and diverse? For my part — and I say it fearlessly, from my heart — if I were writing anything that was to carry supreme authority, I would rather write in such a way that my words carried whatever truth anyone could grasp on these matters, than set down one true opinion so plainly that it shut out the rest — readings with no falsehood in them to offend me. I will not, then, my God, be so rash as to believe that you granted less than this to that great man. When he wrote those words, he perceived and had in mind whatever truth we have managed to find in them — and whatever truth we have not managed to find, or not yet, though it lies in them, waiting to be found.

Last of all, Lord — you who are God, and not flesh and blood — even if a man saw less than the whole, could anything be hidden from your good Spirit, who will lead me into the land of uprightness — anything you yourself were going to reveal through those words to readers still to come, even if the man through whom they were spoken had in mind, perhaps, only one of the many true meanings? If that is how it is, then let the meaning he had in mind stand higher than all the rest. But to us, Lord, show either that same meaning or any other true one you please; so that whether you disclose to us what you disclosed to your servant, or some other meaning by the occasion of those same words, still it is you who feed us, and not error that mocks us. See, Lord my God, how much we have written on a few words — how much! At this rate, what strength of ours, what stretch of ages, would be enough for all your books? Let me then confess to you more briefly in them, and choose some one meaning — true, certain, and good — that you inspire, even where many offer themselves and many may; with this as the good faith of my confession: that if I say what your minister meant, that is right and best, and that is what I must strive for; but if I fail of it, let me still say what your Truth willed to tell me through his words — the same Truth that told him, too, whatever it willed.
