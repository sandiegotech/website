Confessions, Book XI

Time and Eternity

Book XI11 of 13

Lord, since eternity is yours, are you ignorant of what I am saying to you? Do you see in time what happens in time? Then why am I laying out for you this long account of things? Not so that you can learn them from me, certainly, but to stir up my own heart toward you, and the hearts of my readers, so that all of us may say: Great is the Lord, and greatly to be praised. I have said it before and I will say it again: I do this out of love for your love. We pray, after all — and yet Truth has said, Your Father knows what you need before you ask him. So what we are opening to you is our affection: confessing to you our own miseries and your mercies upon us, so that you may finish setting us free, since you have begun; so that we may stop being wretched in ourselves and become blessed in you — for you have called us to be poor in spirit, and meek, and mourners, and hungry and thirsty for righteousness, and merciful, and pure in heart, and peacemakers. See, I have told you many things — what I could, what I wanted to — because you willed first that I confess to you, my Lord God: for you are good, and your mercy endures forever.

But when could the tongue of my pen ever tell all your urgings and all your terrors, your comforts and your steerings, by which you brought me to preach your word and to serve your sacrament to your people? Even if I could tell them all in order — the drops of time are precious to me. For a long time now I have burned to meditate on your law and to confess to you, in its light, what I understand and what I do not: the first daybreak of your illumination, and the darkness still left in me, until weakness is swallowed up by strength. And I do not want anything else to siphon off the hours I find free — free from the needs of the body, the refreshment of the mind, and the service we owe to people, or do not owe, and pay anyway.

O Lord my God, listen to my prayer; let your mercy hear my desire — a desire that burns not for myself alone, but wants to be useful to the brothers I love; and you see my heart, that it is so. Let me offer you the service of my thinking and my tongue, and give me something I can offer. For I am poor and needy; you are rich to all who call on you — you who, beyond the reach of care, care for us. Circumcise my lips, the inner and the outer, of all recklessness and all lying. Let your Scriptures be my chaste delight: let me not be deceived in them, and not deceive others out of them. Listen, Lord, and have mercy, O Lord my God, light of the blind and strength of the weak — and light of the seeing, too, and strength of the strong. Listen to my soul; hear it crying from the depths. For if your ears are not with us even in the depths, where can we go? Where can we cry? Yours is the day and yours is the night: at your nod the moments fly past. Grant me a space of them for meditating on the hidden places of your law; do not shut your law against those who knock. You did not will so many pages of dark secrets to be written for nothing; those forests are not without their deer, who take cover in them and gather strength — who walk and graze, lie down and chew it over. O Lord, perfect me, and open those pages to me. Look — your voice is my joy; your voice is better than a flood of pleasures. Give what I love; for I do love, and this love too is your gift. Do not abandon your own gifts; do not despise this green shoot of yours in its thirst. Let me confess to you whatever I find in your books; let me hear the voice of praise and drink you in and contemplate the wonders of your law — from the beginning, in which you made heaven and earth, all the way to the everlasting kingdom of your holy city with you.

Lord, have mercy on me, and hear my desire. For it is no desire for earthly things, I think — not for gold and silver and jewels, not for splendid clothes or honors or power, not for the pleasures of the flesh, not even for what the body needs in this pilgrim life of ours: all of that is added to those who seek your kingdom and your righteousness. See, O Lord my God, where my desire comes from. The wicked have told me tales of their delights — but they are nothing like your law, O Lord. See: there is my desire. Look, Father; look and see, and approve. Let it please your mercy that I find grace before your eyes, so that the inner rooms of your words may open to me when I knock. I beg it through our Lord Jesus Christ your Son, the man of your right hand, the Son of Man, whom you established for yourself as mediator between you and us — through whom you sought us when we were not seeking you, and sought us precisely so that we would seek you — your Word, through whom you made all things, and me among them; your only Son, through whom you called the people of believers into adoption, and me among them. Through him I beg you — through him who sits at your right hand and intercedes with you for us, in whom all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge lie hidden. Those are the treasures I am looking for in your books. Moses wrote of him: he says so himself; the Truth says so.

Let me hear and understand how in the beginning you made heaven and earth. Moses wrote this; he wrote it and passed on — passed from here, from you, to you — and he is not in front of me now. If he were, I would take hold of him and ask him, and beg him through you to unfold these things to me, and I would bend the ears of my body to the sounds breaking from his mouth. If he spoke Hebrew, the sound would knock at my senses for nothing; none of it would touch my mind. If he spoke Latin, I would know what he was saying. But how would I know whether what he said was true? And if I did know even that, would I know it from him? No: inside me, deep inside, in the house where my thinking lives, Truth — not Hebrew, not Greek, not Latin, not any foreign tongue, with no mouth, no tongue, no clatter of syllables — would say, "He is telling the truth," and at once, with confidence, I would say to that man of yours, "You are telling the truth." Since I cannot question him, then, I ask you — you, the Truth that filled him when he said true things — you, my God, I ask: pardon my sins, and as you gave your servant to say these things, give me to understand them.

Look: heaven and earth exist. They cry out that they were made, for they change and they vary — whereas whatever exists without having been made has nothing in it that was not there before, and having something in you that was not there before is what it means to change and vary. They cry out, too, that they did not make themselves: "We exist because we were made. We did not exist before we existed — how could we have made ourselves?" And the voice that says it is the plain evidence of their being there. It was you, then, Lord, who made them: you who are beautiful, for they are beautiful; you who are good, for they are good; you who are, for they are. But they are not beautiful, not good, and do not exist the way you their maker do; set beside you, they are not beautiful, not good — they are not, at all. We know this, and thanks be to you for it; and our knowledge, set beside your knowledge, is ignorance.

But how did you make heaven and earth? What machinery did you use for a work so vast? You did not work like a human craftsman, who forms one body out of another as his mind directs — a mind that can somehow impose on matter the form it sees inside itself with its inner eye. And how could even that mind do it, unless you had made it? It imposes form on something that already exists and already has being — clay, or stone, or wood, or gold, or the like. And where would those come from, had you not appointed them to be? You made the craftsman his body; you made the mind that commands his limbs; you made the stuff he works from; you made the intelligence by which he masters his craft and sees within what he is making without; you made the body's senses, the interpreter through which he carries the work across from mind to material and reports back to the mind what has been done, so that the mind can consult the truth presiding within it and ask whether the work was done well. All these things praise you, the creator of all. But how do you make them? How, O God, did you make heaven and earth? Certainly it was not in heaven or on earth that you made heaven and earth; not in the air, not in the waters, for these belong to heaven and earth. Nor did you make the whole world inside the whole world, because before it was made there was no place for it to be made in. Nor were you holding something in your hand to make heaven and earth from — for where would you get what you had not made, to make anything with? What exists at all, except because you exist? So you spoke, and things were made; in your Word you made them.

But how did you speak? Was it the way the voice came out of the cloud, saying, This is my beloved Son? That voice happened and was over; it began and it ended. The syllables sounded and passed away, the second after the first, the third after the second, and so on in order, until the last came after all the rest — and silence after the last. From this it is plain and obvious that the motion of a creature uttered it: something temporal, serving your eternal will. And those words of yours, made for a moment, the outer ear reported to the understanding mind, whose inner ear lies open to your eternal Word. And the mind compared those words sounding in time with your eternal Word in silence, and said: "It is different — utterly different. Those words are far below me. They do not even exist: they run away and pass. But the Word of my God stays above me forever." Now, if it was in sounding, passing words that you said heaven and earth should be made, and that is how you made heaven and earth, then some bodily creature already existed before heaven and earth, by whose motions in time that voice could run its course in time. But there was no body before heaven and earth — or if there was, then you had certainly made it without any passing voice, so that from it you could make the passing voice that said, Let heaven and earth be made. For whatever such a voice was made from, unless you had made it, it would not exist at all. So: to make the body from which those words could be made — by what word did you speak that?

You are calling us, then, to understand the Word: God, God with you, spoken eternally — the Word in whom all things are spoken eternally. For it is not that one thing was said, and finished, so the next could be said, everything in sequence; no, everything is said at once and forever. Otherwise there is time, and change, and no true eternity, no true immortality. I know this, my God, and I give thanks. I know it, I confess it to you, Lord, and whoever is not ungrateful to certain truth knows it with me and blesses you with me. We know, Lord, we know: insofar as anything no longer is what it was, or now is what it was not, to that degree it dies and is born. So nothing of your Word ever steps aside or steps in to take another's place, because it is truly immortal and eternal. And that is why, in the Word coeternal with you, you say all that you say at once and eternally, and whatever you say shall be made, is made. You make in no other way than by speaking — and yet the things you make by speaking are not made all at once, nor made eternal.

Why is that, I beg you, O Lord my God? I see it, after a fashion; but how to put it into words I do not know. Perhaps it is this: whatever begins to be, and ceases to be, begins at exactly the moment and ceases at exactly the moment when your eternal reason knows it should begin or cease — that reason in which nothing begins and nothing ceases. And that reason is your Word, which is also the Beginning, because it also speaks to us. So in the Gospel it speaks through flesh; it sounded in the outward ears of men so that it might be believed, and sought inwardly, and found in the eternal truth, where the good and only Teacher instructs all his disciples. It is there, Lord, that I hear your voice speaking to me — for whoever teaches us is speaking to us, and whoever does not teach us, whatever he says, is not speaking to us. And who teaches us now, except the unchanging Truth? Even when a changeable creature prompts us, it only leads us to the unchanging Truth, and there we truly learn: we stand and listen to him, and we are glad with a great gladness at the bridegroom's voice, giving ourselves back to the one we came from. He is the Beginning for this reason: if he did not stand fast while we strayed, there would be nowhere for us to come back to. And when we come back from wandering, we come back by knowing; and so that we may know, he teaches us — because he is the Beginning, and he speaks to us.

In this Beginning, O God, you made heaven and earth: in your Word, in your Son, in your power, in your wisdom, in your truth — speaking in a way beyond wonder, making in a way beyond wonder. Who can comprehend it? Who can put it into words? What is this that flashes through me and strikes my heart without wounding it — so that I shudder, and I catch fire? I shudder because I am so unlike it; I catch fire because I am so like it. It is Wisdom — Wisdom itself — flashing through me, cutting open my fog; and the fog closes over me again as I sink back, faint, into the dark my punishment piles upon me. For my strength has drained away into poverty, until I cannot carry even my own good — until you, Lord, who have been gracious to all my iniquities, heal all my diseases too. For you will redeem my life from the pit and crown me with kindness and mercy; you will satisfy my desire with good things, and my youth will be renewed like the eagle's. For in hope we are saved, and in patience we wait for your promises. Whoever can, let him listen to you speaking within. I will cry out boldly in the words you wrote: How wonderful are your works, O Lord; in Wisdom you have made them all. And that Wisdom is the Beginning, and in that Beginning you made heaven and earth.

Look at them — people still full of their old leaven, who say to us: "What was God doing before he made heaven and earth? If he was at leisure," they say, "and doing nothing, why didn't he stay that way forever, going on doing nothing as he had always done nothing before? If some new motion arose in God — a new will, to make a creature he had never made before — how is that true eternity, where a will springs up that was not there before? The will of God is not a creature; it comes before every creature, since nothing would be created unless the creator's will came first. God's will belongs to his very substance. And if something arose in God's substance that was not there before, then that substance cannot honestly be called eternal. But if God's will that creation exist is from eternity, why isn't creation from eternity too?"

People who say this do not yet understand you, O Wisdom of God, light of minds. They do not yet understand how things come to be that come to be through you and in you. They strain to taste eternity while their heart still flutters among the motions of things past and things to come — and it will not hold still. Who will take that heart and hold it, and fix it so that it stands steady for just a moment, and for just a moment catches the splendor of eternity, which stands forever; and sets it beside the times that never stand; and sees that there is no comparison? Then it would see that a long time gets its length only from many motions passing by, which cannot be stretched out side by side; that in the eternal nothing passes, but the whole is present — while no time is ever present whole; that everything past is driven back by what is coming, everything coming follows hard upon the past, and everything past and coming is created by, and flows out of, what is always present. Who will hold the human heart still, so that it can stand and see how eternity, standing still — neither future nor past — dictates times future and times past? Can my hand do that? Can the hand of my mouth, by mere talking, do a thing that size?

Here, then, is my answer to the man who asks, "What was God doing before he made heaven and earth?" I will not answer as one man is said to have done, ducking the force of the question with a joke: "He was preparing hell for people who pry into deep matters." Seeing is one thing; sneering is another. That is not my answer. I would rather answer "I do not know" where I do not know than mock a man for asking a deep question and win applause for a false answer. Instead I say this: you, our God, are the creator of every creature; and if by "heaven and earth" we mean every creature, then I say it boldly: before God made heaven and earth, God was not making anything. If he was making something, what could it have been but a creature? And I wish I knew everything I want to know for my own good as surely as I know this — that no creature was made before any creature was made.

But if some restless mind goes roving back through images of times before creation, and marvels that you — God almighty, all-creating, all-sustaining, architect of heaven and earth — sat out countless ages before undertaking so great a work: let him wake up and pay attention, because his marveling rests on a mistake. How could countless ages pass by that you had not made — you, the author and founder of all the ages? What times could there have been that you did not create? How could they pass, if they never existed? Since you are the maker of all times, then, if there was any time at all before you made heaven and earth, how can anyone say you were resting from work? You made that very time; no times could pass before you made times. And if there was no time before heaven and earth, why ask what you were doing then? Where there was no time, there was no "then."

Nor is it in time that you go before times — or you would not go before them all. You precede everything past from the height of your ever-present eternity, and you outreach everything future, because the future is future, and once it comes it will be past; but you are the same, and your years do not run out. Your years neither come nor go; ours come and go, so that all of them may come. Your years all stand at once, because they stand: they are not shoved out by years arriving, because they never pass. Ours will all be, only when all of them are no more. Your years are one day, and your day is not day after day but today — for your today does not yield to a tomorrow, and it does not follow a yesterday. Your today is eternity: and so you begot the Coeternal, to whom you said, Today I have begotten you. You made all times, and before all times you are; and there was never a time when there was no time.

So there was no time in which you were making nothing, since you made time itself. And no times are coeternal with you, because you stand fast — and if they stood fast, they would not be times. For what is time? Who can explain it briefly and easily? Who can even grasp it in thought clearly enough to say a word about it? Yet what do we mention in conversation more familiarly, more knowingly, than time? And we understand it when we speak of it; we understand it too when we hear someone else speak of it. What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it to someone who asks, I do not know. Still, I can say with confidence that I know this much: if nothing passed away, there would be no past time; if nothing were coming, there would be no future time; if nothing existed, there would be no present time. Those two times, then, past and future — how can they be, when the past is no more and the future is not yet? And the present — if it were always present, and never passed into the past, it would not be time anymore; it would be eternity. So if the present becomes time only by passing into the past, how can we say that even it is — when its whole reason for being is that it will stop being? In truth we cannot say that time is, except because it tends not to be.

And yet we speak of "a long time" and "a short time" — though only of the past or the future. A hundred years back we call a long time past; a hundred years ahead, a long time to come. A short time past is, say, ten days ago; a short time to come, ten days from now. But how can something be long or short that does not exist? The past is no more; the future is not yet. So let us not say of the past, "it is long"; let us say "it was long," and of the future, "it will be long." My Lord, my light — will your truth not laugh at man even here? That long past time: was it long when it was already past, or before that, when it was still present? It could be long only while there was something that could be long. Once past, it no longer existed — and what did not exist at all could not be long. So let us not say "past time was long" either: we will find nothing there that was long, since from the moment it became past, it is not. Let us say instead, "that time was long while it was present," because while it was present, it was long. It had not yet gone off into nonexistence; there was something there that could be long. But the moment it passed, it stopped being long in the very act of stopping being.

Come then, human soul — you are the one given the power to sense and to measure stretches of time — let us see whether present time can be long. What is your answer? Are a hundred present years a long time? First look at whether a hundred years can be present at all. If the first of those years is running, it is present, and the other ninety-nine are future — so they are not yet. If the second year is running, one is already past, one is present, the rest are to come. Pick any year in the middle of the hundred as present: everything before it is past, everything after it future. So a hundred years cannot be present. Now look at whether even the one year that is running is present. If its first month is running, the rest are future; if the second, the first has already gone and the rest are not yet. So even the current year is not present whole — and if it is not present whole, the year is not present. A year is twelve months, and only the current month, whichever it is, is present; the rest are past or future. And even the current month is not present — only one day of it is: if the first day, the rest are future; if the last, the rest are past; if a middle day, it hangs between past days and future days.

Watch the present time — the only time we found we could call long — shrink to the span of scarcely a single day. And let us take even that apart, for not even one day is present whole. It is filled out by twenty-four hours of night and day: for the first hour, the rest are future; for the last, the rest are past; for any hour between, the hours before are past and the hours after are future. And that one hour itself runs off in fugitive particles: whatever has flown from it is past; whatever is left is future. If we can conceive a point of time that cannot be split into even the smallest particles of moments, that alone is what may be called present — and it flies from future to past so fast that it stretches over no interval at all. If it stretched at all, it would divide into past and future. The present has no extent. Where, then, is the time we can call long? The time to come? We do not say of it, "it is long," because there is nothing yet to be long; we say, "it will be long." When will it be long, then? If even then it is still to come, it will not be long, since what could be long does not yet exist. But if it will be long precisely then, when out of the future — which is not yet — it first begins to be, and becomes present, so that there is something that can be long: then the present time cries out, in the words we heard above, that it cannot be long.

And yet, Lord, we do perceive intervals of time; we compare them and call some longer, some shorter. We even measure how much longer or shorter this time is than that: we answer that this one is double or triple, that one single, or that the two are equal. But it is times in passing that we measure, and we measure them by perceiving them. Past times, which no longer are, or future times, which are not yet — who can measure those? Only someone bold enough to claim he can measure what does not exist. So while time is passing, it can be perceived and measured; once it has passed, it cannot, because it is not.

I am asking, Father, not asserting: my God, govern me and guide me. Who will tell me it is not so — that there are not three times, past, present, and future, as we learned as boys and have taught to boys, but only the present, because the other two do not exist? Or do they exist too — and does time come out of some hiding place when it turns from future to present, and go back into some hiding place when it turns from present to past? For where did those who foretold the future see it, if it does not yet exist? What does not exist cannot be seen. And those who recount the past could not recount it truly unless they saw it in their minds — and if past things were nothing at all, they could not be seen at all. Therefore future things and past things do exist.

Allow me, Lord, to push my search further. O my hope, do not let my attention snap. If future things and past things exist, I want to know where they are. If I cannot manage that yet, I still know this much: wherever they are, they are not there as future or as past, but as present. If the future is there as future, it is not there yet; if the past is there as past, it is there no longer. So wherever they are, whatever they are, they are only as present. When we tell the past truly, what comes out of memory is not the events themselves, which have passed, but words conceived from their images — images they planted in the mind like footprints as they passed through the senses. My boyhood, which no longer is, lies in past time, which no longer is; but its image, when I recall it and describe it, I am looking at right now, in the present, because it is still in my memory. Whether foretelling the future works the same way — images of things that do not yet exist, sensed in advance as already existing — I confess, my God, I do not know. But this I do know: we usually think out our future actions in advance, and that advance thinking is present, while the action we are planning does not yet exist, because it is future. Once we have set to it, and begun to do what we were planning, then the action will exist — because then it is not future but present.

However this hidden foresensing of the future works, then, nothing can be seen except what is. And what is now is not future but present. So when people speak of seeing the future, what they see is not the things themselves — which are not yet, being future — but perhaps their causes or their signs, which already exist. To the one seeing them, they are not future but present, and from them the mind conceives the future and foretells it. Those conceptions exist already too, and those who foretell are gazing at something present inside themselves. Let one example out of the whole teeming crowd of them speak for me. I watch the first light of dawn, and I announce in advance that the sun is going to rise. What I watch is present; what I announce is future — not the sun, which already exists, but its rising, which is not yet. And yet unless I pictured that rising in my mind, as I picture it now while speaking of it, I could not predict it. But the dawn glow I see in the sky is not the sunrise, though it runs ahead of it; and neither is the picture in my mind. Both of these are seen as present, so that the rising, which is future, can be told beforehand. Future things, then, are not yet; and if they are not yet, they are not; and if they are not, they cannot possibly be seen. But they can be foretold from present things, which already exist, and are seen.

You, then, ruler of your creation — how is it that you teach souls what is to come? For you taught your prophets. How do you teach the future, you for whom nothing is future? Or do you rather teach present things about the future? For what does not exist certainly cannot be taught. That way of yours is far beyond my sight; it is too strong for me, and on my own I cannot reach it. But I will reach it from you, when you grant it — sweet light of my hidden eyes.

But this much is now clear and evident: neither future nor past things exist. Strictly speaking, we cannot say, "There are three times: past, present, and future." Strictly, perhaps, we should say: there are three times — a present of things past, a present of things present, and a present of things future. These three do exist, somehow, in the soul; anywhere else, I do not see them. The present of things past is memory; the present of things present is sight; the present of things future is expectation. If we may speak this way, then I see three times, and I grant that there are three. Say too, then, "there are three times, past, present, and future," as loose custom puts it — say it. I do not mind; I raise no objection, no protest — provided we understand what is meant, and do not imagine that the future exists now, or the past. There are few things we say strictly, many things we say loosely; but what we mean gets understood.

I said a little while ago that we measure times as they pass — so that we can say this time is double that one, or equal to it, and report whatever else about the parts of time our measuring can report. So, as I said, we measure times as they pass; and if someone asks me, "How do you know?", I answer: I know because we do measure; because we cannot measure what does not exist; and because past and future do not exist. But how do we measure present time, when it has no extent? It is measured while it passes; when it has passed, it is not measured, for then there is nothing to measure. But from where, by what way, and to where does time pass while it is being measured? From where, but the future? By what way, but the present? To where, but the past? Out of what is not yet, then, through what has no extent, into what is no longer. Yet what do we measure, if not time over some extent? When we say single and double and triple and equal and everything else of the kind, we are naming extents of time. In what extent, then, do we measure time as it passes? In the future, which it comes out of? What is not yet, we cannot measure. In the present, which it passes through? What has no extent, we cannot measure. In the past, which it passes into? What is no more, we cannot measure.

My mind is on fire to solve this knotted riddle. Do not lock it away, Lord my God, good Father — through Christ I beg you — do not lock these things away from my desire, these things at once so ordinary and so hidden. Let my desire break through to them; let them shine out under your illuminating mercy, Lord. Whom else shall I ask about them? And to whom can I more usefully confess my ignorance than to you, who are not wearied by this zeal of mine, blazing over your Scriptures? Give what I love — for I do love, and that love is your gift. Give it, Father, you who truly know how to give good gifts to your children. Give it, because I have taken this knowing upon myself, and it is labor and trouble to me until you open the door. Through Christ I beg you, in the name of the Holy of Holies: let no one shout me down. I believed, and therefore I speak. This is my hope; for this I live: to gaze on the delight of the Lord. Look — you have made my days old, and they pass away, and how, I do not know. And we go on talking about time and time, times and times: "How long ago did he say this?" "How long since he did that?" "How long is it since I saw it?" "This syllable takes double the time of that short one." We say these things and hear them; we are understood, and we understand. They are the most obvious, most everyday things there are — and the same things are hidden too deep to reach, and finding them out would be a discovery.

I once heard a learned man say that time simply is the motion of the sun, moon, and stars. I did not agree. Why not the motions of all bodies, then? If the lights of heaven halted and a potter's wheel kept turning, would there be no time by which to measure its turns — no way to say that it was spinning at even intervals, or, if it ran now slower and now faster, that some turns took longer and some shorter? And while we were saying all this, wouldn't we be speaking in time? Wouldn't some syllables in our words be long and some short — for no other reason than that some sounded for a longer time and some for a shorter? God, grant men to see in a small thing the laws common to things small and great. The stars and the lamps of the sky are for signs and for seasons, for years and for days — yes, they are. But I would no more say that one turn of that little wooden wheel makes a day, than that learned man could say it therefore makes no time.

What I long to know is the force and the nature of time, by which we measure the motions of bodies and say, for instance, that this motion lasts twice as long as that one. I ask this: since "day" means not only the sun's hours above the earth — day as opposed to night — but also its whole circuit from the east around to the east again (as when we say "so many days passed," the nights counted in), and since a day is completed by the sun's motion, its circuit from east to east — I ask whether the day is the motion itself, or the interval the motion takes, or both. If the motion were the day, then we would have a day even if the sun completed its whole course in a stretch as short as one hour. If the interval, then there would be no day if from one sunrise to the next were only as long as an hour: the sun would have to circle twenty-four times to complete a single day. If both, then it could not be called a day if the sun ran its whole round in an hour; nor could it, if the sun stopped and as much time passed as the sun usually spends on its whole circuit, morning to morning. So I will not ask now what it is we call a day; I ask what time is — the time by which we would measure the sun's circuit and say it was completed in half its usual interval, if it were completed in a stretch like twelve hours; the time by which we could compare the two intervals and call one single and the other double, even if the sun sometimes ran from east to east in the single and sometimes in the double. So let no one tell me that times are the motions of the heavenly bodies. When at one man's prayer the sun stood still, so that he could finish his battle in victory, the sun stood — but time went on. That battle was fought and ended in its own tract of time, as much as it needed. I see, then, that time is some kind of stretching-out. But do I see it, or do I only seem to see it? You will show me, Light; you will show me, Truth.

Do you command me to agree if someone defines time as the motion of a body? You do not. That no body moves except in time — that I hear; you say it. But that the motion of a body is time — that I do not hear; you do not say it. When a body moves, it is by time that I measure how long it moves, from when it begins until it stops. If I did not see it begin, and it keeps on so that I never see it stop, I cannot measure — except from the moment I began to watch until I stopped watching. If I watch a long while, all I can report is that it was a long time, not how long; because when we say how long, we say it by comparison: "as long as that," "twice as long as that," and so on. But if we can mark the places a moving body goes from and comes to — or the places its parts pass through, if it is turning like a lathe — then we can say exactly how much time the motion of the body, or of its part, took from this place to that. So a body's motion is one thing, and what we measure its duration by is another — and given that, who can fail to see which of the two better deserves the name of time? A body may sometimes move and sometimes stand still, and we measure not only its motion but its rest, and measure both by time: we say "it stood as long as it moved," or "it stood two or three times as long as it moved," or whatever else our measuring has fixed exactly or roughly guessed — more or less, as we say. Time, therefore, is not the motion of a body.

And I confess to you, Lord, that I still do not know what time is. And yet again I confess to you, Lord, that I know I am saying this in time; that I have been talking about time for a long while now; and that this "long while" is only a long while because of some duration of time. How do I know that, when I do not know what time is? Or maybe I do not know how to say what I do know? Woe is me — I do not even know what I do not know! See me, my God: I stand before you, and I am not lying. As my words are, so is my heart. It is you who will light my lamp; you, O Lord my God, will turn my darkness into light.

Does not my soul confess to you, and confess truly, that I do measure times? So it is, my God: I measure, and I do not know what I am measuring. I measure the motion of a body by time. Then am I not measuring time itself? Could I really measure how long a body moves, and how long it takes to get from here to there, without measuring the time it moves in? So how do I measure time itself? Do we measure a longer time by a shorter, the way we measure a beam by the length of a cubit? That does seem to be how we measure the span of a long syllable by the span of a short one and call it double; how we measure the length of a poem by the lengths of its lines, the lengths of the lines by the lengths of the feet, the lengths of the feet by the lengths of the syllables, and the long syllables by the short — not measuring on the page (that way we would be measuring space, not time), but as the words go past in the speaking: "A long poem — it is built of so many lines; long lines — they run so many feet; long feet — they stretch across so many syllables; a long syllable — it is double a short one." But even so we get no fixed measure of time, because a shorter line, spoken with a drawl, can fill more time than a longer one rattled off. So with a whole poem, so with a foot, so with a syllable. From all this it began to seem to me that time is nothing other than a stretching-out — but of what, I do not know. And I would be surprised if it were not of the mind itself. For what, I beg you, my God, am I measuring when I say, loosely, "this time is longer than that," or even, precisely, "this is double that"? That I am measuring time, I know. But I am not measuring the future — it is not yet; nor the present — it stretches over no extent; nor the past — it is no more. What am I measuring, then? Times passing, not past? So I said earlier.

Keep at it, my mind, and press hard. God is our help; he made us, and not we ourselves. Press on toward the place where truth is beginning to dawn. Suppose a physical voice begins to sound — sounds, goes on sounding — and now stops: silence; the voice is past; there is no voice anymore. Before it sounded, it was future and could not be measured, because it did not yet exist; and now it cannot be measured, because it no longer exists. It could be measured, then, while it was sounding, because then there was something there to measure. But even then it was not standing still: it was going, going away. Is that what made it measurable? For in passing it was being stretched out across some interval of time in which it could be measured — since the present has no extent. If it could be measured then, look: suppose another voice has begun to sound and is sounding still, one continuous unwavering tone. Let us measure it while it sounds; when it falls silent, it will be past, with nothing left to measure. Let us measure it exactly, then, and say how long it is. But it is still sounding — and it can only be measured from its beginning, where it started, to its end, where it stops. What we measure is precisely the interval between some beginning and some end. So a voice that has not yet ended cannot be measured; no one can say how long or short it is, or call it equal to another, or single, or double, or anything of the kind. But once it has ended, it will no longer exist. How, then, can it be measured? And yet we do measure times. Not times that are not yet, not times that are no longer, not times stretched over no duration, not times without end points. Not future times, then, nor past, nor present, nor passing — and yet we measure times.

"Deus creator omnium" — "God, creator of all things." The line runs eight syllables, short and long alternating: the four short — first, third, fifth, seventh — are single against the four long — second, fourth, sixth, eighth. Each long takes double the time of each short. I pronounce them and report it, and it is so, as plain hearing tells. As far as plain hearing goes, then, I measure the long syllable by the short, and I feel it holding the short twice over. But since one sounds only after the other — the short first, the long after — how do I keep hold of the short one? How do I lay it against the long as a measure, to find that the long has it twice, when the long does not begin to sound until the short has stopped sounding? And the long itself — am I measuring it as present, when I cannot measure it before it ends? Its ending is its passing away. What is it, then, that I am measuring? Where is the short syllable I measure with? Where is the long syllable I measure? Both have sounded, flown, passed away; they are no more. And still I measure, and I answer with confidence — as far as a practiced ear can be trusted — that in span of time the one is single, the other double. And I could not do it unless they had already passed and ended. So it is not the syllables themselves I measure, for they no longer exist, but something in my memory that stays fixed there.

It is in you, my mind, that I measure times. Do not shout me down — I mean, do not shout yourself down with the mob of your own impressions. In you, I say, I measure times. The impression that passing things make in you stays in you when they have passed; that impression, still present, is what I measure — not the things that passed in making it. That is what I measure when I measure times. So either that is what times are, or I do not measure times at all. What about when we measure silences, and say that this silence lasted as long as that voice did? Do we not stretch our thought out to the measure of the voice, as though it were still sounding, so as to report something about the intervals of silence within a span of time? With voice and mouth entirely still, we run through poems in thought — lines, whole speeches, the dimensions of any motion — and we report on spans of time, how long this took against that, exactly as if we were saying it all aloud. Suppose a man decides to utter a longish sound and fixes in advance, in thought, how long it will last. He has already gone through that interval of time in silence; he commits it to memory, then begins to utter the sound, and it sounds until it reaches the end he set for it. Or rather: it has sounded and it will sound — for the part of it already done has sounded, and the rest will sound. And so the thing is carried through, as the mind's present attention hauls the future over into the past — the past growing as the future shrinks, until the future is used up and everything is past.

But how can the future shrink or be used up, when it does not yet exist? How can the past grow, when it no longer exists? Only because in the mind, which does all this, there are three acts. The mind expects, it attends, and it remembers: what it expects passes, through what it attends to, into what it remembers. Who denies that the future does not yet exist? Yet an expectation of the future already exists in the mind. Who denies that the past no longer exists? Yet a memory of the past still exists in the mind. Who denies that the present has no extent, since it passes in an instant? Yet attention lasts — and through attention what is arriving is carried off into absence. So it is not the future, which does not exist, that is long: a long future is a long expectation of the future. And it is not the past, which does not exist, that is long: a long past is a long memory of the past.

Say I am about to recite a psalm I know. Before I begin, my expectation reaches across the whole of it. Once I have begun, whatever I have plucked off it and dropped into the past is stretched along my memory instead — and the life of this act of mine is stretched two ways: into memory, because of the part I have said, and into expectation, because of the part I am about to say. Yet all the while my attention is present, and through it what was future is carried across into what is past. And the further the act proceeds, the shorter grows expectation and the longer memory, until the whole of expectation is used up, the whole act is finished, and it has passed entire into memory. And what happens in the psalm as a whole happens in each of its parts, and in each of its syllables; it happens in the longer performance of which that psalm may be one small part; it happens in the whole of a human life, whose parts are all of a person's acts; it happens in the whole age of the children of men, whose parts are all human lives.

But because your mercy is better than lives, look at mine: my life is a stretching-out — pulled many ways at once. And your right hand took hold of me in my Lord, the Son of Man, mediator between you, the One, and us, the many — the many, pulled many ways among many things — so that through him I might lay hold of him who has laid hold of me; that I might be gathered in from my old days to follow the One; forgetting what is behind, not stretched out toward the things that will come and pass away, but stretched forward toward what lies ahead — moving not by stretching-out but by stretching-forward — I press on toward the prize of the call from above, where I will hear the voice of your praise and gaze on your delight, which neither comes nor passes. But for now my years go by in groaning, and you are my comfort, Lord — my Father, everlasting — while I have flown apart into times whose order I do not know. My thoughts, the innermost depths of my soul, are torn to shreds by the noisy crowds of change, until the day I flow together into you, refined and melted clean in the fire of your love.

And now I will stand and grow firm in you, in your truth, the mold that shapes me. I will not put up with the questions of men whose sickness is its own punishment — who thirst for more than they can hold and ask, "What was God doing before he made heaven and earth?" or, "How did it ever occur to him to make something, when he had never made anything before?" Grant them, Lord, to think well about what they are saying, and to discover that where there is no time, there is no "never." To say that someone "never made" something is simply to say that he made it "at no time." Let them see, then, that without creation there can be no time — and let them stop talking that emptiness. Let them too be stretched forward to what lies ahead, and understand that you are before all times, the eternal creator of all times; that no times are coeternal with you, and no creature either — even if there is some creature above all times.

O Lord my God, how deep is the fold of your secret, and how far from it the consequences of my sins have flung me! Heal my eyes, and let me rejoice with your light. Certainly, if there were a mind of such vast knowledge and foreknowledge that it knew all things past and all things future the way I know one well-known psalm, that mind would be a wonder past belief — astonishing to the point of dread — since nothing done in all the ages gone by, and nothing in all the ages remaining, would be any more hidden from it than, while I sang that psalm, it was hidden from me what and how much of it had passed since the beginning, and what and how much remained until the end. But far be it from us to think that you, creator of the universe — creator of souls and bodies — know all things future and past merely like that. Far, far more wonderfully do you know them, and far more secretly. When someone sings a well-known song, or listens to one, his feelings are swung and his attention pulled apart between expecting the sounds to come and remembering the sounds gone by. Nothing of the kind happens to you — the unchangeably eternal, that is, the truly eternal creator of minds. So then: as you knew heaven and earth in the beginning without any variation in your knowledge, so you made heaven and earth in the beginning without any stretching-out of your action. Let whoever understands this confess to you, and let whoever does not understand it confess to you. Oh, how high you are — and the humble in heart are your house! For you raise up the downcast, and those whose high place is you do not fall.
