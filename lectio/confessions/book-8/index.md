# Confessions, Book VIII

Take It and Read

My God, let me remember your mercies to me with thanksgiving, and confess them to you. Let my bones be drenched in your love and say: Lord, who is like you? You have torn my chains apart — let me offer you a sacrifice of praise. How you tore them I will tell, and everyone who worships you will hear it and say: Blessed be the Lord in heaven and on earth; great and wonderful is his name. Your words had lodged deep in my heart, and on every side you had me walled in. Of your eternal life I was now certain, though I had seen it only in a riddle, as in a mirror; and every doubt about an incorruptible substance — the substance every other substance comes from — had been taken from me. I no longer wanted to be more certain of you, only more steady in you. But in my temporal life everything was still swaying, and my heart had yet to be cleared of the old yeast. The Way — the Savior himself — pleased me well; but the narrow passage he led through still made me flinch. And you put it into my mind, and it seemed good in my sight, to go to Simplicianus, who looked to me like a good servant of yours: your grace shone in him. I had heard, too, that from his youth up he had lived in complete devotion to you. He was an old man now, and in a long life spent following your way with such earnestness, I guessed he had learned much — and he had. Out of that store I wanted him, once I had laid my agitations in front of him, to draw out and show me the fittest way for a man in my condition to walk in your path.

For I saw the church full, and one man went this way and another that. But my own life in the world had gone sour on me. It was a heavy load now: desire no longer burned me on, as it once did, with hopes of honor and money, making the slavery bearable. Next to your sweetness, and the beauty of your house, which I loved, those things had lost their savor. But I was still bound tight by my need for a woman. The Apostle did not forbid me to marry, though he urged something better and wished all men could live as he lived. I was weak, so I picked the softer place; and because of that one thing everything else in me pitched and rolled — I was going limp, drying out with cares I could not shed — because married life, to which I had bound myself over, would force compliance from me in matters where I had no wish to comply. From the mouth of the Truth himself I had heard that there are eunuchs who have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven — but, he says, let him accept it who can. Surely all men are hollow who lack the knowledge of God — who could not, from the good things in front of their eyes, find the One who is good. But I was out of that hollowness. I had climbed past it, and by the joint testimony of your whole creation I had found you, our Creator, and your Word — God with you, and with you one God — through whom you created all things. There is a second breed of the godless: the ones who know God and do not glorify him as God, or give him thanks. I had fallen into that as well; and your right hand took hold of me, lifted me out, and set me down where I could get well. For you have said to man, The fear of the Lord — that is wisdom, and, Do not be eager to seem wise: the people who declared themselves wise became fools. And I had already found the pearl of great price. I should have sold everything I had and bought it. And I hesitated.

So I went to Simplicianus, who had been a father to Ambrose — a bishop now — in the receiving of your grace, and whom Ambrose loved like a father in truth. I laid out for him the winding maze of my wanderings. But when I mentioned that I had read certain books of the Platonists, translated into Latin by Victorinus — once professor of rhetoric in the city of Rome, who had died a Christian, as I had heard — he congratulated me on not falling in with the writings of other philosophers, crowded with fallacies and frauds according to the elements of this world, while the Platonists' pages point in every way toward God and his Word. Then, to bend me toward the humility of Christ — hidden from the wise, revealed to little children — he began to talk about Victorinus himself, whom he had known intimately at Rome; and what he told me of him I will not pass over in silence, because it holds a great confession of praise to your grace: how that old man, immensely learned, expert in every liberal art, who had read and weighed so many works of the philosophers, the teacher of so many distinguished senators — a man whose brilliance in his office had earned him, and been granted, what the citizens of this world count a summit of honor, a statue in the Roman Forum — that man, a worshiper of idols into his old age, a partner in the sacrilegious rites that almost the whole nobility of Rome then breathed like air, a nobility that had taught the people to sigh after

Anubis the barking dog-god, and monster gods of every breed, weapons leveled against Neptune, against Venus, against Minerva —

gods Rome had once defeated in battle and then bowed down to; gods the old Victorinus had thundered in defense of for so many years with that terrifying eloquence — this same man was not ashamed to become a child of your Christ, an infant at your font: to bend his neck under the yoke of humility and lower his forehead to the disgrace of the cross.

Lord, Lord, you who bowed the heavens and came down, who touched the mountains and they smoked — by what paths did you make your way into that man's chest? He read holy Scripture, Simplicianus told me; he hunted out all the Christian writings and searched them with the greatest care; and he kept saying to Simplicianus — not openly, but privately, friend to friend — "You should know that I am already a Christian." And Simplicianus kept answering, "I will not believe it, and I will not count you among the Christians, until I see you in the church of Christ." And Victorinus would laugh at him: "So it's walls that make Christians?" He said it often, that he was already a Christian; Simplicianus often gave the same answer; and often the joke about the walls came around again. The truth is he was afraid of offending his friends — proud worshipers of demons — and expected their hostility to come crashing down on him from the height of their Babylonian dignity, as from cedars of Lebanon the Lord had not yet shattered. But once steady reading and thirst had drawn firmness into him, a different fear took hold: that Christ might deny him before the holy angels if he was afraid to confess Christ before men. Being ashamed of the sacraments of the humility of your Word, while feeling no shame at the sacrilegious rites of those arrogant demons — rites he had accepted as their proud imitator — came to look to him like an enormous crime. So he turned brazen toward vanity and shy before the truth, and suddenly, without warning, he said to Simplicianus, as Simplicianus told it, "Let's go to the church. I want to be made a Christian." And Simplicianus, hardly able to contain his joy, went with him. Not long after he had been admitted to the first instruction of the faith, he gave in his name to be reborn through baptism — while Rome stared and the Church rejoiced. The proud looked on and seethed; they ground their teeth and wasted away. But the Lord God was your servant's hope, and he had no eyes for vanities and lying madness.

At last the hour came for his profession of faith. In Rome those about to approach your grace deliver the profession in fixed words, learned by heart, from a raised platform, in full sight of the faithful people; and the priests, Simplicianus said, offered Victorinus the chance to recite it more privately — an offer they used to make to anyone who seemed likely to panic from shyness. But he chose to declare his salvation in front of the holy crowd. What he had taught all those years in rhetoric was not salvation — and he had professed that in public. If he had never feared a mob of madmen when performing his own words, why should he fear your gentle flock when pronouncing yours? So when he climbed up to make the profession, everyone who knew him — and who there did not? — passed his name along with a hum of congratulation, and a low murmur ran through every mouth in the rejoicing crowd: Victorinus! Victorinus! A sudden roar at the sight of him; a sudden hush to hear him. He pronounced the true faith with superb confidence, and all of them wanted to pull him bodily into their hearts — and they did pull him in, by loving him and rejoicing over him. Those were the hands they used.

Good God — what is it in a man, that he rejoices more over a soul saved after despair, delivered out of the thick of danger, than over one who never lost hope, never stood in such peril? You too, merciful Father — even you rejoice more over one sinner repenting than over ninety-nine righteous who need no repentance. And what gladness we feel whenever we hear how the straying sheep comes home on the shepherd's rejoicing shoulders, and the small coin goes back into your treasury while the neighbors celebrate with the woman who found it. The joy of the liturgy in your house forces out tears, when the reading in your house tells of your younger son — he was dead, and came to life; he was lost, and is found. You are rejoicing in us then, and in your holy angels, who are holy with holy love. For you are always the same; and all the things that do not stay the same, and do not stay at all, you know forever in the same way.

What is happening in the soul, then, when it takes more delight in finding or getting back what it loves than if it had held it all along? Everything else agrees; the world is packed with witnesses shouting, "That's how it is!" The victorious general has his triumph — and there is no victory without a battle; the greater the danger in the fight, the greater the joy in the parade. A storm batters the sailors and threatens shipwreck; every face goes white at oncoming death; then sky and sea grow calm, and their joy runs as high as their fear did. A friend is sick, and his pulse says danger, and everyone who wants him well is sick at heart with him; he mends — he cannot yet walk with his old vigor — and already there is a joy in the house that was never there when he walked well and strong. Even the ordinary pleasures of human life men purchase with difficulty — and not only the difficulties that fall on us unlooked-for and unwanted, but difficulty chosen and arranged on purpose. Eating and drinking are no pleasure at all unless the pinch of hunger and thirst comes first. Drinkers eat salty things to raise an uncomfortable burn, and the pleasure comes when the drink puts it out. And custom rules that a promised bride is not handed over at once — so that as a husband a man will never hold cheap the woman he did not sigh for, waiting, as her betrothed.

This law holds in joy that is filthy and damnable; it holds in joy that is permitted and lawful; it holds in the purest honesty of friendship; it held for the son who was dead and came to life, was lost and found. Everywhere the greater joy comes in behind the greater pain. What does it mean, Lord my God — you who are eternally joy to your own self, you yourself, with the beings around you rejoicing in you forever — what does it mean that this lower region of things ebbs and flows, alternately offended and reconciled? Is that the mode you allotted them? Is that all you granted them — while from the heights of heaven to the depths of the earth, from the first of the ages to the end of the ages, from the angel down to the worm, from the first stir of motion to the last, you were setting every kind of good thing and every one of your just works each in its proper place, and bringing each to pass at its proper hour? Woe is me! How high you are in the highest, and how deep in the deepest! You are never anywhere absent — and how slowly, how barely, we come back to you.

Move, Lord; act. Wake us and call us back, kindle us and seize us, blaze up, grow sweet to us: let us love, let us run. Don't many come back to you out of a deeper hell of blindness than Victorinus's? They approach, and they are lit — and whoever receives that Light receives from you the power to become your child. But if such people are less known to the crowd, even those who do know them rejoice less over them; for when many rejoice together, the joy in each of them is richer — they heat and kindle one another. And the widely known carry many with them toward salvation; where they lead, the crowd follows; which is why those who got there before them rejoice over them so — because the rejoicing is not over them alone. May it never be that in your tent the rich would be welcomed ahead of the poor, or the highborn ahead of the low — when you chose instead the weak things of the world to shame the strong, chose the base and despised things of this world, the things that are nothing, to unmake the things that are. And yet even the least of your apostles, the man through whose tongue you sounded out those very words — when the proconsul Paulus had his pride beaten down by the apostle's campaign and was sent under the easy yoke of your Christ, a plain subject now of the great King — even he was glad to drop his former name, Saul, and be called Paul, the banner of that great victory. For the enemy is more soundly beaten in a man he gripped more tightly, and gripped many others through; and he grips the proud more tightly by their rank, and by their authority he grips the many. So the more prized the heart of Victorinus was — the devil had held it like an impregnable fortress — and the tongue of Victorinus, the great keen blade he had used to cut down so many, the more abundant the joy your children owed: because our King had bound the strong man, and they were watching his weapons wrenched away, scrubbed clean, and made fit for your honor — serviceable to the Lord, for every good work.

But when your man Simplicianus had told me all this about Victorinus, I was on fire to do the same — which was exactly why he had told it. And when he added that in the days of the emperor Julian, when a law forbade Christians to teach literature and oratory, Victorinus had obeyed the law and chosen to give up his school of words rather than your Word, which makes even the tongues of infants eloquent — he seemed to me not so much brave as fortunate: he had found his opening to be free for you alone. That was the very thing I was sighing for — I, tied down not by anyone else's irons but by my own iron will. The enemy held my power of willing, and out of it he had forged a chain for me, and it bound me. For the will, twisted, becomes craving; a craving served becomes habit; and a habit unresisted becomes necessity. By links like these, hooked one onto the next — that is why I have called it a chain — a hard slavery kept me pinned. And the new will just beginning in me, the will to worship you with no wage in view and to enjoy you yourself, God, the only sure sweetness — it was not yet able to overpower the old will, hardened in me by the years. So two wills of mine, one old, one new; one of the flesh, one of the spirit; fought each other, and their war laid waste my soul.

So I came to understand from my own experience what I had read: the flesh craves against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh. I was on both sides — but more myself on the side I approved of than on the side I condemned. On that second side it was mostly no longer really I: so much of it I suffered against my will rather than did with my will. And yet it was my own doing that habit had grown this warlike against me; I had arrived, willingly, where I now did not will to be. And who has any standing to complain when just punishment follows the sinner? Nor was my old excuse left to me — that the reason I had not yet thrown over the world and served you was that my sight of the truth was uncertain. By now it was certain. But I was still bound fast to the earth, and refused to soldier for you; and I feared being freed of all the baggage as much as a man ought to fear the baggage itself. So the freight of the world weighed me down — sweetly, the way sleep does; and the thoughts I tried to fix on you were like the struggles of men trying to wake, who go under again, drowned in the depth of drowsiness. No one wants to sleep forever; every sober judgment says waking is better; yet a man usually delays shaking sleep off when his limbs are thick and heavy with it — he takes it back gladly even though it has stopped agreeing with him, even when it is time to get up. Just so, I was certain it was better to give myself over to your love than to give in to my own craving; but the first course persuaded me and kept winning the argument, while the second pleased me and kept winning me. I had no answer at all when you said to me, Wake, you who sleep; rise from the dead, and Christ will give you light. On every side you were showing me your words were true, and, convicted by the truth, I could answer nothing back — only those slow, sleepy words: "In a minute." "Look — any minute now." "Just let me be a little longer." But "any minute" never came to a minute, and "a little longer" stretched on and on. It did me no good to delight in your law in my inmost self, while a different law in my body made war against the law of my mind and marched me off, a prisoner of the law of sin in my own members. For the law of sin is the raw violence of habit, dragging the mind along and holding it, even unwilling — and deservedly, since it slid in willing. Wretched man that I was! Who was going to free me from this body of death — who but your grace, through Jesus Christ our Lord?

And how you freed me from the chain of sexual craving that had me lashed down so tight, and from my slavery to worldly business, I will now tell, and confess to your name, Lord, my helper and my redeemer. I was going about my usual work, with the anxiety climbing, sighing to you every day, going to your church as often as I could get free of the business under whose weight I groaned. Alypius was with me — done, after his third term, with his legal post as assessor, and waiting for clients to buy his counsel, the way I sold skill in speaking, so far as teaching can supply such a thing. Nebridius, as a kindness to our friendship, had agreed to serve as assistant teacher under Verecundus, a citizen of Milan, a teacher of grammar, and a very close friend of us all, who had badly wanted — and by the rights of friendship had demanded from our circle — the dependable help he needed. Nebridius was not drawn to it by profit; he could have made much more from his learning if he had wished. He was simply too kind and gentle a friend to refuse a good turn or wave away our request. And he handled it with great discretion, keeping clear of the notice of the people this world calls important, and so keeping clear of the disquiet of mind that comes with them: he wanted his mind free, and at leisure for as many hours as could be, to search out something of wisdom, to read of it, to hear of it.

One day, then, when Nebridius was away — I forget why — a man named Ponticianus came to the house to see me and Alypius: a countryman of ours in the loose sense, being African, and holding high office at the emperor's court. He wanted something or other from us, and we sat down to talk; and it happened that on the game table in front of us he noticed a book. He picked it up, opened it — and found, to his complete surprise, the apostle Paul. He had assumed it was one of the books I was grinding myself down teaching. He smiled, looked up at me, and told me how glad he was, and how amazed, to have stumbled on this book, and only this book, there at my elbow. For he was a Christian, and baptized; he bowed often before you, our God, in church, in long and frequent prayers. When I told him that I spent the most serious effort on those Scriptures, a conversation began — he telling it — about Antony, the monk of Egypt, whose name stood high in honor among your servants, though up to that hour we had never heard it. When he realized that, he stayed on the subject, introducing the great man to two ignorant listeners, and marveling at the ignorance. We were stunned to hear of your wonders, so fully attested, so recent — practically our own times — done in the true faith and the catholic Church. All of us marveled: we, that these things were so great; he, that we had never heard of them.

From there his talk turned to the flocks of the monasteries, and their way of life that is a sweet fragrance to you, and the fruitful deserts of the wilderness — and we knew nothing of any of it. There was even a monastery at Milan, outside the city walls, full of good brothers under Ambrose's care — and we had not known it. He kept on, talking the subject out, and we listened in intent silence. And so he came to tell how once at Trier, one afternoon while the emperor was held by the races in the circus, he and three companions went out to walk in the gardens along the city walls. As they happened to stroll in pairs — one man going off with him, the other two wandering away by themselves — those two, drifting, came on a hut where certain servants of yours lived, poor in spirit, the kind the kingdom of heaven belongs to; and there they found a little book with the life of Antony written in it. One of the two began to read it — and to marvel, and to catch fire; and while he was still reading, to think of laying hold of such a life himself and leaving his worldly service to serve you. The two of them belonged to the corps they call agents of the imperial administration. Then, filled all at once with holy love and a sober shame, furious at himself, he threw his eyes on his friend and said to him: "Tell me something. All this striving of ours — what are we actually trying to reach? What are we after? Whose interest are we serving? Could we hope for anything higher at court than to be friends of the emperor? And at that height, what is not brittle? What is not crawling with danger? How many dangers do we climb, just to reach a bigger one? And how long will the climb take? But a friend of God — if I want it: look. I become one here and now." He said it, and — in labor with the new life coming to birth in him — he turned his eyes back to the page and went on reading; and he was changed inwardly, where you alone could see, and his mind was shedding the world, as soon appeared. For while he read, and the waves rolled in his heart, he raged at himself a while; then he saw the better course, and decided for it; and, already yours, he said to his friend: "I have just now torn free of those hopes of ours. I have decided to serve God, and I begin this hour, in this place. If you can't bring yourself to do the same, don't stand against me." The other answered that he would stay at his side, for such pay, in such a service. And both of them, yours now, were building the tower at the only cost that counts: leaving everything they had, and following you. Then Ponticianus and the man walking with him, who had wandered through other parts of the gardens, came looking for them in that same place, and, finding them, suggested they head back — the day was going. But they told what they had resolved and undertaken, how such a will had risen in them and set firm, and asked the others, if they would not join them, not to stand in their way. Ponticianus and his friend, in no way changed from what they had been, still wept over themselves — so he told us — and devoutly congratulated the two, and asked to be remembered in their prayers; then, dragging their hearts along the ground, they went off to the palace. The other two, hearts fixed on heaven, stayed in the hut. And each of them was engaged to be married; and when their brides-to-be heard what had happened, they too dedicated their virginity to you.

That was Ponticianus's story. But while he spoke, Lord, you were wrenching me around to face myself. You pulled me out from behind my own back, where I had stationed myself all the years I refused to look at me, and you planted me in front of my own face, so I would see how foul I was — how twisted and grimy, how stained, how covered in sores. I looked, and horror took me — and there was nowhere to run from myself. If I tried to turn my gaze away, there was Ponticianus, going on with his story, and there you were again, holding me up against myself, shoving me into my own line of sight, so that I would find my sin at last and hate it. I had known it all along — and looked away, and winked at it, and let it slip.

But now, the more ardently I loved those two men, hearing of the health in their passion — how they had handed themselves whole to you for healing — the more bitterly I loathed myself beside them. Many years of my life, perhaps twelve, had run out since my nineteenth year, when Cicero's Hortensius stirred me to the love of wisdom — and I was still postponing the day I would despise this world's happiness and clear myself for the search: the search whose mere seeking, let alone finding, should have outweighed every treasure and kingdom of the world already in hand, outweighed all the pleasures of the body laid out around me at a nod. But I — a wretched young man, wretched beyond wretched at the very start of my youth — I had even asked you for chastity. I had said: "Give me chastity and continence — but not yet." I was afraid you might hear me too quickly — heal me too quickly — of the disease of desire, which I preferred to have satisfied rather than put out. And I had gone down crooked roads, in a sacrilegious superstition — not because I was sure of it, but because I preferred it to the teachings I never examined in good faith, only attacked out of spite.

And I had told myself that the reason I kept deferring, day after day, the moment of despising this world's hopes and following you alone, was that nothing certain had shown itself for me to steer toward. But now the day had come when I stood stripped bare in front of myself, and my conscience took up the prosecution: "Where is that tongue of yours? You're the one who kept saying you wouldn't drop your pack of vanities for an uncertain truth. Well: now it is certain — and the pack is still crushing you, while other men, who never wore themselves down searching, who never spent ten years and more brooding on all this, have shrugged the weight off their shoulders and been given wings." So I was gnawed inside, and drowned in a horrible shame, while Ponticianus talked on. And when the talk was done, and the business he had come about, he went his way — and I went into myself. What did I not say against myself? With what whips of judgment did I not flog my soul, to make it follow me now that I was straining to go after you! And it dug in its heels. It refused, and it offered no excuse. Every argument had been spent and answered. What remained was speechless dread: my soul feared, as it would fear death itself, being pulled out of the current of habit — the current in which it was wasting away toward death.

Then, in the middle of that great brawl of my inner house — the fight I had forced on my own soul in the room where we lived, my heart — with my mind as ravaged as my face, I rounded on Alypius and cried out: "What is wrong with us? What is this? What did you just hear? The unschooled are rising up and taking heaven by force, and here we are with our learning and no heart — look at us, wallowing in flesh and blood! Are we ashamed to follow because they went first — and not ashamed that we can't even follow?" I said something like that, and then my agitation ripped me away from him while he stared at me, stunned, and said nothing. I did not sound like myself; and my forehead, my cheeks, my eyes, my color, the pitch of my voice, said more than the words did. There was a little garden attached to our lodging, which we had the use of, as we had the whole house — our host, the owner, was not living there. The storm in my chest had swept me out to it, where no one could interfere with the burning case I had brought against myself — until it should end however you knew it would end; I knew nothing. I only knew that my madness was working toward sanity, my dying toward life: I knew the evil thing I was, and did not know the good thing I was about to become. So I withdrew into the garden, and Alypius followed on my heels; his being there took none of my solitude from me — and how could he abandon me in that state? We sat down as far from the house as we could get. I was groaning in spirit, shaking with fury at myself for not going — not entering into your will and your covenant, my God, when every bone in me was shouting to enter it, praising it to the sky. And the way there is not by ship or carriage or on foot; it is not even as far as I had walked from the house to where we were sitting. To go there — to arrive fully — required nothing at all except the will to go: but to will it strongly and completely, not to heave and pitch, this way and that, a will half-wounded, one part rising while the other part fell.

At last, in the fever of my paralysis, I was making all sorts of movements with my body — the kind men sometimes want to make and cannot, if they are missing the limbs, or their limbs are roped down, or slack with sickness, or somehow blocked. If I tore my hair, if I struck my forehead, if I laced my fingers and gripped my knee — I willed it, and it happened. But I could have willed it and had nothing happen, if the power of movement in my limbs had refused to obey. So I was doing all these many things, where willing was not the same as being able — and I was not doing the one thing my longing set beyond all comparison above the rest: the thing I could do the moment I willed it, because the moment I truly willed it, I would will it indeed. In that act the power is one with the will; the willing itself is the doing. And still it was not done. And my body obeyed the thinnest willing of my soul, moving its limbs at a nod, more readily than my soul obeyed itself, to carry out — in the will alone — this, its own great act of will.

Where does this monstrous thing come from? And why? Let your mercy shine on me while I ask, in case the answer lies in the hidden punishments of men, in the darkest griefs of the sons of Adam. Where does the monstrous thing come from — and why? The mind orders the body, and is obeyed on the instant; the mind orders itself, and is resisted. The mind orders the hand to move, and it goes so smoothly you can barely tell the order from the doing — and mind is mind, while hand is body. The mind orders the mind to will — it is not another thing; it is itself — and it does not do it. Where does this monstrous thing come from? And why? The mind orders itself to will, I say — it would not be giving the order unless it willed — and what it orders does not happen. The answer is that it does not will it whole, so it does not order it whole. It orders exactly as far as it wills, and what it orders fails exactly as far as it does not will. For what the will is ordering is that there be a will — not some other will: itself. And it is not ordering with its whole self; therefore what it orders is not. If the will were whole, it would not order itself into being — the will would already be there. So this is no monster after all, this willing partly and partly not willing. It is a sickness of the mind: lifted by truth, it cannot rise all the way, because habit drags at its weight. There are two wills, then, because one of them is not whole; and what the one lacks, the other has.

Let them perish from your presence, God, as the empty talkers perish, the seducers of the mind — the men who noticed the two wills in our deliberating and decided that we contain two minds with two natures, one good, one evil. They are the evil ones, while they believe those evil things; and the same men will be good if ever they believe the truth and give the truth their yes, so that your apostle may say to them, You were darkness once; now you are light, in the Lord. But they want to be light in themselves rather than in the Lord — they imagine the soul's nature is what God is — and so they became a thicker darkness: in the horror of their arrogance they backed still farther away from you, the true Light that lights every man coming into the world. Watch what you say — and blush; come near him and be lit, and your faces will not be ashamed. When I was deliberating about serving the Lord my God at last, as I had long intended, it was I who willed it, and I who refused it. I. I was the one. I neither willed it whole nor refused it whole. So I fought myself, and I was torn apart by myself; and the tearing happened against my will — yet what it showed was not the presence of some second mind, but the punishment of my own. It was no longer I producing it, but the sin that lived in me — the punishment of a sin committed more freely than mine, because I was a son of Adam.

Besides, if there are as many contrary natures in us as there are wills in conflict, we will have to count not two but a crowd. Suppose a man is turning over whether to go to their meeting or to the theater. The Manichees shout: "There — two natures! The good one pulls him this way, the bad one pulls back that way. What else could explain this standoff of will against will?" But I say both wills are bad — the one that pulls toward them and the one that pulls back to the theater alike. They, of course, cannot imagine the will that pulls their way to be anything but good. Very well: suppose one of ours is deliberating, torn between two wills wrestling — go to the theater, or go to our church? Won't the Manichees be torn over the answer? Either they admit what they have no wish to admit — that the will leading a man to our church is good, just as good as they claim for the will that leads to theirs the men who have received their mysteries and are held by them; or they conclude that two evil natures and two evil minds are locked in combat inside one man — and that ruins their doctrine of the one good and the one evil; or they will have to be converted to the truth, and stop denying that when a man deliberates, it is one soul, surging between different wills.

So let them stop saying, when they feel two wills fighting in one man, that two hostile minds are at war, out of two hostile substances, from two hostile first principles — one good, one evil. For you, God of truth, refute them, expose them, catch them out. Take a case where both wills are bad. A man deliberates: kill his victim with poison, or with a blade? Seize this piece of another man's property, or that one, when he cannot take both? Buy pleasure with extravagance, or hold on to his money out of greed? The circus or the theater, if both open on the same day? Add a third pull — rob a house, if the chance is standing open; and a fourth — commit adultery, if that door too is open at the same moment. Let them all converge on one point of time, all craved equally, and impossible to do at once: they tear the mind four ways among four wills at war — or more than four, given the enormous range of things men want — and even here the Manichees do not claim a separate substance for each. The same holds for good wills. I ask them: is it good to delight in a reading from the Apostle? Good to delight in the gravity of a psalm? Good to discuss the Gospel? To each they will answer: good. What then, if they all delight equally, all at the same time? Aren't different wills straining the human heart while we deliberate which to seize first? All of them good — and all in contest, until one is chosen, and the whole will, split into many before, comes to rest there, one. So it is when eternity delights us from above and the pleasure of a temporal good tugs from below: it is one and the same soul, willing neither with its whole will; and that is why the soul is torn apart, and the tearing grieves it — truth teaching it to put the one first, habit refusing to lay the other down.

That is how sick I was, and how tortured — and I accused myself more bitterly than I had ever done, twisting and wrenching in my chain until the last of it should break: only a little held me now, but it held. And you, Lord, kept up the pressure in my hidden places — mercy severe — doubling the whips of fear and shame so that I would not go slack again, so that the thin last tether would not survive the strain, thicken, and tie me down harder than before. I kept saying inside myself, "Now — let it happen now," and with the words I was already at the edge of doing it. I was that close — and I did not do it. But I did not fall back to where I had been, either; I stood one step off, and caught my breath. And I tried again, and I was a little nearer, and a little nearer still; I was all but touching it; I was holding it — and I was not there at all; I touched nothing; I held nothing. I hung back from dying to death and living to life, and the worse, worn deep into me, had more power than the better I had never tried; and the closer it came, that exact point of time when I would become someone else, the more horror it drove into me. But the horror did not knock me backward, and it did not turn me aside. It held me hanging.

And what held me were the trifles of trifles, the vanities of vanities, those old loves of mine: plucking softly at my garment of flesh and murmuring, "You're sending us away?" and "From that moment we will never be with you again — not ever," and "From that moment this and that will be forbidden you — forever." And what things they meant by "this and that" — what things, my God! May your mercy keep it all far from your servant's soul. What filth they held up, what outrages! But I heard them now at far less than half strength: they no longer came out to face me and contradict me, but muttered, as it were, behind my back, and pinched furtively at a man already leaving, trying to make him look around. And still they slowed me. I could not bring myself to tear loose, to shake them off, to make the jump to where I was being called — while habit, that bully, kept asking me, "Do you really think you can live without them?"

But even the bully's voice was faint now. For on the side I had turned my face toward — the side I trembled to cross to — there opened before me the chaste dignity of Continence: serene, cheerful without a hint of looseness, honorably coaxing me to come and doubt no more, and stretching out to receive and embrace me holy hands full of flocks of good examples. So many boys and girls were with her, so many young people, every age of life, grave widows, women grown old in virginity — and in every one of them Continence herself, in no way barren, but a fruitful mother of children, of joys, by you, Lord, her husband. And she smiled at me — a mocking smile that made me braver — as if she were saying: "Can't you do what these men have done, what these women have done? Or do you think they do it in themselves, and not in the Lord their God? The Lord their God gave me to them. Why do you keep standing on yourself — and standing on nothing? Throw yourself onto him. Don't be afraid: he will not step aside and let you fall. Throw yourself without fear. He will catch you, and he will heal you." And I was blushing hard, because I could still hear the murmuring of those trifles, and I hung there, dangling. And again she seemed to say: "Shut your ears to those unclean members of yours upon the earth, and let them die. They tell you of delights — but not like the delights of the law of the Lord your God." All this was the quarrel in my heart: myself, against myself, and no one else. And Alypius sat close at my side, silently waiting out my strange agitation, to see where it would end.

But when deep reflection had hauled up the whole of my misery from its secret bottom and heaped it in the sight of my heart, an enormous storm broke, bringing an enormous rain of tears. And so that I could pour all of it out, with the sounds that belonged to it, I got up and left Alypius — solitude seemed to me better suited to the business of weeping — and I moved far enough away that even his presence could not weigh on me. That was my state, and he sensed it: I think I had said something, in a voice already swollen with crying, and then had risen. So he stayed where we had been sitting, astonished past words. I dropped down — how, I do not know — under a certain fig tree, and gave my tears free rein; and the floods burst from my eyes, an offering you accept. And I said to you — not in these words, but this was the sense of it — "And you, Lord — how long? How long, Lord? Will you be angry to the very end? Do not remember our old sins against us." For I felt it was those sins that still held me, and I kept flinging out these miserable cries: "How long? How long — tomorrow, and tomorrow? Why not now? Why not, this very hour, an end to my filth?"

I was saying this and weeping in the most bitter contrition of my heart — and then I heard a voice from the house next door: a chant, repeated over and over, in a voice like a young boy's or a girl's, I couldn't tell: "Take it and read. Take it and read." My face changed at once, and I began to think as hard as I could: did children sing anything like that in any of their games? I could not remember ever hearing it anywhere. I dammed the rush of tears and stood up, taking the chant for nothing less than a command from God: open the book, and read the first passage I found. For I had heard how Antony had walked in on a reading of the Gospel and taken the words as an admonition spoken straight to him — Go, sell all you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me — and by that oracle he had been converted to you on the spot. So I went back quickly to the place where Alypius was sitting, because I had left the volume of the Apostle there when I got up. I snatched it, opened it, and read in silence the first passage my eyes fell on: Not in carousing and drunkenness, not in debauchery and indecency, not in quarreling and jealousy — no: clothe yourselves in the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no plans to satisfy the cravings of the flesh. I did not want to read further, and there was no need. The instant the sentence ended, it was as if a light of peace poured into my heart, and all the darkness of doubt scattered.

Then I put my finger in the page, or some other marker, closed the book, and with a face gone calm at last I told Alypius. And this is how he showed me what had been happening in him, which I knew nothing about: he asked to see what I had read. I showed him, and he read past the place where I had stopped — I did not know what came next. This came next: Welcome the man whose faith is weak. He took it to himself, and told me so. That word steadied him; and with a good resolve, and no storm of hesitation — entirely like his character, which had long been far, far better than mine — he joined me. From there we go in to my mother. We tell her: she is filled with joy. We lay out how it happened: she exults, she triumphs, and she blessed you, who are able to do more than everything we ask or understand — because she saw that you had granted her, in me, so much more than her pitiful, grief-worn groaning had ever begged. For you converted me to yourself so fully that I no longer looked for a wife, or for any hope this world offers — standing now on that rule of faith where you had shown me to her, so many years before, in her vision. And you turned her mourning into a joy far richer than the one she had wanted, and far dearer and purer than the joy she had once looked for from grandchildren of my flesh.
