Confessions, Book IV

The Death of a Friend

Book IV4 of 13

For nine years — from my nineteenth year to my twenty-eighth — we lived seduced and seducing, deceived and deceiving, through one desire after another: openly, by way of the studies they call liberal; secretly, under a false name of religion. Proud on the one side, superstitious on the other, empty everywhere. On the one hand we chased the hollowness of popular glory, right down to applause in the theater, prize poems, contests for garlands of hay, the trash of the shows, the appetites that keep no measure. On the other we wanted to be scrubbed clean of all that filth by carrying food to the people called "elect" and "holy," so that in the workshop of their own stomachs they could manufacture angels and gods for us — the gods who were supposed to set us free. That is what I pursued; that is what I practiced with my friends — friends deceived by me, and deceived along with me. Let the arrogant laugh at me, and all those you have not yet struck down and broken for their own healing, my God: I will still confess my shame to you, and make it your praise. Let me do it, I beg you; give me the grace to walk back, in present memory, around the old loops of my wandering, and to offer you a sacrifice of rejoicing. For what am I to myself, without you, but a guide leading myself off the cliff? And what am I even at my best but a creature suckling your milk, feeding on you, the food that never spoils? And what kind of man is any man, seeing that he is a man? Let the strong and the powerful laugh at us, then. We are weak and poor, and we will confess to you.

In those years I taught rhetoric. Defeated by greed, I sold the kind of speech that wins. Even so — Lord, you know this — I preferred to have good students, as students are reckoned good, and without deceit I taught them deceit: not the kind to be used against an innocent man's life, though sometimes for a guilty man's. And you, God, watching from far off, saw my good faith stagger on that slippery ground and throw off a few sparks through all the smoke — the good faith I kept in teaching men who loved emptiness and went hunting for lies, I their fellow traveler. In those years I had a woman. She was not mine in what is called lawful marriage; a restless passion, empty of prudence, had found her out. But she was the only one, and I kept faith with her bed. And with her I learned firsthand the distance between the covenant of marriage, entered for the sake of children, and the bargain of a lustful love — where a child is born against the parents' wishes, though once born, it makes them love it.

I remember, too, that when I had decided to enter a competition for theatrical verse, some soothsayer sent word to ask what I would pay him to win it. I loathed and detested those filthy rites, and I answered that even if the garland were made of gold that never perishes, I would not let a fly be killed to win it — for he was going to kill living creatures in his sacrifices, and by those honors, it seemed, to recruit demons to back me. But even this evil I refused not out of any pure love for you, God of my heart. I did not know how to love you; I did not know how to conceive of anything beyond material light. And a soul that sighs after fictions like those — isn't it whoring away from you, trusting in shams, feeding the winds? I would not have sacrifices offered to demons on my behalf; yet by my superstition I was offering myself to them all the while. For what else is "feeding the winds" but feeding the demons themselves — becoming, by our straying, their entertainment and their joke?

But those frauds they call astrologers I consulted without a scruple, because they seemed to use no sacrifice and to aim no prayer at any spirit for their divinations. Christian and true devotion rejects that art too, and condemns it — consistently. For it is a good thing to confess to you and to say, Have mercy on me; heal my soul, for I have sinned against you; not to twist your mercy into a license to sin, but to remember the Lord's words: Look, you have been made well. Sin no more, or something worse may happen to you. All that healthy counsel the astrologers labor to kill, telling us, "The cause of your sin is fixed, inescapably, in the sky," and "Venus did this, or Saturn, or Mars" — so that man, this flesh and blood, this proud rot, might carry no blame, while the blame lands on the maker and orderer of heaven and the stars. And who is that but our God — the very sweetness and wellspring of justice, who repays each of us according to our works, and does not despise a broken and contrite heart?

There was in those days a shrewd man, deeply skilled in medicine and famous in it, who as proconsul had set that contest garland, with his own hand, on my head — my sick head, though he did not set it there as a doctor. For that disease you alone cure — you who resist the proud and give grace to the humble. But did you fail me even through that old man? Did you leave off healing my soul? I came to know him better, and I hung steadily, intently, on what he said; the words were plain, but the talk was quick, alive, and serious. When he gathered from my conversation that I was devoted to the books of the horoscope-casters, he advised me — kindly, like a father — to throw them away, and not to waste on that nonsense the care and effort that useful things need. He had studied the art himself, he said, in his early years, wanting to make it his living: if he had understood Hippocrates, he could certainly have understood that. And yet he had given it up and taken to medicine for one reason only — he had found it utterly false, and, being a serious man, he would not earn his bread by fooling people. "But you have rhetoric to live on," he said, "so you follow this thing by free choice, not by necessity. All the more reason, then, to believe me about it, since I worked to master it so completely that I meant to live by it alone." I asked him how it was, then, that so many true things were foretold by it. He answered, as well as anyone could, that the force of chance, scattered through the whole order of things, brought it about. If a man opens the pages of some poet at random — a poet singing and thinking of something else entirely — a line often falls out that fits his present business wonderfully. So it was no marvel, he said, if from the soul of a man, by some higher instinct and without the soul's knowing what happens inside it, an answer should come out that chimes with the asker's business and doings — by luck, not by art.

That much you procured for me from him, or through him, and you traced into my memory what I would later search out for myself. But at the time, neither he nor my dearest Nebridius — a young man singularly good and singularly clean of heart, who laughed at the whole business of divination — could persuade me to throw it away. The authority of the authors still weighed more with me, and I had not yet found the certain proof I was looking for: proof that would show, past all doubt, that when these consulted men said true things, it was chance speaking, not the art of the star-readers.

In those years — the time when I first began to teach in the town where I was born — I had made a friend, too dear to me, my own age, sharing my studies, flowering with me in the first flower of young manhood. He had grown up with me as a boy; we had gone to school together, played together. But he was not yet my friend the way he later became — and even then, not the way true friendship is. For there is no true friendship unless you glue it together, between people who hold fast to you, with the love poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who is given to us. Still, it was something almost too sweet, ripened in the heat of shared enthusiasms. I had even bent him away from the true faith — which as a youth he had never taken in deeply or thoroughly — toward those superstitious, soul-destroying fables my mother wept over in me. With me, now, the man wandered in his mind; and my soul could not do without him. But look: you were right behind your runaways — God of vengeance and fountain of mercies at once, who turn us back to you by astonishing means — look: you took the man out of this life when he had scarcely filled one whole year of a friendship sweeter to me than every sweetness of that life of mine.

Who can count up all your praises — even those a single man has felt in his single self? What did you do then, my God? How unsearchable is the abyss of your judgments! For a long time he lay senseless in a death-sweat, burnt through with fever, and when they gave up hope for him, he was baptized — knowing nothing of it. I hardly cared. I took it for granted that his soul would keep what it had learned from me, and not whatever had been done to a body that could not feel it. It turned out very differently: he rallied; he was saved. And the moment I could talk with him — which was the moment he could talk, for I never left him, and we hung on each other far too much — I tried joking with him, expecting him to laugh with me about that baptism he had received while absent in mind and sense, though by now he had learned that he had received it. He recoiled from me as if from an enemy. With a sudden, startling freedom he told me that if I wished to stay his friend, I would stop saying such things to him. I was astonished; I was shaken; and I buried every feeling I had until he should be well again, strong enough for me to handle as I pleased. But he was snatched away from my madness, to be kept safe with you for my consolation. A few days later, while I was away, the fever came back, and he died.

At that grief my heart went black. Everything I looked at was death. My own country was a torture to me, my father's house a strange unhappiness; and everything I had shared with him became, without him, one immense torment. My eyes looked for him everywhere, and he was not given to them. I hated all places because he was not in them — because they could no longer tell me, "Look, he's coming," as they could when he was alive and only away. I had become a great question to myself. I kept asking my soul why it was sad, and why it troubled me so deeply, and it had no answer to give me. And if I said, "Hope in God," it rightly refused to obey — because the man it had lost, that dearest man, was truer and better than the phantasm it was being ordered to hope in. Only weeping was sweet to me, and tears took my friend's place among the delights of my heart.

And now, Lord, all that has passed, and time has softened my wound. May I hear from you, who are Truth — may I put the ear of my heart against your mouth — and will you tell me why weeping is sweet to the miserable? Have you, though present everywhere, flung our misery far away from you? You abide in yourself, while we are rolled through trial after trial. And yet, if we could not cry into your ears, nothing of our hope would be left at all. How is it, then, that sweet fruit is picked from life's bitterness — from mourning, weeping, sighing, complaint? Is the sweetness this, that we hope you hear? In prayer, yes, rightly so: prayer carries the longing to reach you. But in grief for a lost thing — in the sorrow that had buried me then? I had no hope he would come back to life; my tears were not begging for that. I simply grieved, and wept: I was miserable, and I had lost my joy. Or is weeping a bitter thing after all, which pleases us only out of revulsion — only in the moment we recoil from the things we used to enjoy?

But why am I saying all this? Now is no time for questions; now is the time to confess to you. I was miserable, and every soul is miserable that is tied by friendship to mortal things: it is shredded when it loses them, and only then does it feel the misery that was in it even before it lost them. That is how it was with me then. I wept with utter bitterness, and I rested in the bitterness. So miserable was I that I held that miserable life dearer than my friend himself. However much I might have wished to change it, I was less willing to lose it than to lose him — I doubt I would have given it even for him, in the way tradition tells (if it is not invention) of Orestes and Pylades, who wanted to die for each other, or die together, since to them not living together was worse than dying. In me a feeling had risen up quite contrary to that, and I could not explain it: a crushing weariness of being alive, and at the same time a fear of dying. I believe that the more I loved him, the more I hated death, which had taken him from me, and the more I feared it, as the most savage of enemies; and I imagined it would suddenly devour every human being, since it had been able to devour him. That is how it was with me — I remember. Look at my heart, my God; look inside it and see, for I do remember, O my Hope, you who wash me clean of the uncleanness of such affections, turning my eyes toward you, pulling my feet out of the snare. For I marveled that the rest of the mortals went on living, when the man I had loved as though he would never die was dead; and I marveled more that I, his other self, was alive when he was dead. Someone once called his friend "the half of my soul," and said it well. For I felt that my soul and his soul had been one soul in two bodies; and that is why living horrified me — I refused to live as a half. And that, perhaps, is why I was afraid to die: so that the man I had loved so much would not die entirely.

What madness — not to know how to love human beings as human beings! What a fool, to take the human lot with so little measure! That was what I was then. So I seethed, I sighed, I wept, I was in turmoil; there was no rest, and no counsel. For I was carrying my soul around cut open and bloodied — a soul that could not stand being carried by me — and I could find nowhere to lay it down. Not in pleasant groves; not in games and songs; not in sweet-scented places; not in elegant banquets; not in the pleasures of bedroom and bed; not, finally, in books or in poetry would it rest. Everything looked ghastly, even light itself; and whatever was not what he had been was repellent and hateful — all of it, except groaning and tears. In those alone was there a little relief; and whenever my soul was taken from them, a huge freight of misery weighed me down. To you, Lord, it should have been lifted, for you to lighten it. I knew that. But I neither would nor could — all the less because, when I thought of you, you were nothing solid or firm to me. It was not you at all: my god was an empty phantasm, my own error. If I tried to lay my burden down on it, to let it rest, it slid through the void and came crashing back down on me; and I had remained, to myself, an unhappy place, where I could not stay and from which I could not get away. For where could my heart run from my heart? Where could I run from myself? Where would I not follow myself? And yet I fled my own country. My eyes would look for him less, I thought, in places where they were not used to seeing him. And so I left the town of Thagaste and came to Carthage.

Time is not idle; it does not roll uselessly over our senses. It works strange operations in the mind. Look — the days came and went, one after another, and coming and going they slipped other hopes into me, other memories, and little by little they patched me back together out of the old kinds of pleasure. To those my grief gave way. But what followed was not other griefs; it was the causes of other griefs. For why had that grief gone into me so easily and so deep, if not because I had poured out my soul on sand, loving a man who was going to die as if he would never die? What restored me most, what remade me, was the comfort of other friends — friends with whom I loved what I loved instead of you. And that was one enormous fable, one long-drawn lie, and its adulterous friction was corrupting our minds, whose itch was in the ears. But that fable would not die for me, however many of my friends died. There were other things in friendship that took a deeper hold on the mind: to talk together and laugh together; to trade kindnesses turn by turn; to read sweet-tongued books together; to be silly together and serious together; to disagree now and then without hatred, the way a man may disagree with himself, and to find those rarest disagreements the seasoning of countless agreements; to teach one another something, or learn something from one another; to miss the absent with impatience, and to welcome them home with joy. These signs, and a thousand like them, rising from the hearts of people who love and are loved in return — spoken by the mouth, the tongue, the eyes, and countless gestures of pleasure — were the kindling that fused our souls together and made, out of many, one.

That is what we love in friends — and we love it so, that a man's conscience convicts itself if he fails to love the one who loves him, or to love back the one who loved him first, asking of the other's person nothing but the signs of his goodwill. From this comes the mourning when a friend dies: the darkness of sorrows; the heart soaked through, its sweetness turned to bitterness; and, from the lost life of the dying, the death of the living. Blessed is the one who loves you — who loves his friend in you, and his enemy for your sake. For he alone loses no one dear to him, to whom everyone is dear in the One who is never lost. And who is that but our God — the God who made heaven and earth, and who fills them, since it was by filling them that he made them? No one loses you who does not let you go. And the one who lets you go — where does he go? Where can he run, except from you at peace with him to you set against him? For where will he not find your law waiting in his own punishment? And your law is truth, and truth is you.

Turn us, God of hosts; show us your face, and we will be saved. For wherever the soul of man turns, it is nailed to sorrows unless it turns to you — nailed even when it fastens on beautiful things. And those beautiful things, outside you and outside the soul, would not exist at all unless they came from you. They rise and they set: rising, they begin, as it were, to be; they grow, toward their completion; completed, they age, and they perish. Not everything ages — but everything perishes. So when they rise and reach toward being, the faster they grow into being, the harder they hurry toward not-being. That is their law. You gave them that much, because they are pieces of a whole whose pieces do not all exist at once: by giving way and taking over, all of them together act out the whole universe of which they are pieces. Our own speech is carried out the same way, through signs that sound: there is no whole sentence unless each word passes away, once it has sounded its part, so that the next may take its place. For all of this let my soul praise you, God, creator of all — but let it not be stuck fast to these things by the glue of love, through the senses of the body. For they go where they were always going: toward not-being; and they tear the soul with pestilent longings, because the soul wants to be — and it loves to rest in the things it loves. But in them there is nowhere to rest, because they do not stay. They flee: and who can follow them with the senses of the flesh? Who can grasp them even while they are near? The sense of the flesh is slow, because it is the sense of the flesh: that is its boundary. It is enough for what it was made for; it is not enough to hold things as they run their course from their appointed start to their appointed end. For in your Word, through whom they are created, they hear their orders: "Start here; end here."

Don't be empty, my soul; don't let the ear of your heart go deaf with the racket of your own emptiness. Listen — you too. The Word himself is crying out for you to return, and there is the place of rest that cannot be disturbed, where love is not abandoned unless it abandons first. Look: these things pass away so that other things may take their place, so that this lowest order of the universe may be complete in all its parts. "But do I go anywhere?" says the Word of God. Fix your dwelling there. Whatever you have from there, my soul, place it there in trust — now at least, tired out as you are with deceptions. Entrust to the Truth whatever the truth has given you, and you will lose nothing. The rot in you will flower again; all your diseases will be healed; all in you that drains away will be formed anew, made new, bound close around you. They will not drop you where they descend, but will stand with you, and hold, before the God who stands and holds forever.

Why keep following your own flesh, turned backwards? Turn around, and let it follow you. Whatever you sense through the flesh is a part; the whole to which the parts belong, you do not know — yet the parts delight you. But if the flesh's senses were fit to take in the whole — if they had not, for your punishment, been justly confined to a part of it — you would want whatever exists in the present moment to pass away, so that the whole could please you more. When we speak, you hear us through that same sense of flesh — and you certainly do not want the syllables to stand still; you want them to fly past, so that others can come and you can hear the whole. That is how it always is, when one thing is made of many parts that do not exist all at once: all of it, could all of it be perceived, would please more than the pieces do. But better by far than all of it is he who made all of it: he is our God. And he does not pass away, because there is nothing to take his place.

If bodies please you, praise God for them and turn your love back around, onto their maker — lest in the things that please you, you displease. If souls please you, love them in God; for they too are changeable, and only fixed in him do they stand firm — otherwise they would go, and be gone. Love them in him, then, and sweep up with you to him every soul you can, and say to them: "Let us love him — let us love him! He made these things, and he is not far away. He did not make them and then leave; they are from him, and they are in him. Look where he is: he is wherever truth is savored. He is inmost in the heart itself — but the heart has strayed from him. Come back to your heart, you deserters, and hold fast to the one who made you. Stand with him, and you will stand. Rest in him, and you will be at rest. Where are you going, over such rough ground? Where are you going? The good you love is from him — and turned toward him it is good and it is sweet; but it will turn bitter, and justly so, because whatever is from him is loved unjustly if he is deserted for its sake. What do you want, walking on and on down these hard and weary roads? There is no rest where you are seeking it. Seek what you seek — but it is not where you are seeking it. You seek a happy life in the country of death, and it is not there. How can there be a happy life where there is no life at all?

"But our true Life came down here and carried our death away, and killed it out of the abundance of his own life. And he thundered, calling us to return from here to him — back into that secret place from which he first came forth to us: into the Virgin's womb itself, where the human creature was wedded to him, mortal flesh, that it might not be mortal forever; and from there, like a bridegroom coming out of his chamber, he exulted like a champion to run his race. He did not linger — he ran, shouting by his words, his deeds, his death, his life, his descent, his ascent, shouting to us to return to him. And he withdrew from our eyes, so that we would return into our own hearts and find him there. He went away — and look: he is here. He would not stay long with us, and he has not left us. He went back to the place he never departed from, because the world was made through him; and he was in this world, and he came into this world to save sinners. To him my soul confesses, and he heals it, because it sinned against him. You sons of men, how long will you be so heavy of heart? Even now, after Life itself has come down to you, will you not climb up and live? But where can you climb, when you are already high, with your mouths set against the heavens? Come down, so that you can climb — climb, this time, to God. For you fell when you climbed against him." Tell them this, my soul, and let them weep in the valley of weeping — and so carry them up with you to God; for it is by his Spirit that you say this to them, if you say it burning with the fire of love.

None of this did I know then. I loved the lower beauties, and I was sinking toward the bottom, and I would say to my friends: "Do we love anything except the beautiful? Then what is the beautiful? And what is beauty? What is it in the things we love that pulls us and binds us to them? Unless there were some grace in them, some form, they could not possibly draw us." And I looked, and I noticed that in bodies themselves there is one beauty that comes of being a kind of whole, and another that comes of fitting — of answering well to something else, as a part of the body answers to its whole body, or a shoe to a foot, and so on. This thought welled up in my mind from the bottom of my heart, and I wrote some books — "On the Beautiful and the Fitting," I think — two of them, or three. You know, God; it has slipped from me. I no longer have them; they wandered away from me, I don't know how.

But what was it, Lord my God, that moved me to dedicate those books to Hierius, an orator of the city of Rome? I did not know him by sight. I loved the man for his reputation for learning, which was brilliant, and for a few sayings of his I had heard and liked. But he pleased me more because he pleased others: people praised him to the skies, amazed that a Syrian, schooled first in Greek eloquence, should afterward have turned into a marvelous speaker of Latin too, and one supremely learned in everything that belongs to the pursuit of wisdom. A man is praised, and, sight unseen, he is loved. Does the love enter the hearer's heart from the mouth of the one praising? No. One lover kindles another. That is why a man who is praised gets loved: because we believe the praise comes from an undeceiving heart — that is, that the one praising already loves him.

That is how I loved men then: on the judgment of men, not on yours, my God, in which no one is deceived. Still — why not love him the way one loves a famous charioteer, or a beast-fighter adored by the crowds, instead of so very differently, so seriously — exactly the way I wanted to be praised myself? For I would not have wanted to be praised and loved the way actors are — though I myself praised and loved actors. I would sooner have been unknown than known like that; sooner hated than loved like that. Where does one soul store the weights of so many different and contrary loves? Why do I love in another man what — since we are equally men — I would detest and throw off from myself, if I did not hate it? A good horse may be loved by a man who would not choose to be that horse even if he could; but you cannot say the same of an actor, who shares our nature. Do I then love in a man what I would hate to be, though I am a man? Man himself is a vast deep. You, Lord, keep the count of his very hairs, and not one of them is lost in you; and yet the hairs of his head are easier to number than his feelings — than the movements of his heart.

That orator was of the kind I loved in the way of wanting to be him. And I strayed, swollen with conceit, carried around by every wind — and all the while, in deepest secret, you were steering me. And how do I know — how can I confess it to you as a certainty — that I loved him more in the love of his praisers than for the things they praised? Because if those same men, instead of praising him, had torn him down — if they had reported the very same facts about him with scorn and contempt — I would never have caught fire for him, never been stirred. And yet the facts would have been no different; the man himself no different; only the feeling of the tellers. Look where the strengthless soul lies, when it is not yet fastened to the solidity of truth: as the gusts of tongues blow out of the chests of the opinionated, the soul is carried and turned, twisted and twisted back; the light clouds over, and it cannot make out the truth. And look — the truth is right in front of us. To me, back then, it was a very great matter that my style and my studies should become known to that man. If he approved them, I would blaze all the hotter; if he disapproved, my heart — hollow, void of your solidity — would have taken the wound. And yet that "beautiful and fitting" I had written to him about, I liked turning over in my mind, gazing on it and admiring it, with no one to praise it with me.

But I did not yet see the hinge on which this great question turns: your craftsmanship, Almighty — you who alone do wonders. My mind moved among bodily forms. The "beautiful" I defined as what pleases in itself; the "fitting" as what pleases by suiting something else; and I shored up the distinction with examples drawn from bodies. Then I turned to the nature of the mind — and the false notion I held of spiritual things would not let me see what was true. The sheer force of the truth kept beating against my eyes, and I kept turning my quivering mind away from bodiless reality toward outlines and colors and swollen masses; and since I could not see such things in the mind, I concluded that I could not see my mind. And since in virtue I loved peace, and in vice I hated discord, I noted in the one a unity, in the other a kind of division; and it seemed to me that in that unity lay the rational mind and the very nature of truth and of the highest good, while in that division — this was my misery — there was some substance of irrational life, and the nature of a highest evil: not merely a substance, but genuinely a life, and yet not from you, my God, from whom all things are. The first I called a Monad — a mind, as it were, without any sex; the second a Dyad: anger in crimes of violence, lust in crimes of the flesh. I did not know what I was saying. For I had not known, and had not learned, that evil is not a substance at all — and that our own mind is not the highest and unchangeable good.

For as crimes of violence arise when the surge of the soul from which action springs is corrupted and hurls itself forward, insolent and turbid; and as crimes of the flesh arise when the soul's appetite for drinking in bodily pleasures runs unbridled: just so do errors and false opinions poison a life when the reasoning mind itself is corrupt. And so it was then in me — a mind that did not know it must be lit by another light in order to share in the truth, since it is not itself the nature of truth. For you will light my lamp, Lord; my God, you will turn my darkness to light; and from your fullness we have all received. For you are the true light, which gives light to every man who comes into this world; and in you there is no changing — not even the shadow thrown by turning.

I strained toward you, and was pushed back from you so that I might taste death — for you resist the proud. And what is prouder than to claim, with unbelievable madness, that I was by nature what you are? I was changeable, and I knew it — plainly, since the very fact that I wanted to become wise meant I wanted to go from worse to better. Yet I preferred to imagine even you changeable, rather than admit that I was not what you are. So you pushed me back; you set yourself against my stiff, wind-filled neck. And I went on imagining bodily shapes; flesh myself, I put flesh on trial; a wind that walks away and does not come back, I did not return to you, but wandered on and on, toward things that have no being at all — not in you, not in me, not in the body. None of it was created for me by your truth; all of it was invented by my vanity out of body. And I used to say to your faithful little ones, my fellow citizens — from whom, without knowing it, I was living in exile — I used to say to them, glib and fatuous: "Why does the soul go wrong, if God made it?" But I did not want anyone to say to me, "Why does God go wrong, then?" And I argued that your unchangeable substance erred under compulsion, sooner than confess that my changeable substance had swerved of its own free will, and that its erring now was punishment.

I was perhaps twenty-six or twenty-seven years old when I wrote those volumes — turning over inside me bodily fictions that drummed at the ears of my heart. And I was straining those ears, sweet Truth, for your inward melody, thinking on the beautiful and the fitting, longing to stand still and hear you and to rejoice with joy at the voice of the Bridegroom — and I could not. The voices of my own error dragged me outward, and under the weight of my own pride I kept sinking toward the lowest place. You did not give joy and gladness to my hearing; and the bones did not exult, because they had not been humbled.

When I was about twenty, a certain work of Aristotle fell into my hands. They call it the Ten Categories, and at the bare name of it I would stand open-mouthed, as before something great and divine, whenever my rhetoric master at Carthage, and others reckoned learned, pronounced it with their cheeks bursting with pride. And what good did it do me that I read it alone, and understood it? I compared notes with others, who said that even with the most erudite teachers — teachers who did not just lecture on it, but drew diagram after diagram in the sand — they had barely understood it; and they could tell me nothing about it that I had not already gathered reading it by myself. The book seemed to me to speak plainly enough about substances, such as "a man," and about what is in them: the figure of a man, what it is like; his stature, how many feet; his kinship, whose brother he is; where he is placed; when he was born; whether he stands or sits; whether he is shod or armed; whether he is doing something or having something done to him — and all the innumerable things that fall under those nine categories, of which I have set down a few samples, or under the chief category, substance itself.

What good was this to me — when in fact it did me harm? For, supposing that those ten categories covered absolutely everything that is, I tried to understand even you that way, my God — you, wonderfully simple, wonderfully unchanging — as though you too were the subject in which your greatness or your beauty resided, existing in you as they exist in a body. But your greatness and your beauty are you yourself; whereas a body is not great or beautiful simply by being a body — were it less great and less beautiful, it would be a body still. What I was thinking about you was falsehood, not truth: the fictions of my own misery, not the firm ground of your blessedness. For you had commanded it, and so it was being done in me: the earth was bearing me briars and thorns, and I was reaching my bread by the sweat of my face.

And what good did it do me that I read on my own, and understood, every book I could lay hands on of the arts they call liberal — I, at that time the vile slave of vile desires? I delighted in the books, and had no idea where whatever in them was true and certain came from. For I had my back to the light and my face to the things the light fell on; and so my face, by which I saw the lit things, caught no light itself. Whatever was written on rhetoric or on logic, on the measuring of figures, on music, on numbers, I understood without much difficulty, without any man to teach me — you know it, Lord my God, because quickness of understanding and sharpness of analysis are your gift. But I offered you no sacrifice from it. So it worked not for my use but rather for my ruin, since I fought to keep so good a part of my inheritance in my own power, and did not keep my strength for you, but set out from you into a far country, to squander my substance there on whoring passions. For what good was a good thing to me, when I did not use it well? I never realized how hard those arts are — hard even for the studious and the gifted — until I tried to explain them to the studious and the gifted, and found that the most brilliant of them was simply the one who was not too slow in following me.

But what good was any of it to me, while I supposed that you, Lord God of truth, were a vast and luminous body, and I a broken-off piece of that body? Perversity past all measure! But that is what I was. And I am not ashamed now, my God, to confess to you your mercies over me, and to call upon you — I who then was not ashamed to profess my blasphemies in front of men, and to bark at you. What use to me, then, was a mind so nimble in those sciences — all those knotted volumes untied with no help from any human teacher — when in the teaching of true devotion I was going foully, sacrilegiously wrong? And what harm was a far slower mind to your little ones, who never wandered far from you — who could grow their feathers in safety in the nest of your Church, and feed the wings of love on the food of a sound faith? O Lord our God, let us hope in the shelter of your wings: protect us, and carry us. You will carry us — you will carry us when we are small, and when our hair is white you will carry us still; for when our strength is you, it is strength, and when it is our own, it is weakness. Our good lives always with you, and because we have turned away from it, we have gone wrong. Let us turn back now, Lord, so that we are not overturned — because our good lives with you, lacking nothing; and that good is you yourself. And we need not be afraid that there is no place to come back to, just because we fell out of it: in our absence, our house does not fall down — our house, which is your eternity.
