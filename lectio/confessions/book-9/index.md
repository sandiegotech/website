# Confessions, Book IX

Baptism, and the Death of Monica

Lord, I am your servant — I am your servant, and the son of your handmaid. You have broken my chains. I will offer you a sacrifice of praise. Let my heart and my tongue praise you; let all my bones say, Lord, who is like you? Let them say it, and answer me, and say to my soul: I am your salvation. Who am I? What kind of thing am I? What evil have my deeds not been — or if not my deeds, my words, or if not my words, my will? But you, Lord, are good and merciful. Your right hand reached into the depth of my death and drained the pit of corruption from the bottom of my heart. And the whole of it came to this: to stop willing what I willed, and to will what you willed. But where had my free will been through all those years? Out of what deep, secret hiding place was it called up in a single moment, so that I could bend my neck to your easy yoke and my shoulders to your light burden, Christ Jesus, my helper and my redeemer? How sweet it suddenly was to give up the sweetness of those toys! I had been terrified of losing them; now it was a joy to let them go. For you were casting them out of me, you the true, the highest sweetness — casting them out, and coming in to take their place, sweeter than any pleasure, though not to flesh and blood; brighter than any light, and deeper inside me than any secret; higher than any honor, though not to those who stand high in their own eyes. Now my mind was free of the gnawing cares of ambition and acquisition, of wallowing in filth and scratching the itch of lust. And I babbled like a child to you — my brightness, my wealth, my health: the Lord my God.

And I decided, in your sight, not to make a scene of quitting but to withdraw the service of my tongue quietly from the talking-market — so that boys who were studying not your law, not your peace, but lying follies and courtroom combat, should no longer buy weapons for their madness from my mouth. Conveniently, only a few days remained before the vintage vacation, and I decided to endure them and then step down in the regular way — bought back by you, never again to put myself up for sale. Our plan was known to you, but not to men, apart from our own circle. We had agreed among ourselves not to let it spread — although to us, climbing up from the valley of weeping and singing the song of ascents, you had given sharp arrows and burning coals against the treacherous tongue: the tongue that pretends to advise while it opposes, and that consumes us out of love the way it consumes a meal.

You had shot our heart through with arrows of your love, and we carried your words fixed deep within us. The examples of your servants — whom you had turned from black to shining, from dead to alive — lay heaped in the storehouse of our thoughts, and there they caught fire and burned away our heavy sluggishness, so that we would not sink back toward the pit; and they set us so fiercely alight that every blast of contradiction from some cunning tongue only fanned the flame — it could not blow us out. All the same, since your name, which you have made holy across the earth, would win our vow and purpose admirers of its own, it looked like showing off not to wait for the vacation that was so close — abandoning, ahead of time and in front of everyone, a public position. Every eye would be on the gesture; everyone would note how near the vintage was that I could not wait out; and there would be a great deal of talk about me wanting to look like somebody great. And what good would it have done me, for people to speculate and argue over my motives — and for our good to be spoken of as evil?

There was something else. That same summer my lungs had begun to give way under the strain of too much teaching. Breathing came hard; pains in my chest showed the damage; a full or sustained voice was more than they could manage. This had troubled me at first, because it was all but forcing me to lay down the burden of teaching — or at least, if I could be cured and made strong again, to set it aside for a while. But once the full desire to be still and see that you are the Lord had risen in me and taken hold — my God, you know this — I even began to be glad that I had this second, unfeigned excuse, one that might soften the offense taken by men who wanted me never to be free, for their sons' sake. Full of that joy, then, I held out until the interval had run — some twenty days, perhaps — but holding out took strength; the greed that used to carry its share of the heavy load had left me, and I would have been crushed under it alone if patience had not stepped into its place. Some of your servants, my brothers, may say I sinned in this — that with my heart fully enlisted in your service, I let myself sit even one more hour in the chair of lies. I won't argue the point. But you, most merciful Lord — did you not pardon this sin too, and wash it away with the rest of my horrible, deadly sins, in the holy water?

Verecundus was eaten up with worry over this happiness of ours, because he saw that the ties that bound him so tightly would cut him off from our company. He was not yet a Christian; his wife was a believer — and precisely she, more than any other shackle, held him back from the journey we had begun. He would not be a Christian, he said, on any terms but the ones closed to him. Still, he kindly offered us his country place for as long as we stayed in the area. You will repay him, Lord, at the resurrection of the just — indeed you have already given him the portion of the just. While we were away, by then at Rome, he was seized by bodily illness, and in it he was made a Christian, one of the faithful, and departed this life. So you had mercy not only on him but on us — for it would have been an unbearable grief to remember our friend's extraordinary kindness toward us and not be able to count him in your flock. Thanks be to you, our God! We are yours. Your promptings and consolations tell us so: faithful to your promises, you now repay Verecundus for that country house at Cassiciacum — where we rested in you from the fever of the world — with the everlasting freshness of your paradise; for you have forgiven him his sins on earth, on the rich mountain, the mountain flowing with milk, your own mountain.

So he had sorrow then; but Nebridius had joy. He too, not yet a Christian, had fallen into that pit of deadly error, believing the flesh of your Son, the Truth, to be a phantom; but he had climbed out of it and believed what we believed — not yet steeped in any sacraments of your church, but a white-hot searcher after truth. Not long after our conversion and our rebirth through your baptism, he too became a faithful Catholic, serving you in perfect chastity and continence among his own people in Africa, his whole household made Christian through him — and then you released him from the flesh. And now he lives in Abraham's embrace. Whatever that phrase means, there my Nebridius lives — my sweet friend, and your son, Lord: a freedman adopted into your family. There he lives. What other place could hold such a soul? He lives in the very country he used to question me about — me, an ignorant, inexperienced man. He no longer puts his ear to my mouth; he puts the mouth of his spirit to your fountain and drinks all the wisdom he can hold, thirsty and endlessly happy. And I do not think he drinks so deep that he forgets me — since you, Lord, whom he drinks, remember us. So this was our life then: comforting Verecundus, who was saddened by our conversion, with our friendship still unbroken, and urging him toward the faith that fit his own state — married life; and waiting for Nebridius to follow us, which he was on the very edge of doing. And so those days finally rolled past. They felt long, and there were many of them, because of my longing for free, unhurried days in which to sing to you from the very marrow of my being: My heart has said to you, I have sought your face; your face, Lord, I will keep seeking.

And the day came when I was released from the professorship of rhetoric in fact, as I had already been released from it in thought. It was done. You rescued my tongue as you had already rescued my heart, and I blessed you, rejoicing, and went off to the villa with all my own. What I did there in writing — writing now enlisted in your service, though still panting from the school of pride, like a runner catching his breath — my books can testify: the debates with friends, and the debates with myself alone before you; and what passed with the absent Nebridius, my letters can testify. And when would I ever have time to record all your great kindnesses to us in that season, especially when I am hurrying on to greater ones? My memory calls me back, and it is sweet to me, Lord, to confess to you the inward goads by which you finally tamed me: how you leveled me, bringing down the mountains and hills of my thoughts, straightening what was crooked in me and smoothing what was rough; and how you also subdued Alypius, my heart's brother, to the name of your Only-Begotten, our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ — a name he at first refused to allow into our writings. He wanted them to smell of the schools' lofty cedars, which the Lord has since broken to pieces, rather than of the church's healing herbs, the antidote to serpents.

What cries I raised to you, my God, when I read the Psalms of David — those songs of faith, those sounds of devotion that leave no room for a swollen spirit — still a raw beginner in real love of you, a catechumen resting at a villa with Alypius, a catechumen too, and my mother staying close beside us: a woman in dress, a man in faith; with an old woman's calm, a mother's love, a Christian's devotion. What cries I raised to you in those psalms, and how they kindled me toward you, and how I burned to recite them, if I could, to the whole world, against the pride of the human race! And in fact they are sung in the whole world, and no one can hide from your heat. How fierce and bitter my anger at the Manichees was — and then again I pitied them, because they did not know those sacraments, those medicines, and raged like madmen against the very antidote that could have cured their madness. I wish they could have been somewhere near me then, without my knowing it, and watched my face and heard my voice when I read the fourth Psalm in that time of quiet — and seen what that psalm did to me: When I called, the God of my justice heard me; in my distress you gave me room. Have mercy on me, Lord, and hear my prayer. I wish they could have heard what I said over those words without my knowing they heard, so they would not think I was performing for them. And in truth I would not have said the same things, or said them the same way, if I had known they were watching and listening; and if I had said them, they would not have received them as they were — spoken by myself and for myself, in front of you, out of the most intimate feeling of my soul.

I shuddered with fear, and in the same breath I caught fire with hope and with exultation in your mercy, Father. And all of it came pouring out through my eyes and my voice, when your good Spirit turned to us and said: Sons of men, how long will your hearts be heavy? Why do you love emptiness and chase after lies? For I had loved emptiness and chased after lies. And you, Lord, had already glorified your Holy One, raising him from the dead and seating him at your right hand, from where he would send the one he had promised, the Comforter, the Spirit of truth. He had sent him already — and I had not known it. He had sent him because he was now glorified, risen from the dead and ascended into heaven; until then the Spirit was not yet given, because Jesus was not yet glorified. And the prophet cries: How long, heavy hearts? Why do you love emptiness and chase after lies? Know this: the Lord has glorified his Holy One. He cries How long. He cries Know this. And I, for so long, not knowing, had loved emptiness and chased after lies. So I heard, and I trembled, because these words were spoken to exactly the kind of man I remembered having been. In those phantoms I had held for truth there was emptiness and there were lies; and in the bitterness of remembering, I said many things out loud, with force and from the heart. I wish the men who still love emptiness and chase after lies could have heard me. It might have shaken them; they might have vomited it all up; and then you would have heard them when they cried to you — because he who now intercedes with you for us died for us a true death in the flesh.

I read on: Be angry, and do not sin. And how that moved me, my God — I who had now learned to be angry at myself for what was past, so that I would not sin in the time to come. And rightly angry: because it was no alien nature out of some tribe of darkness that had been sinning on my behalf, as they say who are not angry at themselves, and who store up wrath for the day of wrath, when your just judgment is revealed. By now my good was no longer outside me, no longer hunted with the eyes of flesh under that earthly sun. People who want their joy from outside go hollow fast: they spill themselves out on what is visible and temporary, and in their starving imagination they lick the mere images of things. If only they would wear out with hunger and say, Who will show us good things? Then we would answer, and they would hear: The light of your face, Lord, is stamped upon us. For we are not ourselves the light that enlightens every man; we are lit by you, so that we who were once darkness may be light in you. If only they could see the eternal that is within! I had tasted it, and it grieved me that I could not show it to them, as long as they came to me carrying their hearts in their eyes — eyes roving everywhere away from you — and saying, Who will show us good things? For it was there, in the room where I had been angry at myself, inside, where I had been pierced, where I had made my sacrifice — slaughtering the old man in me, and staking the new-begun hope of my life on you — it was there that you began to grow sweet to me, and put gladness in my heart. And I cried out as I read it outside and recognized it inside. And I had no wish to be multiplied in earthly goods, devouring time and devoured by time, when in your eternal simplicity I had other grain, other wine, other oil.

And at the next verse I shouted from the depth of my heart: In peace! In the Selfsame! What a thing to say — I will lie down and sleep. For who will stand against us when the written word comes true: Death is swallowed up in victory? And you, supremely, are the Selfsame — you who do not change; and in you is the rest that forgets every labor, since there is no other beside you, and we were not made to chase the many other things that are not what you are. No: you, Lord — you alone have settled me in hope. I read, and I burned; and I could find nothing to do for the deaf and the dead, one of whom I had been: a walking plague, a bitter, blind barker against the very Scriptures that drip with the honey of heaven and blaze with your own light. And I was eaten alive with zeal over the enemies of that book.

When will I ever recall everything from those days of rest? But I have not forgotten — and I will not pass over — the sting of your whip and the astonishing swiftness of your mercy. You tormented me then with a toothache, and when it grew so bad I could not speak, it rose up in my heart to ask all my friends who were there to pray for me to you, the God of every kind of health. I wrote this on wax and gave it to them to read. The moment we had gone down on our knees in simple supplication, the pain fled. But what pain it was — and how did it flee? I was terrified, my Lord, my God; nothing like it had happened to me since my earliest years. And deep inside me I felt your nod — the mere motion of your will — and rejoicing in faith, I praised your name. And that same faith would not let me rest easy about my past sins, which had not yet been forgiven me in your baptism.

When the vintage vacation ended, I sent the Milanese notice to find their students another seller of words, because I had chosen to serve you, and because my failing breath and the pain in my chest were not equal to the professorship. And I wrote to your bishop, the holy man Ambrose, telling him my old errors and my present resolve, and asking his advice: which of your Scriptures should I read, to make me readier and fitter to receive so great a grace? He recommended Isaiah the prophet — because, I believe, Isaiah is the plainest herald of the gospel and of the calling of the nations. But I did not understand the opening of him, and assuming the whole book was like it, I set it aside, to be taken up again when I had more practice in the Lord's way of speaking.

Then, when the time came to hand in my name for baptism, we left the countryside and returned to Milan. Alypius decided to be reborn in you along with me. He had already put on the humility that fits your sacraments, and he was the bravest of tamers of his own body — daring, beyond all custom, to walk the frozen ground of Italy barefoot. We took with us the boy Adeodatus, my son in the flesh, born of my sin. You had made him a fine boy. He was barely fifteen, and in intelligence he outstripped many grave and learned men. I am confessing your own gifts to you, Lord my God, creator of all things and abundantly able to reshape our deformities — for I had no part in that boy except the sin. That we brought him up in your discipline — that was you and no one else prompting us. I am confessing your own gifts. There is a book of mine called The Teacher: a dialogue between him and me. You know that every idea placed there in the mouth of the person talking with me was his, at sixteen years old. I saw much more in him, and more astonishing still. That mind of his put a kind of awe into me — and who but you could be the craftsman of such wonders? Soon you took his life from the earth; and I remember him now with no anxiety at all, with nothing to fear for his childhood, or his youth, or the whole man he was. We took him with us, our contemporary in your grace, to be brought up in your discipline — and we were baptized, and all anxiety over our past life fled away. In those days I could not get enough of the wonderful sweetness of pondering the depth of your design for the salvation of the human race. How I wept at your hymns and canticles, pierced to the quick by the voices of your sweet-singing church! The voices flowed into my ears, and the truth was distilled into my heart, and feeling for you welled up hot and overflowed, and the tears ran down — and they did me good.

The church of Milan had only recently begun to worship with this kind of consolation and encouragement, the brothers singing together with great fervor, voices and hearts as one. It was about a year earlier, not much more, that Justina, mother of the boy emperor Valentinian, had persecuted your servant Ambrose in the interest of the heresy the Arians had seduced her into. The devout people kept watch in the church, ready to die with their bishop, your servant. There my mother, your handmaid, holding a front place in those anxieties and vigils, lived on prayer. Even we, still cold, still unwarmed by the heat of your Spirit, were stirred by the sight of the city in alarm and turmoil. That was when the practice began — after the custom of the Eastern churches — of singing hymns and psalms, so the people would not grow faint under the grinding weight of sorrow. It has been kept from that day to this, and taken up by many — by now almost all — of your flocks across the rest of the world.

Then, in a vision, you revealed to your bishop where the bodies of the martyrs Gervasius and Protasius lay hidden. You had kept them incorrupt for all those years in your secret treasury, so that you could bring them out at the right moment to check the fury of a woman — a woman, but a royal one. When they had been found and dug up and carried with due honor to the Ambrosian basilica, not only were those tormented by unclean spirits healed, the demons themselves confessing it; there was also a man of the city, blind for many years and well known there, who asked what all the crowd's tumbling joy was about, heard the answer, and sprang up, begging his guide to lead him there. Brought to the place, he asked to be allowed to touch with his handkerchief the bier of your saints, whose death is precious in your sight. He touched it, and put the cloth to his eyes — and at once they opened. From there the story raced outward; from there your praises glowed and blazed; from there the mind of that hostile woman, though not turned to the health of belief, was at least turned back from the madness of persecution. Thanks be to you, my God! From where, and to where, have you led my memory, that I should confess these things to you too — great things I had passed over and forgotten? And yet even then, when the fragrance of your ointments hung so heavy in the air, we did not run after you. That is why I wept the more when your hymns were sung: I had sighed after you for so long, and at last I could breathe you — as far as air can move through this house of grass.

You, who make those of one mind dwell together in one house, joined to our company Euodius as well, a young man of our own town. He had been an officer of the imperial service; he was converted to you and baptized before we were; he quit the state's warfare and buckled on yours. We were together, meaning to live together in our holy resolve. We were looking for the place where we could serve you most usefully, and were making our way back, together, to Africa. And when we had gotten as far as Ostia on the Tiber, my mother died. I pass over many things, because I am in great haste. Receive my confessions and my thanksgivings, my God, for countless things I say nothing of. But I will not pass over whatever my soul can bring to birth about that servant of yours who brought me to birth — in her flesh, so that I was born into this daylight; in her heart, so that I might be born into eternal light. Not her gifts, but your gifts in her, are what I will tell; for she did not make herself, and she did not raise herself. You created her — her father and mother did not know what kind of person would come from them — and it was the scepter of your Christ, the discipline of your only Son, that reared her in your fear, in a believing house, a good member of your church. Yet she used to credit her upbringing less to her mother's diligence than to a certain aged servant woman, who had carried her father on her back when he was a baby, the way little children are carried by the bigger girls. Because of that, and because of her great age and impeccable character, the heads of that Christian household held her in real honor; and so the care of the master's daughters was placed in her hands. She took the charge seriously: fierce with a holy severity in restraining the girls when it was needed, sober and shrewd in teaching them. Outside the hours when they were fed, very moderately, at their parents' table, she would not let them drink so much as water, even if they were burning with thirst. She was heading off a bad habit, and she added the sensible word: "You drink water now because you don't have wine within reach. But when you're married, and find yourselves mistresses of the pantries and the cellars, water will seem beneath you — and the habit of drinking will still be there." By this way of teaching, and the authority she carried, she reined in the greed of a tender age and shaped the girls' very thirst to so decent a measure that what they should not do, they no longer even wanted.

And yet — as your handmaid told me, her son — a love of wine had crept up on her. Her parents, trusting her as a sober girl, used to send her in the usual way to draw wine from the cask. She would dip the cup in at the opening, and before she poured the wine into the flagon she would sip just a little, with the tips of her lips — no more than that, because more than that her own taste refused. She did it not out of any craving for drink, but out of the boiling-over energy of youth, which vents itself in playful impulses and is usually held down, in children, by the weight of their elders. And so, adding to that little a daily little more — for whoever despises small things falls, little by little — she slid into the habit of greedily draining her small cups nearly full of undiluted wine. Where then was the shrewd old woman and her stern forbidding? Would anything be strong enough against a hidden disease, if your medicine, Lord, were not keeping watch over us? Father, mother, guardians absent — you present, who created us, who call us, who work good for the health of our souls even through the people set over us — what did you do then, my God? How did you cure her? How did you heal her? Did you not draw out of another soul a hard, sharp insult, like a surgeon's blade out of your hidden stores, and with one cut take off all the rot? A servant girl who used to go with her to the cellar fell to quarreling, as happens, with her little mistress when the two of them were alone, and threw this fault in her face with the bitterest scorn, calling her a little wine-swiller. The taunt went in like a barb. She saw her own foulness, condemned it on the spot, and stripped it off. As flattering friends corrupt, so needling enemies mostly correct. Yet you repay them not for what you do through them, but for what they themselves intended. That girl, in her anger, wanted to wound her little mistress, not to heal her — and she did it in private, either because that just happened to be where the quarrel caught them, or because she herself might have been in trouble for having kept quiet so long. But you, Lord, ruler of everything in heaven and on earth, who bend the deepest torrents to your own uses and give order to the churning flood of the centuries — out of one soul's sickness you healed another; so that no one who notices this should credit his own power when someone he wanted corrected is corrected through a word of his.

Raised in this modesty and sobriety — made obedient to her parents by you, rather than to you by her parents — when she reached full marriageable age she was given to a husband, and she served him as her lord. And she worked to win him for you, preaching you to him by what she was; for with that you had made her beautiful — a wife her husband could respect, admire, and love. She bore the wrongs done to her bed in such a way that no quarrel over them ever came between her and her husband. She was waiting for your mercy on him: that he might believe in you, and be made chaste. And besides this, he was as hot in temper as he was warm in kindness. But she knew not to cross an angry husband — not in act, not even in a word. Only when he had settled and grown quiet, and the moment was right, would she explain what she had done, if it happened that he had flared up without thinking. In short: while many wives with far milder husbands carried the marks of beatings on their disfigured faces, and in the gossip of friends would blame their husbands' conduct, she blamed their tongues — telling them, as if in jest but in dead earnest, that from the moment they heard the marriage contract read out, they should have understood it as the deed that made them servants; they should keep their station in mind, and not set themselves up against their lords. The women were amazed — knowing what a violent temper she lived with — that it had never been heard, and no mark had ever shown, that Patricius struck his wife, or that a single day's domestic quarrel had come between them; and when they asked her privately why, she taught them her rule, the one I have just given. Those who took it up proved it, and thanked her. Those who did not stayed under the yoke, and went on suffering.

Her mother-in-law, too, was set against her at first by the whisperings of malicious servants. But she won her over by sheer attentiveness, by unbroken patience and mildness, until the older woman, of her own accord, went to her son to expose the meddling tongues that had been poisoning the household peace between herself and her daughter-in-law, and demanded punishment. So, after he — obeying his mother, and looking to the good order of the house — had whipped the servants who had been exposed, just as she who exposed them wished, she promised the same reward to anyone else who tried to please her by speaking ill of her daughter-in-law. No one dared; and from then on they lived together in a sweetness of goodwill that people remembered.

This great gift too you gave, my God, my mercy, to that good servant of yours in whose womb you created me: that wherever she could, between any souls at odds and in discord, she showed herself such a peacemaker that, hearing from each side the bitterest things about the other — the kind of thing that swollen, undigested discord belches up, when raw hatred hisses itself out in sour talk to a present friend about an absent enemy — she would never pass on anything from one to the other except what would work toward their reconciliation. That might seem to me a small virtue, if I did not know, to my grief, the numberless crowds of people who — through some horrible, far-spreading contagion of sin — not only report the words of angry enemies to angry enemies, but pad them with words never spoken; whereas to an ordinarily humane person it ought to be a small thing not to feed and fan human hatreds by speaking evil, unless he is also working to put them out by speaking good. Such a woman she was — and her teacher was you, the innermost instructor, in the school of her heart.

Finally she won her own husband for you, at the very end of his earthly life; and once he believed, she never again had to mourn in him what she had endured before he believed. She was also the servant of your servants. Any of them who knew her found in her much reason to praise you, and honor you, and love you; for they could feel your presence in her heart, and the fruit of her holy way of living testified to it. She had been the wife of one husband; she had repaid the debt she owed her parents; she had governed her house devoutly; her good works spoke for her. She had brought up children, going into labor for them all over again every time she saw them straying from you. Last of all, Lord — since by your gift you allow us to speak as your servants — for all of us who, before she fell asleep in you, were living together as companions in the grace of your baptism, she cared as though every one of us were her child, and served us as though every one of us were her parent.

The day was now approaching when she would depart this life — a day you knew, though we did not. It came about, I believe by your hidden arrangement, that she and I stood alone, leaning at a window that looked onto the garden inside the house where we were staying, at Ostia on the Tiber. There, away from the crowds, after the strain of a long journey, we were gathering strength for the sea voyage. So we were talking, just the two of us, and it was very sweet; and forgetting what lay behind, straining toward what lay ahead, we asked each other, in the presence of the Truth, which is you, what the eternal life of the saints will be like — the life eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it risen into the heart of man. But with the mouth of the heart we strained wide open toward the streams that fall from your fountain on high, the fountain of life which is with you — hoping to be sprinkled from it, each to our capacity, and somehow to think about a thing so great.

And our talk arrived at this point: that the delight of the body's senses, however great, in however bright a bodily light, seemed unworthy — beside the joy of that life — not just of comparison, but even of mention. Then, rising with a more burning love toward the Selfsame, we moved step by step up through all bodily things, up to heaven itself, from which sun and moon and stars shine down on the earth. And we climbed higher still, by inward thought and speech and wonder at your works; and we came into our own minds, and passed beyond them, to reach that region of unfailing plenty where you feed Israel forever on the food of truth — where life is that Wisdom through whom all these things are made, everything that has been and everything that will be. And she herself is not made: she is as she was, and so she will be always. Or rather: "has been" and "will be" are not in her at all — only "is," because she is eternal, and "has been" and "will be" are not eternal. And while we talked, and panted for her, we touched her — just barely, with one full beat of the heart. And we sighed, and left the first fruits of the spirit bound there, and came back down to the noise of our own mouths, where a word has a beginning and an ending. And what is like your Word, our Lord — who remains in himself without growing old, and makes all things new?

So we said: if for anyone the tumult of the flesh fell silent; silent every image of earth and water and air; silent the wheeling sky; silent the very soul to itself, passing beyond itself by not thinking of itself; silent all dreams and imagined revelations, every tongue, every sign, everything that exists only in passing — if all of it, for that person, fell completely silent (since, if you can hear them, all these things are saying one thing: We did not make ourselves; he made us, who abides forever) — if, having said that, they too fell quiet, having pricked our ear toward their Maker, and he alone spoke: not through them, but by himself, so that we heard his word not through any tongue of flesh, not through the voice of an angel, not through thunder out of a cloud, not through the dark riddle of a likeness — but heard him, himself, whom we love in all these things; himself, without them — the way the two of us, just now, reached out and in one racing thought touched the eternal Wisdom that abides over all things — if that could last, and every other vision of a far lesser kind be withdrawn, and this one seize its beholder, and swallow him, and fold him into inward joys, so that eternal life were exactly like that one moment of understanding we had just sighed for — would that not be: Enter into the joy of your Lord? And when will it come? When we all rise again — though we shall not all be changed?

I was saying something like this — even if not in just this way, and not in these words. But you know, Lord, that on that day, while we talked, this world with all its delights grew cheap to us in the middle of our own speaking; and my mother said: "Son, as for me, nothing in this life delights me anymore. What I am still doing here, and why I am here, I do not know. My hope in this world is spent. There was one thing I wanted to stay alive a little longer for: to see you a Catholic Christian before I died. My God has given me that, and given more than that — I see you now his servant, with earthly happiness thrown aside. What am I doing here?"

What I answered her, I do not remember. Hardly five days later, or not much more, she went down with a fever. One day, in the middle of her illness, she fainted, and for a little while was drawn away from everything around her. We came running. She was quickly conscious again, and looked at me and my brother standing over her, and said, like someone searching for something, "Where was I?" Then, watching us struck dumb with grief, she said: "Here you will bury your mother." I stayed silent and fought down my tears. But my brother said something about hoping she would die not in a foreign country but in her own land — the happier lot. When she heard it her face turned anxious, and her eyes rebuked him for still having a taste for such things; then she looked at me: "Listen to what he says," she said. And soon, to us both: "Lay this body anywhere. Don't let the care of it trouble you in the least. I ask only this: that you remember me at the altar of the Lord, wherever you are." When she had gotten this thought out in what words she could, she fell silent, and the sickness, worsening, worked at her.

But I, thinking of your gifts, unseen God — the gifts you plant in the hearts of your faithful, out of which such marvelous fruit springs up — I rejoiced and gave you thanks. For I knew, I remembered, how she had always burned with anxiety about her burial place, which she had provided and prepared for herself beside her husband's body. Because the two of them had lived in such harmony, she had wanted — so little can the human mind grasp things divine — one more happiness added to that one, and remembered among men: that after her pilgrimage across the sea, it be granted her that the earthly remains of husband and wife lie joined under the same earth. When this empty wish had begun, through the fullness of your goodness, to leave her heart, I did not know; and I rejoiced, amazed, that she had shown me it was gone — though indeed in our talk at the window, when she said, "What am I still doing here?" there had been no sign of any desire to die in her own country. I heard later, too, that while we were at Ostia, one day when I was elsewhere, she had been talking with a mother's frankness to some of my friends about the contempt of this life and the good that death is; and when they stood amazed at such courage in a woman — courage you had given her — and asked whether she was not afraid to leave her body so far from her own city, she said: "Nothing is far from God. There is no fear that at the end of the world he won't know the place from which to raise me up." And so, on the ninth day of her illness, in the fifty-sixth year of her life and the thirty-third of mine, that devout and holy soul was released from the body.

I closed her eyes. And an enormous sorrow came flooding into my heart and was about to overflow into tears — and at the same moment my eyes, under the mind's violent command, drank the spring back down until it was dry; and that struggle went very hard with me. Then, at the instant she breathed her last, the boy Adeodatus burst out wailing; we all hushed him, and he went quiet. In the same way something childish in me, too, that was sliding toward sobs, was hushed by the heart's young voice, and went quiet. For we did not think it right to mark that death with wet laments and groaning — that is how people usually mourn a death they think of as misery, or as total extinction. But she was not dying in misery, and she was not wholly dying. Of that we were certain — from the evidence of how she had lived, from her faith that was no pretense, from reasons that were sure.

Then what was it that ached so heavily inside me? It was the fresh wound, torn open by the sudden breaking of that sweetest, dearest habit — the habit of living together. I treasured her testimony: how in that last illness she folded her tenderness into my acts of care and called me her devoted son, remembering with great love that she had never once heard from my mouth a hard or contemptuous word thrown at her. And yet, my God, who made us — what possible comparison is there between the honor I paid her and the slavery she served for me? So, because I was left stripped of so great a comfort, my soul was wounded, and my life torn apart — the life that had been made one life out of hers and mine.

When the boy had been quieted, Euodius picked up the psalter and began to sing — the whole household answering him — the psalm: Of mercy and judgment I will sing to you, Lord. And hearing what was happening, many brothers and devout women gathered; and while those whose duty it was prepared the burial in the usual way, I withdrew to a part of the house where I decently could, with those who thought I should not be left alone, and talked about something suited to the hour. With that balm of truth I eased a torment known to you — they did not know it; they listened intently, and supposed I felt no grief at all. But in your ears, where none of them could hear, I was rebuking the softness of my own feeling and holding back the flood of sorrow. It gave way to me a little; then it came on again in its own surge — never as far as tears, never as far as a change of face — but I knew what I was pressing down in my heart. And because it upset me deeply that these human things had such power over me — things which, in the due order of nature and the lot we are dealt, must come to pass — I grieved over my grief with another grief, and was worn down by a double sorrow.

And then the body was carried out. We went; we came back; without tears. Even during the prayers we poured out to you while the sacrifice of our redemption was offered for her — the body resting beside the grave before being laid in, as the custom there is — even during those prayers I did not weep. But all that day I was heavy with a secret sadness, and with a mind in turmoil I begged you, as well as I could, to heal my pain. You did not. I think you were fixing in my memory, by this one lesson, how strong the chain of habit is, even on a mind that no longer feeds on lying words. It also seemed good to me to go and bathe, because I had heard that the bath got its name — balneum, from the Greek balaneion — because it drives anxiety out of the mind. And this too I confess to your mercy, Father of the fatherless: I bathed, and I was exactly the man I had been before I bathed. The bitterness of grief did not sweat out of my heart. Then I slept; and I woke; and I found my sorrow softened, more than a little. And lying alone in my bed, I remembered the true verses of your Ambrose. For you are —

"God, creator of all things, ruler of the sky: you clothe the day in beautiful light, the night in the grace of sleep, so that rest may hand our slackened limbs back to the use of work, and lift up minds when they are tired, and loosen the knots of anxious grief."

And then, little by little, I began to recover my old sense of your handmaid: her devout walk with you; her holy gentleness and patience with us — all of it suddenly gone from me. And I wanted to weep in your sight, about her and for her, about myself and for myself. And I let go of the tears I had been holding in — let them flow as freely as they wanted, spreading them like a bed under my heart. And my heart rested on them, because the ears there were yours — not the ears of some man who would have read my weeping with contempt. And now, Lord, I confess it to you in writing. Let anyone read it who wants to, and make of it what he will; and if he finds it a sin that I wept for my mother for a small fraction of an hour — the mother who was, for that hour, dead to my eyes, and who for so many years had wept for me, that I might live in yours — let him not sneer. Rather, if his charity is large, let him weep for my sins himself, to you: the Father of all the brothers of your Christ.

But now, with a heart healed of that wound — in which there was perhaps too much of the flesh's affection to be blameless — I pour out to you, our God, on behalf of that servant of yours, a very different kind of tears: tears that flow from a spirit shaken by the thought of the danger facing every soul that dies in Adam. She had been made alive in Christ even before she was released from the flesh; she lived so that your name is praised in her faith and in her conduct. And yet I do not dare to say that from the moment you gave her rebirth in baptism, no word ever left her mouth that crossed your commandment. Your Son, the Truth, has said: whoever calls his brother a fool will answer for it in the fire of hell. And woe even to the most praiseworthy human life, if you set mercy aside and examined it! But because you do not hunt our faults down ferociously, we hope with confidence for some place with you. And anyone who lists his real merits before you — what is he listing but your gifts? If only human beings would know themselves to be human — and let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord.

So I — my Praise and my Life, God of my heart — setting aside for this moment her good deeds, for which I give you thanks with joy, I now plead with you for my mother's sins. Hear me, I beg, through him who is the Medicine of our wounds, who hung on the tree, and who now, seated at your right hand, intercedes with you for us. I know she acted mercifully, and from her heart forgave her debtors their debts. Forgive her too her debts — whatever she contracted in all those years after the water of salvation. Forgive her, Lord; forgive, I beg you; do not enter into judgment with her. Let mercy triumph over judgment — for your words are true, and you have promised mercy to the merciful. That they are merciful at all was your gift: you, who will have mercy on whom you have mercy, and will show compassion to whom you have shown compassion.

And I believe you have already done what I am asking — but accept, Lord, this willing offering of my mouth. As the day of her release drew near, she gave no thought to having her body wrapped in splendor or embalmed in spices; she craved no choice monument, and she no longer cared for a tomb in her own land. None of that did she charge us with. She wanted only this: to be remembered at your altar, which she had served without missing a single day — the altar from which she knew that holy Victim is dispensed by whom the writ of debt that stood against us was wiped out; by whom the enemy was triumphed over — the enemy who tallies our offenses, and hunts for something to charge us with, and finds nothing in him in whom we conquer. Who will pour back to him his innocent blood? Who will refund him the price he paid to buy us, and so take us away from him? To the sacrament of that price of ours, your handmaid bound her soul with the bond of faith. Let no one tear her from your protection. Let neither the lion nor the dragon put himself in her way, by force or by fraud. For she will not answer that she owes nothing — she would be convicted and seized by that cunning prosecutor. She will answer that her debts were forgiven by him to whom no one can repay the price that he, owing nothing, paid for us.

Let her rest, then, in peace, with the husband before whom and after whom she had no other; whom she served, bringing forth fruit for you with patience, so that she might win him too for you. And inspire, Lord my God — inspire your servants my brothers, your sons my masters, whom I serve with voice and heart and pen — that as many as read these confessions may remember at your altar Monica, your servant, together with Patricius, once her husband, through whose flesh you brought me into this life, how, I do not know. Let them remember with devout affection my parents in this passing light, and my brothers and sisters under you, our Father, within our Catholic mother; and my fellow citizens in the eternal Jerusalem, which your pilgrim people sighs for from its going out until its coming home — so that my mother's last request of me may be granted her more richly through the prayers of many, called up by these confessions, than through my prayers alone.
