# Confessions

Saint Augustine

- 397–400 CE
- Western tradition
- Religion
- Lectio Edition 2026

Augustine tells God the story of his life and lets the reader overhear. A restless youth, a long struggle of mind and will, a conversion in a garden. Then he turns to memory, to time, and to the first verses of Genesis. Thirteen books, written in Latin about the year 400.

## A prayer in "Thou" and "hast" keeps the reader outside it.

E. B. Pusey's translation of 1838 is reverent and exact, and every sentence is in the English of the King James Bible. "Too late loved I Thee, O Thou Beauty of ancient days." A modern reader hears church, not a man.

Augustine's Latin is direct, quick and often funny. He prays for chastity, but not yet. He says he stole pears for no reason at all.

The Lectio Edition gives all thirteen books in current English, with "you" for God, and keeps the cries and the wordplay. Pusey stays in the app beside it.

## How it begins.

> **Book I · Infancy and Boyhood**

> Great are you, Lord, and greatly to be praised: great is your power, and your wisdom is beyond all measure. And man wants to praise you — man, one small piece of your creation; man, hauling his mortality around with him, hauling the evidence of his sin and the evidence that you resist the proud. Yet still he wants to praise you, this man, one small piece of your creation. You stir us up to delight in praising you, because you made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you. Grant me, Lord, to know and understand which comes first: calling on you, or praising you? And which first — knowing you, or calling on you? But who calls on you without knowing you? Not knowing you, he could call on something else instead of you. Or is it rather that you are called on in order to be known? Yet how will they call on one they have not believed in? And how will they believe without someone to preach him? And those who seek the Lord will praise him — for those who seek find him, and those who find him will praise him. Let me seek you, Lord, by calling on you; let me call on you by believing in you — for you have been preached to us.

The first 227 words of the Lectio Edition, as they stand in the bundle the app reads.

## What this edition decided.

The review set these rules for the whole work.

- *Distentio*, Augustine's word for what time is, is "stretching-out" every time. Pusey splits it into two unrelated words and the thread is lost.
- *Continentia* is chosen by its sense. For sexual restraint it is "continence", so that the famous prayer matches the figure of Continence who appears in the garden. For the general virtue it is "self-mastery".
- A cry is translated as a cry. *Ei mihi* is "Woe is me". It is never dropped or turned into a description.
- Quoted verse keeps its lines.
- One spelling, Manichaean, and one style of quotation mark across the thirteen books.
- Single words in the wrong register were fixed one at a time.

### Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 39 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 8. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

### *inquietum est cor nostrum, donec requiescat in te*

Book I

**Older:** "our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (Pusey)

**Lectio:** you made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you

The Latin ties restless to rest in one breath. "Repose" breaks the echo.

### *ut essem gratis malus*

Book II

**Older:** "that I should be gratuitously evil" (Pusey)

**Lectio:** that I should be evil for free

*Gratis* is a word from the market: no payment. Augustine's point about the pears is that there was nothing to gain. "Gratuitously" has drifted toward "excessively".

### *tolle lege, tolle lege*

Book VIII

**Older:** "Take up and read; Take up and read." (Pusey)

**Lectio:** Take it and read. Take it and read.

A child's chant over a garden wall, four syllables. It has to be something a child would sing.

### *da mihi castitatem et continentiam, sed noli modo*

Book VIII

**Older:** "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet." (Pusey)

**Lectio:** Give me chastity and continence

The line ends "but not yet". The draft had "self-control". The review restored "continence", the word the line is known by, which also ties it to the scene that follows.

### *sero te amavi*

Book X, chapter 27

**Older:** "Too late loved I Thee, O Thou Beauty of ancient days, yet ever new!" (Pusey)

**Lectio:** Late have I loved you, Beauty so old and so new

*Sero* is "late", not "too late". It is grief at the delay, spoken after arriving. Pusey turns it into a verdict.

### *da quod iubes et iube quod vis*

Book X, chapter 29

**Older:** "Give what Thou enjoinest, and enjoin what Thou wilt." (Pusey)

**Lectio:** Give what you command, and command what you will.

The sentence that provoked Pelagius. God must supply the obedience he demands. "Enjoin" makes it polite.

### *quid est ergo tempus?*

Book XI

**Older:** "If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not." (Pusey)

**Lectio:** What then is time? If no one asks me, I know

Pusey is nearly plain here already. The edition only removes "asketh" and "I know not".

### *distentio*

Book XI

**Older:** "protraction", and later "distraction" (Pusey)

**Lectio:** a stretching-out

One coined term for what time is, used again at the book's close. One English word each time lets the reader follow it.
