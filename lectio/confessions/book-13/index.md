Saint Augustine · Lectio Edition · 2026

# Confessions, Book XIII

The Sabbath Without Evening

I call on you, my God, my mercy, who made me, and who did not forget me when I forgot you. I call you into my soul, which you are making ready to receive you by the very longing you breathe into it. Do not desert me now that I am calling — you who came ahead of my calling, who pressed me over and over with calls of every kind, so that I would hear you from far off, and turn, and call back to you who were calling me. For you, Lord, wiped out everything I had earned by my evil, not paying back into these hands the wages of the deeds by which I fell away from you; and you came ahead of everything I could earn by my good, to repay the work of your own hands, the hands that made me. Before I was, you were; I was nothing — nothing that could be owed existence. And yet here I am, out of your goodness, which came before all this that you have made me and all this you have made me from. You did not need me. I am not some good that is useful to you, my Lord and my God. It is not that serving you would spare you weariness in your working, or that your power would be less without my service; not that I tend you the way a man tends a field, as if you would go uncultivated unless I cultivated you. No — I serve you and worship you so that it may be well with me, from you: from you, from whom I have even this, that I am a being with whom it can be well.

It is from the fullness of your goodness that your creation exists at all — so that a good which could do you no good, and which was not of your own substance, as though it were your equal, might still exist, since it could be made by you. What claim did heaven and earth, which you made in the beginning, ever have on you? Let the spiritual and bodily natures you made in your Wisdom say what they did to deserve you. On you they depended even in their raw and formless state, spiritual or bodily, each ready to slide off into disorder and into a far unlikeness to you — the spiritual, though formless, higher than any formed body; the bodily, though formless, better than sheer nothing. And formless they would have hung upon your Word forever, had not that same Word called them back to your oneness and given them form, so that from you, the one supreme Good, they were all made very good. What did they do to deserve even formlessness, when they would not have been even that, except from you?

What did bodily matter do to deserve even its invisible, formless state? It was not even that, except that you made it; and since it did not exist, it could not deserve to be made. And what did the raw beginning of the spiritual creation do to deserve even its dark drifting, like the deep — so unlike you? It was the Word that turned it toward the Word that made it; the Word lit it and made it light — not the Light's equal, but formed to the likeness of the Form that is equal to you. For a body, to exist is not the same as to be beautiful; otherwise nothing could be ugly. In the same way, for a created spirit, to live is not the same as to live wisely; otherwise its wisdom could never be lost. Its good is to hold fast to you, always — otherwise the light it gained by turning toward you it loses by turning away, and it slides back into the life that is like the dark deep. We ourselves, who in our souls are spiritual creation, turned away from you, our light; in that life we were once darkness, and we labor still among the remnants of our darkness, until in your Only Son we become your righteousness, like the mountains of God. For we were once your judgments, which are like the great deep.

What you said at the beginning of creation — Let there be light, and there was light — I take, not unreasonably, to mean the spiritual creation, because there was already a kind of life there for you to light. But just as it had no claim on you to be the sort of life that could be lit, so, once it existed, it had no claim on you to be lit in fact. Its formlessness could not have pleased you unless it became light — and it became light not by merely existing, but by turning to face the Light that lights it, and holding fast. That it lives at all, and lives in happiness, it owes to nothing but your grace: it was turned, by a change for the better, toward that which can never change for better or worse — toward you; for you alone simply are, and for you, to live is the same as to live in blessedness, because you are your own blessedness.

And what would have been missing from your good — the good that you are to yourself — even if none of this had ever existed, or had stayed formless? You made it not out of any need but out of the overflow of your goodness, reining it in and turning it toward form — not as though your joy were incomplete without it. Your perfection is displeased by imperfection, and so these things were perfected by you, and please you — not as though you were imperfect and needed their perfection to complete your own. For your good Spirit was borne over the waters; the waters did not bear him up, as if he rested his weight on them. When your Spirit is said to rest on people, it is he who makes them rest in him. Your incorruptible and unchangeable will, sufficient in itself and to itself, was borne over the life you had made — the life for which living is not the same as living happily, because it goes on living even while it drifts in its own darkness. What remains for it is to turn to the one who made it, and to live nearer and nearer to the fountain of life; to see light in his light, and be perfected, and lit, and made beautiful.

And look — the Trinity appears to me, in a riddle, in a glass darkly; and the Trinity is you, my God. You, Father, created heaven and earth in the Beginning of our wisdom — in your Wisdom, born of you, equal to you and coeternal with you: that is, in your Son. We have said much already about the heaven of heavens, and the earth invisible and formless, and the dark deep — dark with the drifting instability of spiritual formlessness, until it turned to him from whom it had whatever life it had, and in his light became a life of beauty: the heaven of that heaven which was afterward set between water and water. In the name God, I now recognized the Father, who made these things; in the name Beginning, the Son, in whom he made them; and believing my God to be Trinity, I searched further in his holy words — and there it was: your Spirit was moving over the waters. There is the Trinity, my God: Father and Son and Holy Spirit, creator of all creation.

But why, O Light who speak the truth? To you I lift up my heart — do not let it teach me empty things; scatter its darkness and tell me — I beg you by charity, our mother, I beg you, tell me — why does your Scripture wait until it has named heaven, and the invisible formless earth, and darkness over the deep, and only then name your Spirit? Was it because he had to be introduced as borne above — and that could not be said until something had been named that he could be understood to be borne above? He was not borne above the Father, nor above the Son; and he could not rightly be said to be borne above if he were borne above nothing. First, then, the thing he was borne above had to be named; then he, who had to be named in no other way than as borne above it. But why was it fitting that he be introduced in no other way than as borne above?

From here, let whoever can follow your apostle with understanding, when he says that your love is poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us; and when, teaching about the gifts of the Spirit, he shows us a way that surpasses them all — the way of love; and when he bends his knee to you on our behalf, that we may know the knowledge of the love of Christ, which surpasses knowledge. From the beginning, that surpassing love was borne above the waters. To whom can I say this, and how? How do I speak of the weight of evil desire, dragging down into the sheer drop of the abyss — and of the love that lifts us back up through your Spirit, who was borne above the waters? To whom do I say it? How do I say it? It is not space we sink into and climb out of. What could be more like it — and what less? They are affections; they are loves: the uncleanness of our own spirit, flowing downward under its load of anxious cares, and the holiness of yours, lifting us upward in love of the peace that is past all worry — so that we lift our hearts to you, where your Spirit is borne above the waters, and come at last to the rest that surpasses everything, when our soul has passed through the waters that give no footing.

The angels fell; the human soul fell; and their falling showed how deep the abyss goes — the dark abyss that stood waiting for the whole spiritual creation, if you had not said at the beginning, Let there be light — and there was light, and every obedient intelligence of your heavenly city held fast to you, and rested in your Spirit, who is borne unchangeably above everything that changes. Otherwise even the heaven of heavens would have been, in itself, a dark deep; but now it is light in the Lord. Even in the miserable restlessness of the spirits who fell, who showed their darkness the moment your light no longer clothed them, you make it plain how noble you made your rational creation: nothing less than you will ever be enough to give it rest and happiness — not even itself. For it is you, our God, who will light our darkness; from you comes our garment of light, and then our darkness will be like noon. Give me yourself, my God — give yourself back to me. Look: I love you; and if that is too little, let me love more strongly. I cannot measure my love, to know how far it still falls short of enough — enough for my life to run into your embrace and not turn away again until it is hidden in the hiding place of your face. This much I know: apart from you it goes badly with me — not only around me but inside me; and every abundance that is not my God is, to me, poverty.

But were not the Father and the Son also borne above the waters? If we mean above in space, the way a body is, then not even the Spirit was. But if we mean the unchangeable supremacy of God above everything changeable, then Father, Son, and Holy Spirit were all borne above the waters. Why, then, is it said only of your Spirit? Why only of him — as if he were in a place, who is in no place — of whom alone it is written that he is your Gift? In your Gift we rest; there we enjoy you. Our rest is our place. Love lifts us there, and your good Spirit raises our lowliness up from the gates of death. In your good will is our peace. A body strains by its own weight toward its own place. Weight does not pull only downward; it pulls toward a thing's own place. Fire strains upward, a stone downward: driven by their own weights, they seek their own places. Oil poured in under water rises up above the water; water poured on top of oil sinks down beneath the oil: driven by their own weights, they seek their own places. Out of their place, things are restless; set in their place, they rest. My weight is my love; wherever I am carried, it is love that carries me. By your Gift we catch fire, and we are carried upward; we burn inwardly, and we go. We climb the ascents that are in the heart, and we sing the song of ascents. With your fire — your good fire — we burn, and we go: for we are going up, to the peace of Jerusalem, for I was glad when they said to me, Let us go up to the house of the Lord. There a good will shall set us down, and we shall want nothing else, forever, but to stay there.

Happy the creature that has never known anything else — though it might itself have been something else, had it not been lifted, the moment it was made, with no interval at all, by your Gift, who is borne above everything changeable: lifted by the call in which you said, Let there be light — and there was light. In us the two things happen at separated times: we were darkness, and we are being made light. But of that creature the text says only what it would have been if it had not been lit; and it speaks as though the creature had first been fluid and dark, so that the cause of its being otherwise would stand out — the cause being that it was turned to the unfailing Light, and became light. Whoever can grasp this, let him grasp it; let him ask it of you. Why trouble me about it — as if I were the one who lightens every man who comes into this world?

Who comprehends the almighty Trinity? And yet who does not talk about it — if it really is the Trinity he is talking about? Rare is the soul that knows what it is speaking of when it speaks of that. Men argue and fight about it; and no one sees that vision without peace. I wish they would consider three things inside themselves — three things far other than the Trinity, but worth naming as a place where they can test themselves and feel how far away they are. I mean these three: being, knowing, willing. For I am, and I know, and I will: I am a being that knows and wills; I know that I am and that I will; and I will to be and to know. Let anyone who can see how inseparable a life is in these three — one life, one mind, one essence — and yet how inseparable a distinction: distinction, and yet inseparable. A man has it right in front of him: let him look into himself, and see, and tell me. But when he has found something in these three and said what he can, let him not think he has found the Unchangeable that is above them — that which is, unchangeably; and knows, unchangeably; and wills, unchangeably. Whether God is Trinity because of these three; or whether all three are in each Person, so that each possesses all; or both at once — wondrously simple and wondrously manifold, its own boundary within itself and yet boundless, by which it is, and is known to itself, and suffices to itself, unchangeably the selfsame in the overflowing greatness of its unity — who can easily conceive it? Who can find any words for it at all? Who would dare, in any way, to pronounce on it rashly?

Go forward in your confession, my faith; say to the Lord your God: Holy, holy, holy, Lord my God — in your name we were baptized, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit; in your name we baptize, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. For among us too, in his Christ, God made a heaven and an earth: the spiritual and the fleshly members of his Church. And our earth, before it received the form that teaching gives, was invisible and unformed, and we were covered by the darkness of ignorance — for you disciplined man for his sin, and your judgments were like the great deep over him. But because your Spirit was borne above the waters, your mercy did not abandon our misery, and you said: Let there be light. Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is near. Repent: let there be light. And because our soul was troubled within us, we remembered you, Lord, from the land of Jordan, and from the mountain that is high as you are, and became small for our sake; our darkness disgusted us, and we turned to you — and there was light. And look: we were once darkness, and now we are light in the Lord.

But still by faith, not yet by sight; for by hope we are saved, and hope that is seen is not hope. Still deep calls to deep — but now in the voice of your waterfalls. Still even the one who said, I could not speak to you as spiritual people, but as people of flesh — even he does not consider that he has taken hold of it: he forgets what is behind and strains toward what is ahead; he groans under the weight; his soul thirsts for the living God as the deer thirsts for the springs of water, and he says, When shall I come? He longs to be clothed with his dwelling from heaven; and he calls down to the deep below: Do not be shaped by this world — be transformed by the renewing of your mind. And: Do not be children in your thinking; in malice be infants, so that in thinking you may be grown. And: You foolish Galatians — who has bewitched you? But by now it is not his own voice. It is yours — you who sent your Spirit down from on high, through him who ascended on high and threw open the floodgates of his gifts, so that the rushing of the river might gladden your city. For him the friend of the Bridegroom sighs — he who has the firstfruits of the Spirit already, laid up with him — and still he groans within himself, waiting for the adoption, the redemption of his body. For him he sighs, being a member of the Bride; for him he is jealous, being the friend of the Bridegroom — jealous for him, not for himself. For it is in the voice of your waterfalls, not in his own voice, that he calls to that other deep — the deep he trembles for in his jealousy, afraid that just as the serpent seduced Eve with its cunning, so their minds too might be corrupted away from the purity that is in our Bridegroom, your only Son. And what a light of beauty that will be, when we shall see him as he is — when these tears are gone that have been my bread day and night, while every day they say to me: Where is your God?

Look — I too say it: My God, where are you? There you are — there. In you I catch my breath a little, when I pour out my soul beyond myself in the voice of joy and praise, the sound of a crowd keeping festival. And still the soul turns sad again, because it slips back and becomes a deep — or rather, feels that it is a deep still. My faith speaks to it, the faith you kindled to light my feet in the night: Why are you sad, my soul, and why do you trouble me? Hope in the Lord; his word is a lamp to your feet. Hope, and hold on, until the night passes — the mother of the wicked; until the anger of the Lord passes. We were children of that anger once; we were darkness once, and we carry its remnants around in this body, dead because of sin — until the day breathes, and the shadows slip away. Hope in the Lord. In the morning I will stand in your presence and keep watch; I will confess to you forever. In the morning I will stand in your presence, and I will see the saving health of my face — my God, who will bring even our mortal bodies to life through the Spirit who lives in us, because in mercy he has been borne above the dark drifting deep inside us. From him, on this pilgrimage, we have received the pledge that we are already light. Still we are saved by hope; but we are children of light and children of the day, not children of night and darkness — though we were that, once. Between them and us, in this half-light of human knowledge, only you can divide — you who test our hearts, and call the light day and the darkness night. Who can tell us apart, if not you? And what do we have that we did not receive from you — we, vessels made for honor from the same lump out of which others are made for dishonor?

And who but you, our God, made over us a firmament of authority — your divine Scripture? For the heaven will be folded up like a scroll; but now it is stretched over us like a skin. Your divine Scripture carries an authority all the higher now that the mortal men through whom you dispensed it to us have died their mortal deaths. And you know, Lord, you know how you clothed human beings in skins when by sin they became mortal. So you stretched out the firmament of your book like a skin — your words, so deeply in harmony with one another, which you spread over us through the ministry of mortal men. By their very deaths, the solid firmament of authority in the words you spoke through them was raised higher, stretched above everything beneath it — higher than it was ever raised while they still lived here. You had not yet stretched out the heaven like a skin; you had not yet spread the fame of their deaths to every side.

Let us look, Lord, at the heavens, the work of your fingers. Clear from our eyes the fog you have spread beneath them. There stands your testimony, which gives wisdom to little ones. My God, perfect your praise out of the mouths of babies and nursing infants. We know no other books that so tear down pride — that so tear down the enemy, the self-defender who resists reconciliation with you by defending his own sins. I know no other words so pure, Lord — none that so persuade me to confess, that so gentle my neck to your yoke and invite me to serve you for nothing. Let me understand them, good Father. Grant that to me, who am placed beneath them; for it was for those beneath them that you made them firm.

There are other waters above this firmament — immortal, I believe, and kept clear of earthly decay. Let them praise your name — the peoples above the heavens, your angels, who have no need to look up at this firmament, no need of reading to know your Word. They behold your face always, and there they read, without syllables sounding in time, what your eternal will wills. They read it; they choose it; they love it. They are reading always, and what they read never passes away; for by choosing and by loving they read the very unchangeableness of your counsel. Their book is never closed; their scroll is never rolled up — for you yourself are their book, and are so eternally. You ranked them above this firmament, which you made firm over the weakness of the peoples below, so that we might look up and learn your mercy there — your mercy, announcing in time the one who made time. For your mercy, Lord, is in the heavens, and your faithfulness reaches to the clouds. The clouds pass; the heaven remains. The preachers of your word pass out of this life into the next; your Scripture stays stretched out over the peoples to the end of the age. Heaven and earth too will pass away, but your words will not pass away: the scroll will be rolled up, and the grass over which it was stretched will wither, and its glory with it — but your Word remains forever. Now he appears to us not as he is, but in the riddle of clouds and through the mirror of heaven; for we too, though your Son loves us, what we shall be has not yet appeared. He looks at us through the lattice of our flesh; and he spoke tenderly, and set us on fire, and we run after his fragrance. But when he appears, we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is. To see him as he is, Lord — that seeing is ours, but not yet.

For as you are — altogether — only you know; you who are unchangeably, and know unchangeably, and will unchangeably. Your being knows and wills unchangeably; your knowing is and wills unchangeably; your willing is and knows unchangeably. And it does not seem right in your eyes that the unchangeable Light should be known by the lit and changeable creature the way that Light knows itself. So my soul is like dry ground with no water before you: as it cannot light itself from its own resources, it cannot satisfy itself from its own resources either. For with you is the fountain of life — and it is in your light that we shall see light.

And who gathered the embittered into one fellowship? They all share one end: a temporal, earthly happiness. Everything they do, they do for that, however they pitch and toss on a countless variety of cares. Who gathered them, Lord, if not you — you who said: Let the waters be gathered into one place, and let the dry land appear, the land that thirsts for you? For the sea too is yours; you made it, and your hands formed the dry land. It is not the bitterness of wills that is called sea, but the gathering of the waters. You rein in even the evil cravings of souls; you fix the limits of how far the waters may advance, so that their waves break against each other — and so you make a sea of them, by the order of your rule over all things.

But the souls that thirst for you, and appear before you, set apart from the fellowship of the sea by another boundary — these you water from a sweet hidden spring, so that the earth may give its fruit. And it gives it: at the command of its Lord and God, our soul buds with works of mercy, each after its kind, loving our neighbor in the relief of his bodily needs — works that hold seed in themselves, after their likeness, since it is from feeling our own weakness that compassion moves us to help the needy the way we would want to be helped if we stood in the same want. And not only in the easy things, like the green herb bearing seed, but in the strong shelter of real help, like the tree that bears fruit: the good work of rescuing a wronged man from the hand of the powerful, and stretching over him the shade of protection — the solid strength of just judgment.

So, Lord, so — I beg you: let it rise as you make it rise, as you give joy and strength; let truth spring up from the earth, and righteousness look down from heaven, and let there be lights in the firmament. Let us break our bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor under our roof; let us clothe the naked, and not turn away from our own flesh and blood. When these fruits have sprung from the earth, look and see that it is good; and let our light, the light of this brief season, break forth — so that rising from this lower harvest of action to the delights of contemplation, holding fast the Word of Life above us, we may appear like lights in the world, fastened to the firmament of your Scripture. For there you teach us to divide between the things of the mind and the things of the senses as between day and night — or between souls given to the mind and souls given to the senses. Once, before the firmament was made, you alone divided light from darkness, in the secret of your judgment; but now your spiritual children too, set and ranked in that same firmament — now that your grace shines out across the world — give light over the earth, and divide day from night, and stand as signs of the times: for the old things have passed away, and look, all is made new; and our salvation is nearer now than when we first believed; and the night is far gone, and the day is near; and you crown your year with blessing, sending laborers of your goodness into your harvest, where the sowing was other men's labor — and sending them out to another sowing too, whose harvest will come at the end. You grant the prayers of the one who asks; you bless the years of the righteous. But you are the same; and in your years, which do not fail, you build a granary for our years as they pass. By an eternal counsel you bestow the goods of heaven on the earth, each in its proper season. To one is given through the Spirit the word of wisdom — like the greater light, for those who delight in the light of truth seen clear, as at the break of day; to another the word of knowledge through the same Spirit — like the lesser light; to another faith; to another the gift of healing; to another the working of miracles; to another prophecy; to another the discerning of spirits; to another kinds of tongues — and all these like stars. For one and the same Spirit works them all, giving to each his own portion as he wills, and making stars appear openly, for the good of all. But the word of knowledge, which holds all the sacraments in it and varies with its seasons like the moon, and those other gifts, counted off in turn like stars — they fall short of the brightness of wisdom that gladdens the day, and by just that much they stand at the beginning of the night. Yet they are necessary — necessary for those to whom your most prudent servant could not speak as to spiritual people, but as to people of flesh — he who speaks wisdom among the mature. The natural man, like a baby in Christ still drinking milk, until he grows strong enough for solid food and his eye is steady enough to look at the sun — let him not live in a night stripped of all light; let him be content with the light of the moon and the stars. You reason all this with us, our God, all-wise, in your book — your firmament — so that we may learn to see all things in wondering contemplation, though still among signs and seasons and days and years.

But first: wash yourselves; be clean. Put wickedness away from your souls and away from the sight of my eyes — so that the dry land may appear. Learn to do good; give the orphan justice; take up the widow's cause — so that the earth may bring forth the green herb for food, and the tree that bears fruit. And come, let us reason together, says the Lord — so that there may be lights in the firmament of heaven, and they may shine upon the earth. That rich man asked the good Master what he should do to lay hold of eternal life. Let the good Master tell him — the Master he took for a man and nothing more; but he is good because he is God — let him tell him that if he wants to come into life, he must keep the commandments: put away the bitterness of malice and wickedness; do not kill, do not commit adultery, do not steal, do not bear false witness — so that the dry land may appear, and bring forth the honoring of father and mother, and the love of neighbor. All these, he says, I have kept. Then where do all these thorns come from, if the land is fruitful? Go, tear out the tangled scrub of greed: sell what you have, be loaded with fruit by giving to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and follow the Lord, if you want to be perfect — in the company of those among whom he speaks wisdom, he who knows what to assign to the day and what to the night — so that you too may know it, and for you too there may be lights in the firmament of heaven. And that will not happen unless your heart is there; and your heart will not be there unless your treasure is there, as you heard from the good Master. But the barren earth was grieved, and the thorns choked the word.

But you, chosen race, you weak things of the world, who have left everything to follow the Lord: go after him, and put the strong to shame; go after him, you beautiful feet, and shine in the firmament, so that the heavens may declare his glory — dividing between the light of the mature, who are not yet like the angels, and the darkness of the little ones, who are not beyond hope. Shine over all the earth; and let the day, blazing with the sun, speak to day the word of wisdom, and the night, glowing with the moon, show to night the word of knowledge. The moon and the stars shine for the night, but the night does not darken them; they light it, in its measure. For look — as though God were saying, Let there be lights in the firmament of heaven — suddenly there came a sound from heaven, like the rush of a violent wind, and tongues appeared, divided as if of fire, and one settled on each of them. And there were made lights in the firmament of heaven, holding the word of life. Run everywhere, holy fires, beautiful fires! You are the light of the world, and you are not shut under a basket. He to whom you hold fast has been lifted high, and he has lifted you high. Run everywhere; be known to all the nations.

And let the sea too conceive and give birth to your works; let the waters bring forth the creeping creature that has a living soul. For you who divide the precious from the worthless have become the mouth of God, and through you he said: Let the waters bring forth — not the living soul, which the earth will bring forth, but the creeping creature that has a living soul, and the birds that fly over the earth. For your sacraments, O God, carried by the work of your holy ones, have made their way through the middle of the waves of this world's temptations, to steep the nations in your name, in your baptism. And as they went, great wonders were worked, like the great whales; and the voices of your messengers flew above the earth, close under the firmament of your book — for that was set over them as the authority under which they were to fly, wherever they went. For there is no speech, no language, where their voice is not heard; their sound has gone out into all the earth, and their words to the end of the world — because you, Lord, blessed them and multiplied them.

Am I lying? Am I mixing things together, failing to distinguish between the clear knowledge of these realities in the firmament of heaven and the bodily works done in the surging sea, beneath that firmament? There are things whose knowledge is solid and complete, not growing generation by generation — lights, as it were, of wisdom and of knowledge; and yet these same things are worked out bodily in many changing ways, and one thing grows from another, and they multiply under your blessing, O God. You have relieved the weariness of our mortal senses, letting one thing, grasped once by the understanding of the mind, be figured and voiced in many ways through the motions of the body. The waters brought these forth — but in your Word. The needs of peoples estranged from the eternity of your truth brought them forth — but in your Gospel; for it was the waters themselves that cast them up, and the diseased bitterness of those waters was the very reason they issued forth in your Word.

And everything you have made is beautiful — and look, you who made everything are more beautiful than words can say. If Adam had not fallen away from you, the salt brine of the sea would never have poured out of him — the human race: so deeply curious, so stormily swollen, so restlessly rolling up and down. There would then have been no need for your dispensers to work bodily, among many waters, through the senses — their mysterious acts and sayings. For that is what those creeping and flying creatures now suggest to me: people brought in and consecrated by bodily sacraments will get no further unless their soul comes alive at a spiritual depth — unless, after the word of admission, it looks ahead toward completion.

And so, in your Word, it is not the depth of the sea but the earth, set apart from the bitterness of the waters, that brings forth — not the creeping creature with a living soul, but the living soul itself. For that soul no longer needs baptism, as the heathen need it, as it needed it itself when the waters still covered it — there is no other entrance into the kingdom of heaven, since you appointed this one. It no longer demands marvels and miracles to make it believe; it is not the kind that will not believe unless it sees signs and wonders — for the believing earth now stands separated from the waters that are bitter with unbelief, and tongues are a sign not for believers but for unbelievers. Nor does the earth you founded upon the waters have any need of the flying kind that the waters brought forth at your word. Send your word into it by your messengers. We tell of their working, but it is you who work in them, so that in it they may work a living soul. The earth brings it forth, because the earth is the reason they work this in the soul — just as the sea was the reason they worked upon the creeping creatures and the birds that fly under the firmament of heaven. Of these the earth has no more need — though it feeds on the Fish that was drawn from the deep, at the table you have set before the eyes of believers: he was drawn from the deep in order to feed the dry land. And the birds, though the sea bred them, still multiply upon the earth: the first preachings of the evangelists were called forth by man's unbelief, but the faithful too are urged and blessed by them in a thousand ways, day after day. The living soul, though, takes its beginning from the earth: for only those already faithful profit by holding themselves back from the love of this world, so that their soul may live to you — the soul that was dead while it lived in pleasures. Death-dealing pleasures, Lord; for you, Lord, are the life-giving delight of the pure heart.

So let your ministers work now upon the earth — not as they worked on the waters of unbelief, preaching and proclaiming through miracles and sacraments and mystic words, where ignorance, the mother of wonder, is held rapt in awe of hidden signs — for that is how faith's entrance comes to the sons of Adam who have forgotten you, while they hide themselves from your face and become a dark deep. Let your ministers work now as on the dry land, standing clear of the whirlpools of the abyss; let them be a pattern for the faithful, by living in front of them and rousing them to imitation. Then people listen not just to hear, but to do. Seek God, and your soul will live — so that the earth may bring forth the living soul. Do not be shaped by this world; hold yourselves back from it. The soul lives by avoiding the very things it dies by desiring. Hold yourselves back from the savage wildness of pride, the sluggish pleasure of self-indulgence, and the false name of knowledge — so that the wild beasts may be tamed, the cattle broken to the yoke, the serpents made harmless. In the allegory, these are the motions of the mind: the haughtiness of pride, the delight of lust, and the poison of curiosity are the motions of a dead soul. For the soul does not die by losing all motion; it dies by leaving the fountain of life — and then this passing world picks it up, and it is shaped to the world's likeness.

But your word, O God, is the fountain of eternal life, and it does not pass away. So the soul's drifting away is checked by your word, when it says to us, Do not be shaped by this world — so that the earth, at the fountain of life, may bring forth a living soul: a soul that keeps itself whole, by your word carried through your evangelists, by imitating the imitators of your Christ. That is what after its kind means; for a man models himself on his friend. Become as I am, he says, because I too became as you are. So in this living soul there will be good beasts, gentle in what they do — for you have commanded: Carry out your work in meekness, and you will be loved by all men — and good cattle, which are not glutted if they eat and do not go hungry if they do not eat; and good serpents, not dangerous for doing harm, but shrewd for taking care — probing this temporal nature only as far as is needed for eternity to be seen clearly, understood through the things that are made. For these creatures obey reason when they are held back from their deadly advance; then they live, and they are good.

For look, Lord our God, our creator: when our affections have been reined back from the love of the world — the love we were dying of, as we lived badly — and the living soul has begun to live, by living well; when the word you spoke through your apostle has come true in us, Do not be shaped by this world — then follows what you added at once: But be transformed by the renewing of your mind. This is no longer after your kind, as though you copied the neighbor who went just ahead of you, or lived on the authority of some better man. You did not say, Let man be made after his kind, but: Let us make man in our image and likeness — so that we might test for ourselves what your will is. That is why your steward, who begot children through the Gospel and did not want to keep them babies forever, nursed on milk and tended like a wet nurse's charges — that is why he said: Be transformed by the renewing of your mind, so that you may test what the will of God is — what is good, and pleasing, and perfect. So you do not say, Let man be made, but Let us make man; not after his kind, but in our image and likeness. For a man renewed in his mind, who sees your truth and understands it, needs no other man to point the way, copying his own kind; by your direction he tests for himself what your will is — what is good, and pleasing, and perfect. And you teach him, now that he is able, to see the Trinity of the Unity and the Unity of the Trinity. So after the plural, Let us make man, comes the singular, And God made man; and after the plural, in our likeness, the singular, in the image of God. So man is renewed in the knowledge of God, according to the image of his creator; and having become spiritual, he judges all things — all things, that is, that are his to judge — and is himself judged by no one.

Now, he judges all things — this answers to his dominion over the fish of the sea and the birds of the air, over all cattle and wild beasts, over all the earth and every creeping thing that creeps upon it. And he exercises it by the understanding of his mind, through which he perceives the things of the Spirit of God. Without that, man, set in a place of honor, has no understanding; he is ranked with the mindless beasts, and becomes like them. So in your Church, our God, according to the grace you have given it — for we are your workmanship, created for good works — the spiritual are not only those who preside spiritually but also those who are spiritually subject to the ones presiding; for this too is how you made man male and female, in your spiritual grace, where by bodily sex there is neither male nor female, as there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free. Spiritual persons, then, whether they preside or obey, judge spiritually. But they do not judge the spiritual knowledge that shines in the firmament — it is not for them to sit in judgment over so high an authority. Nor do they judge your book itself, even where something in it does not shine clear: we bow our understanding beneath it, and hold it certain that even what is closed to our sight is said rightly and truly. A man, though spiritual and renewed in the knowledge of God after the image of his creator, must be a doer of the law, not its judge. Nor does he judge that division between spiritual and fleshly men which is known to your eyes, our God, and has not yet shown itself to us in works, so that we might know them by their fruits; you, Lord, know them already — you divided them and called them in secret, before the firmament was made. Nor does he, spiritual as he is, judge the restless peoples of this world. What business is it of his to judge those outside, when he cannot know who will come in from there to the sweetness of your grace, and who will stay out in the enduring bitterness of godlessness?

So man, whom you made in your image, did not receive dominion over the lights of heaven, nor over the hidden heaven itself, nor over the day and the night, which you named before any heaven was founded, nor over the gathering of the waters, which is the sea. He received dominion over the fish of the sea and the birds of the air, over all cattle, over all the earth, and over every creeping thing that creeps upon the earth. For he judges — approving what he finds right, disapproving what he finds wrong — whether in the celebration of the sacraments by which those people are initiated whom your mercy searches out through many waters; or in that celebration in which the Fish is set forth — the Fish drawn from the deep, on which the devout earth feeds; or in the significations and soundings of words, subject to the authority of your book, like birds flying under that firmament: interpreting, expounding, discoursing, disputing, blessing you and calling upon you — the signs bursting from the mouth and ringing out, so that the people may answer: Amen. That all these words must be spoken aloud in the body has its cause in the deep of this world and the blindness of the flesh: thoughts cannot be seen, so they must be sounded in the ears. And so, though the flying birds multiply upon the earth, they still draw their beginning from the waters. The spiritual man also judges by approving what he finds right and disapproving what he finds wrong in the works and morals of the faithful — in their almsgiving, like the earth bearing fruit; and he judges the living soul, made living by the taming of its affections, in chastity, in fasting, in holy reflection; and he judges the things perceived through the senses of the body. In all these he is now said to judge — in all of them, he also holds the power to set right.

But what is this — what kind of mystery is this? Look, Lord: you bless human beings so that they may increase and multiply and fill the earth. Are you not hinting that we should notice something? Why did you not also bless the light, which you called day, or the firmament of heaven, or the sun and moon, or the stars, or the earth, or the sea? I might have said that you, our God, who created us in your image, had chosen to keep this blessing for man alone — except that you blessed the fish and the whales in the same way, to increase and multiply and fill the waters of the sea, and the birds, to be multiplied over the earth. And I might have said the blessing belonged to every kind that propagates its own line, if I had found it given to the fruit trees and the plants and the beasts of the earth. But Increase and multiply is not said to the plants or the trees or the beasts or the serpents — though all of these, just like the fish and the birds and mankind, increase by generation and preserve their kind.

What shall I say then, O Truth, my Light? That it was empty — said for nothing? Surely not, Father of piety; far be it from a servant of your word to say that. And if I do not understand what you mean by this phrase, let my betters — those with more understanding than I — make better use of it, each as you have given him to understand. But let my confession too be pleasing in your eyes, when I confess to you, Lord, my belief that you did not speak this way for nothing; and I will not pass over in silence what this passage stirs in me. For it is true, and I do not see what should stop me from reading the figurative sayings of your books this way. I know that what the mind understands in only one way can be given body in many kinds of signs; and that what the mind understands in many ways can be given body in only one kind of sign. Look at the single love of God and of our neighbor: how many sacraments give it body, how many countless languages, and in every language how many countless turns of phrase! So do the offspring of the waters increase and multiply. And look again, whoever you are who read this: Scripture delivers it in one way only, the voice sounds it in one way only — In the beginning God created heaven and earth — and yet is it not understood in many ways? Not by the deceit of error, but by many kinds of true reading. So do the offspring of man increase and multiply.

So if we think of the natures of things themselves, literally and not allegorically, then increase and multiply fits everything that comes from seed. But if we take the words as figurative — and I rather think this is what Scripture intends, since surely it does not confine this blessing, for no reason, to the offspring of water-creatures and of man — then multitudes are everywhere: in the spiritual and the bodily creations, as in heaven and earth; in righteous and unrighteous souls, as in light and darkness; in the holy authors who ministered the Law to us, as in the firmament made firm between the waters and the waters; in the fellowship of peoples still in their bitterness, as in the sea; in the ardor of holy souls, as in the dry land; in the works of mercy of this present life, as in the herbs bearing seed and the trees bearing fruit; in the gifts of the Spirit, set shining for our profit, as in the lights of heaven; in affections formed to temperance, as in the living soul. In all of these we meet multitude, abundance, increase. But something that increases and multiplies in such a way that one thing is said in many ways, and one saying is understood in many ways — this we find only in signs given bodily form, and in things conceived by the mind. Signs bodily uttered are the generations of the waters, made necessary by the depth of the flesh; things conceived by the mind are the generations of men, because of the fruitfulness of reason. So we believe, Lord, that to both these kinds you said: Increase and multiply. In this blessing, I understand you to have granted us the power and the faculty both to say in many ways what we understand in one, and to understand in many ways what we read stated darkly in one. So the waters of the sea are filled — waters moved only by their many meanings; and so the earth too is filled with the offspring of men — the dry land whose thirst shows in its longing, and which reason rules.

I want to say, too, Lord my God, what the next words of your Scripture press on me; and I will say it and not be afraid. For I will speak the truth, with you breathing into me what you willed me to say out of those words; and I believe I can speak the truth by no breath but yours, since you are the Truth, and every man a liar — so whoever speaks a lie speaks from what is his own. Therefore, to speak truth, I will speak from what is yours. Look: you have given us for food every herb bearing seed on the face of all the earth, and every tree that carries in itself the fruit of a tree bearing seed — and not to us alone, but to all the birds of the air, and to the beasts of the earth, and to all creeping things; but not to the fish and the great whales. We were saying that these fruits of the earth signify, in the allegory, the works of mercy that the fruitful earth supplies for the needs of this life. Such an earth was the devout Onesiphorus, to whose household you gave mercy, because he often refreshed your Paul and was not ashamed of his chain. So were the brothers, and such was the fruit they bore, who supplied out of Macedonia what he lacked. But how he grieves over certain trees that did not give him the fruit they owed him, where he says: At my first defense, no one stood by me; everyone deserted me. May it not be counted against them. For these fruits are owed to those who minister the teaching of the Spirit to us out of their understanding of the divine mysteries; owed to them as men. And they are owed to them as the living soul, since they offer themselves as a pattern in every kind of self-mastery. And owed to them again as flying creatures, because of their blessings which are multiplied upon the earth — for their sound has gone out into all lands.

Only those are fed by these fruits who find their joy in them; and those whose god is their belly find no joy in them. For even in the givers, the fruit is not the thing given but the heart in which it is given. So I can see plainly why he rejoiced — the man who served God, not his belly. I see it, and I rejoice with him. He had received from the Philippians what they had sent by Epaphroditus; and still I see why he rejoiced. What he rejoiced over, he fed on; for he says — and he speaks the truth — I rejoiced greatly in the Lord that now at last your care for me has flowered again: you did care, but the caring had grown weary. So the Philippians, under a long weariness, had dried up — withered, as it were, for bearing this fruit of good work; and Paul rejoices for them, that they have flowered again — not for himself, that they met his need. So he goes on: I am not saying this out of want, for I have learned to be content in whatever state I am. I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound. In anything, and in everything, I have been schooled to be full and to be hungry, to abound and to suffer need. I can do all things in him who strengthens me.

What are you rejoicing over, then, great Paul? What is your joy? What are you fed by — you, a man renewed in the knowledge of God according to the image of your creator, a living soul in such command of itself, a tongue like the flying birds, speaking mysteries? For to such living beings this food is owed. What is it that feeds you? Joy. Hear what comes next: Still, you have done well to share in my affliction. That is his joy; that is his food: that they did well — not that his own strain was eased. For he says to you, In my distress, you gave me room; he knew how to abound and how to suffer want, in you who strengthened him. You Philippians know, he says, that in the early days of the Gospel, when I set out from Macedonia, no church shared with me in the account of giving and receiving except you alone; even in Thessalonica you sent, once and then a second time, for my need. Now his joy is that they have come back to these good works: he is glad that they have flowered again, the way a fruitful field is glad when it turns green once more.

Is it for his own needs, then, since he said, you sent for my need? Does he rejoice over that? No — not over that. And how do we know? Because he says at once: Not that I seek the gift; I seek the fruit. From you, my God, I have learned to tell a gift from fruit. The gift is the thing itself, given by the one who shares these necessities: money, food, drink, clothing, shelter, help. The fruit is the good and right will of the giver. For the good Master did not only say, Whoever receives a prophet — he added, in the name of a prophet; and he did not only say, Whoever receives a righteous man — he added, in the name of a righteous man: for so the one will receive a prophet's reward, and the other the reward of a righteous man. And he did not only say, Whoever gives one of my little ones a cup of cold water to drink — he added, in the name of a disciple, and concluded: Truly I tell you, he shall not lose his reward. The gift is receiving the prophet, receiving the righteous man, handing the disciple the cup of cold water; the fruit is doing it in the name of a prophet, in the name of a righteous man, in the name of a disciple. Elijah was fed with fruit by the widow, who knew she was feeding a man of God, and fed him because of that; by the raven he was fed only with a gift. And it was not the inner Elijah who was fed by that, but only the outer man — the man who could have starved for want of such food.

So I will say what is true in your sight, Lord. When unbelievers and people of the flesh — those for whose winning and initiation the sacraments of entry and the great workings of miracles are necessary, the ones I take to be signified by the names fishes and whales — when such people take up the bodily care of your servants, or help them in something useful to this present life, without knowing why it should be done or to what end, they do not really feed your servants, and your servants are not really fed by them. For the one side does not act from a holy and right will, and the other finds no joy in gifts whose fruit it cannot yet see; and the mind is fed by what it rejoices in. That is why the fishes and the whales do not feed on the foods that only the earth brings forth — the earth set apart, at last, from the bitterness of the waves of the sea.

And you, God, saw everything that you had made, and look: it was very good. We too see it all — and look, it is all very good. Kind by kind of your works, when you had said, Let them be, and they were, you saw each one, that it was good. Seven times, by my count, it is written that you saw that what you made was good; and the eighth time, you saw everything that you had made, and look — it was not only good but very good: all of it, now, together. Taken one by one, they were only good; taken all together, they were both good and very good. Every beautiful body says the same: a body whose members are all beautiful is far more beautiful than the members are separately; the whole is completed by their well-ordered joining, though each member is beautiful by itself as well.

And I looked hard at the text, to find whether it was seven times or eight that you saw your works were good, when they pleased you; and in your seeing I found no times at all — nothing to make me understand that you saw what you made so many times over. And I said: Lord, surely this Scripture of yours is true, for you are truthful — you are Truth itself — and you set it forth. Why then do you tell me there are no times in your seeing, while this Scripture of yours tells me that on each day you saw that what you made was good — and when I counted, I found how many times? And you answer me, for you are my God, and you say it to your servant with a strong voice in his inner ear, breaking through my deafness, crying: Man, what my Scripture says, I say. But Scripture speaks in time, and time does not touch my Word, which stands with me, equal, in eternity. What you see through my Spirit, I see — just as what you say through my Spirit, I say. But when you see these things in time, I do not see them in time; and when you say these things in time, I do not say them in time.

And I heard, Lord my God — I licked up a drop of sweetness from your truth — and I understood. There are people who dislike your works. They say you made many of them under the compulsion of necessity — the fabric of the heavens, for instance, and the composition of the stars — and not from what was yours, but from material already created elsewhere, out of some other source, which you merely gathered and fitted and joined together, building the ramparts of the universe out of your conquered enemies, so that, bound down in that structure, they could never rise against you again. And other things, they say, you neither made nor even assembled — all flesh, and all the smallest living creatures, and everything that grips the earth by its roots. These, they say, a mind hostile to you begot and shaped in the lower stories of the world — an alien nature, not created by you and set against you. They are out of their minds who say so, because they do not see your works through your Spirit, and do not recognize you in them.

But those who see these things through your Spirit — it is you who see, in them. When they see that these things are good, you are seeing that they are good; whatever pleases them for your sake — it is you who please them in it; and whatever pleases us through your Spirit pleases you, in us. For who among men knows the things of a man, except the spirit of the man that is in him? In the same way, no one knows the things of God except the Spirit of God. Now we, he says, have received not the spirit of this world but the Spirit who is from God, so that we may know what God has given us. And I am pressed to say: Truly, no one knows the things of God except the Spirit of God; how then do we also know what God has given us? The answer comes back to me: even the things we know through his Spirit, no one knows except the Spirit of God. For just as it was rightly said to those who would speak through the Spirit of God, It is not you who speak, so it is rightly said to those who know through the Spirit of God, It is not you who know — and no less rightly to those who see through the Spirit of God, It is not you who see. Whatever they see through the Spirit of God to be good — it is not they who see that it is good, but God. It is one thing, then, for a man to think that what is good is bad, like the people I named. It is another for a man to see that what is good is good — for your creation pleases many because it is good, and yet you do not please them in it, because they would rather enjoy it than enjoy you. And it is another thing again that, when a man sees that something is good, God in him should be seeing that it is good — so that God is loved in what he made; and he cannot be loved except through the Holy Spirit he has given, because the love of God is poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit given to us. Through him we see that whatever is, in any degree at all, is good; for it comes from him who does not exist in degrees, but is what he is.

Thanks be to you, Lord! We see heaven and earth — whether that means the higher and the lower bodily regions, or the spiritual and the bodily creation; and for the adorning of these parts, which make up either the whole mass of the world or the whole of creation altogether, we see light made, and divided from the darkness. We see the firmament of heaven — whether that first body of the world, between the spiritual waters above and the bodily waters below, or this stretch of air, which is also called heaven, through which the birds of heaven wander — between the waters carried above it in vapor, falling as dew on clear nights, and the heavy waters that run along the ground. We see the beauty of waters gathered across the plains of the sea; and the dry land, first bare, then formed, made visible and set in order, the mother of plants and trees. We see the lights shining from above — the sun sufficing for the day, the moon and the stars consoling the night — and time marked and signified by them all. We see on every side the wet element, teeming with fish and beasts and birds — for the thickness of the air that bears up the flight of birds condenses out of the rising vapor of the waters. We see the face of the earth arrayed with its land animals; and man, made in your image and likeness, set over all the unreasoning animals by that very image and likeness — that is, by the power of reason and understanding. And as in his soul there is one power that rules by taking thought and another made subject, so that it obeys, so bodily too a woman was made for the man: in the mind of her rational understanding, equal in nature; in the sex of her body, subject to the man's sex, the way the impulse to act is subject to the mind's reasoned skill in acting rightly. We see these things: each of them is good, and all of them together are very good.

Let your works praise you, so that we may love you; and let us love you, so that your works may praise you. In time they have their beginning and their end, their rising and their setting, their growth and their decay, their form and their loss of form. They have, then, their morning and their evening, in succession — partly hidden, partly plain. For they were made from nothing, by you: not out of you, not out of any matter not your own or existing beforehand, but out of matter created together with them — created, that is, by you at the same time, since you gave form to its formlessness with no interval of time between. The matter of heaven and earth is one thing, the form of heaven and earth another; yet you made the matter out of nothing at all, and the form of the world out of the formless matter — and both at once, so that form followed matter with no pause and no delay.

We have also looked into what you meant to prefigure by making these things — or by having them told — in just this order. And we have seen that they are good one by one, and all together very good, in your Word, in your Only-Begotten: heaven and earth, the Head and the body of the Church, predestined before all times, with no morning and no evening. But then you began to carry out in time what you had predestined — to reveal what was hidden and to set straight what was disordered in us; for our sins hung over us, and we had sunk away from you into the dark deep, and your good Spirit was borne above us, to come to our help in due season. You justified the ungodly and divided them from the wicked; you made firm the authority of your book, a firmament between those above, who would be teachable under you, and those beneath, who would be subject to them. You gathered the fellowship of the unbelieving into a single mass, so that the ardor of the faithful might stand out, and they might bring forth for you works of mercy — giving away even their earthly riches to the poor, to gain the riches of heaven. Then you kindled certain lights in the firmament — your holy ones, holding the word of life and shining with an authority lifted high by the gifts of the Spirit. Then, for the initiation of the unbelieving nations, you brought forth out of bodily matter the sacraments and the visible miracles and the sounding voices of words, according to the firmament of your book — and by these the faithful too are blessed and multiplied. Next you formed the living soul of the faithful, its affections set in order by the strength of self-mastery. Then the mind subject to you alone, needing no human authority to copy — you renewed it in your image and likeness; you subjected its rational action to the higher rule of the understanding, as woman to man; and to all your ministries, needed for perfecting the faithful in this life, you willed that the same faithful give good things for temporal use, bearing fruit for the time to come. All these things we see, and they are very good — because you see them in us: you, who gave us the Spirit by which we see them, and in them love you.

Lord God, give us peace — for you have given us everything else — the peace of rest, the peace of the sabbath: peace without an evening. All this order of things — so beautiful, so very good — will pass away when its measures have been fully run; for in these things there was made a morning, and an evening.

But the seventh day has no evening, and it has no setting, because you sanctified it to endure forever. After all your works, which were very good, you rested on the seventh day — though you made them all in unbroken rest — so that the voice of your book might tell us in advance: we too, after our works — very good only because you have given them to us — will rest in you, in the sabbath of eternal life.

For then you will rest in us, as now you work in us; and that rest will be your rest through us, as these works are your works through us. But you, Lord, are always working and always at rest. You do not see in time, or move in time, or rest in time — and yet you make the things that are seen in time, and time itself, and the rest that comes when time is done.

So we see the things you have made, because they are; and they are, because you see them. We see, outside us, that they are; and inside us, that they are good. But you saw them made in the very place where you saw them yet to be made. We were moved to do good at a later time, once our heart had conceived by your Spirit; at the earlier time we were moved to do evil, and we abandoned you. But you, the one good God, have never stopped doing good. Some of our works are indeed good, by your gift, but they are not forever; after them, we hope to rest in your great hallowing. But you, the Good that needs no good, are at rest always, because your rest is you yourself. And what man will give another man to understand this? What angel will give it to an angel? What angel to a man? Let it be asked of you, sought in you, knocked for at your door: so, so it will be received; so it will be found; so it will be opened. Amen.

Thanks be to you, Lord.
