Confessions, Book I

Infancy and Boyhood

Book I1 of 13

Great are you, Lord, and greatly to be praised: great is your power, and your wisdom is beyond all measure. And man wants to praise you — man, one small piece of your creation; man, hauling his mortality around with him, hauling the evidence of his sin and the evidence that you resist the proud. Yet still he wants to praise you, this man, one small piece of your creation. You stir us up to delight in praising you, because you made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you. Grant me, Lord, to know and understand which comes first: calling on you, or praising you? And which first — knowing you, or calling on you? But who calls on you without knowing you? Not knowing you, he could call on something else instead of you. Or is it rather that you are called on in order to be known? Yet how will they call on one they have not believed in? And how will they believe without someone to preach him? And those who seek the Lord will praise him — for those who seek find him, and those who find him will praise him. Let me seek you, Lord, by calling on you; let me call on you by believing in you — for you have been preached to us. My faith calls on you, Lord: the faith you gave me, that you breathed into me through the humanity of your Son and the ministry of your preacher.

And how will I call on my God, my God and Lord — when to call on him is, surely, to call him into myself? And what place is there in me for my God to come into? Where would God come into me — God, who made heaven and earth? Is it true, Lord my God: is there anything in me that could hold you? Do heaven and earth hold you — heaven and earth, which you made, and in which you made me? Or is it that whatever exists holds you, because nothing that exists could exist without you? Then since I exist too, why do I beg you to come into me — I, who would not exist unless you were in me? For I am not now in hell, and yet even there you are; for if I go down into hell, you are there. So I would not exist, my God — would not exist at all — unless you were in me. Or say it better: I would not exist unless I were in you, from whom all things are, through whom all things are, in whom all things are. Yes, Lord: yes. Then where do I call you to, if I am already in you? And from where would you come into me? Where could I withdraw, outside heaven and earth, so that my God could come into me from there — my God, who has said, "I fill heaven and earth"?

So do heaven and earth hold you, since you fill them? Or do you fill them and have some of yourself left over, since they cannot hold you? And where do you pour out what remains of you, once heaven and earth are full? Or do you need nothing to hold you — you who hold all things together — since what you fill, you fill by holding it? The vessels that are full of you give you no footing: even if they shattered, you would not spill. And when you pour yourself out over us, you do not fall down into us; you raise us up. You do not scatter; you gather us in. But you who fill everything — do you fill it with the whole of yourself? Or, since all things together cannot hold the whole of you, do they hold a part of you — all of them the same part, all at once? Or each its own part, the larger things a larger part, the smaller a smaller? Then is one part of you larger and another smaller? Or are you whole everywhere, while nothing holds you whole?

What are you, then, my God? What, I ask, but the Lord God? For who is Lord besides the Lord? Who is God besides our God? Highest, best, most powerful, most almighty; most merciful and most just; most hidden and most present; most beautiful and most strong; constant and ungraspable; unchangeable, changing everything; never new, never old; renewing all things while quietly aging the proud, who never see it happen; always at work, always at rest; gathering, though you need nothing; carrying, filling, sheltering; creating, feeding, finishing; seeking, though you lack nothing. You love without fever; you are jealous and untroubled; you repent and feel no sorrow; you are angry and calm. You change your works without changing your plan; you take back what you find, though you never lost it. Never poor, you delight in gain; never greedy, you demand interest. We pay you more than you require, to make you our debtor — yet who has anything that is not already yours? You pay debts owing no one; you forgive debts and lose nothing. And after all that, what have we said, my God, my life, my holy sweetness? What does anyone manage to say when he speaks of you? Yet woe to those who say nothing of you: even the ones who talk the most are mute.

Who will give me rest in you? Who will send you into my heart to intoxicate it, so that I forget my miseries and hold on to you, my one and only good? What are you to me? Have mercy, so I can even say it. And what am I to you, that you command me to love you — and if I don't, you are angry with me and threaten me with immense miseries? Is failing to love you, then, only a small misery? Oh, by your own mercies, Lord my God, tell me what you are to me. Say to my soul, "I am your salvation." Say it so that I hear it. Look: the ears of my heart are in front of you, Lord. Open them, and say to my soul, "I am your salvation." Let me run after that voice and lay hold of you. Do not hide your face from me. Let me die — so that I do not die — only let me see your face.

The house of my soul is cramped — too small for you to come into. Enlarge it. It is in ruins: rebuild it. It holds things that will offend your eyes: I admit it, I know it. But who is going to clean it? Who else can I cry out to but you? Cleanse me from my hidden faults, Lord, and spare your servant from the sins of others. I believe, and that is why I speak — Lord, you know it. Haven't I laid my offenses out before you, testifying against myself, my God — and you forgave the wickedness of my heart? I do not go to court against you, for you are truth itself; and I have no wish to deceive myself, letting my own wrongdoing lie to itself. So no — I do not go to court against you. For if you kept a record of sins, Lord — Lord, who could stand?

Still, let me speak in front of your mercy — me, dust and ashes — still let me speak. It is your mercy I am speaking to, after all, not some man who would laugh at me. Maybe you laugh at me too; but you will turn and have compassion on me. Because what is it I want to say, Lord, except that I do not know where I came from into this place — this dying life, should I call it, or this living death? I don't know. What met me were the consolations of your mercies, as I heard from the parents of my flesh — the father you formed me from, the mother you formed me in; for I remember none of it myself. So the consolations of human milk received me; but it was not my mother or my nurses who filled their own breasts. You were giving me an infant's food through them, by your own design, out of the riches you have laid down at the very foundation of things. You gave me the gift of wanting no more than you gave, and you gave my nurses the will to pass on to me what you gave to them: by an affection you had set in order, they were glad to give me what they had in such abundance from you. For the good they did me was a good for them — a good not from them, but through them; for every good thing is from you, God, and from my God comes the whole of my rescue. I learned this only later, with you calling out to me through these very gifts of yours, inside me and around me. For back then, all I knew was how to suck, to settle when something pleased me, and to cry at whatever hurt my flesh — nothing more.

Later I began to smile as well — first in my sleep, then awake. So I was told about myself, and I believed it, since we see the same in other infants; of myself I remember none of it. And little by little I began to sense where I was, and to want to show my wants to the people who could satisfy them — and I couldn't, because the wants were inside me and the people were outside, and no sense of theirs could reach into my soul. So I flailed my limbs and threw out sounds — signs, the few I could manage, such as I could manage — and they were nothing much like what I meant. And when I was not obeyed at once, either because I hadn't been understood or because obeying would have harmed me, I raged at my elders for not submitting to me, raged at free people for not being my slaves, and avenged myself on them by crying. That this is what infants are, I have learned from every infant I have been able to watch; and those babies, knowing nothing, have shown me better that I was the same than my nurses ever could, who knew me.

And look — my infancy died long ago, and I am alive. But you, Lord, live forever, and nothing in you dies. Before the first beginnings of the ages, before everything that can even be called "before," you are; and you are God and Lord of everything you have created. In you stand fast the causes of all unstable things; in you the sources of all changing things remain unchanged; in you live the eternal reasons of everything unreasoning and everything that passes. So tell me, God — tell your suppliant; in your mercy, tell your miserable one: did my infancy come after some other age of mine that had already died? Was it the age I spent inside my mother's womb? I have been told a little about that, and I have seen pregnant women with my own eyes. And before even that, God my sweetness — what? Was I anywhere? Was I anyone? I have no one who can tell me: not father or mother, not the experience of others, not my own memory. Or are you laughing at me for asking, and commanding me instead to praise you and confess you for what I do know?

So I confess you, Lord of heaven and earth, and I praise you for my first beginnings and for my infancy, which I do not remember. You have arranged for a man to guess much about himself from others, and to believe much about himself on the word of ordinary women. Even then I existed and lived, and by infancy's end I was already hunting for signs to make what I felt known to other people. Where could a living creature like that come from, if not from you, Lord? Is anyone the craftsman of his own making? Is there some channel running from anywhere else through which being and living pour into us — other than you making us, Lord, you for whom being and living are never two different things, because to be supremely and to live supremely are one and the same? You are supreme, and you do not change; today never runs its course in you — and yet in you it does run its course, because all times are in you too, and they would have no way to pass unless you held them. And because your years do not fail, your years are one single today. How many of our days, and of our fathers' days, have already passed through this today of yours, taking from it their measure and the manner of the existence they had; and others will pass through too, and take their own measure and manner of existing. But you are the same, always; and everything of tomorrow and after, everything of yesterday and before, you will do in this today, you have done in this today. What is it to me if someone cannot grasp this? Let him rejoice anyway and say, "What is this?" Let him rejoice even so — and let him love to find you by not finding, rather than find and not find you.

Hear me, God. How miserable is the sin of men! A man says so, and you have mercy on him — because you made the man, and did not make the sin in him. Who reminds me of the sin of my infancy? For in your sight no one is clean of sin, not even the infant whose life is one day old on the earth. Who reminds me? Doesn't every tiny infant do it, in whom I see what I cannot remember of myself? So what was my sin back then? That I cried and grabbed so greedily at the breast? If I did that now — grabbed that greedily at food fit for my age — I would be laughed at and scolded, and rightly. So what I did then deserved a scolding; but since I could not have understood one, custom and common sense forbade my being scolded. After all, we root those habits out and throw them away as we grow — and I have never seen a man of sense throw away anything good when he prunes a plant. Or was it good, even for a moment, to cry for something that would have harmed it if given? To seethe with rage at free people — grown people, its own parents — because they would not be its slaves; at people far wiser than itself, because they would not jump at its every whim; to fight back, striking and hurting as hard as it could, because they refused commands it could only have obeyed to its own cost? So the innocence of infants is in the weakness of their limbs, not in any innocence of will. I have watched a jealous baby with my own eyes. He could not speak yet, but he went pale and turned a bitter stare on the child who shared his milk. Who hasn't seen this? Mothers and nurses will tell you they charm it away with some remedy or other. But is it really innocence — when the spring of milk is running full and overflowing — to refuse to share it with a companion in utter need, whose whole life still hangs on that one food? We bear with all this gently — not because it is no evil, or a small one, but because it will fall away as the years come on. And here is the proof: the very same behavior, met in someone older, cannot be borne for a moment.

So you, Lord my God — you who gave the infant its life and its body; who fitted it, as we see, with senses, jointed it with limbs, graced it with a shape, and wove into it every drive a living whole needs for its wholeness and its safety — you command me to praise you in all this, to confess to you and sing to your name, Most High. For you are God, almighty and good, and you would be so had you made nothing but this — this, which no one else can make: you, the One, from whom comes all measure; you, most beautiful, who give all things their form and set all things in order by your law. So this stretch of my life, Lord — which I do not remember living, which I take on other people's word, and infer from watching other infants that I must have passed through, likely as the inference is — I am reluctant to count as part of this life of mine that I live in this world. As far as the darkness of my forgetting goes, it is one with the age I spent in my mother's womb. But if I was conceived in iniquity, and in sin my mother nourished me inside her womb — then where, I beg you, my God, where, Lord, and when was I, your servant, ever innocent? But look, I leave that time behind. What is it to me now, when I cannot recover a single trace of it?

Out of infancy I moved on into boyhood — or rather boyhood moved into me, taking infancy's place. And infancy did not leave — where would it have gone? — and yet it was no longer there. For I was no longer an infant, with no speech in me, but a boy, talking. This I do remember; and how I learned to talk, I worked out only later. It was not that grown-ups taught me, handing me words in some fixed order of instruction, as they did the alphabet a little afterward. I taught myself — with the mind you gave me, my God — because the grunts and cries and flung-about limbs I was using to get my wishes across could not carry everything I meant, or reach everyone I meant it for. So I began to seize words and hold them in my memory. When people named a thing, and turned toward it as they made the sound, I watched, and I grasped it: that thing was called by the sound they uttered whenever they meant to point it out. And that this was what they meant was plain from the motion of their bodies — the natural vocabulary of the whole human race, made of facial expressions and glances of the eyes, gestures of the limbs and tones of the voice, showing what the mind feels as it seeks, keeps, rejects, or recoils. So, hearing the same words again and again in their places in different sentences, I gradually gathered what things they were signs of; and once I had trained my mouth to those signs, I used them at last to say what I wanted. In this way I traded the signs of our wants with the people around me, and waded out deeper into the stormy shared life of human beings — still hanging on the authority of my parents and the beck and call of my elders.

God, my God, what misery I met there, what mockery. The right way to live, I was told as a boy, was to obey my instructors — so that I might get on in this world and shine in the arts of the tongue, arts that win the honor of men and serve up counterfeit riches. So I was sent to school to get my letters. What use they were, I — poor wretch — could not see; and yet if I slacked in learning, I was beaten. Our ancestors approved this system; the crowds who had walked that road before us had laid down the paths of misery we were forced along, multiplying labor and grief for the sons of Adam. But we also found men who prayed to you, Lord, and from them we learned — imagining you as far as our powers reached — that you were someone great, someone who could hear us and help us even though our senses could not find you. So as a boy I began praying to you, my help and my refuge; I broke the knots of my tongue calling on you; and small as I was, it was with no small feeling that I prayed not to be beaten at school. And when you did not hear me — which was no foolishness in me — the grown-ups laughed at my stripes, my own parents along with the rest, who wished me no harm at all; and those beatings were, at the time, my great and grievous harm.

Is there anyone, Lord, so great in soul, holding to you with a love so enormous — is there anyone (for a certain thick-headedness can produce the same effect), is there anyone whose devout attachment to you runs so deep that he counts as trifles the racks and the hooks and the rest of the torturer's inventory, which men everywhere beg you in terror to be spared — that he can laugh at the people who dread those things most bitterly, the way our parents laughed at the torments our schoolmasters dealt us as boys? For we were no less afraid of ours, and prayed to you no less to escape them. And we sinned all the same, by writing or reading or thinking over our lessons less than was demanded of us. It was not memory or intelligence we lacked, Lord — you willed us enough of those for our age. But we loved to play; and we were punished for it by men who were doing the very same thing. Except that adult play is called "business." When boys do it, it is exactly the same thing, and the grown-ups punish them for it, and nobody pities the boys, or the men, or both. Would any fair judge of things say I was rightly beaten, as a boy, for playing ball — because the games slowed my learning of letters, letters I was going to use, as a man, to play a far uglier game? And the man who beat me — what else was he doing? If some colleague got the better of him in a petty scholarly quarrel, he was more racked by bile and envy than I ever was when a playmate beat me at ball.

And yet I sinned in this, Lord God — you who order and create everything in nature, though sin you do not create, only order — Lord my God, I sinned by disobeying the commands of my parents and of those masters. For whatever motive my elders had for wanting me to learn, I could later have put the learning to good use. I disobeyed not because I had chosen something better, but out of love of play: I loved the swagger of winning my contests; I loved having my ears tickled by made-up stories, which only made them itch the hotter; and the same curiosity glittered more and more in my eyes for the shows and the games of grown men. The men who stage those games stand so high in public esteem that nearly everyone wishes the same eminence for their little children — and is perfectly content to have those children beaten if the shows keep them from the studies that are supposed to lead them, someday, to be the men who stage the shows. Look on all this with mercy, Lord, and set free those of us who now call on you; set free those who do not yet call on you, so that they may call on you, and you may set them free.

While still a boy, I had already heard of the eternal life promised to us through the humility of the Lord our God, coming all the way down to our pride; and I was signed with the sign of his cross, and seasoned with his salt, fresh from the womb of my mother, who set great hope in you. You saw, Lord, how once, while still a boy, I was seized one day with a sudden cramping fever of the stomach and came close to death — you saw, my God, for you were already my keeper, with what feeling and what faith I begged for the baptism of your Christ, my God and Lord, appealing to the devotion of my mother and of the mother of us all, your Church. The mother of my flesh was shaken — for with a heart chaste in your faith, she was in labor even more lovingly for my eternal salvation — and she would have rushed to have me initiated and washed in the sacraments of salvation, confessing you, Lord Jesus, for the forgiveness of sins, if I had not suddenly recovered. So my cleansing was deferred — as though, if I lived, I was bound to be dirtied again; and because after that washing, the guilt of sin's filth would be greater and more dangerous. So: I already believed, and so did my mother and the whole household — except my father alone. Yet he did not defeat, in me, the claim my mother's devotion had on me: he could not stop me believing in Christ just because he did not yet believe himself. For she strained every nerve so that you, my God, would be my father rather than him; and in this you helped her win out over her husband — she, the better one, serving him, because in serving him she was serving you, who commanded it.

I beg you, my God — I would love to know, if you are willing to tell me — to what end was my baptism deferred? Was it for my good that the reins were slackened on me, so to speak, to let me sin? Or were they not slackened? If not, why does it still ring in our ears from every side, about this person and that: "Leave him alone, let him do it; he isn't baptized yet"? Yet when it is the body's health, we never say, "Let him be wounded worse; he isn't healed yet." How much better, then, if I had been healed at once — if my own diligence and my family's had seen to it that the recovered health of my soul was kept safe in your keeping, who gave it. How much better, truly. But my mother could already see the waves of temptation, how many and how great, massing over me beyond boyhood; and she preferred to let them break on the clay from which I could still be shaped afterward, rather than on the finished image itself.

In boyhood itself, though — which gave less cause for fear on my account than adolescence did — I had no love of study, and I hated being forced to it. And I was forced, and it was good for me; but I was not good, for I would never have learned unless I was made to. And no one does well against his will, even when what he does is good. Nor were the people forcing me doing well: the good that came to me came from you, my God. For they never looked past their own aim for the learning they crammed into me: the glutting of appetites that cannot be filled — appetites for a wealthy kind of poverty and a shameful kind of glory. But you, who have counted every hair on our heads, used the error of everyone who pushed me to learn for my profit; and my own error — my not wanting to learn — you used for my punishment, which I richly deserved: so small a boy, and so great a sinner. So through people who were not doing good, you did good for me; and for my own sin, you paid me back justly. For you have decreed it, and so it stands: every disordered soul is its own punishment.

But why did I so hate the Greek literature I was steeped in as a small boy? Even now I have not quite got to the bottom of it. For Latin I loved — not what the first teachers gave, but what the ones called grammarians teach. Those first lessons — reading, writing, arithmetic — I found as much a burden and a punishment as all my Greek. And yet where did even that come from, if not from sin and the emptiness of this life, since I was flesh, a breath that goes out and does not come back? Those first lessons were the better ones, because they were the surer: through them I got, and keep to this day, the power to read whatever I find written and to write whatever I want myself. In the others, I was forced to memorize the wanderings of some man called Aeneas — forgetting my own wanderings the while — and to weep for the death of Dido, who killed herself for love; while all the time, in the middle of these things, I endured my own wretched self dying far away from you, God my life, and my eyes stayed dry.

For what is more pitiful than a pitiful man with no pity for himself — a man weeping over the death of Dido, which came from loving Aeneas, and not weeping over his own death, which came from not loving you, God? Light of my heart, bread for the inner mouth of my soul, strength wedded to my mind and to the deep lap of my thought: I did not love you. I went whoring away from you, and as I whored, from every side came the cry, "Well done! Well done!" For the friendship of this world is whoring away from you, and "Well done! Well done!" is shouted until one is ashamed not to be that kind of man. And over none of this did I weep. I wept for Dido, "dead, gone by the sword to her uttermost end" — while I myself forsook you and went after the uttermost and lowest of the things you made: earth sinking back into earth. And had I been forbidden to read those stories, I would have grieved that I could not read what made me grieve. Madness of this kind passes for a more honorable and a richer education than the lessons by which I learned to read and write.

But now let my God cry out in my soul, and let your truth tell me: "Not so. Not so. The first learning was far better." Look — I would sooner forget the wanderings of Aeneas and all the rest of it than forget how to read and write. Yes, curtains hang across the doorways of the grammar schools; but they stand less for the honor of a mystery than for the covering of an error. And let the men I have stopped fearing not shout me down while I confess to you whatever my soul wants to confess, my God, and consent to the condemnation of my own bad roads, so that I may love your good ones. Let the buyers and sellers of literature not shout me down either. For if I put the question to them — is it true, as the poet says, that Aeneas ever came to Carthage? — the less educated will answer that they don't know; the more educated will say flatly that it never happened. But if I ask which letters spell the name "Aeneas," everyone who has learned to read will give me the right answer, according to the agreement and convention by which human beings settled those signs among themselves. And if I ask which would do more damage to a person's life to forget — reading and writing, or those poetic fictions — who does not see what anyone would answer who has not completely forgotten himself? So I sinned, as a boy, in loving the empty studies above the useful ones — or rather, in hating the useful and loving the empty. "One and one is two, two and two is four" — that, to me, was a hateful chant; and the sweetest of all spectacles to my vanity was the wooden horse full of armed men, and Troy burning, and the very ghost of Creusa.

Then why did I hate the Greek classics, which sing the same kind of songs? For Homer too was skilled at weaving such tales — he is delicious in his emptiness — and yet to me as a boy he tasted bitter. I suppose Virgil is exactly that to Greek boys, when they are forced to learn him the way I was forced to learn Homer. It was the difficulty — the sheer difficulty of mastering a foreign language at all — that splashed gall, as it were, over the whole sweetness of the Greek tales of wonder. For I did not know one word of the language, and I was pressed hard, with savage terrors and punishments, to make me know it. There was a time, in infancy, when I knew no Latin either; yet Latin I learned just by paying attention, without any fear or torture at all, amid the endearments of my nurses, the jokes of people smiling at me, the delight of people playing with me. I learned it with no weight of punishment driving me on, because my own heart drove me to bring out what it had conceived — and I could not have done that without learning words: taken not from instructors but from anyone who talked with me, into whose ears, in turn, I delivered whatever I was feeling. It is plain enough from this that for learning such things, free curiosity has more power than fearful compulsion. But compulsion reins in curiosity's wandering — through your laws, God: your laws, from the schoolmaster's cane to the martyr's trials, your laws with their power to mix us a wholesome bitterness, calling us back to yourself away from the poisoned pleasure that first drew us from you.

Hear my prayer, Lord: do not let my soul collapse under your discipline, and do not let me collapse in confessing to you all the mercies by which you dragged me out of every one of my worst roads — so that you might grow sweeter to me than all the seductions I used to chase; so that I might love you with all my strength and clasp your hand with everything my heart has; and so that you might rescue me from every temptation, all the way to the end. For look, Lord: you are my king and my God. Let whatever useful thing I learned as a boy be at your service; let it serve you that I speak and write and read and count. For while I was learning emptiness, you were giving me discipline; and my sin of delighting in that emptiness, you have forgiven. In those studies I did learn many a useful word — but the same words can be learned in studies that are not empty, and that is the safe road children ought to walk.

But woe to you, you river of human custom! Who can stand against you? How long before you run dry? How long will you keep sweeping the children of Eve into that vast and dreadful sea, which even those who have climbed aboard the wood of the cross can barely get across? Wasn't it in your current that I read of Jove, thunderer and adulterer at once? Both he could not be, of course; but both he was made, so that fake thunder could stand as authority and play the pimp for real adultery. And which of our gowned masters lends a sober ear to a man formed of the same dust as themselves when he calls out: "Homer invented these stories, and carried the human up to the gods — I would rather he had brought the divine down to us"? But it is truer to say: yes, he invented them — but he did it by conferring divinity on depraved men, so that depravity would no longer count as depravity, and whoever practices it could seem to be imitating, not degenerate men, but the gods of heaven themselves.

And still, you river out of hell, the sons of men are flung into you, fees and all, to learn these things; and it becomes a great public affair when the business is transacted in the forum, in full view of laws that vote the teachers a state salary on top of the students' fees. You slap your rocks and roar: "Here is where words are learned! Here is where men acquire the eloquence essential to persuasion and to unfolding their opinions!" Really? We would never have known the words "shower of gold," "lap," "trick," "temples of heaven," and the other words written in that passage, if Terence had not brought a worthless young man onto the stage, holding up Jupiter as the model for his own seduction.

"He looked up at a painting on the wall, where the story was pictured: Jove, as they tell it, once sent a shower of gold into Danae's lap — the trick he played on the woman."

And watch how he whips himself up to lust, as if with heaven's own coaching:

"And what a god!" he says. "The one who shakes the highest temples of heaven with his thunder."

"And should I, one little man, not do the same? I did it — and I did it gladly."

The words are not one bit easier to learn for all this filth; but by the words, the filth is committed with more confidence. I bring no charge against the words themselves — they are like choice and costly vessels — but against the wine of error in them, which drunken teachers poured out for us; and if we would not drink, we were beaten, with no appeal to any sober judge. And yet, my God — before whose eyes my memory can now rest safe — I learned all of it willingly; I enjoyed it, wretch that I was; and for that, they called me a boy of great promise.

Bear with me, my God, while I say something about the intelligence you gave me, and the ravings I wore it out on. For a task was set me that gave my soul no peace — the prize was praise, the threat was disgrace and the cane: I was to recite the speech of Juno, raging and grieving that she could not

"turn the Trojan king away from Italy."

Words which, I had heard, Juno never spoke. But we were forced to go astray in the footprints of poetic make-believe, and to say in loose prose the kind of thing the poet had said in verse. And the boy whose speaking won the most applause was the one in whom the passions of rage and grief stood out most convincingly, matched to the dignity of the character he was impersonating and dressed in the most fitting words. What was any of it to me, my true life, my God? Why did my recitation earn more applause than those of the many boys my own age, in my own class? Isn't the whole thing smoke and wind? Was there really nothing else on which to exercise my mind and my tongue? Your praises, Lord — your praises rising through your Scriptures could have staked up the young vine of my heart, and it would not have been dragged off through this empty trash, filthy plunder for the birds of the air. For there is more than one way of sacrificing to the angels who fell.

But what wonder is it that I was swept away into emptiness like that, and went out and away from you, my God — when the men held up for me to imitate were men who, if they were caught in a barbarism or a slip of grammar while reporting some perfectly innocent act of their own, were covered in confusion; but when they narrated their own lusts in rich, ornamented, well-built sentences and were praised for it, they gloried? You see all this, Lord, and you keep silent — patient, and rich in mercy and truth. Will you be silent forever? Even now you are drawing up out of that monstrous pit the soul that seeks you, that thirsts for your delights, whose heart says to you: "I have sought your face; your face, Lord, I will keep seeking." For I was far from your face, deep in the darkness of my passions. It is not on foot, not by distance in miles, that we go away from you or come back to you. That younger son of yours in the story did not hire horses or chariots or ships; he did not fly on any visible wing or wear out his legs walking, so as to live in a far country and pour away like a prodigal what you had given him when he set out. You were a sweet father, because you gave it — and sweeter still to him when he came back destitute. To live in lustful passion, then — which is to say, in darkened passion — that is what it is to be far from your face.

Look, Lord God — and look patiently, the way you always look — at how exactly the sons of men keep the compacts of letters and syllables received from the speakers who came before them, while they neglect the eternal compacts of everlasting salvation received from you. It goes so far that a man who holds or teaches the old agreed rules of sounds will offend people more by dropping the "h" and saying "uman being," against the discipline of grammar, than if he — a human being — hates a human being, against yours. As if any human enemy could do a man more damage than the hatred itself with which he burns against him; as if anyone could wreck another person, by hounding him, more thoroughly than he wrecks his own heart by the hating. And surely no science of letters is written deeper in us than the writing of conscience: that he is doing to another what he would refuse to suffer himself. How hidden you are, dwelling in silence on the heights, you the only great God — scattering blindness, by a law that never tires, as punishment on lawless desires! A man hunting a reputation for eloquence stands before a human judge, ringed by a crowd of human beings, denouncing his enemy with the most inhuman hatred — and keeps the most vigilant watch that his tongue not slip and kill the word "human being," while he loses no sleep over whether the fury of his mind might kill an actual human being.

This was the threshold of the world where I lay, a wretched boy; this was the training-floor where I wrestled — more afraid of committing a barbarism than on guard, if I did commit one, against envying the boys who hadn't. I say it, and I confess it to you, my God: this is what I was praised for, by the people whose approval was then my entire definition of living well. For I did not see the whirlpool of shame into which I had been thrown, out of the sight of your eyes. For in their eyes, what was fouler by then than I was? — I even displeased people like myself, deceiving tutor and masters and parents with lie upon countless lie, out of love of play, a craving for trashy shows, and a restless itch to mimic what I watched on the stage. I stole, too, from my parents' pantry and their table, sometimes with gluttony giving the orders, sometimes to have something to hand the other boys, who charged me for their games even though they enjoyed the playing as much as I did. And in the games themselves I would often grab a cheat's victory, because I was already beaten myself — by the empty craving to come first. And what was I so unwilling to have done to me, what did I attack so ferociously when I caught anyone at it, as the very thing I was doing to the others? And when I was caught and attacked myself, I chose to fight rather than give way. Is that the innocence of boyhood? It is not, Lord; it is not — let me say it, my God. For these are the very things — these exactly — that pass on, as tutors and masters give way to governors and kings, and nuts and balls and pet sparrows give way to gold and estates and slaves, and heavier punishments take over from the cane. So it was the small stature of childhood, our King, that you were commending as the emblem of humility when you said, "The kingdom of heaven belongs to such as these."

And yet, Lord — to you, most excellent and most good creator and governor of the universe, our God — thanks would still be owed even if you had willed that I not live past boyhood. For even then I existed; I lived and I felt; and I cared for my own wholeness — a trace of that deeply hidden Oneness from which I came. With an inner sense I stood guard over the wholeness of my outer senses; and in my small thoughts, about small things, I came to delight in truth. I hated to be deceived; my memory was vigorous; I was gaining command of speech; friendship softened me; I fled pain, humiliation, ignorance. In a living creature like that, what was not wonderful, not worth praising? And all of it the gifts of my God. I did not give them to myself. They are good, and all of them together are me. So the one who made me is good, and he himself is my good; and I leap up to him in thanks for every good thing that was in me even as a boy. My sin was this: that I looked for pleasures, for greatness, for truth not in him but in the things he made — in myself and the rest — and so pitched headlong into pains, humiliations, and errors. Thanks be to you, my sweetness and my honor and my confidence; thanks be to you, my God, for your gifts. Only keep them for me. For then you will keep me too, and the things you have given me will grow and be perfected, and I myself will be with you — because even that I exist at all is your gift.
