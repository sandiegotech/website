# Confessions, Book VII

The Unchangeable Light

By now my adolescence was dead — that evil, unspeakable adolescence — and I was passing into manhood: the older in years, the uglier in emptiness. I could not conceive of any substance except the kind these eyes are used to seeing. I did not picture you in the shape of a human body, God; from the moment I first heard anything of wisdom, I always fled that thought, and I was glad to find the same refusal in the faith of our spiritual mother, your Catholic Church. But what else to think you were, I did not know. I — a man, and such a man — was trying to think of you, the supreme, the only, the true God; and from my inmost marrow I believed you incorruptible, inviolable, unchangeable. How I knew this, or from where, I could not say; but I saw plainly, and was certain, that what can be corrupted is worse than what cannot; what cannot be violated I set without hesitation above what can be; what suffers no change is better than what can be changed. My heart cried out violently against all my phantoms, and with that one blow I tried to beat off the swarm of unclean images circling the eye of my mind. And they were scarcely scattered before, in the blink of an eye, they massed again, hurled themselves against my sight, and clouded it — so that even if I did not imagine you in the shape of a human body, I was still forced to imagine you as something bodily, spread through space: whether poured into the world, or diffused beyond it through the infinite. Even that incorruptible, inviolable, unchangeable being that I set above everything corruptible, violable, and changeable — even that I imagined this way. For whatever I stripped of such space seemed to me to be nothing — nothing at all, not even an emptiness. If a body is taken out of its place, and the place remains empty of every body — of earth, of water, of air, of sky — it still remains an empty place: a spacious nothing, as it were.

So thick and dull was my heart — I was not clear even to my own sight — that whatever was not stretched over some expanse of space, or diffused through it, or packed into it, or swollen out into it, whatever did not have or could not have some such dimension, I judged to be nothing at all. Over shapes like the shapes my eyes range over, my heart then ranged; and I did not see that the very act of mind by which I formed those images was not itself anything of that kind — and yet it could not have formed them unless it were something great. So I tried to think of you too, Life of my life, as vast: penetrating the whole mass of the world through infinite space on every side, and beyond it in every direction through immensity without end — so that the earth would have you, the sky would have you, all things would have you, and they would be bounded in you, and you bounded nowhere. For as the body of the air above the earth does not block the sunlight, which passes through it and penetrates it, not by breaking it or cutting it but by filling the whole of it — so I supposed the body not only of sky and air and sea but of the earth itself lay open to you, penetrable in all its parts, greatest and smallest alike, to receive your presence: a hidden breath, within and without, governing everything you have made. This was my guess, because I could conceive of nothing else; and it was false. For in that case a larger part of the earth would hold a larger portion of you, and a smaller part a smaller; and all things would be full of you in such a way that an elephant's body would contain more of you than a sparrow's, by just so much as it is bigger and takes up more room; and so you would deal yourself out piecemeal to the pieces of the world — much of you to the large parts, little to the little. And that is not what you are. But you had not yet lit up my darkness.

Against those deceived deceivers, those talkative mutes — mute, because your word never sounded out of them — it was enough for me, Lord, to hold the argument that Nebridius used to put forward long ago, back in Carthage, which staggered all of us who heard it. That race of darkness which the Manichees like to set against you as an opposing mass: what would it have done to you if you had refused to fight it? If they answered, "It would have done you some harm," then you would be subject to violation and corruption. If, "It could have done you no harm," then no reason remained for fighting at all — and fighting in such a way that some portion of you, some member of you, some offspring of your very substance, gets mixed in with hostile powers, with natures you never created, and is corrupted by them and changed for the worse, driven out of happiness into misery, needing help to be pulled free and cleansed. And this offspring of your substance, they said, was the soul: enslaved, defiled, corrupted, waiting for your Word to come to its rescue — your Word free, pure, and whole; though that Word, being of one and the same substance, must be every bit as corruptible. So then: if they called you — whatever you are, the substance by which you are — incorruptible, then all these tales were false and detestable; if corruptible, that was false on its face, to be abhorred the moment it was spoken. This argument, then, was enough against men who deserved to be vomited up whole from an overloaded stomach; for they had no way out of it that did not commit a horrible sacrilege of heart and tongue, thinking and saying such things about you.

But I, too — though I held and firmly believed that you, our Lord, the true God, maker not only of our souls but of our bodies, and not only of our souls and bodies but of all beings and all things, are undefilable and unalterable and in no way changeable — I still had no clear, untangled grasp of the cause of evil. Whatever it was, I saw this much: the search for it must not force me to believe the unchangeable God changeable, or I would myself become the very thing I was searching for. So I searched with an easy mind on that score, certain that what those men said was untrue. I fled them with my whole heart, because I saw that in asking where evil comes from, they had filled up with evil themselves: they preferred to believe that your substance suffers evil rather than that their own commits it.

And I strained to understand what I was now hearing: that free will is the cause of our doing evil, and your just judgment the cause of our suffering it. But I could not see it clearly. I would struggle to lift the eye of my mind out of the pit, and I would sink back in; as often as I struggled, so often I sank. One thing, though, raised me a little toward your light: I knew that I had a will as surely as I knew that I was alive. When I willed a thing or refused it, I was absolutely certain that it was I who willed and I who refused — no one else — and I was beginning to see that there lay the cause of my sin. What I did against my will, I saw that I suffered rather than did; and I counted it not guilt but punishment — and since I held you to be just, I quickly confessed that the punishment was not unjust. But then I said: Who made me? Was it not my God, who is not only good but goodness itself? Then where did I get this willing of evil and refusing of good, which so justly earns the punishment? Who set it in me? Who grafted this seedling of bitterness into me, when the whole of me was made by my most sweet God? If the devil is the author — then where does the devil come from? And if he too, by his own perverse will, was turned from a good angel into a devil, where did the evil will in him come from, when the whole angel had been made by a Creator wholly good? These thoughts pushed me down again and smothered me; but they never dragged me down into that hell of error where no one confesses to you — the belief that you suffer evil rather than that man does it.

I was straining to work out the rest as a man who had already found this much: that the incorruptible must be better than the corruptible; and therefore you, whatever you were, I confessed to be incorruptible. For no soul ever has been able, or ever will be able, to conceive anything better than you, the supreme and best good. And since, most truly and most certainly, the incorruptible ranks above the corruptible — as I now ranked it — then if you were not incorruptible, I could have reached in thought something better than my God. Seeing, then, that the incorruptible stands above the corruptible, I should have sought you there; and from there I should have traced where evil is — that is, where corruption comes from, which cannot violate your substance in any way at all. For corruption cannot touch our God in any way — by no will, by no necessity, by no unforeseen chance — because he is God, and what he wills is good, and he himself is that good; and to be corrupted is not good. Nor are you forced to anything against your will, since your will is not greater than your power. It would be greater only if you were greater than yourself — for the will of God and the power of God are God himself. And what can take you by surprise, when you know all things? No nature exists at all except because you know it. Why should we go on asking why the substance that God is cannot be corruptible? If it were, it would not be God.

And I asked where evil comes from, and I asked it badly, and failed to see the evil in my own asking. I set the whole creation before the eyes of my spirit: everything in it that we can see — sea, earth, air, stars, trees, mortal creatures — and everything in it that we cannot, the firmament of heaven above, and all the angels, and all its spiritual inhabitants. But even these, as if they were bodies, my imagination arranged in places; and I made of your creation one great mass, sorted by kinds of bodies — some of them real bodies, some the bodies I had invented for spirits — and I made this mass huge: not as it really was, which I had no way of knowing, but as big as suited me, though bounded on every side. And you, Lord, I imagined surrounding the mass on every side and soaking through it, but infinite in every direction — as if there were a sea, one single sea, everywhere and everywhere boundless through unmeasured space; and in the sea a sponge, as huge as you like, but finite; and the sponge filled in every one of its parts from the immeasurable sea. That is how I conceived your creation: finite, and full of you, the infinite. And I said: Here is God, and here is what God has created; and God is good, most mightily and incomparably better than all of it; yet being good he created good things — and see how he surrounds them and fills them. Then where is evil? Where did it come from, and by what crack did it creep in? What is its root, what is its seed? Or does it have no being at all? Then why do we fear and guard against what is not? And if our fear is groundless, then the fear itself is evil — the fear that goads and tortures the heart for nothing; an evil all the heavier because there is nothing to fear, and we fear anyway. So either evil is the thing we fear, or the evil is that we fear. Where does it come from, then, since God made all these things — the good God, and all of them good? He, the greater and highest good, made lesser goods; but maker and made alike, all are good. Where does evil come from? Was there some evil matter he worked from — forming it and ordering it, but leaving something in it that he did not turn to good? But why? Was he unable — the Almighty — to turn and change the whole of it, so that no evil would remain in it? And why would he choose to make anything out of it at all, rather than use that same almightiness to make it simply not exist? Could it have existed against his will? And if it was eternal, why did he leave it alone through the endless reaches of time gone by, and only so much later decide to make something out of it? Or, if he did suddenly resolve to act, would not the Almighty rather have acted to make that evil matter cease to be, so that he alone would be — the whole, true, supreme, infinite good? Or, if it was not good that he who is good should fail to frame and found something good, then why not take away the evil matter, reduce it to nothing, and himself establish good matter, and from that create all things? For he would not be almighty if he could not create something good without the help of matter he had not created. Such things I turned over in my miserable heart, weighed down with gnawing worries and the fear of dying before I had found the truth. But the faith of your Christ, our Lord and Savior, professed in the Catholic Church, stood firm in my heart — a faith still unshaped on many points, swaying off the rule of doctrine; yet my mind did not let go of it, and day by day it drank in more.

By this time I had also rejected the lying divinations and godless ravings of the astrologers. For this too, my God, let your own mercies confess to you from the depths of my soul! For it was you — you and wholly you (for who else calls us back from the death of every error but the Life that does not know how to die, the Wisdom that lights up minds in need of light while needing none itself, the Wisdom that governs the world, down to the leaves fluttering on the trees?) — you who dealt with the stubbornness I set against Vindicianus, that sharp old man, and against Nebridius, a young man of admirable soul: the one declaring vehemently, the other saying often, though with some hesitation, that no art exists for seeing the future — that men's guesses are a kind of lottery, and out of the many things they say, some come to pass; the speakers never know which; they simply stumble into it by never keeping quiet. So you provided me a friend: no lazy consulter of astrologers, and not really versed in their literature either, but, as I said, a curious consulter — and yet he knew a story, heard from his father, which he did not realize went far toward overthrowing the whole reputation of that art. This man, Firminus, educated in the liberal arts and well trained in rhetoric, consulted me as one of his dearest friends about certain affairs of his in which his worldly hopes were riding high: what did I think, given his "constellations," as they call them? I, already beginning to lean toward Nebridius's view of the matter, did not refuse to make my guesses and to say what came into my undecided mind — but I added that I was almost persuaded by now that the whole thing was empty, laughable nonsense. Then he told me that his father had been a passionate student of such books, and had had a friend who pursued them just as hotly; studying together, talking together, they fanned each other's ardor for this rubbish, to the point of watching for the very moments when the dumb animals around their houses gave birth, and noting the position of the sky at each — gathering, as it were, the data of this so-called art. So he said he had heard from his father that when his mother was pregnant with him — with Firminus — a slave woman of his father's friend was carrying a child at the same time; which could hardly escape her master, a man who took care to know, with scrupulous exactness, even the litters of his dogs. And so it fell out, as the two men counted the days and the hours and the smaller fractions of the hours — the one for his wife, the other for his servant — that both women delivered at the same instant: so that both men were forced to cast the identical horoscope, down to the minutest point, the one for his son, the other for his newborn slave. For as soon as the women went into labor, each man sent word to the other of what was under way in his house, and each had messengers standing ready to be dispatched the moment the birth was announced — and each, master in his own domain, had arranged to hear of it instantly. The messengers, he said, met at a point so precisely halfway between the two houses that neither man could record any different position of the stars, any different moment of time. And yet Firminus, born to high standing among his own people, ran up and down the brighter roads of the world, growing in wealth, rising in honors; while that slave went on serving his masters, the yoke never once loosened. Firminus knew him, and said so.

When I heard this and believed it — the teller being the man he was — all my resistance melted and collapsed. And first I tried to pull Firminus himself back from that curiosity. I told him that if I had inspected his constellations and meant to predict truly, I should certainly have seen in them parents eminent among their neighbors, a noble family of its city, good birth, honorable education, liberal learning. But if that slave had consulted me about the same constellations — for they were his too — then to tell him truly, I should have seen in them, in turn, the meanest of households, the condition of a slave, and everything else utterly different from the first reading and utterly remote from it. So it followed that, looking at the same charts, I would have to say different things if I spoke truly; and if I said the same things, I would speak falsely. From which it followed with total certainty that whatever is said truly from the study of constellations is said not by art but by luck; and whatever is said falsely comes not from ignorance of the art, but from luck telling lies.

With that opening made, I chewed the matter over with myself, so that none of those dotards who make their bread by this trade — men I was itching to attack and refute and laugh out of the room — could object that Firminus had told me a false story, or his father a false one to him. I turned my attention to twins. Most of them come out of the womb so close upon one another that the small interval between them — whatever force people claim it carries in the nature of things — cannot be caught by human observation, or written down at all in the charts the astrologer must inspect before he can pronounce the truth. And truth it will not be: for looking into the same charts, he would have had to say the same things about Esau and Jacob — and the same things did not happen to them. So he would speak falsely; or, if he spoke truly, he would have to give different answers while looking at the same charts. Not by art, then, but by luck would he speak the truth. For you, Lord, most just ruler of the universe, know how to work — while consulters and consulted know nothing of it — by your hidden prompting, so that each man who asks hears what he ought to hear, according to the hidden deserts of souls, out of the abyss of your just judgment. Let no man say to you, "What is this? What is that for?" Let him not say it — let him not say it. For he is a man.

By now, my helper, you had freed me from those chains; and still I was asking where evil comes from, and finding no way out. But you did not let any surge of thought carry me away from the faith by which I believed that you are; that your substance is unchangeable; that you care for men and will judge them; and that in Christ your Son, our Lord, and in the holy scriptures which the authority of your Catholic Church commends, you have laid down the way of man's salvation into the life that comes after this death. With these things safe, fixed immovably in my mind, I searched in a fever for the source of evil. What labor pains in my heart, what groaning, my God! And your ears were there, and I did not know it. As I searched hard in silence, the mute contritions of my soul were loud cries to your mercy. You knew what I suffered; no man knew it. How little of it my tongue managed to drop into the ears of my closest friends! Could the whole tumult of my soul reach them, when neither my time nor my mouth was equal to it? Yet the whole of it went up into your hearing — everything I roared out from the groaning of my heart; and my desire lay open before you, and the light of my own eyes was not with me. For the light was within, and I was outside it. The light was not in space; but I kept my mind on things that are contained in space, and I found among them no place to rest. They did not receive me in any way that let me say, "It is enough; it is well"; nor did they let me turn back to where it might have been well enough. For I stood above these things, and below you; and you are my true joy when I am subject to you, just as you had made subject to me the things you created beneath me. This was the right balance, the middle country of my salvation: to remain in your image, and, by serving you, to be master of my own body. But when I reared up in pride against you — when I charged my Lord, stiff-necked, behind the thick boss of my shield — then even those lowest things rose over me and pressed me down, and nowhere was there slack, nowhere breath. They swarmed my sight from every side, in masses and heaps; and when I tried to think my way back, the images of bodies flung themselves across the road, unbidden, as if to say: "Where do you think you are going, unworthy and unclean?" All this had grown out of my wound; for you humble the proud like a man run through with wounds. My swelling separated me from you, and my face, swollen too far, was closing my eyes.

But you, Lord, abide forever, and you will not be angry with us forever, because you take pity on dust and ashes; and it was pleasing in your sight to re-form what was deformed in me. You goaded me from within, and I was restless under the goading until you should stand certain before my inward sight. My swelling went down under the hidden hand of your medicine; and the troubled, darkened eyesight of my mind was healed, day after day, by the stinging salve of healthy griefs.

And first — wanting to show me how you resist the proud and give grace to the humble, and with how great a mercy of yours the way of humility was shown to men when your Word became flesh and lived among them — you procured for me, through a certain man bloated with the most monstrous pride, some books of the Platonists, translated out of Greek into Latin. And in them I read — not in these words, but the very same thing, urged with many arguments of many kinds — that in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; that he was in the beginning with God; that all things were made through him, and without him nothing was made; that what was made is life in him, and the life was the light of men, and the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not laid hold of it. And that the soul of man, though it bears witness to the light, is not itself the light — but the Word, God himself, is the true light that enlightens every man who comes into this world. And that he was in the world, and the world was made through him, and the world did not know him. But that he came to what was his own, and his own did not receive him — and that to all who did receive him he gave power to become children of God, to those who believe in his name: that, I did not read there.

Again, I read there that the Word, God, was born not of flesh, not of blood, not of the will of a man nor the will of the flesh, but of God. But that the Word became flesh and lived among us — that I did not read there. I traced it in those books, said in many ways and many turns of phrase, that the Son, being in the form of the Father, did not count it robbery to be equal with God, because by nature he is that very thing. But that he emptied himself, taking the form of a slave, made in the likeness of men and found in condition as a man; that he humbled himself, becoming obedient all the way to death, even death on a cross; that God therefore exalted him from the dead and gave him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bend, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that the Lord Jesus Christ is in the glory of God the Father — those books do not have. For that your only-begotten Son abides before all times and above all times, unchangeable, coeternal with you; that souls receive of his fullness in order to be blessed; that by sharing in the wisdom that abides in them they are renewed in order to be wise — that is there. But that at the appointed time he died for the ungodly; that you did not spare your only Son but handed him over for us all — that is not there. For you have hidden these things from the wise and revealed them to little children, so that those who labor and are burdened might come to him and be restored by him, because he is meek and lowly of heart; and he guides the meek in judgment, and teaches the gentle his ways, looking on our lowliness and our toil, and forgiving all our sins. But those who strut on the stage-boots of a supposedly loftier doctrine do not hear him say, "Learn from me, for I am meek and lowly of heart, and you will find rest for your souls." Even if they know God, they do not glorify him as God or give him thanks; they evaporate into their own thinking, and their foolish heart goes dark; claiming to be wise, they become fools.

And so I read there, too, that they had traded the glory of your incorruptible nature for idols and images of every kind — for the likeness of the image of corruptible man, and of birds and beasts and crawling things: that Egyptian dish, in fact, for which Esau lost his rights as the firstborn; since your own firstborn people honored the head of a four-footed beast in your place, turning back in their hearts toward Egypt, bending your image — their own soul — before the image of a calf that eats hay. These things I found there, and I did not eat them. For it pleased you, Lord, to lift from Jacob the reproach of being the lesser, that the elder should serve the younger; and you called the Gentiles into your inheritance. And I had come to you out of the Gentiles; and I set my mind on the gold you willed your people to carry out of Egypt, because it was yours, wherever it was. And to the Athenians you said, through your apostle, that in you we live and move and have our being — as some of their own writers had said; and beyond question these books came from there. But I did not set my mind on the idols of Egypt, which men were serving with gold that was yours — men who traded the truth of God for a lie, and worshipped and served the creature rather than the Creator.

Warned by these books to return to myself, I entered into my own depths, with you leading me; and I could enter, because you had become my helper. I entered, and with the eye of my soul, dim as it was, I saw — above that same eye of my soul, above my mind — the unchangeable light. Not this common light, which all flesh can look at; nor a greater light of the same kind, as if this daylight were to shine brighter and brighter until it filled the whole of space. Not that. Something else — utterly other than every light we know. Nor was it above my mind the way oil stands above water or the sky stands above the earth. It was higher, because it made me; and I was lower, because it made me. Whoever knows the truth knows that light; and whoever knows that light knows eternity. Love knows it. O eternal Truth, and true Love, and beloved Eternity! You are my God; to you I sigh day and night. When I first knew you, you lifted me up, so that I could see that there was something to see — and that I was not yet the man who could see it. And you beat back the weakness of my gaze, pouring your light into me with such force that I trembled with love and with dread; and I found that I was far away from you, in the region of unlikeness, as though I heard your voice from the height: "I am the food of the full-grown. Grow, and you will feed on me. And you will not change me into you, the way you change the food of your flesh; you will be changed into me." And I understood that you discipline man for his sin — that you made my soul waste thin as a spider's web — and I said, "Is truth nothing at all, then, just because it is not spread out through space, finite or infinite?" And you cried out from far away: "No indeed. I AM WHO I AM." And I heard it the way the heart hears, and there was no room left for doubting. I could sooner doubt that I am alive than doubt that truth exists — the truth that is seen and understood through the things that are made.

And I considered the other things, the things below you, and I saw that they neither absolutely are nor absolutely are not. They are, because they are from you; they are not, because they are not what you are. For that truly is which abides unchangeably. But for me it is good to hold close to God; for if I do not remain in him, I cannot remain in myself either. He, remaining in himself, renews all things. And you are my Lord, because you have no need of any good of mine.

And it was made plain to me that things which suffer corruption are good. If they were supremely good, they could not be corrupted; and if they were not good at all, there would be nothing in them to corrupt — for corruption harms, and unless it diminished some good, it would do no harm. So either corruption harms nothing, which cannot be; or — and this is beyond question — everything that is corrupted is being robbed of some good. But if things are robbed of all their good, they will not exist at all. For if they still exist, and can no longer be corrupted, they will be better than they were, because they now abide incorruptible — and what could be more monstrous than to say that things are made better by losing everything good in them? So: if they are stripped of all good, they will simply be nothing. Therefore, as long as they are, they are good. Therefore whatever is, is good. And this evil whose origin I was hunting is not a substance; for if it were a substance, it would be good. It would be either an incorruptible substance — a great good indeed — or a corruptible substance, which could not even be corrupted unless it were good. So I saw, and it was made plain to me, that you made all things good, and that there is no substance whatever that you did not make. And because you did not make all things equal, therefore all things are: each single thing good, and all things together very good, because our God made all things very good.

And for you there is no evil at all — and not only for you but for the whole of your creation, because outside it there is nothing that could break in and corrupt the order you have laid upon it. In its parts, some things are counted evil because they clash with certain others; but those same things agree with others still, and there they are good — and they are good in themselves. All these things that quarrel with one another still fit the lower portion of the world we call earth, which has its own cloudy and windy sky, suited to it. Far be it from me now to say, "These things should not be." If I saw nothing but these, I would indeed long for something better; but even for these alone I would owe you praise. For that you must be praised, the things of earth declare: dragons, and all the deeps; fire, hail, snow, ice, and the storm-wind that performs your word; mountains and every hill; fruit trees and all the cedars; wild beasts and all the cattle; things that creep, and birds that fly; kings of the earth and all the peoples, princes and all the judges of the earth; young men and girls, the old together with the young — let them praise your name. And since from the heavens too they praise you — praise you, our God, in the heights: all your angels, all your hosts, sun and moon, all the stars and the light, the heaven of heavens and the waters above the heavens, praising your name — I no longer longed for anything better, because I was thinking of the whole; and with a sounder judgment I held that the higher things are better than the lower, but that all things together are better than the higher things alone.

There is no health in those whom anything you have created displeases — as there was none in me, when many of the things you made displeased me. And because my soul did not dare to be displeased with my God, it refused to admit that anything that displeased it was yours. That is how it had drifted into the doctrine of two substances; and there it had no rest, and raved in words not its own. And doubling back from that, it had made itself a god stretched through the infinite measures of all space, and thought that this god was you, and set it up in its heart — and became once again the temple of its own idol, an abomination to you. But after you had stroked my head, without my knowing it, and closed my eyes so that they would stop looking at emptiness, I let go of myself a little, and my madness was lulled to sleep; and I woke in you, and saw you infinite in another way altogether — and that seeing did not come from the flesh.

And I looked back over the rest of things, and I saw that they owe it to you that they are, and that in you all of them are bounded — not as in a place, but bounded otherwise: because you hold all things in your hand, in your truth. And all things are true insofar as they are; and falsehood is nothing but this — when what is not is thought to be. And I saw that each thing agrees not only with its place but with its time; and that you, who alone are eternal, did not begin to work after unnumbered stretches of time had gone by — for all the stretches of time, those that have passed and those that will pass, could neither go nor come except because you work and you abide.

And I found by experience — and saw nothing strange in it — that the same bread that delights a healthy palate is punishment to a sick one, and that light, hateful to sore eyes, is lovely to clear ones. Your justice displeases the wicked — how much more the viper and the worm! Yet these you created good, fitted to the lower parts of your creation; and the wicked themselves are fitted to those lower parts, the more unlike you they become, and to the higher, the more they come to be like you. And I asked what wickedness is, and I found not a substance, but the twistedness of a will wrenched away from the highest substance — away from you, God — toward the lowest things: a will that throws its own inmost self out of doors and swells toward the outside.

And I marveled that I now loved you — you, and no phantom in place of you. But I could not stand still to enjoy my God. Your beauty swept me up to you, and my own weight promptly tore me away from you, and I fell back groaning among the lower things. That weight was the habit of the flesh. But the memory of you stayed with me; and I did not doubt in the least that there was one to whom I ought to cling — only that I was not yet the man who could cling to you. For the body, which is corrupted, weighs down the soul, and this earthly dwelling presses down the mind and its swarm of thoughts. And I was fully certain that your invisible things, ever since the creation of the world, are seen and understood through the things that are made — your eternal power also, and your divinity. For when I asked myself why I approved the beauty of bodies, in the heavens or on earth, and what I had at hand when I judged soundly about changeable things and said, "This ought to be so; that ought not" — asking, that is, why I judged as I judged, since judge I did — I had found the unchangeable and true eternity of truth, standing above my changeable mind. And so I climbed, step by step: from bodies to the soul that perceives through the body; from there to the soul's inner power, to which the body's senses report the world outside — the highest that the beasts can reach; from there again to the reasoning power, before whose judgment everything taken in by the bodily senses is brought. And this power, finding that in me it too was a changeable thing, raised itself up toward its own understanding. It led my thinking out of the rut of habit; it drew itself back from the mobs of contradictory phantoms, to find what light it was that had been sprinkled on it when it cried out — with no shred of doubt — that the unchangeable is to be preferred to the changeable; for it must somehow have known the unchangeable itself, or it could never have preferred it with such certainty. And it arrived at THAT WHICH IS, in the stroke of one trembling glance. Then indeed I saw your invisible things, understood through the things that are made. But I had no strength to hold my gaze there. My weakness was beaten back, and I was returned to my old habits, carrying with me only a loving memory — the longing, so to speak, for a dish I had caught the fragrance of, but could not yet eat.

So I looked for a way to gather strength enough to enjoy you, and I did not find it until I embraced the mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who is over all things, God blessed forever — calling to me and saying, "I am the way, the truth, and the life" — and mixing into our flesh the food I was too weak to take. For the Word became flesh so that your Wisdom, through whom you created all things, might become milk for our infancy. I did not yet hold my Lord Jesus — I was not yet humbled enough to hold the humble one — and I did not yet know what his weakness had to teach. For your Word, the eternal Truth, towering above the highest parts of your creation, lifts up to itself those who have been brought low; and here below it built itself a humble house out of our clay, so that those who needed bringing low might be pulled loose from themselves and carried across to it: healing their swelling, feeding their love, so that they would not stride on farther in self-confidence, but rather consent to grow weak, seeing at their feet the Godhead made weak by taking our coat of skin — and so that, worn out at last, they would throw themselves down on that weakness, and it would rise, and lift them up.

But I thought otherwise. I thought of Christ my Lord only as a man of surpassing wisdom, whom no one could equal — above all because, being wonderfully born of a virgin to give an example of despising temporal things for the sake of immortality, he seemed, through God's care for us, to have earned so towering an authority as a teacher. But what mystery there was in "the Word became flesh," I could not even begin to guess. From what has been handed down in writing about him — that he ate, drank, slept, walked, was glad, was sorrowful, taught — I had learned only this much: that flesh did not cleave to your Word without a human soul and a human mind. Everyone knows this who knows the unchangeableness of your Word; and I knew it now, as well as I could, and did not doubt it at all. For now to move the limbs of the body by will, and now not; now to be moved by some feeling, and now not; now to deliver wise judgments through signs, and now to keep silence — these belong to a soul and mind that change. And if the writings said these things of him falsely, the whole of them would stand accused of lying, and no faith that saves mankind would be left in those books. But because they were written truthfully, I acknowledged in Christ a complete man: not a man's body only, nor a body with a soul that senses but does not reason, but a whole man; and I judged him greater than everyone else, not as Truth in person, but by some great excellence of his human nature and a more perfect share in wisdom. Alypius, for his part, supposed that Catholics believed in a God so clothed in flesh that in Christ, besides God and flesh, there was no soul at all — he did not think they ascribed a human mind to him. And since he was firmly persuaded that the deeds recorded of Christ could only have been done by a living and reasoning creature, he moved the more slowly toward the Christian faith itself. Later, when he understood that this was the error of the Apollinarian heretics, he rejoiced in the Catholic faith and settled into it. And I confess it was later still that I learned how, in the words "the Word became flesh," Catholic truth is pried apart from the falsehood of Photinus. The refutation of heretics throws into relief what your Church holds and what sound doctrine contains. For there had to be heresies, too, so that the tested might stand out among the weak.

But at that time, after reading those books of the Platonists, once they had prompted me to look for a truth without a body, I caught sight of your invisible things, understood through the things that are made; and though I was driven back, I sensed the thing that the darkness of my soul would not let me contemplate. I was certain that you are, and that you are infinite — yet not poured out through space, finite or infinite; that you truly are, always the same, never becoming other or otherwise in any part or by any motion; and that all other things are from you, on this single surest proof: that they are. Of all this I was certain — and far too weak to enjoy you. I chattered away like an expert; and if I had not gone looking for your way in Christ our Savior, I would have ended not expert but expired. For I had begun by now to want to seem wise. Full of my own punishment, I shed no tears; I swelled, instead, with knowledge. Where was the love that builds on the foundation of humility, which is Christ Jesus? When would those books have taught me that? I believe you willed me to run into them before I had studied your scriptures, so that the way they marked me would be stamped into my memory; so that later, when I had been gentled by your books and your fingers were dressing my wounds, I could tell apart, and know the distance between, presumption and confession — between those who see where they must go but not how to get there, and the way itself, which leads not just to a sight of the country of blessedness but to a home in it. For if I had been formed first by your holy scriptures, and you had grown sweet to me in the long familiar use of them, and I had fallen on those volumes afterward — they might have torn me off the solid ground of piety. Or, even if I had held on to the healthy disposition I had drawn in, I might have supposed that a man could draw the same from those books alone, if those alone he studied.

So I seized — how greedily! — the venerable writings of your Spirit, and before all the rest, the apostle Paul. And the hard questions died away: the places where he had once seemed to me to contradict himself, where the run of his words seemed to quarrel with the testimony of the law and the prophets. That pure speech showed me a single face, and I learned to rejoice with trembling. I began; and whatever truth I had read in those other books, I found here with the praise of your grace added: so that the man who sees may not boast as though he had not received — not only what he sees, but the very power of seeing (for what does he have that he did not receive?) — and so that he may be not only warned to see you, who are always the same, but also healed, so that he can hold you; and so that the man who cannot see from far off may still walk the road by which he will come, and see, and hold. For though a man delights in the law of God in his inmost self, what will he do with that other law in his members, which fights against the law of his mind and drags him captive under the law of sin that is in his members? For you are just, Lord; and we have sinned, we have done wrong, we have dealt wickedly, and your hand has grown heavy upon us, and we have justly been handed over to the old sinner, the warden of death — because he talked our will into the likeness of his own will, that will by which he did not stand fast in your truth. What is wretched man to do? Who will free him from the body of this death, except your grace, through Jesus Christ our Lord — whom you begot coeternal with you, and created at the beginning of your ways; in whom the prince of this world found nothing that deserved death, and killed him anyway; and so the bond of debt that stood against us was wiped out? None of this is in those books. Those pages nowhere carry the face of this devotion: the tears of confession; your sacrifice — a troubled spirit, a broken and humbled heart; the salvation of your people; the city made ready as a bride; the pledge of the Holy Spirit; the cup of our ransom. No one sings there: "Shall not my soul be still under God? For from him comes my salvation; he is my God and my savior, my defender: I shall not be shaken anymore." No one there hears him calling: "Come to me, all you who labor." They scorn to learn from him, because he is meek and lowly of heart; for you have hidden these things from the wise and the clever, and revealed them to little children. For it is one thing to see the country of peace from a wooded ridge, and find no road to it, and struggle toward it in vain through trackless ground, ambushed on every side by runaways and deserters with their captain, the lion and the dragon; and another thing to hold the road that leads there, guarded by the care of the heavenly commander, where no deserters from the heavenly army lie in wait to rob — for they shun that road like torment. All this went down into the marrow of me, in wonderful ways, as I read the least of your apostles; and I had gazed long at your works, and trembled.
