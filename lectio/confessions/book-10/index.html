Confessions, Book X

The Palaces of Memory

Book X10 of 13

Let me know you, Lord — you who know me. Let me know you as I am fully known. Strength of my soul, enter it and shape it to yourself, so that you can have and hold it without spot or wrinkle. This is my hope; that is why I am speaking, and in this hope I rejoice, whenever my joy is a healthy joy. As for the other things of this life — the more we weep over them, the less they deserve our tears; and the more they deserve our tears, the less we weep. Look: you love the truth, and the one who does the truth comes to the light. I want to do the truth — in my heart, before you, in confession; and in this writing, before many witnesses.

And from you, Lord, before whose eyes the pit of human conscience lies naked, what could stay hidden in me even if I refused to confess it? I would be hiding you from myself, not myself from you. But now — my groaning is the proof that I am sick of what I am — you shine out, and you please me, and I love and long for you; so that I blush at myself, and throw my old self away, and choose you, and please neither you nor myself except in you. To you, then, Lord, I lie open, whatever I am. What I gain by confessing to you, I have said already. And I do not make the confession with the body's words and sounds, but with the soul's words, with the shout of my thinking, which your ear knows. When I am evil, confessing to you is nothing but being displeased with myself; when I am good, confessing to you is nothing but refusing to take the credit — because you, Lord, bless the righteous, but first you take the unrighteous and make them righteous. So my confession, my God, is made in your sight silently — and not silently. In sound it is silent; in feeling it cries out. For I never say a true word to other people that you have not heard from me first, and you never hear such a word from me that you have not first said to me.

Then what do other people have to do with it? Why should they hear my confessions, as though they were going to heal everything that is sick in me? A curious breed, eager to know someone else's life, lazy about mending their own. Why do they want to hear from me what I am, when they refuse to hear from you what they are? And when they hear me talk about myself, how do they know I am telling the truth, since no one knows what goes on in a person except that person's own spirit within? But if they heard from you about themselves, they could not say, "The Lord is lying." For what is hearing from you about themselves except coming to know themselves? And who can come to know a thing and say, "It is false," unless he himself is lying? But because love believes all things — at least among those it binds together into one — I too, Lord, will confess to you in such a way that people can hear it, though I cannot prove to them that my confession is true. Those whose ears love opens to me — they will believe me.

But you, physician of my inmost self, make clear to me what good comes of doing this. When the confessions of my past sins — sins you have forgiven and covered over, to make me blessed in you, remaking my soul by faith and by your sacrament — when those confessions are read and heard, they rouse the heart out of the sleep of despair, out of saying "I can't," and wake it in the love of your mercy and the sweetness of your grace — the grace by which every weak person is made strong, once it brings home to him his own weakness. And good people love to hear about the past sins of those who are rid of them now: not because the sins are evils, but because they were, and are no more. So with what profit, my Lord and God — you to whom my conscience confesses every day, more secure in the hope of your mercy than in its own innocence — with what profit, I ask, do I confess in this book, before you and before other people too, not what I was, but what I am now? The profit of the first kind I have seen and described. But what I am now, right now, in the very moment of these confessions — this is what many want to know: people who have known me and people who have not, who have heard something from me or about me. Their ear is not pressed to my heart, where I am whatever I am. So they want to hear me confess what I am inside, where neither their eye nor their ear nor their mind can reach; and they are ready to believe it — but will they know it? Love, which makes them good, tells them I am not lying about myself; and the love in them believes me.

But what do they gain by hearing it? Do they want to celebrate with me, when they hear how near to you I have come by your gift — and to pray for me, when they hear how far I am dragged back by my own weight? To people like that I will show myself. It is no small profit, my Lord and God, if thanks are offered to you by many on our account, and many plead with you for us. Let a brother's mind love in me what you teach is worth loving, and grieve in me what you teach is worth grieving. Let it be a brother's mind that does this — not an outsider's, not the minds of those alien children whose mouths speak emptiness and whose right hand is a right hand of fraud — but a brother's mind, which rejoices over me when it approves of me and grieves over me when it does not, because approving or not, it loves me. To such people I will show myself. Let them breathe easy at my good and sigh at my evil. My good deeds are your arrangements and your gifts; my evil ones are my own faults and your judgments. Let them breathe easy at the one and sigh at the other, and let hymn and weeping rise together into your sight from my brothers' hearts, your censers. And you, Lord, delighting in the incense of your holy temple — have mercy on me according to your great mercy, for your own name's sake; and since you never abandon what you have begun, complete what is incomplete in me.

This is the profit of confessing not what I was, but what I am: to confess it not only before you, in a hidden gladness with trembling and a hidden sorrow with hope, but in the ears of the believing children of men as well — companions in my joy and sharers in my mortality, my fellow citizens, fellow pilgrims with me: those who went ahead, those who will follow, those walking beside me on the road. They are your servants and my brothers, whom you have willed to be your sons — my masters, whom you have commanded me to serve if I want to live with you and from you. And this word of yours would be a small thing if it only gave the command and did not lead the way in doing it. So I do the same, in deed and in word; I do it under your wings — and the danger would be far too great, if my soul were not sheltered under your wings and my weakness known to you. I am a small child, but my Father lives forever, and my protector is enough for me. For he who begot me is the same one who keeps me safe; you yourself are all my good — you, the Almighty, who are with me even before I am with you. To those you command me to serve, then, I will show not what I was, but what I am now, and what I still am. But I do not even judge myself. This is how I want to be heard.

For you, Lord, are the one who judges me. No one knows what is in a person except the person's own spirit within him — and yet there is something in a person that even his own spirit does not know. But you, Lord, know everything about him, for you made him. As for me: though in your sight I despise myself and count myself dust and ashes, I know something about you that I do not know about myself. Truly, we see now through a glass, darkly — not yet face to face. So as long as I am journeying away from you, I am more present to myself than to you; and yet I know that you can in no way be violated, while I do not know which temptations I can withstand and which I cannot. And there is hope, because you are faithful: you do not let us be tempted beyond what we can bear, but with the temptation you make a way out as well, so that we can stand it. So let me confess what I know of myself, and confess too what I do not know — because even what I know of myself I know only because you shine on me, and what I do not know I will not know until my darkness turns to noon before your face.

Not with a wavering but with a settled certainty, Lord, I love you. You struck my heart with your word, and I loved you. And heaven and earth and everything in them — look, from every side they tell me to love you, and they never stop telling everyone, so that no one has an excuse. But deeper than that: you will have mercy on whom you will have mercy, and show compassion to whom you will show compassion — otherwise heaven and earth are singing your praises to the deaf. And what do I love when I love you? Not the beauty of a body, not the glory of a season, not the brightness of light — so kind to these eyes — not the sweet melodies of every kind of song, not the fragrance of flowers and oils and spices, not manna or honey, not limbs that welcome the body's embrace. These are not what I love when I love my God. And yet I do love a kind of light, a kind of voice, a kind of fragrance, a kind of food, a kind of embrace when I love my God: light, voice, fragrance, food, embrace of the self inside me — where a light shines into my soul that no place can hold, where a voice sounds that time cannot snatch away, where a fragrance drifts that no wind can scatter, where a flavor stays that no eating makes less, where a closeness holds that no satiety can pull apart. This is what I love when I love my God.

And what is this? I asked the earth, and it said, "I am not he," and everything in it confessed the same. I asked the sea and the deeps and the creatures that crawl and swim, and they answered, "We are not your God. Look above us." I asked the blowing winds, and the whole air with all its inhabitants answered, "Anaximenes was wrong. I am not God." I asked the sky, the sun, the moon, the stars: "Neither are we," they said, "the God you are looking for." And I said to all these things standing around the doors of my flesh, "Tell me about my God — you who are not he — tell me something about him." And they cried out with a great voice: "He made us." My question was my gazing at them; their answer was their beauty. Then I turned toward myself and said to myself, "And you — who are you?" And I answered: "A man." Here at my disposal are body and soul, one outside, one within. Which of these should I have used to look for my God? I had already searched for him with the body, from earth to sky, as far as I could send the rays of my eyes out as messengers. But the inner one is better. All the body's messengers report back to it; it presides and judges over the answers of sky and earth and everything in them, all of them saying, "We are not God," and, "He made us." The inner self knows these things through the service of the outer self. I, the one within, knew them — I, the mind, through my body's senses. I asked the whole huge frame of the world about my God, and it answered me: "I am not he, but he made me."

Doesn't this beauty stand visible to everyone whose senses are sound? Then why doesn't it say the same thing to all? Animals small and great see it, but they cannot question it, because no reason has been set over their senses to judge what the senses report. Human beings can question it — so that the invisible things of God are seen and understood through the things he has made — but by loving those things they fall under them, and once under them, they can no longer judge. And the world does not answer its questioners unless they judge. It does not change its voice — that is, its look — appearing one way to this person who merely sees and another way to that person who sees and questions; it appears the same to both. But to one it is mute, and to the other it speaks. Or rather, it speaks to everyone, but only they understand who take its voice in from outside and compare it with the truth inside. For the truth tells me: your God is not heaven or earth or any body at all. Their own nature says it. Anyone can see: a body is a mass, smaller in the part than in the whole. Even you, my soul, are better than that — I am telling you — for you animate the mass of your body, giving it life, and no body can do that for a body. But your God is the life even of your life.

What then do I love when I love my God? Who is he, there above the summit of my soul? Through my soul itself I will climb up to him. I will pass beyond the power that fastens me to my body and fills its frame with life: that is not the power by which I could find my God. If it were, then the horse and the mule, which have no understanding, could find him too, since the same power gives life to their bodies as well. There is another power, by which I not only give life to the flesh the Lord fashioned for me but give it sense: ordering the eye not to hear and the ear not to see, but giving me the one to see through and the other to hear through, and to each of the other senses its own post and its own duty; and through all their different offices I act — I, the one mind. But I will pass beyond this power of mine as well, for the horse and the mule have this one too: they also perceive through the body.

So I will pass beyond this power of my nature too, climbing step by step toward him who made me. And I come to the fields and vast palaces of memory, where the treasures of countless images are stored, carried in from everything the senses have perceived. There too is laid up all our thinking — whatever enlarges or shrinks or otherwise rearranges the things the senses touched — and everything else that has been handed over there for safekeeping, everything that forgetting has not yet swallowed and buried. When I am in there, I call for whatever I want brought out. Some things come at once. Some take longer, as if they were being fetched up from deeper vaults. Some come charging out in a mob, and while something else entirely is being asked for, they leap into the middle, as if to say, "Maybe you want us?" I wave them away from the face of my remembering with the hand of my heart, until the thing I want comes clear of the cloud and steps out of hiding into view. Other things come easily, in unbroken sequence, exactly as called for — each one in front giving way to the one behind, and giving way, sinking out of sight, ready to come again when I want it. All of which happens whenever I recite something by heart.

There everything is kept sorted by kind, each thing carried in through its own entrance: light and all colors and the shapes of bodies through the eyes; every sort of sound through the ears; every smell through the door of the nostrils; every taste through the door of the mouth; and by the sense that runs through the whole body, what is hard and what is soft, hot or cold, smooth or rough, heavy or light, outside the body or inside it. The great harbor of memory takes all of this in, in its countless secret, unmappable folds, to be brought out and reclaimed when needed — everything entering by its own gate and laid away. Not that the things themselves go in: only the images of the things perceived stand ready in there, for thought to recall. And who can say how these images were formed, even though it is plain which sense caught each one and stored it? Sitting in darkness and in silence, I can bring out colors in my memory if I like, and tell white from black and any shade from any other; sounds do not come barging in to disturb the picture my eyes drew in and I am now considering — though the sounds are there too, lying apart in their own storage, as it were. I can call for them as well, and they appear at once; and with my tongue still and my throat silent I sing as much as I please, and those images of color, present all the while, do not push in and interrupt while I am handling this other treasure, the one that flowed in through my ears. And so with everything else the other senses hauled in and piled up: I recall each one at will. I tell the breath of lilies from violets while smelling nothing. I prefer honey to sweet wine, smooth to rough, while tasting nothing, touching nothing — only remembering.

All this I do inside, in the enormous hall of my memory. There I have sky and earth and sea at hand, with everything I have ever perceived in them — except what I have forgotten. There I meet myself, too: I recall myself — what I did, when and where I did it, and how I felt while doing it. Everything is there that I remember, whether from my own experience or on someone else's word. And out of the same hoard I stitch together, over and over, new and ever newer likenesses of things I have experienced, or believed on the strength of experience, into what is past; and from these I spin out future actions, events, hopes — and I contemplate all of it, again, as present. "I will do this, and I will do that," I say to myself, inside this huge inward space of my mind, crowded with the images of so many great things, "and this or that will follow." "Oh, if only this would happen — or that!" "God keep this away — and that!" I say these things to myself, and as I say them, the images of everything I name stand by, out of the same treasury of memory. If they were missing, I could say none of it at all.

Great is this power of memory — immensely great, my God: a vast, unbounded inner chamber. Who has ever touched its floor? And yet this is a power of my own mind, and belongs to my nature — and I myself cannot hold all that I am. So the mind is too narrow to contain itself. Then where does the part it doesn't contain go? Outside itself rather than in itself? Then how is it not contained? Wonder rises up in me at this; astonishment takes hold of me. And people go traveling to marvel at the heights of mountains, the enormous waves of the sea, the broad falls of rivers, the circuit of the ocean, the wheeling of stars — and pass themselves by without a second look. It does not amaze them that while I was naming all those things I was not seeing them with my eyes; and yet I could not have named them unless I were seeing, inwardly, in my memory, the mountains and waves and rivers and stars I have seen, and the ocean I take on trust — and seeing them at the same enormous scale as if I were looking at them outside. But I did not swallow them into myself by looking at them when I saw them with my eyes. It is not the things that are in me, but their images. And I know which sense of my body impressed each one.

And even these are not all that the immeasurable capacity of my memory carries. Here too is everything from the liberal arts that I have learned and not yet forgotten, set back somewhere further in — in a place that is not a place. And here it is not images I carry, but the things themselves. What grammar is, what skill in debate is, how many kinds of questions there are — whatever I know of such things is in my memory in a peculiar way: not as an image left behind while the thing stays outside; not like a voice that sounded and faded, leaving a trace through the ears by which it can be replayed as if it were sounding when it sounds no more; not like a smell that passes and thins into the wind, brushing the nostrils and dispatching an image of itself into memory, which remembering renews; not like food, which has no taste at all once in the belly and yet still tastes, in a fashion, in memory; not like anything the body feels by touch, which memory can still shape once the thing is far away. None of these things is taken into memory. Only their images are seized, with astonishing speed, laid away in astonishing storerooms, and by remembering — astonishingly — brought out.

But when I hear that there are three kinds of questions — whether a thing is, what it is, and what kind it is — I hold the images of the sounds those words are made of, and I know the sounds crossed the air with a noise and now no longer exist. But the things themselves that the sounds signify, I never touched with any sense of my body, never glimpsed anywhere but in my mind — and what I laid up in memory was not their images but the things themselves. Let them tell me, if they can, how they got in. I go around all the doorways of my flesh and find none they could have entered by. The eyes say, "If they had color, we announced them." The ears say, "If they made a sound, we reported it." The nostrils say, "If they had a smell, they came through us." The sense of taste says, "If there was no flavor, don't ask me." Touch says, "If it wasn't a body, I never handled it; and if I never handled it, I never gave word of it." Then where did these things come from? How did they get into my memory? I do not know. When I learned them, I was not trusting another mind's word for them: I recognized them in my own, approved them as true, and gave them into its keeping, setting them where I could bring them out whenever I wished. So they were there even before I learned them — but not in my memory. Where, then? And why did I recognize them when they were spoken, and say, "That's it — that's true," unless they were already in my memory, but so far back, so buried in its deepest recesses, that if someone had not prodded them up I might never have been able to think them at all?

So we find that learning things like these — things whose images we do not drink in through the senses, but which we perceive within, by themselves, as they are, without images — amounts to just this: by thinking, we gather up what memory held here and there and in no order, and by attending we see to it that these things are set, so to speak, within easy reach in the same memory where they had lain scattered and ignored — so that now they come readily to a mind grown familiar with them. And how many things of this kind my memory carries — things already found and set within reach, things we are said to have learned and to know! If I stop revisiting them for even a short stretch of time, down they sink again, gliding off into the deeper recesses, and they have to be thought out again, as good as new, from the same place — there is no other home for them — and rounded up again so they can be known. In other words, they have to be collected out of their scattering. Hence the Latin word for thinking, cogitation: cogo — I gather — stands to cogito — I keep gathering — as ago stands to agito, as facio to factito. But the mind has claimed this word as its own: what is collected — that is, pulled together — in the mind, and nowhere but the mind, is what we properly say is cogitated: thought.

The memory also holds the relations and laws of numbers and dimensions, past counting — and none of them was stamped in by any bodily sense, for they have no color, no sound, no smell, no taste, no feel. I have heard the sounds of the words that name them when they are discussed, but the sounds are one thing and the things another: the sounds are different in Greek than in Latin, but the things are not Greek or Latin or any tongue at all. I have seen the lines architects draw, fine as a thread of spider's silk; but the lines themselves are something else — not images of the ones my body's eye showed me. Whoever knows them, knows them inside himself, without any thought of a body of any kind. I have perceived with every sense of my body the numbers of the things we count; but the numbers we count with are something else again. They are not images of the counted things; they simply, and in their own right, are. Let whoever cannot see them laugh at me for saying so; and while he laughs at me, I will feel sorry for him.

All these things I hold in memory, and how I learned them I hold in memory too. And I have heard, and hold in memory, many arguments falsely urged against them — false arguments, but my remembering of them is not false; and I remember, further, that I have told the true apart from the false, and I see that telling them apart now is a different thing from remembering that I told them apart often before, each time I thought them over. So I remember having understood these things many times; and what I sort out and understand now, I stow in memory, so that later I may remember having understood it today. I even remember having remembered — and if, later on, I recall that I was able to remember these things now, it will be by the power of memory that I recall it.

The same memory holds the feelings of my mind — not the way the mind holds them while it feels them, but in a very different way, a way proper to the power of memory itself. Without being glad, I remember having been glad. Without sorrow, I recall my old sorrow. Without fear, I look back on what I once feared; without desire, I remember an old desire. Sometimes it is even reversed: I remember a finished sadness gladly, and a finished gladness sadly. No surprise, if it were a matter of the body: mind is one thing, body another, and if I gladly remember a bodily pain now past, that is nothing strange. But memory is mind — when we hand someone a thing to keep, we say, "Keep it in mind"; when we forget, we say, "It wasn't in my mind," and, "It slipped my mind," calling memory itself mind. Since that is so, how is it that when I gladly remember my past sadness, my mind holds gladness and my memory holds sadness — and the mind is glad from the gladness in it, but the memory is not sad from the sadness in it? Does memory perhaps not belong to the mind? Who would say that? No: memory is something like the mind's stomach, and gladness and sadness are like sweet and bitter food. Committed to memory, they pass, so to speak, into the stomach — a place where they can be stored, but not tasted. It sounds ridiculous to compare the two; and yet they are not wholly unlike.

But look — it is from my memory that I produce it when I say the mind has four disturbances: desire, joy, fear, sorrow. Whatever I can argue about them — dividing each into its species, defining each — it is in memory that I find what to say, and from memory that I bring it out. And none of these disturbances disturbs me while I remember them; and before I recalled them and brought them back, they were already there — which is exactly why remembering could fetch them. Perhaps, then, they are brought up out of memory the way cud is brought up from a ruminant's stomach. But then why doesn't the man discussing them — remembering them — taste the sweetness of joy or the bitterness of grief in the mouth of his thinking? Or is this where the likeness fails — because the two aren't alike in everything? Who would ever willingly speak of these things, if every time we said "grief" or "fear" we were forced to grieve or be afraid? And yet we could not speak of them at all unless we found inside our memory not just the sounds of their names, printed there from images the bodily senses made, but notions of the things themselves — notions that never came in through any doorway of the flesh, but that the mind itself, living through its own passions, entrusted to memory; or that memory kept for itself even without being given them.

Whether all this works through images or not, who can easily say? I name a stone, I name the sun, while the things themselves are absent from my senses; their images, of course, stand ready in my memory. I name a pain of the body: the pain is not here, while nothing hurts; yet if its image were not in my memory, I would not know what I was saying, and could not, in an argument, tell pain apart from pleasure. I name bodily health: while healthy, I have the thing itself with me; yet unless its image too were in my memory, there is no way I could recall what the sound of the name means — and the sick, when health is named, would not recognize what was said unless the power of memory held that image, even with the thing itself gone from the body. I name the numbers we count with: and it is they themselves, not their images, that are in my memory. I name the image of the sun, and that image is in my memory — not an image of the image, but the image itself: that is what stands by when I remember. I name memory, and I recognize what I name — and where do I recognize it but in memory itself? Can memory, too, be present to itself through its image, rather than through itself?

And when I name forgetting, and recognize what I name just the same — how could I recognize it if I did not remember it? I don't mean the sound of the word; I mean the thing it signifies. If I had forgotten that, the sound would be empty to me. So when I remember memory, memory is present to itself through itself. But when I remember forgetting, both memory and forgetting are present: memory, by which I remember; forgetting, which I remember. But what is forgetting except the absence of memory? Then how is it present for me to remember, when, while it is present, I cannot remember? Yet if we hold what we remember in memory — and if, without remembering forgetting, we could never hear the word and recognize the thing it means — then forgetting is held in memory. So it is present, so that we don't forget it — and when it is present, we forget. Do we conclude that when we remember it, forgetting is present to memory not by itself but by its image — since, if it were present by itself, it would make us forget, not remember? Who will ever get to the bottom of this? Who can grasp how it works?

Lord, I am laboring at it — truly — and laboring on myself: I have become for myself a soil of hard work and heavy sweat. For we are not surveying the tracts of the sky now, or measuring the distances between stars, or asking after the balance of the earth. It is I who remember — I, the mind. It is no great wonder if whatever I am not lies far from me. But what is nearer to me than I? And look — the power of my own memory is beyond my understanding, when without it I could not even say my own name. What am I to say, when I am certain that I remember forgetting? Shall I say that what I remember is not in my memory? Or that forgetting is in my memory precisely so that I won't forget? Both are absurd beyond words. Is there a third way? Can I say it is the image of forgetting my memory holds, and not forgetting itself? But how could I say that either — since, for the image of anything to be printed in memory, the thing itself must first be present for the image to be taken from it? That is how I remember Carthage; that is how I remember every place I have been, the faces of people I have seen, everything the other senses reported, the health or the sickness of the body itself. When those things were present, memory took images from them — images that stayed with me to be looked at and turned over in my mind when I remembered the absent things. So if forgetting is held in memory by its image and not by itself, then forgetting itself must once have been present, for its image to be captured. But when it was present, how did it write its image into memory — when forgetting, by its mere presence, erases whatever it finds already written there? And yet — somehow, in some way past understanding and past explaining — I am certain of this: I remember forgetting itself, the very thing that buries whatever we remember.

Great is the power of memory. Something in it makes me shudder, my God — a deep and boundless multiplicity. And this thing is my mind; this thing is I. What am I, then, my God? What kind of nature am I? A life endlessly various, many-formed, immense beyond measure. Look: in the fields and caves and caverns of my memory — countless, and countlessly filled with countless kinds of things, present through their images, like all bodies; or in themselves, like the arts; or through who knows what notions and notations, like the mind's feelings, which memory keeps even while the mind is not feeling them, though whatever is in the memory is in the mind — through all of this I run; I dart back and forth; I dive as deep as I can go, and nowhere is there a bottom. Such is the power of memory; such is the power of life, in a human being living toward death. What shall I do, then, my God — you, my true life? I will pass beyond even this power of mine called memory. I will pass beyond it and keep reaching toward you, sweet Light. What are you telling me? Here I am, climbing through my own mind toward you, who abide above me. I will pass beyond even this power of mine called memory, wanting to touch you where you can be touched, to hold fast to you where holding fast is possible. Even beasts and birds have memory; otherwise they could never find their dens and nests again, or do any of the things they are used to doing — and without memory, nothing could become usual at all. So I will pass beyond memory too, to reach him who set me apart from the four-footed animals and made me wiser than the birds of the sky. I will pass beyond memory too — and where will I find you, my truly good, my safe sweetness? Where will I find you? If I find you outside my memory, I have already forgotten you. And how will I find you at all, if I do not remember you?

The woman who lost her coin and hunted for it with a lamp would never have found it unless she had remembered it. When it turned up, how would she have known it was hers, if she did not remember it? I remember hunting for many a lost thing and finding it, and I know this from those hunts: while I was searching, and someone asked me, "Is this it? Is that it?" I kept saying, "No — not that," until the thing itself was put in front of me. Had I not remembered it — whatever it was — I would not have found it even when it was handed to me, because I would not have recognized it. And that is how it always goes when we hunt for a lost thing and find it. If something drops out of sight but not out of memory — any visible thing — its image is kept within, and we hunt for it until it comes back into view; and when it is found, it is recognized by the image within. We do not say we have found the lost thing unless we recognize it, and we cannot recognize it unless we remember it. It was lost to the eyes; the memory held it.

But what about when memory itself loses something — as happens when we forget and grope to recollect? Where do we hunt, in the end, but in memory itself? And there, if one thing is offered up in place of another, we keep refusing it until the thing we want appears; and when it appears, we say, "That's it." We wouldn't say that without recognizing it, and we couldn't recognize it without remembering it — and yet we had, quite certainly, forgotten it. Or had it not slipped away whole? Was part of it still held, and by that part the other part hunted — the memory sensing that it was not carrying its full accustomed load, and, limping on its maimed habit, demanding the missing piece back? It is like seeing or thinking of a man we know while his name escapes us: we grope for it, and any other name that presents itself refuses to connect, because we never used to think it together with him; so it is rejected, until the one arrives on which our knowledge settles evenly, as on its familiar object. And where does that name arrive from, if not from memory itself? Even when we recognize it because someone else has reminded us, it comes from there: we do not accept it as new information, but agree, remembering, that yes, that was the name. If the name had been wiped from the mind completely, we would not remember it even when prompted. For if we can so much as remember that we forgot a thing, we have not entirely forgotten it. So a thing utterly forgotten we cannot even hunt for as lost.

How then do I hunt for you, Lord? For when I hunt for you, my God, I am hunting for the happy life. Let me hunt for you so that my soul may live: my body lives from my soul, and my soul lives from you. How then do I hunt for the happy life? It is not mine until I can say, there where it ought to be said: "Enough. It is here." So how do I hunt for it? By remembering, as though I had forgotten it but still knew that I had forgotten? Or through the appetite to learn it as something unknown — something I either never knew, or forgot so thoroughly that I do not even remember forgetting it? But isn't the happy life the thing everyone wants — everyone, without a single exception? Where did they come to know it, that they want it so? Where did they see it, that they love it? Somehow we do possess it, though how, I cannot say. There is a sense in which a person possesses it and is happy in fact; and there are those who are happy in hope. These hold it in a lesser way than those who hold the thing itself, but they are better off than those who are happy neither in fact nor in hope — and even these, if they did not possess it in some fashion, would not want so badly to be happy; and want it they do, that much is beyond all doubt. Somehow they have come to know it, and so they hold it by some kind of knowledge I cannot name. And I am working out whether this knowledge is in the memory — because if it is, then we were happy once: whether each of us singly, or all of us in that man who first sinned, in whom we all died and from whom we were all born into misery, I do not ask now. I ask only this: is the happy life in the memory? We would not love it if we did not know it. We hear the words, and every one of us admits to wanting the thing itself; it is not the sound that pleases us. A Greek who hears the words in Latin gets no pleasure, since he does not know what was said; we Latins are pleased, as he would be if he heard them in Greek — because the thing itself is neither Greek nor Latin, this thing that Greeks and Latins and speakers of every other tongue strain to reach. So it is known to all of them; and if they could all be asked in one voice, "Do you want to be happy?" — they would answer, with no hesitation at all, "We do." And that could not happen unless the thing itself, and not just its name, were held in their memory.

But held how? Not the way a man who has seen Carthage remembers Carthage: the happy life is not seen with the eyes, because it is not a body. The way we remember numbers, then? No — whoever has numbers in his knowledge is done seeking them; but the happy life we hold in knowledge, and therefore love, and go on seeking all the same, so that we may be happy. The way we remember eloquence, then? No. It is true that at this word too, some people call the thing to mind who are not yet eloquent, and many of them long to be — so the thing is evidently in their knowledge. But they have watched other people being eloquent, through the senses of the body; they took delight in it, and they want to be the same. (Without some inward knowledge they would feel no delight, and without the delight, no desire.) But the happy life is not something we experience in other people by any sense of the body. The way we remember joy, then? Perhaps. Even while sad, I remember my joy — the way, while miserable, I remember the happy life. And it was never by any sense of the body that I saw or heard or smelled or tasted or touched my joy: I experienced it in my mind when I rejoiced, and knowledge of it stuck fast in my memory, so that I can recall it — sometimes with disgust, sometimes with longing, depending on what the joys were that I remember. Even filthy things once soaked me in a kind of joy, and recalling it now, I loathe it and curse it. Other joys were in good and honorable things, and those I recall with longing — even when the things themselves are gone; and then it is with sadness that I recall the old joy.

Where, then, and when did I experience my happy life, that I should remember it, love it, long for it? And not just I, or a few others with me: every last one of us wants to be happy. Unless we knew the thing with certain knowledge, we could not want it with so certain a will. Yet look at this: ask two men whether they want to serve in the army, and one may say yes, the other no; ask the same two whether they want to be happy, and each says yes, instantly, without a shadow of doubt — and the one wants to serve, and the other wants not to, for no other reason than to be happy. Is it that different people take joy in different things? All agree in wanting to be happy, just as they would all agree, if asked, in wanting joy — and joy itself is what they call the happy life. One reaches it here, another there, but the thing all of them are straining to arrive at is one and the same: joy. And since joy is a thing nobody can claim never to have experienced, it is found in the memory, and recognized whenever the words "the happy life" are heard.

Far be it, Lord — far from the heart of your servant confessing to you — far be it from me to count myself happy for any joy whatever. There is a joy not given to the godless — given only to those who worship you freely, whose joy is you yourself. And the happy life is this: to rejoice toward you, about you, on account of you. This is it, and there is no other. Whoever thinks there is another is chasing another joy, and not the true one. Still, even his will has not torn itself free of every image of joy.

It is not settled, then, that everyone wants to be happy — for those who do not want to find their joy in you, which is the only happy life, do not, in fact, want the happy life. Or does everyone want it, but — because the flesh desires against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh, so that people cannot do what they would — they drop down onto what they can do, and make do with it, because what they cannot do they do not will hard enough to make themselves able? Ask anyone: would he rather rejoice in truth or in falsehood? He will say "in truth" with as little hesitation as he says he wants to be happy. And in fact the happy life is joy in the truth — that is, joy in you, who are the Truth, God my light, salvation of my face, my God. This happy life everyone wants; this life, the only happy one, everyone wants; joy in the truth, everyone wants. I have met plenty of people who wanted to deceive; I have never met one who wanted to be deceived. Where did they come to know this happy life, then, if not where they came to know the truth? For they love the truth too — since they refuse to be deceived — and in loving the happy life, which is nothing but joy in the truth, they are certainly loving the truth; and they could not love it if there were no knowledge of it in their memory. Then why does it not make them happy? Why aren't they rejoicing in it? Because other things occupy them with more force — things with more power to make them miserable than that thin remembrance has to make them happy. For there is still a little light left in human beings. Let them walk — let them walk — before the darkness closes over them.

But why does truth give birth to hatred? Why does your man become their enemy by telling them the truth, when what they love is the happy life, which is nothing but joy in the truth? It can only be because truth is loved in such a way that whoever loves something else wants the thing he loves to be the truth — and, since he refuses to be deceived, he refuses to be shown that he is. So people hate the truth for the sake of the very thing they love in truth's place. They love truth when it shines; they hate it when it accuses them. Because they refuse to be deceived and insist on deceiving, they love truth when it shows itself, and hate it when it shows them. And truth will pay them back in kind: those who will not be exposed by it, it exposes anyway, against their will — and does not show itself to them. This is how it is — this, this is how it is with the human mind: blind, sluggish, base, and indecent, it wants to stay hidden, and wants nothing hidden from itself. And what it receives is the exact opposite: it cannot hide from the truth, and the truth stays hidden from it. Yet even in this misery it would rather rejoice in true things than in false ones. Happy it will be, then, when — every obstruction gone — it comes to rejoice in the one Truth itself, by whom all true things are true.

See how far I have ranged through my memory looking for you, Lord — and I have not found you outside it. Nor have I found anything about you that I have not kept in memory since the day I learned you. Since the day I learned you, I have never forgotten you. For where I found truth, there I found my God, Truth itself; and since I learned the truth, I have not forgotten it. And so, since the day I learned you, you stay in my memory, and there I find you whenever I remember you and delight in you. These are my holy delights — your gift to me in mercy, with a look at my poverty.

But where in my memory do you stay, Lord? Where do you stay in it? What sort of room have you fashioned for yourself? What kind of sanctuary have you built there? You have granted my memory the honor of your staying in it — but in which part of it you stay, that is what I am considering. When I remembered you, I went past the parts of memory that the beasts also have, for I did not find you there among the images of bodily things. I came to the parts where I had stored the feelings of my mind, and I did not find you there. I went into the very seat the mind keeps for itself in my memory — since the mind remembers itself too — and you were not there either. For you are not a bodily image, and you are not the feeling of a living creature, the kind we have when we rejoice, grieve, desire, fear, remember, forget, and all the rest; and neither are you the mind itself, because you are the Lord God of the mind. All these things change; you remain, unchangeable, above them all — and yet you have stooped to live in my memory, ever since I learned you. And why do I keep asking in which region of it you live, as if there were regions in there at all? What is certain is this: you live in it, because I have remembered you ever since I learned you, and I find you in it whenever I remember you.

Where then did I find you, that I might learn you? You were not in my memory before I learned you. Where then did I find you, that I might learn you, if not in yourself, above me? Place has nothing to do with it: we go away from it, we come back to it, and there is no place. Truth: everywhere you preside over everyone who consults you, and you answer all of them at once, though they ask about all different things. You answer clearly, but not everyone hears clearly. Everyone consults you about whatever he wants, but the answer he hears is not always the one he wants. Your best servant is the one less bent on hearing from you what he wants, and more on wanting whatever he hears from you.

Late have I loved you, Beauty so old and so new — late have I loved you! And look — you were inside me, and I was outside, and out there I hunted for you: misshapen myself, I threw myself at the shapely things you made. You were with me, and I was not with you. The things that held me far from you were things that would not even exist if they did not exist in you. You called, you shouted, and you broke through my deafness. You flashed, you blazed, and you drove off my blindness. You breathed out fragrance, and I drew in breath, and now I pant for you. I tasted, and now I hunger and thirst. You touched me, and I burned for your peace.

When I cling to you with the whole of myself, nowhere will there be pain or labor for me, and my life will be alive — entirely full of you. But as things are — since whomever you fill, you also lift up — I am a weight on myself, because I am not full of you. Joys that ought to be wept over wrestle with sorrows that ought to be rejoiced in, and I do not know which side is winning. Woe is me! Lord, have mercy on me. My evil sorrows wrestle with my good joys, and I do not know which side is winning. Woe is me! Lord, have mercy on me. Woe is me! Look — I am not hiding my wounds. You are the physician, I am the patient; you are merciful, I am miserable. Isn't human life on earth one long trial? Who wants troubles and hardships? You command us to endure them, not to love them. No one loves what he endures, even if he loves enduring it: however glad he is to endure, he would rather have nothing to endure. In hard times I long for good fortune; in good fortune I dread the hard times. Between the two, is there any middle ground, where human life is not a trial? Woe, and woe again, to the world's good fortunes — from the fear of adversity, from the spoiling of joy! Woe, and woe again, and woe a third time to the world's adversities — from the longing for good fortune, from the sheer hardness of adversity itself, from the fear that endurance will crack. Isn't human life on earth a trial without a single intermission?

And my whole hope is nowhere but in your very great mercy. Give what you command, and command what you will. You command us to master ourselves. And "since I knew," a man once said, "that no one can be master of himself unless God grants it — even to know whose gift this was, was itself wisdom." By self-mastery we are collected and gathered back into the One, from whom we trickled away into the many. For a man loves you too little if he loves anything alongside you that he does not love for your sake. O Love that always burns and is never put out! O Charity, my God, set me on fire! You command self-mastery: give what you command, and command what you will.

You command me, certainly, to hold myself back from the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the ambition of the world. You commanded me to give up sleeping with a woman not my wife; and for marriage itself you counseled something better than what you permitted. And because you gave it, it was done — even before I became a steward of your sacrament. But in my memory, which I have said so much about, the images that my old habit fixed there still live. They rush at me — drained of strength while I am awake; but in sleep they come bringing not only pleasure but even consent, something very like the deed itself. The illusion in the image has such power over my soul and my flesh that these false visions persuade me, sleeping, to what the true sights cannot persuade me to, awake. At those moments, am I not myself, Lord my God? And yet how much difference there is between myself and myself, inside the instant when I cross from waking into sleep or come back again! Where is reason then — the reason that, awake, stands up to suggestions like these and stays unshaken even if the realities themselves press in? Does it shut with my eyes? Does it doze off with the body's senses? Then why is it that even in sleep we often do resist — remembering our resolve, standing by it in perfect chastity, giving these enticements no assent at all? And yet the difference holds: when it goes otherwise, we wake and come back to peace of conscience, and by that very distance we discover that it was not we who did the thing — however sorry we are that, in some way or other, it was done in us.

Is your hand not strong enough, almighty God, to heal all my soul's diseases and, with a more lavish grace, to put out even the lewd stirrings of my sleep? You will increase your gifts in me more and more, Lord, so that my soul, unstuck from the birdlime of lust, may follow me all the way to you — so that it will not be in rebellion against itself, and even in dreams will not merely refrain from those degrading corruptions that sensual images push toward the body's very pollution, but will not so much as consent to them. For you, the Almighty, who can do more than all we ask or imagine — for you it is no great matter to see to it that nothing of the kind gives even the faintest pleasure, not even the flicker a waking thought could check, to the chaste affections of a man asleep — and to do it not only within this life, but now, at my present age. But for now, what I still am in this species of my evil, I have confessed to my good Lord: rejoicing with trembling over what you have given me, mourning what is still unfinished in me, and hoping that you will perfect your mercies in me into full peace — the peace my inner self and my outer self will have with you when death is swallowed up in victory.

There is another evil that belongs to the day — and I wish the day's own evil were enough for it. By eating and drinking we patch the daily crumbling of the body, until the day you destroy both food and stomach: then you will kill my emptiness with an amazing fullness, and clothe this perishable body in everlasting imperishability. But for now the necessity is sweet to me, and I fight that sweetness so as not to be taken prisoner by it. I wage daily war by fasting, again and again forcing my body into slavery — and my pains are driven off by pleasure. For hunger and thirst are pains of a sort: like fever they burn, and like fever they kill, unless the medicine of food comes to the rescue. And since the medicine lies ready at hand, out of the comfort of your gifts — earth, water, and sky pressed into the service of our weakness — the calamity gets the name of pleasure.

This you have taught me: to come to the taking of food the way I come to taking medicine. But in the very passage from the ache of emptiness to the calm of fullness, the trap of appetite lies waiting for me — for the passage itself is a pleasure, and there is no other passage than the one necessity forces us through. And though the reason for eating and drinking is health, a dangerous pleasantness attaches itself to health like a lady's maid — and keeps trying to walk in front, so that what I claim to be doing, or mean to be doing, for my health's sake gets done for pleasure's sake instead. And the two do not share one measure: what is enough for health is too little for pleasure; and often enough it is honestly unclear whether it is the body's necessary care asking for more support, or the deceiving voluptuousness of greed demanding to be waited on. At this uncertainty the unhappy soul brightens, and gets busy building itself a defense of excuses — delighted that no one can tell exactly what health requires, so that under the cover of health it can run the business of pleasure. Every day I try to stand against these temptations. I call your right hand to my side; I refer my perplexities to you — because on this question my own deliberations have not yet reached a verdict.

I hear the voice of my God commanding: Do not let your hearts grow heavy with gorging and drunkenness. Drunkenness is far from me: by your mercy, may it never come near. But overeating sometimes steals up on your servant: by your mercy, may it be driven far off. For no one can master himself unless you grant it. Much that we pray for, you give us; and whatever good we received before we prayed, we received from you — and it was from you, too, that we came to know it afterward. I was never a drunkard, but I have known drunkards you made sober. So it is your doing that some have never been drunkards, and your doing that others should not stay drunkards forever, and your doing that both should know whose doing it was. I heard another voice of yours: Do not go chasing your appetites, and turn yourself away from your own pleasure. And by your gift I heard the saying I have loved so much: Neither if we eat will we have more, nor if we go without will we have less — which is to say, the one will not make me rich, and the other will not make me wretched. And I heard another: I have learned to be content in whatever state I am; I know how to have plenty, and I know how to endure want. I can do all things in him who strengthens me. There speaks a soldier of the camp of heaven — not dust, like us. But remember, Lord, that we are dust: that out of dust you made man, and that he was lost, and found. Nor could Paul do it by his own power — he was the same dust — that man I loved so much for saying these things through the breath of your inspiring: I can do all things, he says, in him who strengthens me. Strengthen me, so that I can. Give what you command, and command what you will. He confesses that he has received; and when he glories, he glories in the Lord. And I have heard another begging to receive: Take from me, he says, the cravings of the belly. From which it is clear, my holy God, that when what you command to be done gets done, the doing is your gift.

You have taught me, good Father, that to the pure all things are pure, but that it goes ill with the man whose eating makes another stumble; that every creature of yours is good, and nothing to be refused that is received with thanksgiving; that food does not commend us to God; that no one should judge us in what we eat or drink; that the one who eats must not despise the one who does not, and the one who does not eat must not judge the one who does. These things I have learned — thanks be to you, praise to you, my God, my teacher, knocker at my ears, enlightener of my heart. Pluck me out of every temptation. It is not unclean food I fear, but unclean appetite. I know that Noah was permitted to eat every kind of meat that was fit for food; that Elijah was fed and restored with meat; that John, ascetic to an astonishing degree, was not defiled by the living things — the locusts — that fell to him as food. And I know that Esau was swindled by his hunger for lentils; that David rebuked himself for craving a drink of water; and that our King was tempted not with meat, but with bread. And the people in the wilderness earned their rebuke not because they wanted meat, but because, wanting food, they grumbled against the Lord.

Set down in the middle of these temptations, then, I fight every day against greed in eating and drinking. It is not the kind of thing I can decide to cut off once for all and never touch again, the way I could with sleeping with a woman. So this bridle on the throat must be held at a tension between too slack and too tight. And is there anyone, Lord, who is never pulled even slightly past the boundary line of need? Whoever he is, he is a great man — let him magnify your name. I am not that man, for I am a sinful man. Yet I too magnify your name; and for my sins he intercedes with you who conquered the world — counting me among the weak members of his body, because your eyes have seen even what is imperfect in that body, and in your book all shall be written.

With the enticements of smells I am not much troubled. Absent, I do not look for them; present, I do not push them away; and I am prepared to do without them for good. So I seem to myself — perhaps I am fooled. For here too is a grievous darkness: the darkness in which my own capacities lie hidden from me, so that when my mind questions itself about its powers, it does not lightly trust its own report — since what is inside is mostly out of sight until experience drags it into the open. And in this life, which is called one long trial from end to end, no one should feel secure: whoever proved able to go from worse to better may prove able, just as well, to go from better to worse. One hope, one confidence, one firm promise — your mercy.

The pleasures of the ear had wound me tighter and mastered me more; but you untied me, and set me free. Even now, I admit, in those melodies your words breathe life into, when they are sung by a sweet and disciplined voice, I find a little rest — not the kind that pins me down; I can get up and go when I want. But together with the words that are their life — the words that got them their admission into me — the melodies themselves ask for some place of honor in my heart, and I can hardly find them the right one. Sometimes I seem to be giving them more honor than is fitting: I feel our minds lifted into the flame of devotion more fervently, more religiously, when the holy words are sung this way than when they are not — and I sense that all the varied feelings of our spirit have modes proper to them in voice and song, and are stirred by some hidden kinship between them. But the pleasure of my flesh — to which the mind must never be handed over to go soft — often deceives me: the senses do not accompany reason patiently, walking behind her; admitted only for her sake, they try to run out in front and take the lead. So in these things I sin without noticing, and notice afterward.

At other times, over-wary of exactly that trap, I err on the side of harshness — sometimes so far that I want every melody of the sweet songs that carry David's Psalter banished from my ears, and from the church's ears too; and then the safer course seems to me the one I have often heard told of Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, who had the reader deliver the psalm with so slight a bend in the voice that it was closer to speaking than to singing. And yet when I remember the tears I wept at the songs of your church in the first days of my recovered faith — and how even now I am moved, not by the singing but by the things sung, when they are sung in a clear voice with the aptest modulation — I recognize again the great usefulness of the practice. So I swing between the danger of pleasure and the experience of its healing power; and on the whole — though I do not offer this as a final ruling — I incline to approve the custom of singing in church, so that through the ear's delight a weaker mind may rise into the feeling of devotion. And yet, whenever it happens that the singing moves me more than the thing sung, I confess it as a sin that deserves its penalty — and then I would rather not hear the singer at all. Look at me — this is my condition. Weep with me, and weep for me, you who carry inside yourselves some good from which good deeds come. Those of you who carry none — none of this touches you. But you, Lord my God — listen. Look down; see; have mercy; heal me — you, in whose sight I have become a question to myself: and that is my sickness.

There remains the pleasure of these eyes of my flesh. Let me make my confessions about it in the hearing of the ears of your temple — brotherly, devout ears — and so finish with the temptations of the lust of the flesh, which still batter me, groaning as I am, longing to be clothed with my dwelling from heaven. The eyes love beautiful and various forms, bright and pleasing colors. Let these not take hold of my soul; let God hold it — God who made these things, and made them very good indeed; but my good is he, not they. They press on me all day, every waking hour, and I get no rest from them, the way I get rest from singing voices — sometimes, in silence, from every voice. For the queen of colors herself, this light that bathes everything we see — wherever I am through the day, she slips past me in a thousand guises and soothes me even while I am busy at something else and paying her no attention. And she works her way in so forcefully that if she is suddenly taken away, we look for her with longing; and if she is gone a long time, the mind grows sad.

O Light — the light Tobit saw when, with these eyes shut, he taught his son the road of life, and walked ahead of him with the feet of love, never straying. The light Isaac saw when his fleshly eyes were dimmed and closed with age, and it was granted him to bless his sons without knowing them — and, in blessing them, to know them. The light Jacob saw when he too, blind with great age, shone from a lit heart onto the tribes of the people to come, carried in the persons of his sons; and laid hands mystically crossed on his grandsons through Joseph — not as their father, seeing from outside, tried to correct, but as he himself discerned from within. This is the light itself, and it is one; and all who see it and love it are one. But that bodily light I was speaking of seasons this world's life for its blind lovers with a sweetness that tempts and endangers. Those who know how to praise you for it — "O God, creator of all things" — take it up into your hymn, and are not taken by it in their sleep. That is what I want to be. I resist the seductions of the eyes, so that these feet that walk your road are not snared; and I raise invisible eyes to you, asking you to pull my feet out of the snare. Again and again you pull them free, for again and again they are snared. You never stop pulling them out, while I keep catching fast in the traps laid on every side — because you who keep Israel will neither slumber nor sleep.

And what an uncountable host of things men have added to tempt their own eyes — things made by every art and every craftsman: clothes, shoes, vessels, manufactures of every kind, paintings and images of all sorts — far past any necessary or modest use, past any devout meaning. Outwardly they chase what they make; inwardly they abandon the one by whom they were made, destroying what they themselves were made to be. But I, my God and my glory — I take even these things as a reason to sing you a hymn, and I offer praise as a sacrifice to the one who offered sacrifice for me: because the beautiful designs that travel through souls into skillful hands all come down from that Beauty which stands above souls — the Beauty my soul sighs for day and night. The makers of outward beauties, and their devotees, draw from it their standard for judging them, but not their standard for using them. And it is there, though they do not see it — there, so that they need not wander so far; so that they might keep their strength for you, instead of scattering it out on delights that end in exhaustion. And I, saying all this and seeing it clearly, still tangle my steps in these beauties — but you pull me out, Lord; you pull me out, because your mercy is before my eyes. I am caught, pitifully; and you pull me out, in pity — sometimes without my even feeling it, when I had only brushed the trap; sometimes with pain, when I had already stuck fast.

To this, another form of temptation is added — one dangerous in more ways still. Besides the lust of the flesh, which lies in the enjoyment of all the senses and all pleasures, and whose slaves rot away as they wander far from you, there is in the soul a certain hollow, itching craving that works through those same bodily senses: dressed up in the names of knowledge and learning, it is a lust not for pleasure in the flesh, but for experiments through the flesh. It is rooted in the appetite for knowing; and since the eyes are the chief of the senses for gaining knowledge, the word of God calls it the lust of the eyes. Seeing belongs, strictly, to the eyes; but we use the word of the other senses too, whenever we put them to work finding things out. We do not say, "Listen how it glitters," or "Smell how it gleams," or "Taste how it shines," or "Feel how it flashes": all of these, we say, are seen. We say not only "See how it shines" — which only the eyes can perceive — but "See how it sounds," "See how it smells," "See how it tastes," "See how hard it is." So the whole business of the senses, as I said, is called the lust of the eyes: because the office of seeing, in which the eyes hold first rank, is borrowed by the other senses too, by a kind of likeness, whenever they go prospecting after some piece of knowledge.

And here the difference between pleasure and curiosity in the senses shows itself plainly. Pleasure goes after the beautiful, the melodious, the fragrant, the savory, the soft; curiosity goes after their opposites too — to test them — not to take on discomfort, but out of the sheer itch of experiencing and knowing. What pleasure is there in looking at a mangled corpse and shuddering? Yet let one lie somewhere, and people come running — to be saddened, to turn pale. They are even afraid of seeing it again in their dreams — as if anyone had forced them to look at it awake, or some rumor of its beauty had drawn them there! So too with the other senses, though it would take long to go through them all. From this disease of curiosity come the marvels exhibited in the theater. From it people go on to pry into the hidden workings of nature — the ones beyond our concern — which it profits nothing to know, and which men want to know for no reason but the knowing. From it, too, with the same perverted aim of knowledge, come the inquiries of magic. From it, even in religion, God is put to the test: signs and portents are demanded of him — not wanted for anyone's salvation, but purely for the experience.

In this immense forest, so full of traps and dangers, look how much I have cut away and swept out of my heart — as you have enabled me to do, God of my salvation. And yet, with so many things of this kind buzzing around our daily life on every side, when can I dare to say — when? — that nothing of the sort makes me stop and stare, or catches me in idle fascination? True, the theaters no longer carry me off; I do not bother to know the courses of the stars; my soul has never gone asking answers of the shades of the dead; every sacrilegious rite I detest. But from you, Lord my God, to whom I owe humble and single-hearted service — with what elaborate suggestions the enemy keeps working on me to demand from you some sign! By our King I beg you, and by Jerusalem our homeland, pure and chaste: as consenting to such things has been far from me, so may it always be farther and farther away. But when I pray to you for someone's salvation, what I am aiming at is different altogether; and you, doing what you will, give me — and will give me — the grace to follow you willingly.

Even so, who can count the tiny, contemptible ways our curiosity is tempted every day, and how often we slip? How often we begin by putting up with people telling empty tales, so as not to wound the weak — and then, little by little, find ourselves attending with relish! I no longer watch a dog chase a hare at the circus games; but if I happen to be crossing a field and the chase is on, it may well tug me away from some serious thought and gather me to itself — forcing no swerve from the body of my mount, but a swerve of the heart. And unless you showed me my weakness and warned me quickly — either to lift myself toward you by some reflection rising out of the very sight, or to scorn the whole thing and pass on — I would stand there gaping, gone dull. And what about when a lizard catching flies, or a spider wrapping them up as they blunder into her web, stops me in my tracks as I sit at home? Is the thing any different because the animals are small? From them I move on, yes, to praising you, the marvelous creator and arranger of everything — but that is not what first caught my attention. It is one thing to get up quickly, another never to fall. And my life is packed full of such moments, and my one hope is your enormous mercy. For when our heart becomes the receptacle for things like these, hauling its crowds of teeming vanity, then our very prayers are cut short and disturbed by them; and while, in your presence, we direct the voice of the heart toward your ears, that great business is broken off by frivolous thoughts rushing in from I know not where. Shall we file this too under things that don't matter? Or shall anything lead us back to hope but your mercy, known and tried — since you have begun to change us?

And you know how far you have already changed me — you who first healed me of the passion for revenge, so that you might then forgive all the rest of my iniquities, heal all my diseases, redeem my life from corruption, crown me with mercy and compassion, and satisfy my desire with good things: you who curbed my pride with your fear, and tamed my neck to your yoke. And now I carry the yoke, and it is light on me — because that is what you promised, and what you have made it; and truly it was so all along, and I did not know it, back when I was afraid to put it on.

But Lord, you who alone rule without arrogance, because you alone are the true Lord, who have no lord above you — has this third kind of temptation left me? Can it leave me at all in this life: the wish to be feared and loved by men for no reason but the joy of it — a joy that is no joy? A wretched life, that, and an ugly showing-off. Here above all is why men fail to love you and fear you purely; and so you set your face against the proud and give grace to the humble: you thunder down on the world's ambitions, and the foundations of the mountains shake. Now, because certain offices in human society make it necessary to be loved and feared by men, the adversary of our true happiness presses his advantage, strewing his snares everywhere with "Well done! Well done!" — hoping that as we snatch at the praise we will be caught off guard, unhook our joy from your truth and hang it on men's flattery, and come to enjoy being loved and feared not for your sake but in your place; so that, having made us like himself, he can keep us for his own — not bound together in love, but bound together in punishment: he who decided to set his throne in the north, so that in the dark and the cold they might slave for him as he apes you in his warped, crooked way. But we, Lord — look, we are your little flock. Keep us; we are yours. Spread your wings, and let us run under them. Be our glory yourself. Let us be loved for your sake; let it be your word that is held in awe in us. Whoever wants men's praise while you find fault will not be defended by men when you judge, or rescued by men when you condemn. And when it is not the sinner being praised in the desires of his soul, nor the doer of wrong being blessed, but a man being praised for some gift you gave him — if he takes more joy in the praising than in having the gift he is praised for, then he is praised while you find fault; and the one who praised is better than the one who was praised: for the one delighted in God's gift in a man, while the other delighted more in the gift of men than in the gift of God.

By these temptations we are tested every day, Lord — tested without rest. Our daily furnace is the human tongue. And in this matter too you command us to master ourselves: give what you command, and command what you will. In this matter you know the groans of my heart toward you, and the rivers that run from my eyes. For I cannot easily make out how far I am cleansed of this plague, and I am very afraid of my hidden faults — the ones your eyes see and mine do not. In other kinds of temptation I have at least some way of examining myself; in this one, almost none. With the pleasures of the flesh, and with useless curiosity, I can see what I have managed in reining my mind back: I do without the thing — by choice, or because it isn't there — and then ask myself how much or how little it troubles me to be without it. Riches, too, which people want in order to feed one of the three lusts, or two of them, or all three: if the soul cannot tell whether it despises them while holding them, it can throw them away and put itself to the test. But praise? To do without praise in order to test our powers — must we live so badly, so viciously and monstrously, that everyone who knows us detests us? Can a greater madness be spoken or imagined? No: if praise is the usual and rightful companion of a good life and good works, we should no more walk away from its company than from the good life itself. And yet, whether I can be calm or in pain at being without a thing, I have no way of knowing until it is gone.

So what do I confess to you, Lord, in this kind of temptation? What, except that praise delights me? — but truth delights me more than praise. If I were given the choice — to be out of my mind, wrong about everything, and praised by every man alive, or to be steady and grounded in the truth and abused by all of them — I know which I would choose. And yet I could wish that another person's approval added nothing to my joy over whatever good is in me. But it does add to it, I confess — and not only that: blame subtracts from it. And when this wretchedness of mine rattles me, an excuse slides in — you know what it is worth, God; it leaves me unsure. For you have commanded of us not only self-mastery — that is, what things to withhold our love from — but righteousness too — that is, where to bestow it — and you willed that we love not only you but our neighbor as well. And so I often tell myself that what pleases me is my neighbor's progress or promise, when I am pleased by his intelligent praise; and that it is his fault that grieves me, when I hear him tear down what he does not understand, or what is good. For sometimes I really am grieved by praise of myself — when things are praised in me that displease me, or when small and trivial goods are weighed as heavier than they should be. But again, how do I know whether I feel this only because I do not want the man who praises me to disagree with me about myself — not moved by any care for his good, but because the same goods that please me in myself please me more when they please someone else too? For somehow I am not praised at all when my judgment of myself goes unpraised — when what gets praised is what displeases me, or what gets more praise is what pleases me less. So am I not simply unsure of myself in this whole affair?

Look, Truth: in you I see that when I am praised, I ought to be moved not for my own sake, but for the good of my neighbor. Whether that is how it is with me, I do not know. In this, I know less of myself than I know of you. I beg you, my God: show me myself too, so that I can confess to my brothers, who will pray for me, whatever damage I discover in myself. Let me question myself again, more carefully. If, in my own praise, it is my neighbor's good that moves me, why am I stung less when another man is unjustly attacked than when I am? Why does an insult bite deeper when it is thrown at me than when, before my eyes and with the same injustice, it is thrown at someone else? Do I not know even this? Or does it come down, at last, to this — that I deceive myself, and do not do the truth before you, in my heart and on my tongue? Put such madness far from me, Lord — do not let my own mouth become the sinner's oil that makes my head glisten. I am poor and needy; and I am at my best when, in hidden groaning, I displease myself and seek your mercy — until what is broken in me is mended and made complete, into that peace which the eye of the arrogant does not know.

The word that leaves the mouth, and the deeds that people come to know of, carry with them a most dangerous temptation — the love of praise, which goes canvassing for compliments and collecting them, to prop up a private eminence of one's own. It tempts me even when I condemn it in myself — tempts me by the very fact of being condemned; and often a man preens more emptily on his very contempt for empty glory. And then it is no longer contempt for glory he is preening on: for no one despises what he is glorying in.

Inside — inside us — lives another evil of the same family: the vanity of those who please themselves, however little they please anyone else, or however much they displease them, or however little they even care to please. But pleasing themselves, they deeply displease you — not only because they treat bad things as good, but because they treat your good things as their own; or admit them to be yours, but claim them as earned; or admit them to be unearned grace, and still refuse to rejoice in that grace with their brothers, envying it to others. In all these dangers and labors, and others like them, you see the trembling of my heart; and as for my wounds — I do not so much manage to stop dealing them as feel you healing them, again and again.

Where have you not walked with me, O Truth — teaching me what to avoid and what to seek, whenever I laid before you the things I could see down here, and asked your counsel? With my outer senses I surveyed the world as far as I was able; I studied the life my body has from me, and these senses of mine. From there I went inward, into the storerooms of my memory — those manifold immensities, wondrously filled with uncountable riches — and I contemplated them and stood awestruck: of all that was there, I could make out nothing without you, and none of it was you. Nor was the finder you — I who went through it all, straining to distinguish each thing and value it according to its rank, taking some things as the senses reported them, questioning others I felt mixed into my very self, counting and sorting the reporters themselves, and in memory's broad treasury turning some things over, laying some away, drawing others out. Nor, when I was doing this, was the power that did it you: for you are the abiding Light that I kept consulting about them all — whether they were, what they were, what they were worth — and I heard you teaching and commanding. And I do this often now. It is my delight; whenever I can loosen the grip of necessary duties, I take refuge in this pleasure. And in all the things I range through as I consult you, I find no safe place for my soul except in you: there let what is scattered in me be gathered, and let nothing of me slip away from you. And sometimes you bring me, deep inside, to a feeling utterly unlike anything usual — a strange sweetness that, if it were made complete in me, would be something no longer this life, whatever it would be. But I fall back down under my miserable weights; the old ways swallow me again, and hold me; I weep hard, but I am held hard. So heavy is the baggage of habit! Here I can be, and do not want to be; there I want to be, and cannot — miserable in both.

So I have looked over the diseases of my sins under the threefold lust, and I have called your right hand to my rescue. For with a wounded heart I saw your splendor, and, beaten back by the blaze, I said: "Who can reach it? I am flung away from before your eyes." You are the Truth, presiding over all things; and I, in my greed, did not want to lose you — I wanted to hold a lie in the same hands with you, just as no one wants to lie so thoroughly that he no longer knows, himself, what is true. And that is how I lost you: you do not consent to be held alongside a lie.

Whom could I find to reconcile me to you? Should I have gone begging to angels? With what prayer? With what rites? Many who struggled to return to you, and could not do it by their own strength, have tried that road, I am told, and fallen into a hunger for exotic visions, and been found worthy of delusions. High-minded, they went looking for you with the pride of their learning, chests puffed out instead of beaten; and by the likeness of their hearts they drew to themselves the powers of the air, fellow conspirators in pride, who tricked them with magical powers. They were looking for a mediator to purify them, and it was not one. It was the devil, passing himself off as an angel of light — and it was a great lure to their proud flesh that he had no body of flesh himself. They were mortals, and sinners; you, Lord, with whom they proudly sought reconciliation, are immortal and without sin. A mediator between God and humanity had to have something like God and something like human beings: like humans in both, he would be far from God; like God in both, far from humans — and either way, no mediator. So that counterfeit mediator, by whom, in your secret judgment, pride earned its mockery, has one thing in common with human beings — sin — and pretends to another thing in common with God: since he wears no mortal flesh, he parades himself as immortal. But since the wages of sin is death, what he truly has in common with men is this: that with them, he is condemned to death.

But the true Mediator — whom in your secret mercy you showed to the humble, and sent so that by his example they might learn humility itself — the Mediator between God and humanity, the man Christ Jesus, appeared between mortal sinners and the immortal Just One: mortal, as men are; just, as God is. And because the wage of justice is life and peace, he came so that, through a justice joined to God, he might cancel the death of the wicked he had justified — the death he willed to share with them. He was shown to the holy men of old, so that they might be saved through faith in his suffering still to come, as we are saved through faith in his suffering now past. For as man he is Mediator; but as Word he is not something in between, being equal to God, God with God, and with him one God.

How you loved us, good Father — you did not spare your only Son, but handed him over for us, the godless! How you loved us — us, for whom he who did not think it robbery to be your equal was made subject even to death on a cross: he alone free among the dead, having power to lay down his life and power to take it up again. For us he stood before you as victor and victim — and victor because victim. For us he stood before you as priest and sacrifice — and priest because sacrifice. He made us sons to you instead of slaves, by being born of you and becoming our servant. With good reason my hope in him is strong: that you will heal all my diseases through him who sits at your right hand and intercedes with you for us. Otherwise I would despair. For my diseases are many and great — many, and great; but your medicine is greater. We might have thought your Word too remote to be joined to man, and despaired of ourselves, had he not become flesh and made his home among us.

Terrified by my sins and by the dead weight of my misery, I had turned it over in my heart and resolved on flight into the wilderness. But you stopped me, and steadied me, saying: This is why Christ died for all — so that the living should live no longer for themselves, but for him who died for them. See, Lord: I throw my care on you, so that I may live; and I will contemplate the wonders of your law. You know my clumsiness and my weakness: teach me, and heal me. He — your only Son, in whom all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge lie hidden — has redeemed me with his blood. Let the proud not slander me: for I keep the price of my ransom in mind; I eat it and drink it and hand it out to others; and, poor myself, I long to be filled from it among those who eat and are filled — and they shall praise the Lord who seek him.
