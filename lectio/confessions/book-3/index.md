# Confessions, Book III

The Cauldron of Carthage

I came to Carthage, and a cauldron of shameful loves hissed and seethed around me. I was not in love yet, but I was in love with loving, and out of a deeper, hidden need I hated myself for not needing more. I hunted for something to love, in love with loving itself; I hated safety, hated a road without traps. For inside me was a famine of the inner food — of you yourself, my God — yet the famine gave me no hunger. I had no appetite for the food that does not spoil, not because I was full of it, but because the emptier I was, the more it turned my stomach. That is why my soul was sick, and broke out in sores, and flung itself wretchedly outward, itching to be scratched by the touch of things of sense. Yet if those things had no soul in them, they would not be loved at all. To love and to be loved was sweet to me, and sweeter if I could also enjoy the body of the one who loved me. So I fouled the spring of friendship with the dirt of lust; I clouded its brightness with a hell of desire; and, foul and disgraceful as I was, in my overflowing vanity I longed to pass for elegant and refined. I fell headlong into the love I was aching to be caught by. My God, my mercy, how much gall you sprinkled over that sweetness — and what goodness it was to do it! For I was loved in return; I came in secret to the bond of enjoying; and I was glad to be tied up in chains of misery, so that I could be flogged with the burning iron rods of jealousy and suspicion, of fear and anger and quarrels.

Stage shows swept me away too, full of images of my own miseries and fuel for my own fire. Why is it that a man in the theater wants to feel grief, watching sorrowful and tragic things he would never willingly suffer himself? Yet as a spectator he wants to suffer grief at them, and the grief itself is his pleasure. What is that but wretched madness? The more a man is stirred by these performances, the less he has recovered from such passions himself — though when he suffers in his own person we call it misery, and when he feels with someone else we call it compassion. But what sort of compassion is there for fictions on a stage? The listener is not being called to help; he is only being invited to grieve, and the more he grieves, the louder he applauds the actor of those fictions. And if these human calamities — ancient ones, or pure inventions — are played so that the spectator feels no sorrow, he walks out disgusted and muttering criticism; but if sorrow is stirred in him, he stays riveted to his seat, weeping for joy.

So griefs are loved too? Surely joy is what everyone wants. Or is it this — that though no one likes to be miserable, we do like to be merciful, and since mercy cannot exist without grief, for this one reason griefs are loved? This too springs from that vein of friendship. But where does it go? Where does it flow? Why does it run down into a torrent of boiling pitch, into those monstrous tides of foul lust into which it is changed and twisted — by its own choice bent and hurled down out of its heavenly clearness? Should compassion be thrown out, then? By no means. Let griefs, then, sometimes be loved. But beware of uncleanness, my soul — with my God for your guardian, the God of our fathers, to be praised and exalted above all forever — beware of uncleanness. Even now I have not stopped pitying. But in those days, in the theaters, I rejoiced with the lovers when they wickedly enjoyed one another, though it was all make-believe in the play; and when they lost one another I grieved with them as if I were deep in compassion — and both delighted me all the same. Now I pity the man who rejoices in his wickedness far more than the man who is thought to have suffered hardship because he missed some ruinous pleasure and lost some miserable happiness. This is surely the truer mercy, but there is no delight in its grief. For though a man who grieves for the wretched is commended for his act of love, the man who is genuinely compassionate would far rather there were nothing to grieve about. Only if goodwill could be ill will — which can never be — could a man who truly and sincerely pities wish for wretched people to exist so that he could pity them. Some sorrow, then, can be approved; none can be loved. For this is your way, Lord God: you love souls far more purely than we do, and pity them more incorruptibly, because no sorrow can wound you. And who is equal to these things?

But I, wretched then, loved to grieve, and went looking for something to grieve over. In another man's misery — faked, staged — the performance I liked best, the one that pulled at me hardest, was the one that shook tears out of me. Was it any wonder that an unhappy sheep, straying from your flock, sick of your keeping, broke out in an ugly mange? That was where my love of griefs came from — not griefs that would cut me deep, since I had no wish to suffer the things I loved to watch, but griefs that, heard and invented, scraped me lightly on the surface. And after them, as after raking fingernails, came the hot swelling, the abscess, the festering sore. That was my life — but was it life, my God?

And your faithful mercy circled over me, far off. On what enormous iniquities I wasted myself! I followed a sacrilegious curiosity, so that, having deserted you, it might drag me down to the treacherous depths, to the cheating service of demons — and to them I sacrificed my evil deeds. And in all of it you were flogging me. I even dared, while your solemn rites were being celebrated inside the walls of your own church, to feel desire, and to set about a piece of business whose fruit was death. For that you lashed me with heavy punishments, but nothing next to my fault, O you, my exceedingly great mercy, my God, my refuge from the terrible destroyers among whom I wandered, stiff-necked, drawing further and further away from you — loving my own ways and not yours, loving a runaway's freedom.

Those studies too, the ones people called honorable, had their eye on the law courts: I was to excel in them, and the more crooked I was, the more I would be praised. So great is the blindness of men, who glory even in their blindness. And I was now the senior student in the rhetoric school, and I rejoiced in it proudly and swelled with conceit — though I was far quieter, Lord, you know it, and stood completely apart from the wrecking done by the "Wreckers." That crooked, devilish name was worn like a badge of sophistication. I lived among them, ashamed with a shameless shame that I was not one of them. I was with them; at times I enjoyed their friendships; but their doings I always abhorred — their "wreckings," the insolent way they hounded the shyness of strangers, tormenting it with unprovoked mockery and feeding their own malicious glee on it. Nothing could be more like the conduct of demons. So what truer name could they have than Wreckers? — wrecked and twisted themselves first, while the lying spirits secretly laughed at them and seduced them in the very thing they loved to do: laughing at others, and deceiving them.

Among such men, at that impressionable age, I studied the books of eloquence, in which I longed to stand out — a damnable and windy purpose, aimed at the joys of human vanity. Following the usual course of study, I came to a book by one Cicero, whose tongue almost everyone admires; his heart, not so much. That book contains his exhortation to philosophy, and it is called the Hortensius. That book changed what I loved. It turned my prayers toward you yourself, Lord, and gave me new purposes, new desires. All at once every vain hope was worthless to me; I longed for the immortality of wisdom with an unbelievable burning of the heart; and I had begun to rise, so that I might return to you. For it was not to sharpen my tongue — the thing I was supposedly buying with my mother's money, in my nineteenth year, my father two years dead — it was not to sharpen my tongue that I took up that book. What had won me was not the way it spoke, but what it said.

How I burned then, my God — how I burned to fly up from earthly things back to you! And I did not know what you were doing with me. For wisdom is with you. The love of wisdom has a Greek name, "philosophy," and that book set me on fire with it. There are men who use philosophy to seduce, coloring and painting over their own errors with that great, smooth, honorable name; and nearly all who did so, in Cicero's day and before it, are marked and exposed in that book. It even makes plain that saving warning of your Spirit, spoken through your good and devout servant: "See to it that no one takes you captive through philosophy and empty deceit, following human tradition, following the elements of this world and not Christ; for in him the whole fullness of deity dwells in bodily form." And at that time — you know it, light of my heart — those words of the apostle were still unknown to me. What delighted me in Cicero's exhortation was just this: that his words roused me, kindled me, set me ablaze to love and seek and win and hold and strongly embrace not this school or that school, but wisdom itself, whatever it might be. Only one thing dampened the blaze: the name of Christ was not there. For that name, by your mercy, Lord — the name of my Savior, your Son — my tender heart had drunk in devoutly with my mother's milk, and it stayed deep in me; and whatever came without that name, however learned, however polished, however true, could not carry me away entirely.

So I resolved to turn my mind to the holy Scriptures, and see what kind of thing they were. And what I see there is a thing not opened to the proud, not laid bare to children: lowly at the threshold, lofty as you go deeper in, and veiled in mysteries. I was not the kind of man who could enter it, or bend my neck to walk at its pace. When I first turned to Scripture, I did not feel what I am saying now. It seemed to me unworthy to stand beside the stateliness of Cicero. My swollen pride recoiled from its plainness, and my sharp eye could not pierce its depths. Yet Scripture is exactly the thing that grows up with a little child. But I sneered at being a little child; puffed up with self-importance, I took myself for a great man.

And so I fell in with men proudly raving, all flesh and all talk, whose mouths held the devil's snares — a birdlime mixed from the syllables of your name, and of the Lord Jesus Christ, and of the Paraclete, our Comforter, the Holy Spirit. These names never left their mouths, but only as sound, as the noise of the tongue; their hearts were empty of truth. "Truth! Truth!" they kept saying, and they said it to me constantly, and it was nowhere in them. They spoke falsehoods — not only about you, who truly are the Truth, but even about the elements of this world, your creation. Even the philosophers who say true things about such matters I should have passed by, for love of you, my Father, supremely good, beauty of all things beautiful. O Truth, Truth — how the marrow of my soul sighed for you, even then, from the inside, while those men sounded your name at me over and over, in many ways, with the voice alone and with many enormous books! Those books were the platters on which — while I starved for you — they served me the sun and the moon in your place: beautiful works of yours, but works of yours all the same. Not you. Not even your first works: for your spiritual works come before these bodily ones, bright and heavenly though they are. But I was hungry and thirsty not even for those first works, but for you yourself, the Truth, in whom there is no change, no shadow of turning. And still they kept setting glittering fantasies before me on those platters. Better to love this actual sun — real at least to these eyes — than those falsehoods that use the eyes to cheat the mind. And yet, because I took them for you, I ate — not eagerly, since in my mouth you had no taste of what you are (you were not those empty fictions), and I was not nourished by them; I was drained. Food in dreams looks exactly like the food of waking life; yet the sleeping are not fed by it — they are asleep. But those doctrines were not like you in any way, as you have now spoken to me, for they were bodily fantasies — false bodies. More certain than they are these true bodies we see with the sight of the flesh, in the sky or on the earth. The beasts and the birds see them just as we do, and they are more certain than when we picture them in the mind; and again, we picture them with more certainty than when we start from them and guess at other bodies, vaster and infinite, that do not exist at all. Such were the empty husks I was fed on then — and I was not fed. But you, my Love, for whom I grow faint so that I may grow strong — you are neither those bodies we see, though they are in heaven, nor those we do not see there; for you created them, and you do not even count them among your greatest works. How far you are, then, from those fantasies of mine, fantasies of bodies that have no being at all! More certain than those are the images of bodies that do exist, and more certain than the images the bodies themselves — and even they are not you. Nor are you the soul, which is the life of bodies — and the life of bodies is better and more certain than the bodies. No: you are the life of souls, the life of lives, alive by your own self, and you do not change — O life of my soul.

Where were you then, for me? How far away? I was wandering far from you, shut out even from the husks of the pigs whom I was feeding on husks. How much better are the little fables of the poets and the schoolmasters than those traps! Verses, songs, "Medea in flight" — these are truly more useful than those men's five elements, painted up in different disguises to match their five dens of darkness: things that do not exist at all, and that kill whoever believes in them. A verse and a song I can turn into real food. And though I sang of Medea flying, I never claimed it happened; though I heard it sung, I never believed it. But those things I believed. Woe, woe! By what steps was I dragged down into the depths of hell — toiling and seething for lack of the truth! For I was seeking you, my God — to you I confess it, who took pity on me before I could yet confess — I was seeking you not by the understanding of the mind, by which you willed me to stand above the beasts, but by the senses of the flesh. And all the while you were more inward than my inmost part, and higher than my highest. I had walked into that brazen woman of Solomon's riddle, poor in prudence, who sits on a stool at her door and says, "Eat hidden bread gladly, and drink sweet stolen water." She seduced me, because she found me living outside, in the eye of my flesh, chewing over in myself the food I had gulped down through it.

For I did not know that other thing, the one that truly is. And I was subtly nudged — sharp fellow that I was — to side with fools and deceivers when they put their questions to me: Where does evil come from? Is God bounded by a bodily shape — does he have hair and nails? Are men to be counted righteous who had several wives at once, and killed men, and sacrificed animals? In my ignorance these questions shook me, and while I was walking away from the truth I imagined I was walking toward it. For I did not yet know that evil has no being of its own — that it is nothing but the privation of good, thinning down toward the point where nothing at all is left. How could I have seen that, when my eyes' seeing stopped at bodies, and my mind's seeing stopped at a phantasm? And I did not know that God is spirit: not something with limbs stretched out in length and breadth, not something whose being is bulk. For bulk is smaller in its part than in its whole; and if it were infinite, it would be smaller in any part you marked off by a fixed space than in its whole infinity — so it is not whole everywhere, the way spirit is, the way God is. And what there is in us by which we exist, and by which Scripture rightly says we are made in the image of God — of that I was completely ignorant.

Nor did I know the true inner righteousness, which judges not by custom but by the perfectly straight law of almighty God — the law by which the moral codes of regions and ages were shaped to fit those regions and ages, while the law itself is the same always and everywhere, not one thing in one place and something else in another. By that law Abraham and Isaac and Jacob and Moses and David were righteous — all those men praised by the mouth of God himself. It is fools who judge them unrighteous, judging by man's judgment and measuring the moral conduct of the whole human race against the yardstick of their own small habits. It is as if a man in an armory, with no idea what fits which limb, tried to cover his head with a shin-guard and put a helmet on his foot, and then complained that nothing fits. Or as if, on a day when trade is publicly banned after noon, a shopkeeper fumed that he could not sell in the afternoon, since he had been allowed to in the morning. Or as if, in a single house, a man watched one servant handle something the cup-bearer is not allowed to touch, or saw a thing done behind the stables that is forbidden at the dinner table, and grew indignant that in one dwelling, in one household, the same thing is not granted to everyone everywhere. Such are the people who bristle when they hear that something was lawful for righteous men in that age which is not lawful in this one, or that God commanded those men one thing and these men another, for reasons belonging to the times — though both alike were obeying the same righteousness. Yet they can see that in one man, one day, one house, different things fit different members; that a thing lawful for years may cease to be lawful; that something permitted or commanded in one corner is justly forbidden and punished in another. Is justice then fickle and changeable? No. But the times over which justice presides do not run alike — they are times, after all. And men, whose life on earth is short, cannot stitch together by their own experience the circumstances of earlier ages and other nations, which they never knew, with what they know first-hand — though within a single body, a single day, a single household, they can easily see what suits each member, each moment, each part and person. At the one they take offense; to the other they submit.

All this I did not know then, and did not notice. It struck my eyes on every side, and I did not see it. I wrote verses, and in them I was not free to set any foot in any position; each meter demanded something different, and even within one meter the same foot could not stand in every place. Yet the art by which I composed did not have one rule here and a different rule there; it held them all at once. And still I could not see that the righteousness which good and holy men obeyed held everything it commands together in one — far more excellently, far more sublimely — varying in no part of itself, and yet, for varying times, distributing and prescribing not everything at once, but what was proper to each. Blind as I was, I faulted the devout fathers — not only for using the things of their own day as God commanded and inspired them, but also for foretelling what was to come, as God revealed it through them.

Can it ever, anywhere, be unjust to love God with the whole heart, the whole soul, and the whole mind — and to love your neighbor as yourself? So the offenses that are against nature are everywhere and always to be detested and punished; such were the offenses of Sodom. If every nation on earth committed them, all would stand guilty of the same crime under God's law, which did not make human beings to use one another so. For the very fellowship that ought to hold between God and us is violated when the nature of which he is the author is defiled by perverted lust. But offenses against the customs of men are to be avoided according to the customs of each place, so that a compact settled among themselves by a city's or a nation's custom or law may not be broken at the whim of anyone, citizen or foreigner. For any part that will not fit its own whole is ugly. When God commands something contrary to the custom or the compact of any people, however, then even if it was never done there, it must be done; if it has lapsed, it must be restored; if it was never instituted, it must be instituted now. For if a king, in the city he rules, may lawfully command what no one before him — nor he himself until then — had ever commanded, and obeying him is no offense against the community of that city (indeed it is an offense not to obey him, since the general compact of human society is to obey its kings), then how much more must God, the ruler of all his creation, be obeyed without hesitation in whatever he commands. For just as, among the powers of human society, the greater authority is set above the lesser and is obeyed first, so God is set above all.

The same holds for acts of violence, where there is an appetite to hurt — by insult or by injury — and either for revenge, as enemy against enemy; or to seize some advantage that belongs to another, as robber against traveler; or to ward off an evil, against someone who is feared; or out of envy, as the wretched envy the fortunate, or a man who has prospered envies the one he fears will pull level with him, or aches to see already level; or for the sheer pleasure of another's pain, like the spectators at the gladiators, or the people who mock and jeer at whomever they please. These are the chief heads of iniquity, and they sprout from the lust to rule, the lust to look, the lust to feel — from one of them, or two, or all three at once. And so men live evil lives against the three and the seven — that harp of ten strings, your Ten Commandments, O God most high, most sweet. But what can foul offenses do to you, who cannot be defiled? What violence can reach you, who cannot be harmed? What you avenge is what men do to themselves; for even when they sin against you, they are doing wicked harm to their own souls. Iniquity lies to itself — whether by corrupting and perverting its own nature, which you made and set in order; or by using permitted things without measure; or by burning for forbidden things, for that use which is against nature; or men stand guilty of raging against you in heart and word, kicking against the goad; or, bursting the fences of human society, they brazenly exult in private alliances and private feuds, as this or that tempts or offends them. And all of it happens when you are forsaken, O fountain of life, the one true creator and ruler of the universe — when private pride fastens its love on one false thing, a mere part of the whole. So the way back to you is a humble devotion: and you cleanse us of our evil habit; you are merciful to the sins of those who confess; you hear the groaning of the shackled, and you loose us from the chains we made for ourselves — if only we stop lifting against you the horns of a false liberty, greedy to have more at the risk of losing everything, and loving our own private good more than you, the good of all things.

And yet among these offenses of foulness and of violence and so many iniquities, there are the sins of people who are making progress. Those who judge rightly fault these sins by the rule of perfection, but praise the persons in hope of the harvest, as one praises the green blade for the grain to come. And there are certain acts that look like foul or violent offenses and are not sins at all, because they offend neither you, our Lord God, nor the bond of society: as when a man gathers things suited to his season of life for its use, and we cannot tell whether a lust for owning drove him; or when established authority punishes in order to correct, and we cannot tell whether a lust for hurting drove it. Many an act, then, that seems blameworthy in men's sight is approved by your testimony, and many an act praised by men is condemned with you as witness — because the look of the deed is one thing, the mind of the doer another, and the hidden hinge of the moment another still. But when you suddenly command something unaccustomed and unforeseen — even a thing you once forbade — and for the time being keep the reason of your command hidden, and it cuts against the compact of some human society, who can doubt that it must be done? — for the only human society that is just is the one that serves you. But blessed are those who know that it is you who command. For everything your servants did was done either to show what the present hour needed, or to foreshadow what was to come.

Ignorant of all this, I laughed at those holy servants and prophets of yours. And what did my laughing accomplish, except that you laughed at me? For little by little, step by step, I was drawn into absurdities like these: that a fig weeps when it is picked, and that its mother the tree sheds tears of milk. And yet if some Manichaean "saint" ate that fig — picked, naturally, by someone else's guilt, not his own — he would blend it into his guts and breathe angels out of it; better still, as he groaned and moaned in prayer, he would belch up particles of God — particles of the most high and true God that would have stayed locked in that fruit forever had they not been freed by the teeth and belly of one of the Elect. And I, wretch that I was, believed that more mercy was owed to the fruits of the earth than to the human beings for whose sake they grow. If a hungry man who was not a Manichee had asked for a bite, giving it to him would have seemed like sentencing that morsel to death.

And you sent down your hand from on high and drew my soul out of that deep darkness — because my mother, your faithful one, wept to you for me, more than mothers weep over the bodies of dead children. By the faith and the spirit she had from you, she saw my death; and you heard her, Lord. You heard her, and did not despise her tears when they streamed down and watered the ground beneath her eyes in every place where she prayed. You heard her. For where else did that dream come from, by which you comforted her, so that she consented to live with me and share the same table in her house? She had begun to refuse it, turning away in loathing from the blasphemies of my error. In the dream she saw herself standing on a kind of wooden rule, and a radiant young man coming toward her, cheerful and smiling at her, while she grieved, crushed under her grief. He asked her the causes of her sorrow and her daily tears — asking, as such visitors do, to teach and not to learn — and she answered that she was mourning my perdition. Then he told her to rest easy, and bade her look and see: where she was, there I was also. And she looked, and saw me standing beside her on the same rule. Where did this come from, if not that your ears were bent to her heart? O good Omnipotent — you who care for each one of us as if you cared for that one alone, and care for all as if for one.

And where did this come from too? When she told me the vision, and I tried to twist it to mean that she should not despair of one day being what I was, she answered at once, without a moment's hesitation: "No. It was not said to me, Where he is, there you will be. It was said, Where you are, there he will be." I confess to you, Lord, as far as I remember — and I have told it often — that this answer of yours through my mother, wide awake, moved me even then more than the dream itself, in which the joy of that devout woman, to be fulfilled so long afterward, was foretold so long in advance to console her present anguish. She was not thrown by my false reading, plausible as it was; she saw in an instant what had to be seen, which I certainly had not seen until she said it. For almost nine years followed, in which I wallowed in the mud of that deep pit and in the darkness of falsehood — often trying to rise, and slammed down all the harder. And through all that time, that chaste, devout, and sober widow — the kind you love — brisker now with hope, but no slacker in weeping and mourning, never stopped lamenting over me to you in every hour of her prayers. Her prayers came into your presence — and still you let me roll, and roll again, in that darkness.

Meanwhile you gave her another answer, one I remember — for I am passing over much, hurrying toward the things that most press me to confess to you, and much I have simply forgotten. You gave her another answer, then, through a priest of yours, a certain bishop reared in the church and well trained in your books. When that woman begged him to be so kind as to talk with me — to refute my errors, unteach me my evils and teach me what was good (he did this whenever he found people fit for it) — he refused, and wisely, as I understood afterwards. He answered that I was still unteachable: I was puffed up with the newness of that heresy, and had already needled plenty of untrained people with my little trick questions, as she herself had told him. "Leave him alone for a while," he said. "Only pray to the Lord for him. Reading on his own, he will discover what that error is, and how great its impiety." At the same time he told her how he himself, as a small boy, had been handed over to the Manichees by his mother, who had been led astray; how he had not only read but copied out nearly all their books; and how it had become plain to him, with no one arguing or persuading him, that the sect was to be fled — and he had fled it. When he had said this and she still would not be satisfied, but pressed him harder, pleading and weeping freely, that he would see me and talk with me, he lost patience a little at her insistence and said: "Go now, leave me. As surely as you live, it cannot be that the son of these tears should be lost." In her talks with me afterward she often recalled that she had received those words as if they had sounded from heaven.
