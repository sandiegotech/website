# Confessions, Book II

The Stolen Pears

I want to remember now my past foulness and the carnal corruptions of my soul — not because I love them, but so that I can love you, my God. For love of your love I do this: I go back over my most vicious ways in the very bitterness of the remembering, so that you may turn sweet to me — you sweetness that never fails, sweetness happy and secure — you who gather me back from the scattering in which I was cut into pieces when, turned away from you, the One, I dissolved into the many. For in my adolescence there was a time when I burned to gorge myself on the lowest things, and I dared to run wild in one shadowy love after another. My beauty wasted away, and I rotted in your sight — pleased with myself, and eager to please the eyes of men.

And what was it I delighted in, except to love and be loved? But I did not hold to the measure of mind reaching to mind, that bright boundary-line of friendship. Instead, out of the muddy lust of the flesh and the bubbling spring of adolescence, mists steamed up that clouded and darkened my heart, until I could not tell the clear sky of love from the fog of lust. Both boiled in me together, confused, and dragged my unsteady young self over the cliffs of desire and drowned me in a whirlpool of disgrace. Your anger had built up over me, and I did not know it. I had gone deaf from the clanking of the chain of my own mortality — the punishment for the pride of my soul — and I moved further from you, and you let me go. I was tossed about, poured out, scattered in every direction; I boiled over in my fornications — and you said nothing. O my joy, how slow you were in coming! You said nothing then, and I traveled further and further from you, into more and more sterile seedbeds of sorrow, proud in my dejection, restless in my exhaustion.

If only someone back then had set my chaos in order — had taken the fleeting loveliness of these lowest things, the far edge of your creation, and turned it to some use; had fixed a limit to their sweetness, so that the surf of my youth, if it could not come to calm, might at least have spent itself on the shore of marriage, content to raise children, as your law prescribes, Lord. For that is how you shape the offspring of our mortality, able to lay a gentle hand on thorns that never grew in your paradise, and blunt them; your omnipotence is not far from us even when we are far from you. Or else I should have listened more sharply to the voice out of your clouds: "Still, those who marry will have trouble in the flesh, and I would spare you." And: "It is good for a man not to touch a woman." And: "The unmarried man thinks about the things of the Lord, how to please the Lord; but the married man cares about the things of this world, how to please his wife."

I should have listened harder to those words. I should have made myself a eunuch for the kingdom of heaven's sake and waited, happier, for your embrace. But instead I seethed, poor wretch, like a sea churning after its own current; I abandoned you and overran every limit you had set — and I did not escape your lash. What mortal can? You were with me the whole time, mercifully severe, sprinkling the bitterest disappointments over every one of my forbidden delights, so that I would learn to look for delight with no bitterness in it — and find that nowhere could I find it but in you, Lord. Nowhere but in you, who use sorrow to teach, and wound us to heal us, and kill us so that we will not die away from you. Where was I? How far was I exiled from the delights of your house, in that sixteenth year of the age of my flesh, when the madness of lust took up the scepter over me — lust licensed by human shamelessness, unlicensed by your laws — and I surrendered to it completely? Meanwhile my family took no care to catch me as I fell by marrying me off; their one care was that I learn to make the finest speeches and to persuade with words.

That year my studies were interrupted. I was back from Madaura — the neighboring city where I had gone to study literature and public speaking — while the money for the longer journey to Carthage was being scraped together, more by my father's determination than by his means, for he was a citizen of Thagaste with very little to his name. Who am I telling this to? Not to you, my God. But in your presence I am telling it to my own kind — to the human race, or whatever tiny fraction of it may happen upon these pages. And why? So that whoever reads this may consider how deep the pit is from which we must cry out to you. For what comes closer to your ears than a heart that confesses and a life of faith? Back then, who did not have praise for my father — a man spending beyond the strength of his estate to outfit his son for a distant journey, all for the sake of study? Many citizens far richer did nothing of the kind for their children. Yet this same father never troubled himself with how I was growing toward you, or how chaste I was — only that I turn out fluent, however much of a desert I was, untilled by you, O God, the one true and good Lord of that field of yours, my heart.

But during that sixteenth year, home with my parents, out of school for a while — a season of idleness forced on us by the family's thin finances — the briers of lust grew up over my head, and there was no hand to root them out. Worse: when my father saw me at the baths, becoming a man now, dressed in a restless adolescence, he told my mother about it delightedly, as if he were already dreaming of grandchildren — delighted with that drunkenness in which the world forgets you, its Creator, and falls in love with your creature instead of you, drunk on the invisible wine of a will bent and tilted toward the bottom. But in my mother's breast you had already begun your temple and laid the first foundation of your holy dwelling — my father was still only a catechumen, and a recent one at that. So she startled up with a holy fear and trembling; and though I was not yet baptized, she was afraid for me — afraid of the crooked roads walked by those who turn toward you their backs and not their faces.

Woe is me! Do I dare to say that you were silent, my God, while I wandered further from you? Were you really silent to me then? Then whose words were they, if not yours, that you sang in my ears through my mother, your faithful one? Not that any of them sank into my heart deep enough to act on. She wanted — I remember how privately, how anxiously she warned me — that I keep clear of fornication, and above all that I never sleep with another man's wife. This sounded to me like women's advice; I would have blushed to obey it. But the advice was yours, and I did not know it. I decided you were silent and she was the one talking — she, through whom you were not silent to me; and in her it was you being despised, by me, her son, the son of your servant woman, and your servant. But I did not know it. I went headlong, so blind that among boys my own age I was embarrassed to be less shameless than they were, hearing them brag about their outrages — bragging louder the filthier they got. I came to like doing the thing not only for the pleasure of it, but for the praise. What deserves contempt, if not vice? Yet to duck contempt I made myself out more vicious than I was; and where I had not sinned up to the level of the worst of them, I would claim to have done what I had not done — terrified of seeming more despicable for being more innocent, and cheaper the more chaste I was.

Look at the companions I ran with through the streets of Babylon — how I rolled in its filth as though in cinnamon and precious perfumes. And to fasten me all the tighter to Babylon's dead center, the invisible enemy trampled me and seduced me, because I was easy to seduce. The mother of my flesh had already fled the center of that Babylon herself — though she still went slowly along its outskirts. She had counseled me to chastity; but what her husband had told her about me — this thing she could feel was diseased already and dangerous for the years ahead — she did not take care to hem inside the boundary of married love, if it could not be cut back to the quick. She let it be, because she feared a wife would be a shackle on my hopes. Not that hope of the world to come, which my mother rested in you — the hope of my education, which both my parents wanted for me far too much: my father, because he gave you next to no thought, and had only empty ambitions for me; my mother, because she reckoned the usual course of studies would be no obstacle on my way to you and might even be some help. So I guess, at least, recalling as well as I can what my parents were like. Meanwhile the reins were slackened on me far beyond any just strictness, and I was left to play — play shading into dissipation — at whatever I had a taste for. And over all of it, my God, hung a fog that shut me off from the clear sky of your truth; and my iniquity came oozing out of me, as if out of fat.

Theft is punished by your law, Lord — by the law written in human hearts, which not even iniquity itself can rub out. For what thief calmly puts up with being robbed? Not even a rich thief puts up with one who steals from need. Yet I chose to steal, and steal I did, driven by no need — unless the need was my poverty of justice, my disgust with doing right, my glut of iniquity. For I stole what I already had plenty of, and better. And I had no wish to enjoy the thing I stole: what I wanted to enjoy was the theft itself, the sin itself. There was a pear tree near our vineyard, loaded with fruit that tempted nobody, in color or in taste. In the dead of night — having strung out our street games till then, in our pestilent fashion — a pack of us worthless young men went to shake it down and haul it off. We carried away enormous loads, not for our own eating — though we tasted a few — but to throw, in the end, to the pigs; and all of it just to do a thing that pleased us because it was not allowed. Look at my heart, O God, look at my heart, which you pitied at the bottom of the bottomless pit. Let my heart tell you now what it was after down there: that I should be evil for free — that my evil should have no cause but the evil itself. The thing was foul, and I loved it. I loved my own ruin; I loved my own falling — not the thing I fell toward, the falling itself. A filthy soul, leaping off your firmament into sheer destruction — reaching for nothing through the shame, but for the shame.

There is a real allure in beautiful bodies, in gold and silver, in all of it; in the sense of touch, bodily affinity counts for much, and every other sense finds objects fitted exactly to it. Worldly honor has its own grace, and so do the power to command and the power to prevail — which is also where the thirst for revenge springs from. And yet, to get any of these things, we are not to walk away from you, Lord, or swerve from your law. Even this life we live here has its enchantment, through a certain proportion of its own and its harmony with all these lower beauties. And human friendship is a sweet knot, dear to us, because it ties many souls into one. Sin gets committed on account of all this and things like it: in an immoderate lunge toward these goods of the lowest order, the better and higher goods are abandoned — you, our Lord God, and your truth, and your law. The lowest things have their delights, but nothing like my God, who made everything; for in him the righteous man delights, and he is the joy of the upright in heart.

So when we ask why a particular crime was committed, we do not usually believe the answer unless it turns out there was an appetite to get one of those goods we called lower — or a fear of losing one. For they are beautiful and fitting; even if, next to the higher goods, the goods that make life blessed, they are shabby and small. A man has murdered another man. Why did he do it? He was in love with the man's wife, or wanted his land; or he meant to rob him and live off it; or he was afraid the man would take some such thing from him; or he had been wronged, and burned to be avenged. Would anyone commit murder over nothing, delighted by the murder alone? Who would believe it? Even for that raging, savage man of whom it was written that he was evil and cruel for no reason, a reason is given a sentence later: "so that idleness," the historian says, "should not slow his hand or his mind." And why even that? So that by this daily rehearsal of crimes he might take the city, and grab honors, commands, and wealth; and be free of the fear of the laws, and of the money troubles and the guilty knowledge of his own villainies that were pressing him. No — not even Catiline loved his crimes. He loved something else, and the crimes were for its sake.

So what did I love in you, my theft — you crime of mine committed at night, in the sixteenth year of my life? There was nothing beautiful about you: you were theft. Are you even a thing at all, that I should be talking to you? The pears were beautiful, those pears we stole — because they were made by you, the most beautiful of all, Creator of all, good God: God the highest good, and my true good. The pears were beautiful; but they were not what my miserable soul wanted. I had plenty of better ones. I picked those only to be stealing; once picked, I threw them away. My only meal there was the wickedness itself, and I ate it with relish. If any bite of those pears entered my mouth, the sin was its seasoning. And now, Lord my God, I ask what it was in that theft that delighted me — and look: it has no loveliness at all. I do not mean the loveliness that lives in justice and prudence, or in the human mind, in memory, in the senses, in the life that quickens us; not the beauty of the stars, glorious in their stations, or of the earth and the sea, teeming with newborn lives that replace, by being born, the lives that die. It does not even have that stunted, shadowy beauty that belongs to the vices when they deceive us.

For pride is a counterfeit of height — while you alone are God, high above everything. And what does ambition hunt but honors and glory — while you alone are to be honored before all things, and glorious forever? The cruelty of the powerful wants to be feared — but who should be feared except God alone? From his power what can be snatched or smuggled away — when, or where, or to where, or by whom? The wanton's caresses want to pass for love — but nothing caresses more tenderly than your charity, and no love is healthier than the love of your truth, beautiful and luminous past everything. Curiosity poses as a passion for knowledge — while it is you who supremely know all things. Even ignorance and sheer stupidity dress up in the names of simplicity and harmlessness — because nothing simpler than you can be found; and what is less harmful than you, since it is a sinner's own works that come back against him? Sloth presents itself as a longing for rest — but what rest is sure, except the Lord? Self-indulgence would rather be called fullness and plenty — but you are the fullness, the never-failing store of pleasure that cannot spoil. Wastefulness throws the shadow of generosity — but you are the most lavish giver of every good. Greed wants to own many things — and you own everything. Envy quarrels over excellence — and what is more excellent than you? Anger demands vengeance — and who avenges more justly than you? Fear flinches at the strange and the sudden, threats to the things it loves, and scrambles to keep them safe — but to you what is strange? What is sudden? Who can take from you what you love? And where, except with you, is safety that cannot be shaken? Grief pines over lost things its desire once fed on with delight, wishing nothing could ever be taken from it — the way nothing can be taken from you.

This is how the soul commits fornication: it turns away from you and looks outside you for what it can never find pure and unclouded until it comes back to you. All those who put themselves far from you and raise themselves up against you are imitating you — perversely. But even by imitating you that way, they admit that you are the Creator of the whole of nature, and that therefore there is nowhere to withdraw from you altogether. So what did I love in that theft? In what way was I imitating my Lord — even viciously, even perversely? Did it please me to break your law by fraud, since I could not break it by force — to play at a maimed freedom, prisoner that I was, by getting away with what was not allowed, in a shadow-play of omnipotence? Look at him: a slave running from his master, and what he catches is a shadow. What rottenness! What a monstrosity of life, what a depth of death — that I could like a thing I was not allowed to do, for no reason except that I was not allowed!

What can I give back to the Lord for this — that my memory can revisit all of it and my soul feel no terror? I will love you, Lord, and thank you, and confess to your name, because you have forgiven me such evils, such unspeakable acts of mine. I put it down to your grace and your mercy that you melted my sins away like ice. And I put down to your grace, too, all the evil I did not do. What was I not capable of — I, who could love a crime for nothing? I confess it has all been forgiven me: the evils I did by my own choice, and the evils that, with you steering, I did not do. Where is the man who will weigh his own weakness and still dare to credit his purity and innocence to his own strength — and so love you less, as though he had less need of that mercy of yours by which you forgive the sins of everyone who turns to you? A man you called, who followed your voice and kept clear of the things he now reads me remembering and confessing about myself — let him not laugh at me. I was sick, and I was healed by the same Physician who kept him from falling sick, or rather kept him less sick. Let him love you for that as much as I do — no, more: for he sees that the one who pulled me out of so deep a wasting sickness of sin is the very one who kept him from wasting that way at all.

What harvest did I ever get, wretched as I was, from the things I blush now to remember — above all from that theft, in which the theft itself was all I loved? The theft itself was nothing — and I was that much more wretched for loving it. And yet, alone, I would not have done it. I remember what I was then: alone, I would never have done it. So I also loved the company of the ones I did it with. Then I did love something besides the theft after all? No — nothing besides; because the company, too, was nothing. What is it really? Who can teach me, except the one who lights up my heart and clears out its dark corners? What is this thing that has come into my mind to be asked about, argued, weighed? If I had loved the pears I stole, and wanted the taste of them, I could have done it alone — if the wrong itself had been enough to deliver my pleasure. I would not have needed to kindle the itch of my desire against the friction of accomplice minds. But my pleasure was not in those pears. It was in the crime itself, and the crime was made by the company of us sinning together.

What was that state of mind? Whatever it was, it was foul beyond words, and woe to me for having it. But what was it? Who understands his own faults? It was laughter — a kind of tickle in the heart at the thought that we were fooling people who never suspected us and would have hated what we were doing. Why, then, did it delight me that I was not doing it alone? Is it that nobody laughs easily alone? Not easily, no — and yet laughter will sometimes overpower a person who is completely alone, with no one else anywhere near, if something funny enough strikes his senses or his mind. But this — I would not have done this alone. Alone, I would never have done it. Here it is before you, my God: the living memory of my soul. By myself I would not have committed that theft — a theft in which what I stole gave me no pleasure, only the stealing did; and stealing by myself would have given me none, and I would not have stolen. Unfriendly friendship! Seduction of the mind that no one can trace: out of games and jokes, a greed to do harm; an appetite for another's loss, with nothing in it for me — no gain, no grudge. Just someone saying, "Come on — let's do it," and the shame of not being shameless.

Who can untangle that twisted, matted knot? It is foul. I do not want to keep staring at it; I do not want to see it. I want you — Justice, Innocence, beautiful and honorable to honest eyes, a fullness no one ever tires of. With you is rest, rest to the very bottom, and life that nothing disturbs. Whoever enters into you enters into the joy of his Lord; he will not be afraid, and he will be at his best in the One who is best. I drained away from you and I wandered, my God — too far off every path from you, my steadiness, in those years of my youth — and I became to myself a land of famine.
