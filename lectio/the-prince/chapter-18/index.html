The Prince, Chapter XVIII

How Princes Should Keep Their Word

Chapter XVIII18 of 26

Everyone understands how praiseworthy it is in a prince to keep his word and to live with integrity and not by cunning. Nevertheless, experience in our own times shows that the princes who have done great things are the ones who have held their word cheap, who have known how to run rings around men's wits with cunning — and in the end they have beaten the ones who founded themselves on honesty. You must know, then, that there are two ways of fighting: one with laws, the other with force. The first is proper to man, the second to beasts; but because the first is often not enough, one must have recourse to the second. So a prince needs to know how to use both the beast and the man well. This point was taught to princes in veiled form by the ancient writers, who tell how Achilles and many other princes of antiquity were given to Chiron the centaur to be raised and brought up under his discipline. Having a half-beast, half-man for a teacher means nothing else than this: a prince needs to know how to use both natures, and the one does not last without the other. Since a prince is forced, then, to know how to use the beast well, he should pick from among the beasts the fox and the lion; for the lion cannot defend himself from snares, and the fox cannot defend himself from wolves. So one must be a fox to recognize the snares, and a lion to frighten off the wolves. Those who stand simply on the lion do not understand their business. A prudent lord, therefore, cannot keep his word — and should not — when keeping it works against him and when the reasons that made him promise are gone. If men were all good, this precept would not be good; but because they are wicked, and would not keep their word to you, you need not keep yours to them. Nor has a prince ever lacked legitimate grounds with which to color over his breach of faith. Of this one could give countless modern examples, and show how many peace treaties and how many promises have been made void and empty by the faithlessness of princes; and the one who has known best how to use the fox has come out best.

But this nature has to be painted over well: one must be a great pretender and dissembler. And men are so simple, and so obedient to the necessities of the moment, that the deceiver will always find someone willing to be deceived. Of recent examples I will not keep silent about one. Alexander the Sixth never did anything else, never thought about anything else, than deceiving men, and he always found subjects it could be done to. Never was there a man with greater force in asserting a thing, or who affirmed it with heavier oaths and kept it less; and yet his deceptions always came off exactly as he wished, because he knew this side of the world so well.

A prince, then, need not actually have all the qualities listed above, but he very much needs to appear to have them. Indeed, I will dare to say this: that having them and observing them always is harmful, and appearing to have them is useful — to appear merciful, faithful, humane, upright, religious, and to be so; but with your mind so built that, if you need not to be so, you can and do know how to change into the contrary.

And this has to be understood: a prince, and especially a new prince, cannot observe all the things for which men are called good, since to maintain his state he is often forced to act against faith, against charity, against humanity, against religion. So he needs a mind ready to turn as the winds of fortune and the shifting of things command him; and, as I said above, not to depart from good while he can, but to know how to enter into evil when he has to.

A prince must therefore take great care that nothing ever falls from his mouth that is not full of the five qualities named above, so that to see him and hear him he seems all mercy, all faith, all integrity, all humanity, all religion. And nothing is more necessary to appear to have than this last quality. Men in general judge more by the eye than by the hand; for everyone gets to see you, few get to touch you. Everyone sees what you appear to be; few feel what you are, and those few do not dare set themselves against the opinion of the many, who have the majesty of the state defending them. And in the actions of all men, and most of all of princes, where there is no court of appeal, one looks to the outcome.

So let a prince win and hold his state: the means will always be judged honorable, and everyone will praise them; because the crowd is taken by appearances and by the way a thing comes out — and in the world there is no one but the crowd; the few find no footing when the many have something to lean on.

A certain prince of the present time, whom it is better not to name, never preaches anything but peace and good faith, and he is the bitterest enemy of both; and if he had ever observed either one, it would more than once have cost him his reputation or his state.
