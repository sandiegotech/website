# The Prince, Chapter II

Hereditary Principalities

I will leave republics aside, since I have discussed them at length elsewhere. I will turn only to principalities, taking up the threads laid out above, and ask how these principalities can be governed and kept.

I say at once that hereditary states, accustomed to the bloodline of their prince, are far less difficult to hold than new ones. It is enough not to break the customs of one's ancestors, and to play for time when events turn; then a prince of ordinary industry will always keep his state, unless some extraordinary and overwhelming force strips him of it — and even stripped of it, he wins it back the moment the usurper stumbles.

Italy gives us an example in the Duke of Ferrara, who held out against the assaults of the Venetians in '84, and of Pope Julius in '10, for no other reason than that his house was old in that dominion. For the hereditary prince has fewer reasons to give offense, and less need to; hence he is more loved; and unless outrageous vices make him hated, it stands to reason that his own people are naturally well disposed toward him. In the age and continuity of his rule, the memories of innovation and the causes of it are extinguished — for one change always leaves jutting brickwork for the next to build on.
