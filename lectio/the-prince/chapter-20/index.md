# The Prince, Chapter XX

Fortresses and Other Expedients

Some princes, in order to hold their states securely, have disarmed their subjects; some have kept their subject towns divided; some have fed enmities against themselves; some have set out to win over the men they suspected at the start of their rule; some have built fortresses; some have torn them down and destroyed them. And though no fixed judgment can be passed on any of this without coming to the particulars of the state where such a decision would have to be taken, nevertheless I will speak in the broad way the matter itself allows.

There has never been a case, then, of a new prince disarming his subjects; on the contrary, whenever he has found them disarmed he has always armed them. For when you arm them, those arms become yours; the men you suspected become faithful, and the ones who were faithful stay so; and from subjects they are made into your partisans. And since all your subjects cannot be armed, when you favor the ones you do arm you can deal more securely with the rest: the difference in treatment that the armed recognize toward themselves puts them in your debt, and the others excuse you, judging it necessary that men who carry more danger and stricter obligation should have more reward. But when you disarm them, you begin to offend them: you show that you distrust them, whether for cowardice or for lack of loyalty, and either of those opinions breeds hatred against you. And because you cannot stay unarmed, you are forced to turn to mercenary soldiery, which is of the kind described above; and even were it good, it cannot be good enough to defend you against powerful enemies and suspect subjects at once. Therefore, as I have said, a new prince in a new principality has always set up arms there; the histories are full of such examples. But when a prince acquires a new state that is joined like a limb to his old one, then that state must be disarmed — except for the men who were your partisans in acquiring it; and those too, with time and occasion, must be made soft and effeminate, and things arranged so that the arms of your whole state are in the hands of your own soldiers, the ones who live close to you in your old state.

Our forefathers, and the men who were counted wise, used to say that Pistoia had to be held with factions and Pisa with fortresses; and on that principle they nursed quarrels in some of their subject towns, the more easily to keep hold of them. In those days, when Italy stood in a certain balance, this may have been sound practice; but I do not think it can be given as a precept today, for I do not believe divisions ever did anyone any good. On the contrary: when the enemy approaches, divided cities are inevitably lost at once, because the weaker faction will always attach itself to the outside forces, and the other will not be able to stand. The Venetians, moved, I believe, by the reasons given above, fostered the Guelf and Ghibelline sects in their subject cities; and though they never let them come to blood, they still nursed these differences among the citizens so that, being occupied with their own quarrels, they would not unite against them. Which, as we saw, did not afterward turn out to their purpose: for when they were routed at Vailà, one of the factions immediately took heart and seized their whole dominion. Such methods, besides, betray weakness in the prince; in a vigorous principality such divisions are never permitted, for they profit you only in peacetime, letting you manage your subjects more easily by their means — but when war comes, an order of this kind shows its own falsity.

Without doubt, princes become great when they overcome the difficulties and the opposition put in their way; and so fortune — above all when she wants to make a new prince great, who has more need of winning reputation than a hereditary one — raises up enemies for him and sets them on campaigns against him, so that he may have cause to overcome them and to climb higher up the very ladder his enemies have carried to him. For this reason many judge that a wise prince, when he has the occasion for it, should cunningly feed some enmity against himself, so that by crushing it his greatness rises the higher.

Princes, and especially new ones, have found more faith and more usefulness in the men they held suspect at the beginning of their rule than in those they trusted at first. Pandolfo Petrucci, prince of Siena, governed his state more with the men he had suspected than with the others. But of this one cannot speak at large, because it varies with the case. I will say only this: men who were enemies of a prince at the beginning of his rule, if they are of the kind that need support to hold their position, can always be won over with the greatest ease; and they are the more tightly bound to serve him faithfully because they know how much they need to cancel, by their deeds, the bad opinion he had formed of them. And so the prince always draws more use from them than from men who, serving him in too much security, neglect his affairs. And since the matter requires it, I will not leave out this reminder to a prince who has newly taken a state with help from inside it: let him weigh well what reason moved the men who supported him to support him. If it was not natural affection for him, but only discontent with the old state, he will keep them friends only with labor and great difficulty, because it will be impossible for him to content them either. Turning over the reason for this in the examples drawn from ancient and modern affairs, he will see that it is far easier to win as friends the men who were content with the old state, and were therefore his enemies, than the men who, being discontent with it, became his friends and helped him seize it.

It has been the custom of princes, the better to hold their states securely, to build fortresses that serve as bridle and bit to those who might design to move against them, and as a safe refuge from a sudden assault. I praise this way of doing things, because it has been used since antiquity. Nevertheless, in our own times Messer Niccolò Vitelli was seen to raze two fortresses in Città di Castello in order to hold that state; Guidobaldo, Duke of Urbino, returning to the dominion Cesare Borgia had driven him from, tore every fortress in that province down to its foundations, judging that without them the state would be harder to lose a second time; and the Bentivogli, returning to Bologna, took similar measures. Fortresses, then, are useful or not according to the times: if they help you in one quarter, they hurt you in another. The question can be put like this: the prince who fears his own people more than foreigners ought to build fortresses; the one who fears foreigners more than his own people ought to leave them alone. The castle of Milan, which Francesco Sforza built there, has made and will make more wars against the house of Sforza than any other disorder in that state. So the best fortress there is, is not to be hated by the people; for though you hold fortresses, if the people hate you the fortresses will not save you — once the people have taken up arms, they never lack for foreigners ready to come to their aid. In our times no fortress can be seen to have profited any prince, except the Countess of Forlì, when her consort Count Girolamo was killed: her fortress let her escape the popular uprising, wait for help from Milan, and recover her state; and the times then stood so that no foreigner could come to the people's aid. But afterward her fortresses too were worth little, when Cesare Borgia attacked her and the people, hostile to her, joined the foreigner. So then, and earlier too, it would have been safer for her not to be hated by the people than to hold the fortresses. Considering all these things, then, I will praise both the prince who builds fortresses and the one who does not; and I will blame any prince who, trusting in fortresses, counts it a small thing to be hated by his people.
