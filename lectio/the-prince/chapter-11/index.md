# The Prince, Chapter XI

Ecclesiastical Principalities

It remains now only to speak of ecclesiastical principalities. With these, every difficulty comes before you possess them: they are acquired by virtù or by fortune, and kept without either, because they are sustained by the ancient institutions of religion, which have grown so powerful, and are of such a kind, that they keep their princes in power however those princes act and live. These princes alone have states and do not defend them, subjects and do not govern them; and the states, though undefended, are not taken from them, and the subjects, though ungoverned, do not care — they neither think of breaking away, nor could they. Only these principalities, then, are secure and happy. But since they are upheld by a higher cause, which the human mind cannot reach, I will say no more about them; they are raised up and maintained by God, and it would be the work of a presumptuous and reckless man to pass judgment on them.

Still, someone might ask me how it happens that the Church has risen to such greatness in temporal power — given that until Alexander the Italian powers, and not only the ones called powers but every baron and lord down to the smallest, held that temporal power cheap, and now a king of France trembles before it, and it has been able to throw him out of Italy and to ruin the Venetians. Well known as this is, I do not think it wasted effort to call some part of it back to mind.

Before Charles, King of France, came down into Italy, this country was under the rule of the Pope, the Venetians, the King of Naples, the Duke of Milan, and the Florentines. These powers had two chief anxieties: that no foreigner should enter Italy in arms, and that none of their own number should seize more territory. The two watched most closely were the Pope and the Venetians. To hold back the Venetians it took a league of all the rest, as in the defense of Ferrara; and to hold down the Pope they used the barons of Rome. Divided into two factions, Orsini and Colonnesi, the barons never lacked a pretext for disorder; standing with weapons in hand under the Pontiff's own eyes, they kept the papacy weak and sickly. Now and then a spirited pope arose — a Sixtus — but neither fortune nor wisdom could ever rid him of this nuisance. The shortness of papal lives was the cause. In the ten years an average pope reigns, he could barely beat down one of the factions; and if one pope, so to speak, had all but wiped out the Colonnesi, up came a successor who was enemy to the Orsini — which raised the Colonnesi again — and there was no time left to wipe out the Orsini. This is why the Pope's temporal powers were held in little esteem in Italy.

Then came Alexander the Sixth, who showed better than any pontiff who ever lived what a pope with money and arms could do. Using Duke Valentino as his instrument and the French invasion as his occasion, he brought about everything I described above in discussing the duke's actions. His intention was to make the duke great, not the Church; but what he did made the Church great anyway, and after his death, and the duke's ruin, the Church inherited his labors.

Then came Pope Julius, and he found the Church strong: mistress of all the Romagna, the barons of Rome crushed, the factions beaten flat under Alexander's blows — and the road open to piling up money by methods never used before Alexander's time. Julius not only continued all this but improved on it. He set out to take Bologna, to snuff out the Venetians, and to drive the French from Italy; and every one of these enterprises succeeded, with the more credit to him because he did it all to strengthen the Church and no private man. He also kept the Orsini and Colonnesi factions within the bounds in which he found them; and though there were heads among them itching to make trouble, two things held them still: the greatness of the Church, which cowed them, and the fact that they were allowed no cardinals of their own — the origin of all their disorders. These factions will never be quiet while they have cardinals, because cardinals feed the factions, in Rome and beyond, and the barons are forced to defend them: from the ambitions of prelates come the discords and tumults of the barons. So His Holiness Pope Leo has found the papacy at the height of its power; and if others made it great with arms, one may hope that he, by his goodness and his infinite other virtues, will make it greater still, and revered.
