The Prince, Chapter XIII

Auxiliaries, Mixed Troops, and One's Own

Chapter XIII13 of 26

Auxiliaries — the other useless kind of arms — are what you have when you call in a powerful outsider to come with his forces to aid you and defend you, as Pope Julius did in recent memory. Having seen in the Ferrara campaign what a sorry showing his mercenaries made, he turned to auxiliaries, and arranged with Ferdinand, King of Spain, to help him with his men and armies. These arms can be useful and good in themselves, but for the man who calls them in they are almost always ruinous: if they lose, you are undone; if they win, you are their prisoner.

Ancient histories are full of such examples, but I will stay with this fresh one of Pope Julius the Second, whose choice could hardly have been less considered: to get Ferrara, he put himself entirely into a foreigner's hands. Only his good fortune brought forth a third outcome, so that he did not reap the fruit of his bad choice. His auxiliaries were routed at Ravenna; then the Swiss rose up and — against every expectation, his own and everyone else's — chased out the victors. So he ended prisoner neither of his enemies, who had fled, nor of his auxiliaries, since the winning had been done by arms other than theirs.

The Florentines, having no arms at all of their own, brought ten thousand Frenchmen to take Pisa; and by that policy they ran more danger than at any other moment of their troubles.

The Emperor of Constantinople, to oppose his neighbors, put ten thousand Turks into Greece; and when the war was over, they would not leave. That was the beginning of Greece's servitude to the infidel.

So: the man who wants to make sure he cannot win should use these arms. They are far more dangerous than mercenaries, because with them the ruin comes ready-made: they are all one body, and all obedient to someone other than you. Mercenaries, even once they have won, need more time and a better opening to hurt you: they are not one body, and you raised them and you pay them; the outsider you set at their head cannot at once take enough authority to do you damage. In short — with mercenaries the deadliest thing is their cowardice; with auxiliaries, their virtù. Wise princes have therefore always shunned these arms and turned to their own, and have preferred to lose with their own men rather than win with another's, judging no victory real that is won with borrowed arms.

I will never hesitate to cite Cesare Borgia and his actions. This duke entered the Romagna with auxiliary arms, bringing in nothing but French soldiers, and with them he took Imola and Forlì. When those arms then struck him as unsafe, he turned to mercenaries, judging the danger there smaller, and hired the Orsini and the Vitelli. Finding these, in the handling, doubtful, disloyal, and dangerous, he destroyed them, and turned to his own men. And the difference between these kinds of arms is easily seen by considering the difference in the duke's reputation when he had the French, when he had the Orsini and Vitelli, and when he stood on his own soldiers and on himself. His reputation grew at every step; and he was never rated so high as when everyone saw that he was complete master of his own forces.

I meant not to go beyond Italian and recent examples, but I am unwilling to leave out Hiero the Syracusan, since he is one of those I named above. Made head of the army by the Syracusans, as I said, he saw at once that mercenary soldiery was useless — its captains were built just like our Italian condottieri. And concluding that he could neither keep them nor let them go, he had them all cut to pieces; after which he made war with his own arms, and not with anyone else's.

I want to recall, too, a figure from the Old Testament made for this subject. When David offered himself to Saul to go and fight Goliath, the Philistine champion, Saul, to give him heart, dressed him in his own armor — and David, once he had it on, refused it, saying he could not give a true account of himself in it: he would meet the enemy with his sling and his knife. In the end, the armor of others falls off your back, or weighs you down, or binds you tight.

Charles the Seventh, father of King Louis the Eleventh, having freed France from the English by his fortune and his virtù, understood this necessity of arming himself with his own arms, and set up in his kingdom the ordinances of the men-at-arms and the infantry. Then his son King Louis abolished the infantry and began to hire the Swiss — a mistake which, followed by others, is now plainly seen to be the source of that kingdom's dangers. For by raising the reputation of the Swiss, he degraded all his own arms: the infantry he wiped out altogether, and his men-at-arms he made dependent on the arms of others, since, being accustomed to fight alongside the Swiss, they no longer believe they can win without them. Hence the French are not enough against the Swiss, and without the Swiss they do not attempt much against anyone else. The armies of France, then, have been mixed — part mercenary, part her own; arms which, taken together, are much better than pure auxiliaries or pure mercenaries, and much inferior to one's own. The example already given is enough: the kingdom of France would be unbeatable today if the ordinance of Charles had been enlarged, or even kept.

But the small wisdom of men will begin a thing that tastes good at the time and never notice the poison underneath — as I said earlier about wasting fevers. The ruler of a principality who does not recognize evils at their birth is not truly wise; and that is given to few. And if you look for the first cause of the ruin of the Roman Empire, you will find it was nothing but the hiring of the Goths: from that beginning the strength of the Roman Empire started to slacken, and all the virtù that drained out of it was handed to them.

I conclude, then, that no principality is secure without arms of its own; it is instead wholly dependent on fortune, having no virtù to defend it faithfully in adversity. And it was ever the opinion and verdict of wise men that nothing is so weak or so unstable as a reputation for power that does not rest on strength of one's own. And one's own arms are those composed of subjects, or of citizens, or of dependents of yours: all others are mercenary or auxiliary. The way to organize your own arms is easily found if you go back over the arrangements of the four men I have named, and if you see how Philip, father of Alexander the Great, and how many republics and princes have armed and ordered themselves. To their methods I defer entirely.
