The Prince, Chapter XIV

The Prince and the Art of War

Chapter XIV14 of 26

A prince, then, should have no other object, no other thought, and take nothing else for his craft, but war, its rules, and its discipline; for that is the only art expected of the man who commands. And it is an art of such virtù that it not only keeps in place those who were born princes, but many times lifts men from private station to that rank — while the reverse is also on record: princes who thought more of luxury than of arms have lost their states. The first thing that loses you your state is neglect of this art; the thing that wins you a state is mastery of it. Francesco Sforza, because he was armed, rose from private citizen to Duke of Milan; his sons, because they fled the hardships of arms, sank from dukes to private citizens. For among the other evils that being unarmed brings you, it makes you despised — one of those disgraces, as will be said below, that a prince must guard against. Between an armed man and an unarmed man there is no proportion at all; and it is not reasonable that the armed should obey the unarmed willingly, or that the unarmed should be safe among armed servants. With disdain on one side and suspicion on the other, they cannot work well together. So a prince who understands nothing of war, beyond the other miseries already mentioned, cannot be respected by his soldiers, and cannot trust them. He should never, therefore, lift his thoughts from the exercise of war; and he should exercise it harder in peace than in war. He can do this in two ways: with action, and with the mind.

As for action: besides keeping his men well ordered and well drilled, he should always be out hunting, hardening his body to exertion and learning the nature of ground as he goes — how the mountains rise, how the valleys open, how the plains lie; what the rivers and marshes are like — and in this he should take the greatest care. The knowledge pays twice. First, he learns to know his own country, and can better see how to defend it. Second, by knowing that terrain and having it under his feet, he grasps with ease any new terrain he must scout; for the hills, valleys, plains, rivers, and marshes of Tuscany, say, bear a certain resemblance to those of other provinces, so that from knowing the ground of one province you come easily to know the ground of others. And the prince who lacks this skill lacks the first quality a captain must have; for it is what teaches you to find your enemy, choose your quarters, lead armies, order a battle, and lay siege to towns with the advantage on your side.

Philopoemen, prince of the Achaeans, among the praises writers have given him, is praised for this: that in peacetime he never thought about anything but the ways of war. When he was in the country with friends, he would often stop and reason with them: "If the enemy were on that hill and we stood here with our army, which of us would have the advantage? How could we go at them, keeping formation? If we wanted to retreat, how would we manage it? If they retreated, how should we pursue?" And as he went he would set before them every chance that can befall an army; he heard their opinions, gave his own, and backed it with reasons — so that, through this endless rehearsal, no accident could ever arise, with his armies in the field, for which he did not have the remedy.

As for exercising the mind, the prince should read histories, and study in them the actions of excellent men: see how they carried themselves in war; examine the causes of their victories and their defeats, so as to flee the one and imitate the other; and above all, do as some excellent man before him has done — take as his model some earlier man who was praised and glorified, and keep that man's deeds and actions always by him, as Alexander the Great is said to have imitated Achilles; Caesar, Alexander; Scipio, Cyrus. And whoever reads the life of Cyrus written by Xenophon will then recognize, in the life of Scipio, how much glory that imitation earned him, and how closely, in chastity, affability, humanity, and generosity, Scipio kept to what Xenophon wrote of Cyrus. Such are the ways a wise prince must observe; and he should never stand idle in peaceful times, but work in them industriously to build up capital he can draw on in adversity — so that when fortune turns, she finds him ready to resist her.
