# The Prince, Chapter XVI

Generosity and Miserliness

Beginning, then, with the first of the qualities named above, I say that it would be good to be thought generous. Nevertheless, generosity practiced in such a way that you get the name for it does you harm; for if it is practiced virtuously, and as it ought to be practiced, it will not be known, and you will not escape the infamy of its opposite. And so a man who wants to keep up the name of generous among men cannot leave out any kind of lavish display; a prince of that sort will consume his whole substance in such displays, and in the end, if he wants to keep the name of generous, he will be forced to burden his people beyond measure, to squeeze them with taxes, and to do everything that can be done to raise money. This will begin to make him hateful to his subjects and, as he grows poor, little regarded by anyone; so that, having offended the many with this generosity of his and rewarded the few, he feels the first pinch of trouble and totters at the first real danger; and when he recognizes this and tries to pull back, he runs at once into the infamy of a miser.

A prince, then, since he cannot practice this virtue of generosity in a way that is recognized without hurting himself, ought, if he is prudent, not to mind being called a miser. With time he will be thought more and more generous, once men see that his thrift makes his revenues sufficient, that he can defend himself against whoever makes war on him, and that he can undertake campaigns without burdening his people — so that he ends up practicing generosity toward all those from whom he takes nothing, who are countless, and miserliness toward all those to whom he gives nothing, who are few.

In our times we have not seen great things done except by those who were held miserly; the rest were wiped out. Pope Julius the Second, though he made use of a name for generosity to reach the papacy, did not try to keep it afterward, because he wanted to be able to make war on the King of France; and he waged all his wars without laying any extraordinary tax on his subjects, because his long thrift covered the extra expense. The present King of Spain, if he had been held generous, would not have undertaken or won so many campaigns. A prince, then, so long as he does not have to rob his subjects, can defend himself, is not forced into poverty and contempt, and is not driven to become rapacious, ought to count the name of miser for little, since it is one of the vices that make his rule possible.

And if someone says: Caesar rose to empire through his generosity, and many others have reached the highest ranks because they were generous and were held to be so — I answer: either you are already a prince, or you are on the way to becoming one. In the first case this generosity is harmful; in the second it is quite necessary to be thought generous. And Caesar was one of those trying to reach the rule of Rome; but if, after reaching it, he had lived on and had not moderated his spending, he would have destroyed that empire. And if someone replies: many who were held most generous have been princes and have done great things with their armies — I answer: a prince spends either what is his own and his subjects', or what belongs to others. In the first case he ought to be sparing; in the second he ought not to leave out any part of generosity. For the prince who marches with his armies, living on plunder, sack, and ransom, is handling other men's property, and there this openhandedness is necessary — without it his soldiers would not follow him. With what is not yours or your subjects' you can be a freer giver, as Cyrus, Caesar, and Alexander were; for spending what belongs to others takes away none of your reputation but adds to it; only spending your own hurts you.

And there is nothing that consumes itself the way generosity does: while you practice it, you lose the power to practice it, and you become either poor and despised or, to escape poverty, rapacious and hated. And among all the things a prince must guard against is being despised and hated — and generosity leads you to both. So it is wiser to keep the name of miser, which breeds an infamy without hatred, than, by straining after the name of generous, to be forced into the name of rapacious, which breeds an infamy with hatred.
