The Prince, Chapter V

Cities That Lived Free

Chapter V5 of 26

When states acquired in the way I have described are accustomed to living free under their own laws, there are three ways to hold them: the first is to ruin them; the second is to go and live there in person; the third is to let them live on under their own laws, taking a tribute from them and setting up inside a government of a few men who will keep the state friendly to you. For such a government, being the prince's creature, knows it cannot stand without his friendship and his power, and will do everything to keep him standing. And a city used to living free, if you mean to preserve it at all, is held more easily through its own citizens than by any other means.

Take the Spartans and the Romans. The Spartans held Athens and Thebes by setting up governments of a few men in them; they lost them anyway. The Romans, to hold Capua, Carthage, and Numantia, demolished them, and did not lose them. They tried to hold Greece more or less as the Spartans had held it — leaving it free, leaving it its laws — and it failed; to keep the province they were forced to demolish many of its cities. For the truth is that there is no secure way to possess such cities except to ruin them. And whoever becomes master of a city accustomed to living free, and does not destroy it, should expect to be destroyed by it; for in rebellion it will always find its rallying cry in the name of liberty and in its own old order, which neither the length of years nor any amount of good treatment ever makes it forget. Do what you like, provide what you like: unless the inhabitants are split apart or scattered, they never forget that name or that order, and at every accident they run straight back to both — as Pisa did after a hundred years in bondage to the Florentines.

But when cities or provinces are used to living under a prince and his bloodline is extinguished, then — trained to obey, their old prince gone — they can neither agree on a new prince from among themselves nor manage to live free. They are slower to take up arms, and a prince can win them more easily and hold them more securely. In republics there is more life, more hatred, more appetite for revenge. The memory of their old liberty gives them no rest — it cannot. So the safest way is to snuff them out — or to go and live there.
