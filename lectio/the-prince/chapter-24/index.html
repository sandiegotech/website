The Prince, Chapter XXIV

Why Italy's Princes Lost Their States

Chapter XXIV24 of 26

The measures set out above, prudently observed, make a new prince seem an old one, and settle him faster and more firmly in his state than if he had grown old in it. For a new prince is watched far more closely in his actions than a hereditary one; and when his actions are recognized as able, they take hold of men far more, and bind them far tighter, than ancient blood does. For men are taken much more by present things than by past ones, and when they find the present good they enjoy it and look for nothing else — indeed they will mount every defense of such a prince, so long as he does not fail them in the rest. And so he will have a double glory: to have founded a new principality, and to have adorned and strengthened it with good laws, good arms, good allies, and good examples — just as the man born a prince who loses his state through lack of prudence carries a double shame.

And if you consider the lords who have lost their states in Italy in our times — the King of Naples, the Duke of Milan, and the rest — you will find in them, first, one common defect in the matter of arms, for the causes discussed at length above; and then you will see that some of them either had the people for enemies, or, if they had the people friendly, did not know how to secure the great men. Without these defects, states are not lost that have sinew enough to keep an army in the field.

Philip of Macedon — not Alexander's father, but the one Titus Quintius defeated — did not have much of a state, measured against the greatness of the Romans and of the Greece that attacked him. Yet because he was a military man who knew how to hold the people and secure the great, he sustained the war against them for years; and if in the end he lost his grip on a few cities, the kingdom stayed his.

So let these princes of ours, who held their principalities for years on end and then lost them, blame not fortune but their own laziness. In quiet times they never once considered that things can change — it is the common defect of men not to reckon on the storm while the sea is calm — and when the hard times came, their thoughts ran to flight and not to defense, and they hoped the people, disgusted by the winners' insolence, would call them back. That plan, when all others are gone, is good; but it is very bad to have let the other remedies go for its sake. You should never accept a fall on the faith that someone will be there to pick you up. Either it does not happen, or, if it happens, it brings you no safety — because that kind of rescue is a coward's defense and does not depend on you. The only defenses that are good, that are certain, that are lasting, are the ones that depend on you yourself and on your own virtù.
