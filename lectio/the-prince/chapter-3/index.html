The Prince, Chapter III

Mixed Principalities

Chapter III3 of 26

But in the new principality the difficulties begin. First, if it is not new through and through but a limb joined to a larger state (call the whole thing, taken together, a mixed principality), its instability springs first of all from a natural difficulty found in every new principality: men change masters willingly, believing they will be better off, and that belief puts arms in their hands against the man who rules them. In this they are deceived, for experience soon teaches them that they are worse off. And that follows from another necessity, natural and ordinary: a new prince is always forced to injure the very people he acquires, both with his soldiers and with the countless other abuses a new conquest drags behind it.

So you have for enemies everyone you injured in seizing the principality, and you cannot keep as friends the men who put you there, since you cannot satisfy them as they had expected — and you cannot use strong medicine against them either, being in their debt. For however strong your armies, you always need the goodwill of the locals to enter a province.

For these reasons Louis the Twelfth, King of France, took Milan quickly and lost it quickly; and to take it from him the first time, Lodovico's own forces were enough. For the people who had opened the gates to him, finding themselves cheated of the future they had promised themselves, could not stomach the abuses of the new prince. It is quite true that when rebel territory is taken a second time it is not lost so easily, for the prince, with fewer scruples now, uses the rebellion as his license to punish the guilty, expose the suspects, and reinforce himself wherever he is weakest. So the first time, to make France lose Milan, it was enough for Duke Lodovico to raise a revolt on the borders; to make her lose it the second time took the whole world combined against her, her armies destroyed and driven out of Italy — and that came about for the reasons given above.

Nevertheless, Milan was taken from France both the first time and the second. The general reasons for the first have been given; it remains to give the reasons for the second, and to see what remedies Louis had — what remedies anyone in his place would have — to hold a new acquisition more firmly than the King of France held his.

Now I say that the dominions a conqueror gains and joins to an older state of his own either belong to the same country and language, or they do not. When they do, holding them is very easy, especially when they are not used to living free; to possess them securely, it is enough to have extinguished the line of the prince who ruled them. For the rest, leave the old conditions alone; where customs match, men live quietly together — as they have in Brittany, Burgundy, Gascony, and Normandy, so long bound to France. There may be some difference of language, but the customs are alike, and people manage easily among themselves. Whoever acquires such lands and means to keep them must see to just two things: that the old prince's bloodline is extinguished, and that neither laws nor taxes are altered. In a very short time the new territory and the old principality become one body.

But when states are acquired in a province that differs in language, in customs, and in institutions, here the difficulties begin, and here you need great fortune and great industry to keep hold. One of the best and most immediate remedies is for the conqueror to go and live there in person. That makes the possession more secure and more lasting — as it has for the Turk in Greece, who, with every other measure he took for holding that state, could not have kept it without settling in it. On the spot, you see disorders as they are born, and you can put them down at once; from a distance, you hear of them only when they are full grown, and past curing. Besides this, the province is not stripped bare by your officials; the subjects are satisfied by quick access to their prince, which gives them more cause to love him if they mean to be good, and more cause to fear him if they do not. Any foreign power thinking of attacking the state hesitates; a prince who lives in it is dispossessed only with the greatest difficulty.

The other and better remedy is to plant colonies in one or two places that will serve as shackles on that state; either you do this, or you must keep large numbers of cavalry and infantry there instead. Colonies cost little. At little or no expense of his own, the prince sends them out and keeps them there, and he injures only those he strips of fields and houses to give to the new settlers — a minority of that state, and one left poor and scattered, which can never do him harm. All the rest remain uninjured on one side, and therefore easily quieted, and frightened on the other, in case what happened to the despoiled should happen next to them. I conclude: colonies cost nothing, are more loyal, and give less offense; and the offended, poor and scattered, cannot hurt you, as I have said. For here is a thing to note: men should be either treated well or snuffed out. They can avenge a light injury; a fatal one they cannot. So the injury done to a man must be of the kind that leaves no revenge to fear.

But keep armed men there instead of colonies and the cost multiplies: the whole revenue of the state goes to feed its garrison, until the acquisition runs at a loss. And it gives far more offense, since the whole state feels the injury as the garrison shifts its quarters from place to place. Everyone tastes the hardship; everyone becomes an enemy — and they are enemies who can still hurt you, beaten but still at home. On every count, then, a garrison is as useless as a colony is useful.

Again, a prince holding a province unlike his own, as I have said, should make himself the head and protector of the smaller powers around him, work to weaken the strong ones, and take care that no foreigner as powerful as himself finds a way in by any accident. For such a one will always be called in by the discontented, out of too much ambition or out of fear — as the Ætolians once brought the Romans into Greece; indeed, into every province the Romans entered, they were brought by its own inhabitants. And the order of things is this: the moment a powerful foreigner enters a province, all its lesser powers attach themselves to him, driven by envy of the power that has stood over them — so that as regards these lesser powers he need not lift a finger to win them, since all together they immediately and willingly make one mass with the state he has taken there. He has only to see that they do not lay hands on too much strength and too much authority; then, with his own forces and their goodwill, he can easily bring down the powerful and remain, in everything, the arbiter of that province. And whoever fails to manage this part well will quickly lose what he has gained — and while he holds it, will find in it infinite difficulties and vexations.

In the provinces they seized, the Romans kept these rules exactly: they planted colonies; they humored the lesser powers without increasing their strength; they beat down the strong; and they never let a powerful foreigner win credit there. The province of Greece will do as the one example. The Romans kept the Achaeans and the Ætolians as friends; the kingdom of Macedonia was humbled; Antiochus was driven out. Yet no merit of the Achaeans or the Ætolians ever bought them leave to enlarge a state of theirs; no persuasion from Philip ever induced the Romans to be his friends until they had brought him low; and all the power of Antiochus never made them consent to his keeping any state in that province. For the Romans did in these cases what every wise prince must do: attend not only to present disorders but to future ones, and ward those off with every kind of effort. Seen from far off, they are easily remedied; wait until they are on top of you, and the medicine is too late — the disease has become incurable. It is as the physicians say of the wasting fever: early on, it is easy to cure and hard to detect; with time, neither detected nor treated, it becomes easy to detect and impossible to cure. So it goes in matters of state. Recognize evils while they are still forming — a gift given only to the prudent — and they are cured quickly. Fail to recognize them, let them grow until anyone can see them, and no remedy remains. So the Romans, seeing troubles while they were still distant, always dealt with them then, and never let one run on merely to escape a war; they knew that a war is not escaped — only postponed, to the advantage of others. So they chose to fight Philip and Antiochus in Greece rather than have to fight them in Italy. They could, at the time, have avoided both. They chose not to. And they never cared for the saying that is forever in the mouths of the wise men of our day — enjoy the benefit of time. They preferred the benefit of their own virtù and prudence. For time drives everything before it, and can bring good as easily as evil, evil as easily as good.

But let us return to France and consider whether she did any of the things I have named. I will speak of Louis and not of Charles, since Louis held possessions in Italy longer, and his course is the easier to read; and you will see that he did the contrary of everything that must be done to hold a state unlike one's own.

King Louis was brought into Italy by the ambition of the Venetians, who meant his coming to win them half the state of Lombardy. I will not blame the king for the part he took: wanting a first foothold in Italy, and having no friends in the province — every door barred to him, indeed, by the conduct of Charles — he had to take whatever friendships he could get; and the plan would have succeeded quickly enough had he made no errors elsewhere. With Lombardy taken, the king at once won back the standing Charles had thrown away: Genoa yielded; the Florentines became his friends; the Marquess of Mantua, the Duke of Ferrara, the Bentivogli, my lady of Forlì, the lords of Faenza, of Pesaro, of Rimini, of Camerino, of Piombino, the Lucchese, the Pisans, the Sienese — all came forward to offer him their friendship. Then the Venetians had leisure to consider the rashness of what they had done: to gain themselves two towns in Lombardy, they had made the king master of two-thirds of Italy.

Consider now how little trouble the king would have had in holding his standing in Italy if he had observed the rules set down above and kept all those friends of his secure and defended. They were many, they were weak, they were afraid — some of the Church, some of the Venetians — and so they were bound always to stand with him; and through them he could easily have secured himself against the powers that remained great. But he was no sooner in Milan than he did the reverse, lending Pope Alexander a hand to seize the Romagna. And it did not occur to him that with this decision he was weakening himself — casting off his friends, and the people who had thrown themselves into his lap — while making the Church great, stacking so much temporal power onto the spiritual power that already gives it such authority. Having made the first error, he was dragged into the rest, until finally, to check Alexander's ambition and keep him from becoming master of Tuscany, he was forced to come into Italy himself.

And as if it were not enough to have made the Church great and stripped himself of his friends, he then wanted the kingdom of Naples — and divided it with the King of Spain. Where he had been sole arbiter of Italy he installed a partner, so that the ambitious men of that province, and the malcontents of his own party, would have somewhere to turn; and where he could have left in that kingdom a king who was his pensioner, dependent on him, he removed him — and put in his place a man capable of driving out Louis himself.

The wish to acquire is a thing truly natural and ordinary; when men who can, acquire, they will be praised for it, or at least not blamed. The error, and the blame, come when men who cannot are set on acquiring anyway. If France could have taken Naples with her own forces, she should have taken it; since she could not, she should not have split it. The split she made of Lombardy with the Venetians had an excuse: it bought her entry into Italy. This one had no such necessity behind it, and so it stands condemned.

Louis, then, had made five errors: he destroyed the lesser powers; he increased the power of one already great in Italy; he brought into Italy a foreign power of the first rank; he did not come to live there; he did not plant colonies. And even these five, in his lifetime, might not have hurt him had he not made a sixth: taking their dominions from the Venetians. Had he never made the Church great, never brought Spain into Italy, then cutting the Venetians down would have been reasonable, even necessary. But having taken those first steps, he should never have consented to their ruin: while the Venetians were strong, they would have kept everyone else clear of any venture against Lombardy — partly because the Venetians would allow no such venture unless it ended with Lombardy theirs, partly because the others had no wish to take it from France only to hand it to the Venetians, and no nerve to take on both together.

And if anyone says that King Louis ceded the Romagna to Alexander, and the kingdom to Spain, to avoid a war — I answer with the reasons given above: a disorder should never be allowed to run on for the sake of avoiding a war, because the war is not avoided; it is deferred, to your own disadvantage. And if others cite the pledge the king had given the Pope — this campaign in exchange for the dissolution of his marriage and a cardinal's hat for Rouen — I answer with what I will say further on about the word of princes, and how far it should be kept.

So King Louis lost Lombardy by keeping none of the conditions kept by others who have taken provinces and meant to hold them. Nor is there any miracle in this — it is ordinary, and entirely reasonable. I said as much at Nantes to the Cardinal of Rouen, when Valentino — as Cesare Borgia, Pope Alexander's son, was commonly called — was occupying the Romagna. When the Cardinal of Rouen told me that the Italians understood nothing of war, I answered that the French understood nothing of statecraft; for if they did, they would not be letting the Church grow so great. And experience has borne it out: the greatness of the Church and of Spain in Italy was made by France, and France's ruin was made by them. From which a general rule can be drawn that never fails, or rarely: whoever makes another man powerful ruins himself. He builds that power with either cunning or force — and cunning and force are exactly what the man he raised will never trust.
