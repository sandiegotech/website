# The Prince, Chapter XIX

Avoiding Contempt and Hatred

Now that I have spoken of the most important of the qualities mentioned above, I want to run briefly through the others under this general heading: that the prince should attend, as was partly said above, to avoiding the things that make him hated or held in contempt. Whenever he avoids these, he will have done his part, and he will find no danger at all in his other bad names.

What makes him hated above everything, as I said, is to be rapacious — a usurper of the property and the women of his subjects. From these he must keep away: whenever you take neither property nor honor from the generality of men, they live content, and you have only to contend with the ambition of the few, which can be checked easily, and in many ways.

What makes him contemptible is to be held changeable, frivolous, effeminate, faint-hearted, irresolute. A prince must guard against these as a ship guards against a reef, and contrive that in his actions men recognize greatness, spirit, gravity, and strength; and concerning the private dealings of his subjects, he should insist that his sentence be irrevocable, and keep up such an opinion of himself that no one thinks of deceiving him or getting around him.

The prince who gives this impression of himself stands in high repute, and against a man of such repute conspiracy is difficult, and attack is difficult, so long as it is understood that he is excellent and revered by his own people. For a prince must have two fears: one inside, on account of his subjects; one outside, on account of foreign powers. From the outside he defends himself with good arms and good allies — and if he has good arms he will always have good allies. And things inside will always stand firm when things outside stand firm, unless a conspiracy has already disturbed them; and even if the outside does move against him, if he has ordered himself and lived as I have said, and does not lose heart, he will withstand every assault — as I said Nabis the Spartan did.

But as for his subjects, when things outside are quiet, his fear is that they may conspire in secret. From this the prince secures himself well enough by escaping hatred and contempt and keeping the people satisfied with him — which it is necessary to accomplish, as was said above at length. And among the most powerful remedies a prince has against conspiracies is precisely this: not being hated by the people at large. For whoever conspires always believes the prince's death will please the people; when he believes it will offend them, he cannot screw up the courage for such a course, because the difficulties on the conspirators' side are infinite. Experience shows that conspiracies have been many, and few have ended well. For a conspirator cannot act alone, and he can take companions only from those he believes discontented; and the moment you open your mind to a discontented man, you give him the means to content himself, since by informing on you he can hope for every kind of reward. Seeing sure gain on that side, and on the other nothing but doubt and danger, he must be either a rare friend to you, or an utterly implacable enemy of the prince, to keep faith with you.

And to put the thing briefly, I say that on the conspirator's side there is nothing but fear, jealousy, and the prospect of punishment, which terrifies him; but on the prince's side there is the majesty of the principality, the laws, the protection of friends and of the state, all defending him. Add to these the goodwill of the people, and it is impossible that anyone should be reckless enough to conspire. For where a conspirator ordinarily has cause to fear before the execution of his crime, in this case he must fear afterward as well — having the people for his enemy once the deed is done — and for that reason he can hope for no refuge anywhere.

On this subject one could give endless examples, but I will content myself with one, which happened within the memory of our fathers. Messer Annibale Bentivogli, grandfather of the present Messer Annibale, was prince in Bologna, and the Canneschi, conspiring against him, murdered him; no one of his line remained except Messer Giovanni, then in swaddling clothes. The moment the murder was done, the people rose up and killed all the Canneschi. This came from the goodwill the house of Bentivogli enjoyed among the people in those days — a goodwill so great that, with Annibale dead and no one of the house left in Bologna who could rule the state, the Bolognese, having word that in Florence there was a man of Bentivogli blood who until then had passed for a blacksmith's son, went to Florence to find him and gave him the government of their city; and it was governed by him until Messer Giovanni came of age to rule.

I conclude, then, that a prince should take little account of conspiracies when the people are well disposed toward him; but when the people are his enemy and hold him in hatred, he must fear everything and everyone. And well-ordered states and wise princes have taken every care not to drive the great to desperation, and to keep the people satisfied and content: for this is one of the most important matters a prince has.

Among the well-ordered and well-governed kingdoms of our times is France, and in it one finds countless good institutions on which the liberty and security of the king depend. The first of these is the Parlement and its authority. For the man who ordered that kingdom, knowing the ambition of the powerful and their insolence, judged that they needed a bit in their mouths to hold them; and knowing on the other side the hatred, rooted in fear, that the people at large bear toward the great, and wanting to reassure them, he did not wish this to be the particular charge of the king — so as to spare him the blame he would get from the great for favoring the people, and from the people for favoring the great. So he set up a third judge, to be the one who, without any burden falling on the king, would beat down the great and favor the small. There could be no better or more prudent arrangement, nor a greater cause of the security of the king and the kingdom. From this one can draw another notable rule: that princes should have unpopular duties administered by others, and hand out the favors themselves. And again I conclude that a prince should value the great, but not make himself hated by the people.

Perhaps it will seem to some, looking at the lives and deaths of certain Roman emperors, that they are examples contrary to this opinion of mine — men who lived excellently throughout and showed great virtù of spirit, and nevertheless lost the empire, or were killed by their own men conspiring against them. Wishing to answer this objection, I will take up the qualities of some of the emperors, showing that the causes of their ruin were no different from the ones I have set out; and along the way I will put forward the things worth noting for anyone who reads the affairs of those times.

It will be enough to take all the emperors who succeeded to the empire from Marcus the philosopher down to Maximinus: these were Marcus, his son Commodus, Pertinax, Julianus, Severus, his son Antoninus Caracalla, Macrinus, Heliogabalus, Alexander, and Maximinus.

And first it is to be noted that, where in other principalities one has only to contend with the ambition of the great and the insolence of the people, the Roman emperors had a third difficulty: they had to bear with the cruelty and greed of the soldiers. This was so hard a thing that it caused the ruin of many, since it was difficult to satisfy the soldiers and the people at once. The people loved quiet, and for that reason loved unassuming princes; the soldiers loved a prince of military spirit — insolent, cruel, and rapacious — and wanted him to practice those qualities on the people, so that they could double their pay and vent their own greed and cruelty. The result was that the emperors who did not have, by nature or by art, a reputation great enough to hold both in check always came to ruin; and most of them, especially those who came to the principate as new men, once they recognized the difficulty of these two opposed humors, turned to satisfying the soldiers, counting it a small thing to injure the people. That choice was necessary: since princes cannot avoid being hated by somebody, they are bound first to try not to be hated by any whole class of their subjects; and when they cannot manage that, they must contrive with every effort to escape the hatred of the classes that are most powerful. And so those emperors who, being new men, had need of extraordinary support held to the soldiers rather than to the people — which turned out useful to them or not according to whether the prince knew how to keep his reputation up with the troops.

From these causes it followed that Marcus, Pertinax, and Alexander — all men of modest life, lovers of justice, enemies of cruelty, humane, and kind — all, except Marcus, came to a sad end. Marcus alone lived and died with the highest honor, because he succeeded to the empire by hereditary right and did not have to acknowledge it as a gift of either the soldiers or the people; and then, being furnished with many virtù that made him revered, he held both orders within their bounds as long as he lived, and was never hated or despised.

But Pertinax was made emperor against the will of the soldiers, who, being used to living licentiously under Commodus, could not stand the honest life Pertinax wanted to bring them back to; and having thus made himself hated — and with the hatred came contempt, for he was old — he was ruined at the very start of his administration. And here it must be noted that hatred is earned by good deeds as much as by bad ones; and so, as I said above, a prince who wants to hold his state is often forced not to be good. For when the body you judge you need in order to maintain yourself — the people, the soldiers, or the great — is corrupt, you must follow its humor to satisfy it, and then good deeds are your enemy.

But let us come to Alexander. He was of such goodness that among the praises given him is this: in the fourteen years he held the empire, no one was ever put to death by him without trial. Nevertheless, being held effeminate — a man who let himself be ruled by his mother — he fell into contempt for it, and the army conspired against him and killed him.

Turning now to the opposite qualities of Commodus, Severus, Antoninus Caracalla, and Maximinus: you will find them utterly cruel and utterly rapacious — men who, to satisfy their soldiers, spared the people no kind of injury. And all of them, except Severus, came to a bad end. For in Severus there was so much virtù that, by keeping the soldiers his friends, he could always reign happily even while the people were being crushed by him; his virtù made him so remarkable in the eyes of soldiers and people alike that the people stood, in a manner, dazed and stunned before him, and the soldiers reverent and satisfied. And because his actions were great, and notable in a new prince, I want to show briefly how well he knew how to play the fox and the lion — the two natures I said above a prince must imitate.

Knowing how slack the emperor Julianus was, Severus persuaded the army he commanded in Slavonia that it would be right to march on Rome and avenge the death of Pertinax, whom the praetorian soldiers had killed. Under that pretext, and without showing any appetite for the empire, he moved his army against Rome, and was in Italy before his departure was even known. When he reached Rome, the Senate, out of fear, elected him emperor, and Julianus was killed. After this beginning, two obstacles remained if Severus was to master the whole state: one in Asia, where Niger, commander of the Asian armies, had had himself proclaimed emperor; the other in the west, where Albinus also aspired to the empire. And because he judged it dangerous to declare himself the enemy of both, he decided to attack Niger and deceive Albinus. To Albinus he wrote that, having been elected emperor by the Senate, he wished to share that dignity with him; he sent him the title of Caesar and had the Senate take him on as colleague — all of which Albinus accepted as true. But once Severus had defeated and killed Niger and pacified the East, he returned to Rome and complained in the Senate that Albinus, unmindful of the benefits received from him, had treacherously sought to kill him, and that he was therefore obliged to go and punish his ingratitude. He then went to find him in Gaul, and stripped him of his state and his life. Whoever examines this man's actions in detail, then, will find a most ferocious lion and a most cunning fox; will find him feared and revered by everyone, and not hated by his armies; and will not marvel that he, a new man, was able to hold so great an empire — for his enormous reputation defended him always from the hatred the people might have conceived for his plunderings.

But his son Antoninus was also a man of the most excellent parts, which made him marvelous in the sight of the people and welcome to the soldiers: he was a military man, able to bear every hardship, contemptuous of every delicate food and every other softness, and this made all the armies love him. Nevertheless, his ferocity and cruelty were so great and so unheard of — after countless individual killings, he had put to death a great part of the people of Rome and all the people of Alexandria — that he became hateful to the whole world and began to be feared even by those he kept around him; and he was killed by a centurion in the middle of his own army. Here it is to be noted that deaths like this one, which follow from the deliberate resolve of an unshakable mind, are impossible for princes to avoid, since anyone who does not care about dying can strike the blow; but a prince may fear them less, because they are very rare. He must only keep from doing grave injury to any of those who serve him and whom he keeps close around him in the service of the state. Antoninus had not: he had shamefully killed a brother of that centurion, and threatened the man himself every day, yet kept him in his bodyguard — a reckless course, fit to ruin him, and so it did.

But let us come to Commodus, who could have held the empire with great ease, since as the son of Marcus he had it by inheritance; he had only to walk in his father's footsteps, and he would have satisfied soldiers and people alike. But being of a cruel and bestial spirit, in order to practice his rapacity on the people he set about indulging the armies and making them licentious; and on the other side, by not keeping up his own dignity — going down into the arenas again and again to fight with the gladiators, and doing other things utterly vile and unworthy of the imperial majesty — he became contemptible in the sight of the soldiers. Hated on the one side and despised on the other, he was conspired against and killed.

It remains to tell the qualities of Maximinus. He was a most warlike man; and the armies, disgusted with the softness of Alexander, whom I have discussed above, killed him and elected Maximinus to the empire. He did not keep it long, for two things made him hated and despised: one, that his origin was of the basest — he had once herded sheep in Thrace, a fact known everywhere, which lowered him enormously in every eye; the other, that at the start of his reign he put off going to Rome to take possession of the imperial seat, and meanwhile earned a name for the utmost ferocity, having through his prefects, in Rome and everywhere else in the empire, carried out many cruelties. So the whole world was stirred at once with disdain for the meanness of his blood and with hatred born of fear of his savagery; and first Africa rebelled, then the Senate with the whole people of Rome, and then all Italy conspired against him. His own army joined them: laying siege to Aquileia and finding it hard to take, disgusted with his cruelty, and fearing him less when they saw how many enemies he had, they killed him.

Of Heliogabalus, Macrinus, and Julianus I will not speak: being wholly contemptible, they were snuffed out at once. I will come instead to the close of this discourse. The princes of our own times suffer far less from this difficulty of giving the soldiers extraordinary satisfaction in their governments; for though some consideration must still be shown them, the thing is soon settled, since none of these princes keeps standing armies grown old together in the government and administration of provinces, as the armies of the Roman empire were. If it was then necessary to satisfy the soldiers before the people, it was because the soldiers could do more than the people; now it is necessary for all princes, except the Turk and the Sultan of Egypt, to satisfy the people before the soldiers, because the people can do more than they can.

I make an exception of the Turk, because he always keeps about him twelve thousand foot soldiers and fifteen thousand horse, on whom the security and strength of his kingdom depend; and it is necessary for that lord to keep them his friends, setting every other consideration of the people aside. The kingdom of the Sultan of Egypt is similar: being entirely in the hands of the soldiers, he too must keep them his friends, without regard for the people. And note that this state of the Sultan is unlike all other principalities: it resembles the Christian pontificate, which cannot be called a hereditary principality or a new principality. For it is not the sons of the old prince who inherit and remain lords, but the man raised to that rank by those who have the authority to elect him, the sons remaining noblemen only; and since this order is ancient, the state cannot be called a new principality. None of the difficulties of new ones are found in it: for though the prince is new, the orders of the state are old, and arranged to receive him as if he were their hereditary lord.

But let us return to our subject. I say that whoever considers the discourse written above will see that either hatred or contempt was the cause of the ruin of the emperors named; and he will also see how it happened that, some proceeding one way and some the contrary way, in each group one man ended happily and the rest unhappily. For Pertinax and Alexander, being new princes, it was useless and harmful to want to imitate Marcus, who stood in the principate by hereditary right; and likewise for Caracalla, Commodus, and Maximinus it was fatal to imitate Severus, since they did not have virtù enough to follow in his footsteps. So a new prince in a new principality cannot imitate the actions of Marcus, and yet has no need to follow those of Severus; rather, he should take from Severus the parts necessary to found his state, and from Marcus the parts fitting and glorious for preserving a state already established and firm.
