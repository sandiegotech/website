# The Prince, Chapter XVII

Cruelty and Mercy: Loved or Feared

Coming now to the other qualities named before, I say that every prince ought to want to be held merciful and not cruel; nevertheless, he must take care not to use this mercy badly. Cesare Borgia was held cruel; and yet that cruelty of his mended the Romagna, united it, and brought it back to peace and loyalty. Looked at closely, he will be seen to have been far more merciful than the Florentine people, who, to escape a name for cruelty, let Pistoia be destroyed. A prince, then, ought not to mind the infamy of cruelty when it keeps his subjects united and loyal; for with a very few exemplary punishments he will be more merciful than those who, out of too much mercy, let disorders run on until they end in killings and plunder — for these hurt a whole community, while the executions that come from a prince hurt only a single man.

And of all princes, it is the new prince who cannot escape the name of cruel, since new states are full of dangers. Hence Virgil, through Dido's mouth, excuses the inhumanity of her rule by the fact that it was new, saying:

Res dura, et regni novitas me talia cogunt moliri, et late fines custode tueri.

Hard necessity, and the newness of my kingdom, force me to such measures — to guard my borders far and wide with sentries.

Nevertheless, he should be slow to believe and slow to act, and should not conjure fears for himself; he should proceed in a temperate way, with prudence and humanity, so that too much confidence does not make him incautious and too much distrust does not make him unbearable.

From this a dispute arises: whether it is better to be loved than feared, or the reverse. The answer is that one would want to be both; but since it is hard to combine them, it is much safer to be feared than loved, if one of the two must be given up. For this can be said of men generally: they are ungrateful, fickle, pretenders and dissemblers, quick to run from danger, greedy for gain; and while you are doing them good they are entirely yours — they offer you their blood, their property, their lives, their children, as I said above, so long as the need is far off; but when it comes near, they turn away. And the prince who has rested entirely on their words, and finds himself bare of other preparations, is ruined; for friendships bought at a price, and not with greatness and nobility of spirit, are paid for but not owned, and at the moment of need they cannot be spent. And men have less hesitation about harming one who makes himself loved than one who makes himself feared; for love is held by a chain of obligation which, men being wicked, is broken at every turn of their own advantage; but fear is held by a dread of punishment that never lets you go.

Nevertheless, a prince should make himself feared in such a way that, if he does not win love, he escapes hatred; for being feared and not being hated go together very well. And he will always manage this if he keeps his hands off the property of his citizens and his subjects, and off their women. And when he does have to proceed against someone's life, he should do it where there is proper justification and manifest cause; but above all he must keep off other men's property, for men forget the death of a father more quickly than the loss of a patrimony. Besides, pretexts for taking property are never lacking: a man who begins to live by plunder will always find a reason to seize what belongs to someone else; whereas reasons for taking a life are rarer, and give out sooner. But when a prince is with his armies and has a multitude of soldiers under his command, then it is absolutely necessary not to care about the name of cruel, for without that name no army was ever kept united or fit for any action.

Among Hannibal's admirable actions this one is counted: that though he led an enormous army, a mixture of countless races of men, to fight in foreign lands, no dissension ever arose in it — not among the men, and not against their leader — in bad fortune or in good. This could come from nothing but his inhuman cruelty, which, together with his boundless virtù, made him always revered and terrible in his soldiers' eyes; and without it, his other virtù would not have been enough to produce that effect. Writers who have thought about this too little admire his achievement on the one hand and condemn its chief cause on the other. And that his other virtù would not have sufficed can be seen in Scipio — a man of the rarest kind, not only in his own times but in the whole memory of human affairs — whose armies mutinied against him in Spain. That came from nothing but his excessive leniency, which had given his soldiers more license than fits military discipline. Fabius Maximus threw it in his face in the Senate and called him the corrupter of the Roman soldiery. The Locrians, after one of Scipio's legates had wrecked them, went unavenged by him, and the legate's insolence went uncorrected — all of it born of that easy nature of his; so that when someone in the Senate wanted to excuse him, he said there were many men who knew better how to avoid erring themselves than how to correct the errors of others. In time that nature would have stained Scipio's fame and glory, had he kept command long enough; but living under the government of the Senate, this damaging quality of his not only stayed hidden but brought him glory.

I conclude, then, returning to being feared and loved: since men love at their own pleasure and fear at the pleasure of the prince, a wise prince should build on what is his own, not on what belongs to others; he must only contrive to escape hatred, as was said.
