The Prince, Chapter VIII

Rising by Crime

Chapter VIII8 of 26

But since there are two more ways a private citizen can become a prince, neither of which can be put down entirely to fortune or to virtù, I cannot leave them out — though one of them could be treated at more length where republics are discussed. These are: when a man rises to the principality by some criminal and wicked path, and when a private citizen becomes prince of his native city by the favor of his fellow citizens. Speaking of the first way, I will show it by two examples, one ancient and one recent, without otherwise weighing the merits of this course — because for anyone who needs it, I judge these two are enough to imitate.

Agathocles the Sicilian became King of Syracuse not merely from private life but from the bottom — from a low and abject start. The son of a potter, he lived a criminal life at every age. Yet he joined his crimes to such virtù of mind and body that, after joining the army, he rose through its ranks to be Praetor of Syracuse. Once settled in that rank, he decided to become prince — to hold by violence, and owing nothing to anyone, what had been granted him by consent. He reached an understanding about his plan with Amilcar the Carthaginian, who was campaigning with his armies in Sicily; then one morning he assembled the people and the senate of Syracuse as if he had business of the Republic to discuss, and at a prearranged signal his soldiers killed all the senators and the richest of the people. With them dead, he seized and held the rule of that city without any civil strife. And though the Carthaginians twice defeated him and finally besieged him, he not only defended his city: leaving part of his forces to hold off the siege, he attacked Africa with the rest, lifted the siege of Syracuse in short order, and drove the Carthaginians to such extremity that they were forced to come to terms with him — to content themselves with Africa and leave Sicily to Agathocles.

Whoever weighs this man's actions and virtù, then, will find little or nothing that can be credited to fortune. As I said, he came to the principality not by anyone's favor but up through the ranks of the army, earning every step with a thousand hardships and dangers, and afterward held his place by many bold and risky decisions. And yet it cannot be called virtù to kill your fellow citizens, betray your friends, and be without faith, without pity, without religion. Such methods can win empire, but not glory. If you consider Agathocles' virtù in walking into dangers and walking out of them, and his greatness of spirit in bearing and beating adversity, there is no reason to rank him below the most celebrated captains. All the same, his savage cruelty and inhumanity, and his countless crimes, do not allow him to be honored among the truly excellent men. So what he achieved cannot be credited to fortune — and it cannot be credited to virtù.

In our own times, under Alexander the Sixth, there is Oliverotto da Fermo. Left fatherless years before as a small child, he was raised by a maternal uncle, Giovanni Fogliani, and in his early youth sent to soldier under Pagolo Vitelli, so that a training in that discipline might carry him to some high rank in the profession. After Pagolo died he served under his brother Vitellozzo, and in a very short time — being clever, and vigorous in body and mind — he became the first man of his company. But it seemed servile to him to take orders from others, so he resolved to seize Fermo — with the help of certain citizens of Fermo who preferred their city enslaved to their city free, and with the backing of the Vitelleschi. He wrote to Giovanni Fogliani that after so many years away he wanted to come see him and his city, and to have some look at his own inheritance; and since he had labored for nothing but honor, he wished to arrive honorably, escorted by a hundred horsemen of his friends and servants, so that his fellow citizens would see he had not spent his years for nothing; and he begged Giovanni to arrange an honorable reception from the people of Fermo — which would do honor not only to him but to Giovanni himself, who had raised him.

Giovanni failed in no courtesy owed his nephew. He had him honorably received by the Fermians and lodged him in his own house — where Oliverotto, after a few days spent quietly arranging what his coming crime required, gave a formal banquet and invited Giovanni Fogliani and all the leading men of Fermo. When the food and the usual entertainments of such banquets were done, Oliverotto artfully steered the talk to weighty matters — the greatness of Pope Alexander and his son Cesare, and their undertakings. Giovanni and the others were answering when he rose all at once, saying these were things to discuss in a more private room, and withdrew to a chamber; Giovanni and all the other citizens followed him in. No sooner were they seated than soldiers came out of hiding and killed Giovanni and all the rest. After the slaughter, Oliverotto mounted his horse, rode the town, and besieged the chief magistracy in its palace; so that out of fear the people obeyed him and formed a government, of which he made himself prince. And with everyone dead who, being discontented, could have hurt him, he strengthened himself with new civil and military ordinances — so that within the year he held the principality, he was not only secure inside the city of Fermo but feared by all its neighbors. Rooting him out would have been as hard as rooting out Agathocles, had he not let himself be tricked by Cesare Borgia, when at Sinigalia, as was told above, the duke took the Orsini and the Vitelli and took him with them. There, one year after the parricide he had committed, he was strangled, together with Vitellozzo, who had been his teacher in virtù and in crime.

Some may wonder how it could happen that Agathocles and men like him, after countless betrayals and cruelties, could live long and secure in their own country, defend themselves against foreign enemies, and never face a conspiracy from their own citizens — while many others, using cruelty, could not hold their states even in peacetime, let alone in the uncertain times of war. I believe it comes down to cruelties badly used or well used. Well used we may call those — if it is permissible to speak well of evil — that are done at a single stroke, out of the necessity of securing oneself, and then not persisted in, but turned, as far as possible, to the subjects' benefit. Badly used are those which, though few at the start, grow with time instead of dying out. Those who take the first way can, with God and with men, find some remedy for their position, as Agathocles did; for the others, it is impossible to hold on.

So it should be noted that in seizing a state, the man who takes it should run through all the injuries he will need to commit, and commit them all at once, so as not to have to renew them every day — and then, by not renewing them, reassure men and win them over with benefits. Whoever does otherwise, out of timidity or bad advice, has to keep the knife in his hand forever; and he can never lean on his subjects, since their fresh and continual injuries keep them from ever trusting him. For injuries should be done all together, so that, being tasted less, they offend less; benefits should be handed out little by little, so that they are savored longer.

And above all, a prince should live with his subjects in such a way that no accident, good or bad, forces him to change course; because when hard times bring the necessity, you are too late for the evil, and the good you do buys you nothing — it is judged forced, and earns you no thanks at all.
