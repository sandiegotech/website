The Prince, Chapter XXIII

Avoiding Flatterers

Chapter XXIII23 of 26

I do not want to leave out an important topic, and a mistake princes can hardly defend themselves against unless they are very shrewd, or choose their men very well. I mean the flatterers, and the courts are full of them. For men take such pleasure in their own doings, and deceive themselves about them so thoroughly, that they can barely protect themselves from this plague; and the attempt to protect yourself brings a danger of its own — the danger of becoming despised. There is no way to guard against flattery but to have men understand that telling you the truth does not offend you; yet when everyone may tell you the truth, the reverence goes.

Therefore a prudent prince takes a third way. He chooses wise men from his state, and to them alone he gives free leave to speak the truth to him — and only on the matters he asks about, nothing else. But he should ask them about everything, hear their opinions, and then decide by himself, in his own manner. With these counselors, singly and together, he should carry himself so that every one of them knows the more freely he speaks, the more welcome he will be. Outside of them he should listen to no one, go straight after the thing once decided, and be stubborn in his decisions. Whoever does otherwise is either wrecked by the flatterers, or changes course so often under the pull of contrary opinions that he comes to be despised.

On this point I want to give a modern example. Fra Luca, servant of Maximilian, the present emperor, said of his majesty that he took counsel with no one, and yet never did anything the way he wanted. This came of his doing the opposite of what I have described. For the emperor is a secretive man: he tells no one his plans and takes no advice on them. But as the plans go into effect they begin to show and be discovered, and then the men around him begin to talk him out of them; and he, being pliable, is turned aside. So what he does one day he undoes the next; no one ever understands what he wants or intends to do; and no one can build on his decisions.

A prince, therefore, should always take counsel — but when he wants it, not when others want to give it. In fact he should discourage anyone from volunteering advice on anything unless he asks for it. But he himself should be a generous asker of questions, and then, about the things he asked, a patient hearer of the truth; indeed, when he learns that anyone out of some scruple is keeping the truth from him, he should let his anger show.

Many people suppose that a prince who gives an impression of prudence owes it not to his own nature but to the good advisers around him. They are certainly deceived. For this is a general rule that never fails: a prince who is not wise himself cannot be advised well — unless by chance he hands himself over entirely to a single man of great prudence who governs everything for him. In that case he might indeed be well governed, but it would not last long, because that governor would in short order take the state away from him.

But taking counsel from more than one, a prince who is not wise will never get counsels that agree, and he will not know how to reconcile them himself. Each counselor will think of his own interest; the prince will know neither how to correct them nor how to see through them. And counselors come no other way, because men will always turn out bad for you unless some necessity makes them good. So the conclusion is this: good counsel, wherever it comes from, must be born of the prince's prudence — the prince's prudence is not born of good counsel.
