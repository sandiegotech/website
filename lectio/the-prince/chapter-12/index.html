The Prince, Chapter XII

The Kinds of Troops, and Mercenaries

Chapter XII12 of 26

Having discussed one by one the kinds of principality I proposed at the outset, having considered in some measure why they do well or badly, and having shown the methods by which so many men have tried to win and hold them, it remains for me now to speak generally of the offense and defense that each of them may require.

I said above that a prince must have solid foundations under him, or he necessarily comes to ruin. The principal foundations of all states — new, old, or mixed — are good laws and good arms. And since there cannot be good laws where there are not good arms, and where there are good arms there are bound to be good laws, I will leave the laws aside and speak of arms.

I say, then, that the arms with which a prince defends his state are either his own, or they are mercenary, auxiliary, or mixed. Mercenaries and auxiliaries are useless and dangerous. If a man rests his state on mercenary arms, he will never stand firm or safe; for they are disunited, ambitious, undisciplined, disloyal — swaggering among friends, cowardly before the enemy; no fear of God, no faith with men. Your ruin is put off only as long as the assault is put off: in peace you are stripped by them, in war by the enemy. The reason is that they have no love for you, and no cause to keep them in the field beyond a little pay — which is not enough to make them want to die for you. They are delighted to be your soldiers while you are not making war; when war comes, they desert or run. It should cost me little effort to prove this, since the ruin of Italy has been caused by nothing else than her resting all her hopes, for so many years, on mercenary arms. They did once make headway for certain men, and they looked brave enough against one another; but when the foreigner came, they showed what they were. So it was that Charles, King of France, was allowed to take Italy with a piece of chalk; and the man who said our sins were the cause of it told the truth — only they were not the sins he meant, but the ones I have described. And since they were the sins of princes, it is the princes who have paid the penalty.

I want to show the wretchedness of these arms more fully. Mercenary captains are either excellent soldiers or they are not. If they are, you cannot trust them: they will always be working toward greatness of their own, either by crushing you, their master, or by crushing others against your intent. And if the captain is no good, he ruins you in the ordinary way.

If the answer is that anyone with arms in hand will act this way, mercenary or not, I reply: arms are wielded either by a prince or by a republic. The prince should go in person, and do the captain's job himself. The republic must send its own citizens; when it sends a man who proves worthless, it must replace him, and when it sends a good one, it must bind him with laws so that he does not overstep the mark. Experience shows princes acting alone, and republics armed with their own arms, making enormous progress, and mercenary arms doing nothing but damage. And a republic armed with its own arms is far harder to bring under the rule of one of its citizens than a republic armed with foreign arms. Rome and Sparta stood armed and free for centuries. The Swiss are the best-armed people alive, and the freest.

As for ancient examples of mercenaries, there are the Carthaginians, who at the end of the first war with Rome were nearly crushed by their own mercenary soldiers, though the Carthaginians had their own citizens for captains. After the death of Epaminondas, the Thebans made Philip of Macedon captain of their forces; and after he had won, he took away their liberty.

When Duke Filippo died, the Milanese hired Francesco Sforza against the Venetians. He beat the enemy at Caravaggio — and then joined with the enemy to crush the Milanese, his employers. Sforza his father, in the pay of Queen Johanna of Naples, abandoned her defenseless in a stroke, so that to save her kingdom she had to throw herself into the arms of the King of Aragon. And if the Venetians and the Florentines did once enlarge their dominions with such arms, and their captains did not make themselves princes but defended them, I answer that in this the Florentines were favored by chance. Of the able captains they had cause to fear, some did not win, some met opposition, and some turned their ambition elsewhere. The one who did not win was Giovanni Acuto; and since he did not win, his loyalty was never put to the proof — but everyone will grant that had he won, the Florentines stood at his discretion. Sforza always had the Bracceschi against him, and each kept watch on the other; Francesco turned his ambition on Lombardy, Braccio against the Church and the kingdom of Naples. But come to what happened only yesterday. The Florentines made Pagolo Vitelli their captain, a very shrewd man who from private life had risen to great renown. If he had taken Pisa, no one can deny that the Florentines would have been bound to him: had he gone over to their enemies, they had no defense, and so long as they kept him, they had to obey him. As for the Venetians, look at their record, and you will see that they acted safely and gloriously so long as they made war with their own men — this was before they turned to enterprises on land — when gentlemen and armed commoners together fought with the greatest virtù. The moment they began to fight on land, they abandoned this virtù and took up the customs of Italy. In the early days of their expansion on land, having little territory there and a great reputation, they had little to fear from their captains; but as they expanded — under Carmignuola — they got a taste of the mistake. They had seen how supremely able he was; under his command they had beaten the Duke of Milan. But they also saw how cold he had grown toward the war, and they judged that with him they could win no longer, because he had no wish to win; yet they could not dismiss him, or they would lose again what they had gained. So, to secure themselves against him, they were forced to murder him. Since then they have had for captains Bartolomeo da Bergamo, Roberto da San Severino, the count of Pitigliano, and the like — men under whom the fear was of loss, not the hope of gain; as happened later at Vailà, where in one day's battle they lost what eight hundred years of hard labor had won them. From these arms come conquests that are slow, late, and feeble; and losses that are sudden and astonishing.

And since these examples have brought me to Italy, ruled now for many years by mercenary arms, I want to trace them from further back, so that once their origin and course are in view they can be better corrected. You must understand, then, how in recent times, as soon as the Empire began to be pushed out of Italy and the Pope gained more standing in temporal affairs, Italy broke up into more states. Many of the great cities took up arms against their nobles, who, favored by the emperor, had held them down, while the Church favored the cities to build its temporal power; and in many other cities, citizens rose to be princes. So Italy fell largely into the hands of the Church and of certain republics; and the Church being priests and the republics being citizens unaccustomed to arms, both began to hire foreigners.

The first to give this soldiery a reputation was Alberigo da Conio, of the Romagna. From his school came, among others, Braccio and Sforza, who in their day were the arbiters of Italy. After them came all the other captains who down to our own times have commanded the arms of Italy — and the sum of all their virtù is that Italy has been overrun by Charles, plundered by Louis, violated by Ferdinand, and insulted by the Swiss. Their method was this: first, to raise their own standing, they destroyed the standing of the infantry. They did it because, having no state of their own and living off their trade, a few foot soldiers gave them no reputation and many were more than they could feed; so they kept to cavalry, where a manageable number could be both fed and honored. Matters came to the point where in an army of twenty thousand soldiers you would not find two thousand foot. Beyond this, they had used every device to spare themselves and their soldiers hardship and fear: in the skirmishes they did not kill one another, but took prisoners and freed them without ransom; they made no assault on towns by night, and the men in the towns made none on the camps; they built neither stockade nor ditch around their quarters; they did not campaign in winter. All of this their military code permitted, and all of it, as I have said, they invented to escape hardship and danger — until they had brought Italy to slavery and contempt.
