The Prince, Chapter VI

By Your Own Arms and Virtù

Chapter VI6 of 26

No one should be surprised if, in speaking of principalities entirely new, I bring in the greatest examples of prince and of state. Men almost always walk in paths beaten by others, and conduct their affairs by imitation; but the paths of others cannot be followed exactly, and you cannot reach the virtù of the man you imitate. So a prudent man should always enter by paths beaten by great men, and imitate those who have been most excellent — so that if his own virtù does not reach theirs, at least it carries some scent of it. He should do as the clever archers do when the target looks too far off: knowing how far their bow will carry, they aim well above the mark — not to drive the arrow to that height, but to hit, by so high an aim, the mark they intend.

I say, then, that in principalities entirely new, where there is a new prince, holding on is more or less difficult according to the virtù of the man who acquires them. And since becoming prince from private citizen presupposes either virtù or fortune, one or the other of these, it seems, must ease many of the difficulties. Even so, the man who has leaned less on fortune has held on longer. It also helps when the prince, having no other state, is forced to come and live in this one in person.

But to come to those who became princes by their own virtù and not by fortune: the most excellent, I say, are Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, Theseus, and their like. And though Moses should hardly be discussed, being a mere executor of things ordered by God, still he deserves admiration, if only for the grace that made him worthy to speak with God. But consider Cyrus and the others who acquired or founded kingdoms: you will find them all admirable; and if you weigh their particular actions and arrangements, they will not look much different from those of Moses, who had so great a teacher. Examine their actions and their lives, and you will find they owed nothing to fortune but opportunity — the raw material they shaped into whatever form seemed best to them. Without that opportunity the virtù of their minds would have gone out; without that virtù, the opportunity would have come in vain.

It was necessary, then, that Moses find the people of Israel in Egypt, enslaved and ground down by the Egyptians, so that to escape their servitude they would be ready to follow him. It was necessary that Romulus not stay in Alba, and that he be exposed at birth, if he was to become King of Rome and founder of that fatherland. It was necessary that Cyrus find the Persians sick of the empire of the Medes, and the Medes gone soft from long peace. Theseus could not have shown his virtù had he not found the Athenians scattered. These opportunities made these men fortunate, then, and their surpassing virtù made them recognize the opportunity; and by it their countries were ennobled and made supremely happy.

Men like these, who become princes by paths of virtù, acquire their principality with difficulty but hold it with ease. The difficulties in acquiring come partly from the new orders and methods they are forced to introduce to found their state and secure it. And it should be kept in mind that nothing is harder to take in hand, more doubtful of success, or more dangerous to manage than making yourself the leader in introducing a new order of things. The innovator has enemies in everyone who did well under the old order, and lukewarm defenders in everyone who might do well under the new. The lukewarmness comes partly from fear of the opponents, who have the laws on their side, and partly from the incredulity of men — who never truly believe in anything new until solid experience has shown it to them. So whenever the enemies of the new order get their chance to attack, they attack like partisans, and the others defend half-heartedly; and between them the prince is put at risk.

If we want to examine this part thoroughly, we must ask whether these innovators stand on their own or depend on others — that is, whether they must beg to carry their work through, or whether they can force it. When they must beg, they always end badly and accomplish nothing. But when they depend on their own strength and can use force, they are rarely in danger. This is why all armed prophets have conquered, and the unarmed have been destroyed. For besides everything said already, the nature of peoples is fickle: it is easy to persuade them of a thing, and hard to hold them to the persuasion. So matters must be ordered in such a way that when they no longer believe, they can be made to believe by force.

Moses, Cyrus, Theseus, and Romulus could not have made their orders obeyed for long had they been unarmed — as happened in our own time to Fra Girolamo Savonarola, who was ruined along with his new order the moment the crowd stopped believing him; and he had no way to hold firm the ones who had believed, or to make the unbelievers believe. Men like these, then, face great difficulty in carrying the work through: all their dangers lie on the way up, and virtù must get them past. But once they are past — once they are held in reverence, and have wiped out the men who envied them their standing — they remain powerful, secure, honored, and happy.

To such lofty examples I want to add a lesser one; but it keeps some proportion with them, and I mean it to stand for every other case of its kind: Hiero the Syracusan. From private citizen, he became Prince of Syracuse — and he owed nothing to fortune but opportunity. The Syracusans, being oppressed, elected him their captain; from there he earned the right to be made their prince. And he was of such virtù even in private life that the man who wrote of him said he lacked nothing of a king but the kingdom. He abolished the old militia and raised a new one; he dropped the old friendships and made new ones; and once the friendships and the soldiers were his own, he could build any building on that foundation — so that what cost him great effort to acquire cost him little to keep.
