The Prince, Chapter XV

What Princes Are Praised or Blamed For

Chapter XV15 of 26

It remains now to see what the conduct and bearing of a prince ought to be toward his subjects and his friends. And because I know that many have written on this, I fear that in writing on it too I will be thought presumptuous — the more so because in treating the matter I depart from the paths laid down by others. But since my intention is to write something useful to whoever understands it, it has seemed to me more fitting to go straight after the effectual truth of the thing than after the imagination of it. Many have imagined republics and principalities that no one has ever seen or known to exist in the world as it is; for the way men live is so far from the way men ought to live that whoever lets go of what is done for what ought to be done learns his ruin sooner than his preservation. A man who insists on being good in everything must come to ruin among so many who are not good.

So it is necessary for a prince who wants to keep his state to learn how not to be good, and to use that knowledge, or not use it, as necessity requires. Setting aside, then, the things imagined about a prince, and speaking of the things that are true, I say that all men, whenever they are spoken of — and princes most of all, being set so high — are marked with some one of those qualities that draw blame or praise. One is held generous, another a miser (I use the Tuscan word, since in our tongue an avaricious man is still one who craves to get by plunder, while we call a miser the man who holds back too hard from using what is his own); one is a giver, another grasping; one cruel, another merciful; one a breaker of faith, another a keeper of it; one effeminate and cowardly, another fierce and full of spirit; one humane, another haughty; one lecherous, another chaste; one straightforward, another sly; one hard, another easygoing; one grave, another frivolous; one religious, another unbelieving; and so on. And I know that everyone will admit it would be a most praiseworthy thing for a prince to possess, of all the qualities named above, the ones held to be good. But since they cannot all be had, nor all wholly practiced — the conditions of human life do not allow it — a prince must be prudent enough to know how to escape the infamy of those vices that would take his state from him, and to guard, if he can, against the vices that would not; and if he cannot, he may indulge in them with less concern. And further, he must not care about incurring infamy for vices without which the state can hardly be saved; because, when everything is weighed, he will find some things that look like virtue and would be his ruin if he followed them, and others that look like vice and bring him, if he follows them, his security and his well-being.
