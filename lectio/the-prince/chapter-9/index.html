The Prince, Chapter IX

The Civil Principality

Chapter IX9 of 26

But coming to the other case — when a leading private citizen becomes prince of his native city not by crime or any intolerable violence but by the favor of his fellow citizens — this can be called a civil principality. Reaching it does not take pure virtù or pure fortune; it takes, rather, a kind of lucky cunning. I say a man rises to this principality by the favor of the people or by the favor of the nobles. For these two opposed humors are found in every city, and they arise from this: the people do not want to be ruled or oppressed by the nobles, and the nobles want to rule and oppress the people. From these two appetites, one of three effects follows in a city: principality, liberty, or license.

A principality is set up either by the people or by the nobles, depending on which of the two gets its chance. When the nobles see they cannot hold out against the people, they begin building up the reputation of one of their own and make him prince, so that under his shadow they can satisfy their appetite. The people likewise, seeing they cannot hold out against the nobles, build up the reputation of one man and make him prince, to be defended by his authority. The man who comes to the principality with the nobles' help holds it with more difficulty than the one who comes to it with the people's, because he finds himself prince surrounded by many who think themselves his equals — and for that reason he can neither command them nor manage them his own way. But the man who reaches the principality by popular favor finds himself alone at the top, with no one around him, or almost no one, who is not ready to obey.

Beyond that, you cannot satisfy the nobles honestly and without wronging others, but you can satisfy the people that way — because the people's aim is more decent than the nobles': the nobles want to oppress, and the people only want not to be oppressed. Add to this that against a hostile people a prince can never make himself safe, because there are too many of them; against the nobles he can, because they are few. The worst a prince can expect from a hostile people is to be abandoned by them. From hostile nobles he must fear not only abandonment but attack; for the nobles see further and move earlier, they always act in time to save themselves, and they court favor with whichever side they expect to win. And besides, the prince has to live with the same people always, but he can do perfectly well without the same nobles, since he can make them and unmake them every day, and give or take away their standing as he pleases.

To make this part clearer, I say the nobles should be considered in two main ways: either they run their course so as to bind themselves entirely to your fortune, or they do not. Those who bind themselves, and are not greedy, should be honored and loved. Those who do not bind themselves come in two kinds. If they hold back out of faint-heartedness and a natural lack of courage, then make use of them, especially the ones with good counsel in them; in prosperity they do you honor, and in adversity you have nothing to fear from them. But when they keep clear of you deliberately, for reasons of ambition, it is a sign they are thinking more of themselves than of you; and against those a prince must guard himself, and fear them as if they were declared enemies, because in adversity they will always help bring him down.

Therefore, one who becomes prince by the favor of the people must keep the people his friend — which is easy, since all they ask is not to be oppressed. But one who becomes prince against the people, by the favor of the nobles, must before anything else try to win the people over — also easy, once he takes them under his protection. And because men, when they receive good from someone they expected only harm from, bind themselves all the more tightly to their benefactor, the people become better disposed toward him at once than if he had been raised by their own favor. A prince can win them in many ways, but the ways vary so much with circumstance that no fixed rule can be given, and I will leave them out. I will conclude only this: a prince must have the people friendly — otherwise, in adversity, he has no remedy.

Nabis, Prince of the Spartans, withstood the siege of all Greece and of a triumphant Roman army, and against them defended his country and his own rule; and when the danger came, all he needed was to secure himself against a few — which would not have been enough if the people had been his enemy. And let no one counter this opinion of mine with the worn proverb that 'he who builds on the people builds on mud.' That is true when a private citizen lays his foundation there and lets himself believe the people will rescue him when his enemies or the magistrates come for him; in that case a man will often find himself deceived, as the Gracchi were in Rome and Messer Giorgio Scali was in Florence. But if it is a prince who builds on the people — one who can command, a man of spirit, unshaken in adversity, who does not neglect his other preparations, and who keeps the whole city's nerve up by his own courage and his own orders — he will never find himself betrayed by them, and he will find he has built on solid ground.

These principalities usually come into danger at the moment they are climbing from the civil order to the absolute one. For these princes rule either in their own person or through magistrates. In the second case their position is weaker and more dangerous, because it rests entirely on the will of the citizens who hold the magistracies — and they, especially in hard times, can take the state from him with great ease, either by moving against him or simply by not obeying. And in the middle of the danger there is no time for the prince to seize absolute authority: citizens and subjects who are used to taking their orders from the magistrates are not about to obey his in that crisis, and in uncertain times he will always be short of men he can trust. A prince like this cannot build on what he sees in quiet times, when the citizens need the state — then everyone runs to him, everyone promises, and with death nowhere in sight, everyone is ready to die for him. But in hard times, when the state needs its citizens, few are to be found. And this experiment is all the more dangerous because it can only be run once. So a wise prince must think of a way to make his citizens need the state and need him — always, in every kind of weather. Then they will always be faithful.
