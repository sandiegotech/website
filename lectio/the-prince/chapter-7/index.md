# The Prince, Chapter VII

By Others' Arms and Fortune

Those who rise from private citizen to prince by fortune alone rise with little effort but hold on with a great deal. They have no trouble on the way up, because they fly there; all the trouble begins once they land. Such are the men who are handed a state, either for money or as a favor from whoever grants it — as happened to many in Greece, in the cities of Ionia and the Hellespont, where Darius made princes so they would hold the cities for his security and his glory; and as happened to those emperors who rose from private citizens to the throne by bribing the soldiers. Men like this stand simply on the goodwill and the fortune of whoever raised them — two utterly fickle and unstable things — and they neither know how to hold the position nor can they. They do not know how, because unless a man has great intelligence and virtù, it is not reasonable that he should know how to command after a lifetime as a private citizen; and they cannot, because they have no forces of their own that they can count on to be loyal and faithful.

Besides, states that spring up overnight, like everything else in nature that is born and grows too fast, cannot have put down roots and connections deep enough to survive the first storm — unless, as I said, the men who become princes overnight have so much virtù that they know at once how to hold what fortune has dropped in their laps, and can lay afterward the foundations that others lay before they become princes.

For these two ways of becoming prince — by virtù or by fortune — I want to offer two examples from within living memory: Francesco Sforza and Cesare Borgia. Francesco, by the right means and by great virtù of his own, rose from private citizen to Duke of Milan, and what he had acquired with a thousand pains he kept with little effort. Cesare Borgia, on the other hand — called Duke Valentino by the people — acquired his state by his father's fortune and lost it the same way, even though he did everything and used every means a prudent and capable man should use to sink his roots into the states that the arms and fortune of others had given him.

Because, as was said above, whoever does not lay his foundations in advance might, with great virtù, lay them later — though it costs the architect trouble and puts the building at risk. If you consider all the steps the duke took, you will see that he laid great foundations for his future power; and I do not think it beside the point to go through them, because I would not know what better lessons to give a new prince than the example of his actions. And if his arrangements came to nothing in the end, it was not his fault: it was an extraordinary and extreme malice of fortune.

Alexander the Sixth, wanting to make his son the duke great, faced plenty of difficulties, present and future. First, he saw no way to make him master of any state that was not a state of the Church; and he knew that if he moved to take from the Church, the Duke of Milan and the Venetians would not stand for it — Faenza and Rimini were already under Venetian protection. Beyond that, he saw the arms of Italy, especially the ones he might have used, in the hands of men with every reason to fear a greater Pope — the Orsini, the Colonnesi, and their followers. It was necessary, then, to upset this arrangement and throw their states into disorder, so as to make himself securely master of part of them. That proved easy, because he found the Venetians, for reasons of their own, already working to bring the French back into Italy; he not only did not oppose it, he smoothed the way by annulling King Louis's old marriage. So the king came into Italy with the help of the Venetians and the consent of Alexander; and no sooner was he in Milan than the Pope had troops from him for the campaign in the Romagna, which was surrendered to him on the king's reputation. Having taken the Romagna, then, and beaten the Colonnesi, the duke wanted to hold what he had and push further, but two things stood in his way: his own forces, which did not look loyal to him, and the will of France. He feared that the Orsini arms he had been using would fail under him — that they would not only block further gains but take from him what he had already gained — and that the king would do the same. He got his proof about the Orsini when, after taking Faenza, he attacked Bologna and watched them go cold on the assault; and he learned the king's mind when, after seizing the Duchy of Urbino, he moved on Tuscany and the king made him back off. At that point the duke resolved to depend no longer on the arms and fortune of others.

His first move was to weaken the Orsini and Colonna factions in Rome. He won over all their gentlemen adherents by making them his own gentlemen — paying them well and honoring them, according to their rank, with commands and offices — so that within a few months their old party loyalties died out and the whole allegiance turned to the duke. After that, having already scattered the men of the Colonna house, he waited for his chance to crush the Orsini, and when it came he used it well. The Orsini, realizing too late that the greatness of the duke and the Church meant their ruin, called a meeting at Magione, in Perugian territory. Out of that came the revolt of Urbino, the uprisings in the Romagna, and endless dangers for the duke — all of which he overcame with the help of the French. Then, his reputation restored, and trusting neither France nor any other outside force enough to put them to the test, he turned to deception. And he knew so well how to disguise his mind that the Orsini themselves — through Signor Pagolo, whom the duke took care to reassure with every courtesy, giving him money, clothes, and horses — were reconciled to him; and their simplicity delivered them into his hands at Sinigalia. So with the leaders wiped out and their partisans turned into his friends, the duke had laid quite good foundations for his power: he held all the Romagna along with the Duchy of Urbino, and above all he had won the Romagna itself, whose people had begun to taste prosperity under him. And because this part deserves notice, and imitation by others, I do not want to pass over it.

When the duke took the Romagna, he found it had been run by weak lords, quicker to strip their subjects than to govern them, who had given the province more cause for division than for unity, so that it was rife with theft, feuds, and every sort of outrage. He judged that to make it peaceful and obedient to the ruler's arm, it needed good government; so he set over it Messer Ramiro d'Orco, a cruel and efficient man, and gave him the fullest power. In a short time Ramiro made the province peaceful and united, and won an enormous reputation doing it. Then the duke judged that such excessive authority was no longer wanted, fearing it would breed hatred; and he set up a civil court in the middle of the province, under an excellent president, where every city had its advocate. And because he knew the past severities had bred some hatred against himself, he wanted to purge the minds of the people and win them over completely — to show that whatever cruelty had been done had come not from him but from the harsh nature of his minister. So, having found his moment, one morning at Cesena he had Ramiro laid out in two pieces on the piazza, with a block of wood and a bloody knife beside him. The ferocity of that spectacle left the people at once satisfied and stunned.

But let us return to where we left off. I say that the duke now found himself powerful enough, and partly secured against present dangers — armed his own way, and having largely destroyed the neighboring forces that could hurt him — and what remained, if he meant to go on conquering, was France; because he knew the king, who had seen his mistake too late, would not tolerate it. So he began to look for new alliances and to stall France over the expedition she was making toward the kingdom of Naples, against the Spaniards besieging Gaeta. His intention was to secure himself against them, and he would soon have managed it if Alexander had lived.

That was how he handled things present. As for things to come, he had to fear, first, that a new head of the Church would be no friend to him and would try to take back what Alexander had given him. He thought to secure himself against that in four ways. First, by wiping out the bloodlines of the lords he had despoiled, to strip the Pope of that pretext. Second, by winning over all the gentlemen of Rome, as I have said, so as to hold the Pope in check through them. Third, by making the College of Cardinals his, as far as he could. Fourth, by acquiring so much power before the Pope died that he could withstand a first attack on his own. Of these four things, by the time Alexander died, he had accomplished three. Of the despoiled lords he had killed as many as he could reach, and very few escaped; the Roman gentlemen were won; and in the College he had the largest party. As for new acquisitions, he had designs on Tuscany; he already possessed Perugia and Piombino, and had taken Pisa under his protection. And since he no longer had to defer to France — the French had already been stripped of the kingdom of Naples by the Spaniards, so that each side now had to buy his friendship — he would have pounced on Pisa. After that, Lucca and Siena would have folded at once, partly out of spite toward the Florentines and partly out of fear; and the Florentines would have had no remedy. Had he brought this off — and he was bringing it off the very year Alexander died — he would have gathered such force and such reputation that he would have stood on his own, no longer depending on the fortune and forces of another, but on his own power and virtù.

But Alexander died five years after the duke first drew his sword. He left him with only the state of Romagna consolidated, everything else in the air, caught between two very powerful enemy armies, and deathly ill. Yet there was such ferocity and such virtù in the duke — he understood so well how men are won or lost, and the foundations he had laid in so short a time were so sound — that if he had not had those armies on his back, or if he had been healthy, he would have ridden out every difficulty. And that his foundations were good, you could see: the Romagna waited for him more than a month; in Rome, half dead, he was safe; and though the Baglioni, the Vitelli, and the Orsini came to Rome, they could raise nothing against him. If he could not make Pope the man he wanted, he could at least keep out the man he did not want. Had he been healthy when Alexander died, everything would have gone easily for him. He told me himself, on the day Julius the Second was elected, that he had thought through everything that could happen when his father died, and had found a remedy for all of it — except that he never imagined that when the day came, he too would be at the point of death.

Gathering up all the duke's actions, then, I would not know how to fault him. On the contrary, it seems right to hold him up, as I have done, as a model for all those who rise to rule by fortune and the arms of others. With his great spirit and his high aims, he could not have conducted himself otherwise; only the shortness of Alexander's life and his own sickness stood against his designs. So whoever judges it necessary, in a new principality, to secure himself against enemies; to win friends; to conquer by force or by fraud; to make himself loved and feared by the people, followed and respected by the soldiers; to wipe out those who can or must hurt him; to replace the old order with new; to be severe and gracious, magnanimous and open-handed; to destroy a disloyal army and build a new one; to keep the friendship of kings and princes on such terms that they help him gladly and cross him carefully — such a man can find no fresher example than this man's actions.

The one thing he can be blamed for is the election of Julius as Pope: there he chose badly. Because, as I said, if he could not make a Pope of his own, he could keep the papacy from anyone he chose — and he should never have consented to the elevation of any cardinal he had injured, or any who, once Pope, would have reason to fear him. For men do injury out of fear or out of hatred. Those he had injured included San Pietro ad Vincula, Colonna, San Giorgio, and Ascanio; all the rest, on becoming Pope, would have had cause to fear him — except Rouen and the Spaniards: the Spaniards through kinship and obligation, Rouen through power, having the kingdom of France behind him. So the duke's first choice should have been a Spanish Pope; failing that, he should have consented to Rouen, not San Pietro ad Vincula. Whoever believes that with great men new favors erase old injuries is deceiving himself. The duke erred in this election, and it was the cause of his final ruin.
