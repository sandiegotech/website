# The Prince, Chapter X

Measuring a State's Strength

In examining the qualities of these principalities, there is one more question to consider: whether a prince has a state strong enough to stand on its own at need, or whether he will always need someone else's protection. To make the point clearer, I say that in my judgment those princes can stand on their own who have enough men or enough money to put together a proper army and meet in the field whoever comes to attack them; and those always need others who cannot take the field against the enemy, but are forced to fall back behind their walls and defend them. The first case has been discussed, and later I will say whatever more it needs. In the second case there is nothing to say except to urge such princes to fortify and provision their own town, and to take no account of the countryside. Whoever has fortified his town well, and has handled his subjects in the way described above and to be described again, will never be attacked except with great hesitation; for men are always wary of ventures where the difficulty is plain to see, and no one sees an easy target in a prince whose town is strong and whose people do not hate him.

The cities of Germany are completely free, they have little surrounding territory, and they obey the emperor when they choose to; they fear neither him nor any other power near them, because they are fortified in such a way that everyone reckons taking them would be slow, grinding work. They all have proper moats and walls; they have artillery enough; they keep enough in the public stores to eat, drink, and burn for a year. Beyond that — to keep the common people fed without cost to the public — they always hold in common a year's worth of work to give out in the trades that are the nerve and life of the city, the industries the people live by. They keep military exercises in good repute, and have many ordinances besides to maintain them.

So a prince who has a strong city and does not make himself hated cannot be attacked; and even if someone did attack him, the attacker would leave in disgrace — because the affairs of this world are so changeable that it is next to impossible to sit idle with an army for a whole year while you besiege him. And to whoever answers that if the people have their property outside the walls and watch it burn, they will lose patience, and the long siege and their own self-interest will make them forget the prince — I reply that a powerful and spirited prince will always get past those difficulties: now giving his subjects hope that the evil will not last long, now stoking fear of the enemy's cruelty, now deftly securing himself against the ones who seem too bold.

Besides, the enemy will naturally burn and wreck the countryside on arrival, while the defenders' blood is still hot and willing; so the prince has all the less reason to worry, because after a few days, when tempers have cooled, the damage is already done, the losses already taken, and there is no undoing them. And then the people close ranks with their prince all the more, feeling he is now obliged to them, their houses burned and their lands ruined in his defense. For it is the nature of men to be bound by the benefits they give as much as by the benefits they receive. So all things considered, it will not be hard for a prudent prince to keep his citizens' minds firm through a siege, from first day to last — so long as they lack neither food nor the means to fight.
