The Prince, Chapter XXI

How a Prince Wins Esteem

Chapter XXI21 of 26

Nothing makes a prince so esteemed as great enterprises and rare displays of his own quality. Our own age has Ferdinand of Aragon, the present King of Spain. He can almost be called a new prince, because from a weak king he has become, by fame and glory, the first king in Christendom; and if you examine his deeds you will find them all great, and some of them extraordinary. At the start of his reign he attacked Granada, and that campaign was the foundation of his state. He began it at his leisure and with no fear of interference, for it kept the minds of the barons of Castile fixed on the war and away from any thought of change at home; and meanwhile, without their noticing, he was gaining standing and authority over them. He fed his armies on the money of the Church and of the people, and that long war let him lay a foundation for his own soldiery, which has since brought him honor. Beyond this, always using religion as the pretext for greater designs, he turned to a pious cruelty, hunting the Moors down and clearing them out of his kingdom — no example could be more wretched, or more rare. Under the same cloak he attacked Africa; he made his descent on Italy; he has lately attacked France. So he has never stopped weaving great designs, and they have kept his subjects in suspense and wonder, absorbed in waiting to see how things would come out. And each of his moves has been born out of the last, so that between one and the next, men have never had a quiet stretch in which to work against him.

It also helps a prince a great deal to give rare examples of himself in domestic affairs, like those told of Messer Bernabo da Milano: whenever anyone in civic life did something extraordinary, good or bad, he seized the occasion to reward or punish him in a way that people would talk about for a long time. And above all, a prince must strive in everything he does to earn the name of a great man of exceptional intelligence.

A prince is also esteemed when he is a true friend and a true enemy — when, without hedging, he comes out openly for one side against another. That course is always more useful than standing neutral. For if two of your powerful neighbors come to blows, either the winner will be a threat to you afterward or he will not. In either case it will always be better for you to declare yourself and wage war in earnest. In the first case, if you do not declare yourself, you will be the prize of whoever wins — to the pleasure and satisfaction of whoever loses — and you will have no claim and nothing else to defend you or take you in. The winner does not want doubtful friends who would not stand by him in hard times; the loser will not shelter you, because you would not take up your sword and run his fortune with him.

Antiochus came into Greece, brought in by the Ætolians to drive out the Romans. He sent envoys to the Achaeans, who were friends of the Romans, urging them to stay neutral; the Romans, meanwhile, were urging them to take up arms for their side. The question came before the council of the Achaeans, and when the legate of Antiochus pressed them toward neutrality, the Roman legate answered: “As for the course these men praise as best and most useful for your state — that you keep out of our war — nothing is further from your interest: keep out, and without thanks and without dignity you will be the winner's prize.” So it will always go: the one who is not your friend will ask you for neutrality, and the one who is your friend will ask you to declare yourself with arms. Irresolute princes, trying to escape the danger of the moment, take the neutral road most of the time, and most of the time they are ruined. But when a prince declares himself boldly for one side, then if his ally wins — however powerful the winner, however much you lie at his mercy — he is in your debt; a bond of affection has been formed; and men are never so shameless as to crush you with so flagrant a show of ingratitude. Besides, victories are never so clean that the winner can throw off every restraint, least of all justice. And if your ally loses, he takes you in; while he can, he helps you; and you become companions of a fortune that can rise again.

In the second case, when the two who fight are such that you have nothing to fear from whichever of them wins, it is all the more prudent to take a side: you help destroy the one with the aid of the other, who ought to have rescued him if he had been wise; and since with your help he cannot fail to win, he wins and remains at your discretion. And here it should be noted that a prince ought never to make common cause with someone more powerful than himself in order to attack others, unless necessity forces him, as was said above: for if your stronger partner wins, you are his prisoner, and princes should do all they can to avoid lying at anyone's discretion. The Venetians allied themselves with France against the Duke of Milan — a ruinous alliance they could have avoided. But when it cannot be avoided, as it could not be for the Florentines when the Pope and Spain marched their armies into Lombardy, then the prince should commit himself, for the reasons given.

And let no state ever believe it can choose a safe course; let it expect instead to choose among doubtful ones. For this is in the order of things: you never escape one trouble without walking into another. Prudence consists in knowing how to weigh the troubles against each other, and to take the least bad as good.

A prince should also show himself a lover of virtù, giving a place to able men and honoring those who are outstanding in any art. Beyond that, he should encourage his citizens to follow their occupations in peace — in trade, in farming, in every other human pursuit — so that no one is afraid to improve his property for fear it will be taken from him, and no one afraid to open a business for fear of taxes. Instead he should hold out rewards for whoever wants to do these things, and for anyone who thinks of any way at all of enlarging his city or his state.

Beyond this, at the fitting seasons of the year, he should keep the people occupied with festivals and spectacles. And since every city is divided into guilds or wards, he should take account of those bodies, meet with them now and then, and show himself in his own person an example of courtesy and generosity — all the while holding the majesty of his rank firm, for that must never be allowed to slip in anything.
