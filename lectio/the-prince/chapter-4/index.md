# The Prince, Chapter IV

Why Darius's Kingdom Did Not Rebel

Considering how hard it is to hold a newly acquired state, one might wonder at this: Alexander the Great made himself master of Asia in a few years and died when he had barely settled it — the very moment, it would seem, for the whole empire to rebel. Yet Alexander's successors held it, and the only difficulty they had in keeping it was the one their own ambition bred among themselves.

I answer that all the principalities on record have been governed in one of two ways: either by one prince, with everyone else his servant, some of them helping him govern the kingdom as ministers, by his grace and license; or by a prince and by barons, who hold their rank not by their master's favor but by ancient blood. Such barons have states and subjects of their own — subjects who recognize them as lords and bear them a natural affection. In states governed by a prince and his servants, the prince stands higher in men's eyes, for in the whole province no one is acknowledged superior but him; if people obey anyone else, they obey a minister and an official, and feel no particular love for him.

The living examples of these two kinds of government are the Turk and the King of France. The whole monarchy of the Turk is governed by one lord; the rest are his servants. He divides his kingdom into sanjaks, sends out administrators, and moves and replaces them as he pleases. The King of France, by contrast, sits in the midst of an ancient multitude of lords, each acknowledged and loved by subjects of his own; they have their privileges, and the king cannot touch them without danger to himself. Consider these two states, then, and you will find the difficulty is in conquering the Turk's — but once conquered, it is held with great ease. The reasons his kingdom is hard to seize are these: an invader cannot be called in by the princes of the realm, and cannot hope for a revolt in the ruler's own circle to ease his enterprise. That follows from what was said above: the ruler's ministers, being all slaves and beholden to him, are corrupted only with great effort, and even corrupted they are worth little, since they cannot pull the people after them, for the reasons given. So whoever attacks the Turk must expect to find him united, and had better count on his own strength rather than on the other side's disorders. But once the Turk is beaten — broken in the field past raising new armies — there is nothing left to fear but the prince's bloodline. Extinguish that, and no one remains to be feared, for none of the others has any credit with the people; and just as the conqueror could expect nothing from them before his victory, he need fear nothing from them after it.

The opposite holds in kingdoms governed like France. There you can enter with ease by winning over some baron of the realm, for malcontents are always to be found, and men who want things changed. Such men, for the reasons given, can open the road into the state and hand you the victory. But keeping it afterward brings infinite trouble, from those who helped you and from those you crushed alike. Nor is it enough to have extinguished the prince's line, because the lords remain, ready to make themselves heads of new movements; and being able neither to satisfy them nor to snuff them out, you lose the state the first time the occasion offers.

Now consider what kind of government Darius had, and you will find it like the kingdom of the Turk. So Alexander had first to smash him in the field and sweep him from open country; and after that victory, with Darius dead, the state stayed securely in Alexander's hands, for the reasons argued above. Had his successors stayed united, they could have enjoyed it at their leisure — no tumults arose in that kingdom but the ones they raised themselves.

But states ordered like France cannot be possessed so quietly. Hence the repeated rebellions of Spain, of Gaul, and of Greece against the Romans — all because of the many principalities those provinces contained. As long as the memory of them lasted, Rome's hold was never sure; when that memory was extinguished by the power and long duration of the empire, the hold became secure. And later, when the Romans fought among themselves, each commander could carry with him the part of those provinces where his own authority had taken root; the bloodlines of the old lords being extinguished, the provinces acknowledged no one but Romans.

Keep all this in mind, and no one will wonder at the ease with which Alexander kept his hold on the empire of Asia, or at the trouble others — Pyrrhus among many — have had in keeping what they won. It comes not from the little or great virtù of the conqueror, but from the difference in what was conquered.
