Lectio Edition · 2026

The Prince

Niccolò Machiavelli

  • 1513
  • Western tradition
  • Politics
  • Lectio Edition 2026

A short handbook on power, written in 1513 by a Florentine diplomat who had lost his post. Machiavelli sets aside how rulers ought to behave and describes what they must do to keep a state.

Why this editionGoal and purpose

One word carries the book, and the old translation hides it.

The standard free translation is W. K. Marriott's of 1908. It is clear and faithful. But Machiavelli runs his whole argument on one word, virtù, and Marriott turns it into a different English word almost every time: ability, valour, energy, talent.

A reader of Marriott cannot see that the book is one argument about one quality set against fortune. A reader of the Italian cannot miss it.

The Lectio Edition renders the twenty-six chapters in current English and leaves virtù in Italian wherever Machiavelli uses it in this sense. Marriott stays in the app beside it.

The openingFrom the Lectio Edition

How it begins.

Chapter I · The Kinds of Principalities

All states, all dominions that have held and hold power over men, have been and are either republics or principalities.

Principalities are either hereditary, where one bloodline has ruled for a long time, or they are new.

The new ones are either new outright, as Milan was to Francesco Sforza, or they are like limbs grafted onto the hereditary state of the prince who acquires them, as the kingdom of Naples is to the King of Spain.

Dominions acquired this way are accustomed either to living under a prince or to being free; and they are acquired either with the arms of others or with one's own, either by fortune or by virtù.

Chapter II · Hereditary Principalities

I will leave republics aside, since I have discussed them at length elsewhere. I will turn only to principalities, taking up the threads laid out above, and ask how these principalities can be governed and kept.

I say at once that hereditary states, accustomed to the bloodline of their prince, are far less difficult to hold than new ones. It is enough not to break the customs of one's ancestors, and to play for time when events turn; then a prince of ordinary industry will always keep his state, unless some extraordinary and overwhelming force strips him of it — and even stripped of it, he wins it back the moment the usurper stumbles.

The first 227 words of the Lectio Edition, as they stand in the bundle the app reads.

The choicesPolicies and divergences

What this edition decided.

The review graded twenty of twenty-six chapters as needing no change. The rules it set are few.

  • Virtù stays untranslated from Chapter I to Chapter XXVI. It means skill, force and nerve together, with no moral content. No single English word holds it, and five different English words hide that it is one idea. The draft had kept it in some chapters and translated it in others.
  • The adjective virtuoso is "able". The ordinary moral plural, virtues set against vices, stays English.
  • Spegnere, to put out a flame, is "snuff out" each time Machiavelli uses it of a family or a republic.
  • No explanation is written into Machiavelli's sentence. The draft defined virtù inside Chapter I and glossed the Parlement of Paris inside Chapter XIX. Both glosses were removed.
  • Roman Gaul is Gaul, not France. Names carry their accents: Forlì, Vailà.

Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 62 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 7. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

o per fortuna o per virtù

Chapter I

Older
"or else by fortune or by ability" (Marriott)
Lectio
either by fortune or by virtù

The first appearance of the pair the whole book is built on. The Italian word is kept here and at every later use.

o vezzeggiare o spegnere

Chapter III

Older
"either to be well treated or crushed" (Marriott)
Lectio
men should be either treated well or snuffed out

The verb is the one for putting out a flame. Keeping the image, and keeping it the same each time, lets the reader hear the maxim return.

Non si può ancora chiamare virtù

Chapter VIII

Older
"Yet it cannot be called talent to slay fellow-citizens" (Marriott)
Lectio
it cannot be called virtù to kill your fellow citizens

The one sentence where Machiavelli refuses his own word to a man who seems to have earned it. "Talent" loses the refusal.

la verità effettuale della cosa

Chapter XV

Older
"the real truth of the matter" (Marriott)
Lectio
the effectual truth of the thing

Machiavelli coined the phrase: truth measured by what works. "Real truth" makes it ordinary emphasis. The edition keeps it strange.

imparare a potere essere non buono

Chapter XV

Older
"to know how to do wrong" (Marriott)
Lectio
to learn how not to be good

Marriott moralizes. Machiavelli says the prince must be able to be not good, and use that or not as need requires.

la fortuna è donna

Chapter XXV

Older
"it is necessary to beat and ill-use her" (Marriott)
Lectio
fortune is a woman, and if you mean to keep her down you must beat her and shove her

The most notorious sentence in the book, at full force. Softening it would misreport the writer. The reader should meet it as written and judge him.

il parlamento e la sua autorità

Chapter XIX

Older
"the parliament and its authority" (Marriott)
Lectio
the Parlement and its authority

He means the Parlement of Paris, a court of law. "Parliament" suggests a legislature, which it was not. The French name marks the difference.

Still open

What the first review left for the editor to decide or restore.

  • The editor's own read of all twenty-six chapters.
  • The text now leaves virtù unexplained and points to the margin. The margin entry for it has not been written.
  • Chapter VI: "He abolished the old militia" may have the same fault that was fixed in Chapter VIII. It is a judgment call and was left.
  • The lines of Virgil in Chapter XVII and of Petrarch in Chapter XXVI are verse. The build runs them together as prose. The fix is in the pipeline, not the text.
  • Several of the edition's notes quote the text loosely. They need one pass to match it exactly.
