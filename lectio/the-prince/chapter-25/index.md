# The Prince, Chapter XXV

Fortune, and How to Resist Her

I am well aware that many men have held, and hold, the opinion that the affairs of the world are so governed by fortune and by God that human prudence cannot correct them — that there is no remedy at all; and on this account they might judge that there is no point sweating over anything, and that we should let ourselves be governed by chance. This opinion has gained more credit in our times because of the great upheavals in affairs that have been seen, and are seen every day, beyond anything anyone could have guessed. Thinking it over, I have myself leaned some way toward their opinion at times. Nevertheless — so that our free will is not extinguished — I judge it may be true that fortune is arbiter of half of our actions, but that she leaves the other half, or close to it, for us to govern.

I compare her to one of those ruinous rivers that, when they grow angry, flood the plains, tear down trees and buildings, lift the earth from this bank and lay it on that one. Everyone flees before the water; everyone gives way to its fury, powerless to stand against it anywhere. And yet, though rivers are made this way, it does not follow that men in quiet weather cannot prepare — with embankments and dikes — so that when the waters rise again they either run off down a channel, or their rush is neither so wild nor so ruinous. It is the same with fortune. She shows her power where no ordered virtù stands ready to resist her, and she turns her floods where she knows no dikes and no embankments have been raised to hold her.

And if you consider Italy — the seat of these upheavals, and the country that set them moving — you will see an open plain without dikes and without a single embankment. Had she been protected by proper virtù, as Germany, Spain, and France are, either this flood would never have made the great changes it has made, or it would not have come at all. And let this be enough said about resisting fortune in general.

But narrowing myself to particulars, I say that one sees a prince prosper today and fall tomorrow with no visible change in his nature or in any quality he has. This, I believe, comes first from the causes already discussed at length: the prince who leans entirely on fortune falls the moment she turns. I believe, further, that the man prospers who matches his way of proceeding to the quality of the times, and likewise that the man fails whose proceedings are at odds with the times. For in the things that carry men toward the end each one aims at — glory and riches — you see them proceed differently: one with caution, another with a rush; one by force, the other by craft; one by patience, the other by its opposite; and each can arrive by his own road. You also see two cautious men, one reaching his goal and the other not; and again two men prospering equally by opposite methods, one cautious, the other impetuous. All of this comes from nothing but the quality of the times, which either agrees with a man's way of proceeding or does not. From this follows what I said: that two men working differently produce the same effect, and of two working alike, one arrives and the other does not.

On this also hangs the swing of prosperity. If a man governs himself with caution and patience, and times and affairs revolve in a way that makes his method the right one, he prospers; but if times and affairs change, he is ruined, because he does not change his way of proceeding. And no man is found so prudent that he knows how to fit himself to this — both because he cannot swerve from what his own nature inclines him to, and because, having always prospered by walking one road, he cannot be persuaded to leave it. So the cautious man, when the moment comes to strike out, does not know how to do it, and down he goes; whereas if he could change his nature with the times and with affairs, his fortune would not change.

Pope Julius the Second proceeded impetuously in everything he did, and he found the times and circumstances so agreeable to his way of proceeding that things always came out well for him. Consider his first campaign, against Bologna, while Messer Giovanni Bentivogli still lived. The Venetians did not like it; neither did the King of Spain; with France he still had the enterprise under negotiation — and nonetheless, with his usual ferocity and drive, he led the expedition in person. The move left Spain and the Venetians standing frozen, the latter out of fear, the former out of appetite to recover the whole Kingdom of Naples; and at the same time it dragged the King of France in behind him, for the king, seeing him already in motion and wanting the Pope as a friend against the Venetians, judged he could not refuse him troops without an open insult. So with one impetuous stroke Julius accomplished what no other pontiff, with all human prudence, would ever have accomplished. Had he waited in Rome until his plans were settled and everything arranged, as any other pontiff would have done, he would never have succeeded: the King of France would have found a thousand excuses, and the others would have raised a thousand fears.

I will pass over his other deeds, which were all of a piece, and all of them turned out well; and the shortness of his life never let him taste the contrary. For if times had come that required him to proceed with caution, his ruin would have followed: he would never have swerved from the ways his nature inclined him to.

I conclude, then, that since fortune changes and men stand stubborn in their ways, they prosper so long as the two run together, and fail the moment they part. For my own part I judge this: it is better to be impetuous than cautious, because fortune is a woman, and if you mean to keep her down you must beat her and shove her. And plainly she lets herself be won by such men sooner than by those who proceed coldly. And so, like a woman, she is always the friend of the young — because they are less cautious, fiercer, and command her with more audacity.
