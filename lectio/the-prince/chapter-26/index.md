# The Prince, Chapter XXVI

Exhortation to Free Italy

Having considered everything discussed above, I have been asking myself whether in Italy, right now, the times are ripe to honor a new prince — whether the material lies here that would give a prudent and able man the occasion to shape it into a form that would bring honor to him and good to the whole body of her people. And it seems to me that so many things now run together in a new prince's favor that I do not know what time was ever more fit for one.

If, as I said, the people of Israel had to be slaves in Egypt for the virtù of Moses to show itself; if the Persians had to be crushed by the Medes for the greatness of Cyrus's spirit to be known, and the Athenians scattered for the excellence of Theseus to appear — then now, for the virtù of one Italian spirit to be known, Italy had to be brought down to her present extremity: more enslaved than the Hebrews, more servile than the Persians, more scattered than the Athenians; without a head, without order; beaten, stripped, torn, overrun; made to endure ruin of every kind.

And though before now a glimmer showed in one man, enough to let us think God had ordained him for her redemption, still we saw how afterward, at the very height of his course, fortune cast him off. So she lies there as if lifeless, waiting for whoever it may be that can heal her wounds — put an end to the sacking of Lombardy, to the extortion of the kingdom and of Tuscany — and clean out the sores that have festered now for so long. See how she prays God to send someone to redeem her from these cruelties and this barbarian insolence. See how ready she is, how willing, to march behind a flag — if only someone would raise one.

Nor is there anyone now in view in whom she can place more hope than in your illustrious house, which — with its fortune and its virtù, favored by God and by the Church, of which it is now the head — can make itself the leader of this redemption. That will not be very hard if you hold before your eyes the deeds and the lives of the men named above. They were rare and marvelous men; but they were men, and none of them had an opportunity as good as this one: their enterprise was not more just than this, nor easier, nor was God more a friend to them than he is to you.

Here justice is great: for war is just for those to whom it is necessary, and arms are holy where there is no hope left except in arms. Here the willingness is very great; and where the willingness is great, the difficulty cannot be great, provided your house takes as its mark the methods of the men I have held up. Beyond this, extraordinary things without precedent have been seen here, God leading the way: the sea has opened; a cloud has shown the road; the rock has poured out water; manna has rained down. Everything has run together toward your greatness. The rest you must do yourselves. God does not want to do everything, lest he take from us our free will and the share of the glory that falls to us.

And it is no wonder if none of the Italians named before could do what we may hope from your illustrious house, or if in so many revolutions and so many campaigns of war in Italy it always looks as if military virtù were spent. The truth is that her old institutions were bad, and no one has appeared who knew how to find new ones. Nothing brings a newly risen man so much honor as the new laws and new institutions he devises: when they are well founded and have greatness in them, they make him revered and admired. And in Italy the material is not lacking — it will take any form you can give it.

Here there is great virtù in the limbs, if only it were not missing in the heads. Look at the duels and the small combats: how superior the Italians are in strength, in dexterity, in quickness of wit. But when it comes to armies they make no showing. And all of it traces back to the weakness of the heads: the men who know are not obeyed, every man thinks he knows, and no one until now has known how to lift himself, by virtù or by fortune, so far above the rest that the others would yield to him. That is why, over so long a time, through so many wars in the past twenty years, whenever an army has been all Italian it has always failed the test. The first witness is Il Taro; then Alessandria, Capua, Genoa, Vailà, Bologna, Mestri.

If your illustrious house, then, means to follow those excellent men who redeemed their countries, one thing is necessary before all others, the true foundation of every such enterprise: to arm yourself with your own troops, for there can be no more faithful, truer, or better soldiers. And good as each man of them is singly, together they will be better still when they see themselves commanded by their own prince, honored by him, and kept at his charge. These arms must be prepared, so that with Italian virtù we can defend ourselves against the foreigner.

The Swiss and the Spanish infantry are counted terrifying, it is true; yet each has a defect, so that a third order of troops could not only stand against them but be confident of beating them. For the Spaniards cannot withstand cavalry, and the Swiss have reason to fear infantry that meets them in close combat as stubbornly as they fight. So we have seen, and will see again by experience, that the Spaniards cannot hold against French cavalry, and the Swiss are broken by Spanish infantry. Of this last there is no complete proof yet, but a taste of it was given at the battle of Ravenna, when the Spanish infantry met the German battalions, which keep the same order as the Swiss: the Spaniards, with the agility of their bodies and the help of their bucklers, got in under the Germans' pikes and stood there safe, cutting at them while the Germans had no remedy; and had the cavalry not charged them, the Spaniards would have finished them all. Knowing the defect of both these infantries, then, one can order a new kind, able to stand against horse and unafraid of foot — and this will be done by the kind of arms chosen and a change of formations. These are among the things which, ordered fresh, give reputation and greatness to a new prince.

This opportunity, then, must not be let slip, so that Italy, after so long, may at last see her redeemer. I cannot express with what love he would be received in all the provinces that have suffered these foreign floods; with what thirst for vengeance, what stubborn faith, what devotion, what tears. What doors would be shut against him? What people would refuse him obedience? What envy would set itself against him? What Italian would withhold his homage? To every one of us this barbarian rule stinks. Let your illustrious house take up this cause, then, with the spirit and the hope in which just enterprises are taken up; so that under its banner this country may be made noble again, and under its auspices Petrarch's words may come true:

Virtù contro al Furore prenderà l'arme, e fia il combatter corto: ché l'antico valore negli italici cuor non è ancor morto.

Virtue against fury will take up arms; and the fight will be short: for the ancient valor in Italian hearts is not yet dead.
