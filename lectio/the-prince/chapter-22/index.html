The Prince, Chapter XXII

A Prince's Ministers

Chapter XXII22 of 26

The choice of ministers is no small matter for a prince: they are good or not according to the prince's own prudence. The first guess one makes about a ruler's brains comes from seeing the men he keeps around him. When they are capable and faithful, you can always count him wise, because he knew how to recognize their capacity and how to keep them faithful. When they are otherwise, you can always judge him badly, for the first error he made was in this choice.

No one who knew Messer Antonio da Venafro as the minister of Pandolfo Petrucci, prince of Siena, could help judging Pandolfo a most able man for having Venafro as his minister. For there are three kinds of brains: one understands things on its own; the second discerns what others understand; the third understands neither on its own nor through others. The first is most excellent, the second excellent, the third useless. So it necessarily followed that if Pandolfo was not in the first rank, he was in the second: for whenever a man has the judgment to recognize the good and the bad in what another says and does, then even if he has no invention of his own, he can tell his minister's good work from his bad, praise the one and correct the other; and the minister, who cannot hope to fool him, keeps himself good.

But as for how a prince may know his minister, there is a test that never fails. When you see a minister thinking more about himself than about you, and hunting his own profit inside everything he does, a man made that way will never be a good minister, and you will never be able to trust him. For the man who holds another's state in his hands must never think of himself but always of his prince, and must never put before him any business that is not the prince's own.

And on the other side, to keep the minister good, the prince should think of him in turn: honor him, enrich him, bind him with obligations, share the honors and the burdens with him — so that the minister sees he cannot stand without the prince; so that abundant honors leave him wanting no more honors, abundant riches leave him wanting no more riches, and his full load of responsibilities makes him dread any change of regime. When ministers, and princes in their dealings with ministers, are made this way, each can trust the other; and when they are otherwise, it ends badly for one or the other, every time.
