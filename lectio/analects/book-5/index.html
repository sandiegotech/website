The Analects, Book V

Weighing Men

Book V5 of 20

The Master said of Kung-ye Ch'ang that he was fit to be given a wife: he had been in bonds, but the guilt was never his. And he gave him his own daughter in marriage.

Of Nan Yung the Master said that in a well-governed country he would never be out of office, and in an ill-governed one he would keep clear of punishment and disgrace. He gave him his elder brother's daughter in marriage.

The Master said of Tsze-chien, "There is a gentleman! If Lu held no gentlemen, where did this man get it?"

Tsze-kung asked, "And I, Ts'ze — what do you say of me?" The Master said, "You are a vessel." "What kind of vessel?" "A jeweled vessel for the altar."

Someone said, "Yung is human-hearted, but he has no ready tongue." The Master said, "What is a ready tongue for? Meet men with a quick mouth and they will hate you for it, more often than not. Whether Yung is human-hearted I do not know — but why should he need a ready tongue?"

The Master wanted Ch'i-tiao K'ai to take office. He replied, "I cannot yet trust myself so far." The Master was pleased.

The Master said, "The Way makes no headway. I will board a raft and drift out to sea — and the one who follows me will be Yu, I expect." Tsze-lu, hearing it, was glad. The Master said, "Yu loves daring more than I do. But he does not stop to weigh the thing."

Mang Wu asked whether Tsze-lu was human-hearted. The Master said, "I do not know." He asked again, and the Master said, "In a state of a thousand chariots, Yu could be given the levies to manage. Whether he is human-hearted, I do not know." "And what of Ch'iu?" The Master said, "In a town of a thousand households, or a house of a hundred chariots, Ch'iu could be made steward. Whether he is human-hearted, I do not know." "And what of Ch'ih?" The Master said, "With his sash bound, standing in court, Ch'ih could be set to talk with the guests and visitors. Whether he is human-hearted, I do not know."

The Master said to Tsze-kung, "Which of you is the better, you or Hui?" Tsze-kung replied, "How could I dare look as high as Hui? Hui hears one thing and knows ten. I hear one thing and know two." The Master said, "No, you are not his equal. You and I — neither of us is his equal."

Tsai Yu lay asleep in the daytime. The Master said, "Rotten wood cannot be carved. A wall of dung and mud takes no trowel. Why should I even scold Yu?" The Master said, "There was a time when I heard a man's words and trusted his deeds to match. Now I hear the words and watch the deeds. Yu changed that in me."

The Master said, "I have never seen a man who is truly unbending." Someone answered, "Shan Ch'ang." The Master said, "Ch'ang? Ch'ang is full of desires. How could he be unbending?"

Tsze-kung said, "What I do not want done to me, I want to do to no one." The Master said, "Ts'ze, that is beyond you."

Tsze-kung said, "The Master's culture and conduct — these we are given to hear. What the Master says of our nature and of the Way of Heaven, no one gets to hear."

When Tsze-lu had heard a thing and not yet carried it out, his one fear was hearing another.

Tsze-kung asked, "Why was Kung-wan given the name Wan?" The Master said, "He was quick, and he loved learning, and he was not ashamed to ask those beneath him. That is why they called him Wan."

The Master said of Tsze-ch'an that he had four marks of the gentleman: in his own conduct he was humble; in serving his superiors, respectful; in providing for the people, kind; in commanding them, just.

The Master said, "Yen P'ing knew how to keep a friend. However long he knew a man, his respect was what it had been on the first day."

The Master said, "Tsang Wan housed a great tortoise in a hall of its own, with hills carved on the pillar-tops and duckweed painted on the posts above the beams. What kind of wisdom is that?"

Tsze-chang asked, "The minister Tsze-wan took office three times and showed no pleasure, and was removed three times and showed no resentment — and each time he gave the incoming minister a full account of his government. What do you say of him?" The Master said, "He was loyal." "Was he human-hearted?" "I do not know. Where is the human-heartedness in it?" Tsze-chang went on, "When the officer Ch'ui killed the prince of Ch'i, Ch'an Wan, who owned forty horses, abandoned them and left the country. In the next state, he said, 'They are like our officer Ch'ui here,' and left. He came to a third, said the same, and left again. What do you say of him?" The Master said, "He was clean." "Was he human-hearted?" "I do not know. Where is the human-heartedness in it?"

Chi Wan thought three times before he acted. When the Master heard of it, he said, "Twice is enough."

The Master said, "While the country kept the Way, Ning Wu was wise. When the country lost it, he played the fool. His wisdom can be matched. His folly cannot."

When the Master was in Ch'an, he said, "Home — let me go home! The young men of my school are bold and hasty. They weave brilliant cloth, and they do not know how to cut it."

The Master said, "Po-i and Shu-ch'i did not keep old wrongs in mind, and so the grudges against them were few."

The Master said, "Who calls Wei-shang Kao upright? A man begged vinegar of him, and he begged it from his neighbor and gave it as his own."

The Master said, "Clever words, a winning face, respect laid on thick — Tso Ch'iu-ming was ashamed of them, and so am I. To hide a grudge against a man and treat him as a friend — Tso Ch'iu-ming was ashamed of that, and so am I."

Yen Yuan and Chi Lu were at his side. The Master said, "Come, why not each of you tell me his wish?" Tsze-lu said, "I would share my chariots and horses and light furs with my friends, and if they wore them to ruin, think nothing of it." Yen Yuan said, "I would never boast of what is good in me, nor parade what I have done." Tsze-lu said, "And now, sir, we would hear your wish." The Master said, "To give the old rest. To keep faith with friends. To cherish the young."

The Master said, "It is hopeless. I have yet to see a man who could look at his own faults and press the case against himself."

The Master said, "In a hamlet of ten houses there is surely someone as loyal and true as I am. But no one who loves learning as I do."
