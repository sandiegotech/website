The Analects, Book XX

Yao Said

Book XX20 of 20

Yao said, "Ah, Shun. The count of Heaven's succession rests now on your shoulders. Hold faithfully to the center. If the four seas fall into hardship and want, Heaven's gift ends forever." Shun laid the same charge on Yu. T'ang said, "I, the little child Li, dare to offer this dark bull, and dare to declare it plainly to you, most august and sovereign Lord: the guilty I dare not pardon; your servants I do not hide from you. The judging of them is in your own heart, Lord. If there is guilt in me, let it not touch the ten thousand regions. If there is guilt in the ten thousand regions, let it rest on me."

Chau gave great gifts, and good men grew rich. "I have kinsmen of my own," said the king, "but better than kinsmen are human-hearted men. If the hundred families do wrong, let it lie on me — the One Man." He set the weights and measures right, went over the laws again, restored the officers who had been dismissed — and government flowed through the whole kingdom. He raised up states that had been wiped out, mended lines of succession that had been cut, called back to office men who had gone into hiding — and the hearts of everyone under heaven turned to him. What he gave most weight to: the people's food, mourning, and sacrifice. He was generous, and won the crowd. He kept faith, and the people leaned on him. He was diligent, and the work was done. He was just, and they were glad.

Tsze-chang asked Confucius, "What must a man do, to be fit to govern?" The Master said, "Let him honor the five fine things and cast out the four ugly things. Then he is fit to govern." Tsze-chang said, "What are the five fine things?" The Master said, "The gentleman in authority is generous without expense. He works the people, and they do not resent it. He desires, and is not greedy. He is at his ease, and is not proud. He is imposing, and never fierce."

Tsze-chang said, "What is 'generous without expense'?" The Master said, "Benefit the people by way of the things that already benefit them — generous, is it not, and at no expense? Choose the labors that are rightly theirs and set them to work — who resents that? Desire human-heartedness, and obtain human-heartedness — where is the greed in that? Many or few, great or small, the gentleman never dares to brush a man aside — at ease, is he not, and never proud? He sets his robe and cap straight and lifts a level gaze, and men look at him with awe — imposing, is he not, and never fierce?" Tsze-chang said, "And the four ugly things?" The Master said, "To execute people without ever teaching them — that is called cruelty. To demand the finished count without fair warning — that is called tyranny. To let an order lie slack and then insist on it to the day — that is called laying a trap."

"And when a thing must be given to men in any case, to count it out grudgingly — that is called playing the petty clerk." The Master said, "A man who does not know Heaven's decree has no way to be a gentleman. A man who does not know ritual has no way to stand. A man who does not know words has no way to know men."
