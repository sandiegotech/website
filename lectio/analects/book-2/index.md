# The Analects, Book II

Governing

The Master said, "Govern by virtue, and you are like the north star: it keeps its place, and all the stars turn about it."

The Master said, "The Book of Poetry holds three hundred pieces, and one phrase covers them all: no crooked thoughts."

The Master said, "Lead the people with laws and level them with punishments, and they will dodge the punishment and feel no shame. Lead them with virtue and level them with ritual, and they will feel shame — and set themselves right."

The Master said, "At fifteen, I set my heart on learning. At thirty, I stood firm. At forty, I had no doubts. At fifty, I knew Heaven's will. At sixty, my ear was tuned to it. At seventy, I could follow what my heart desired without crossing the line."

Mang I asked about filial devotion. The Master said, "Never disobey." Later, as Fan Ch'ih was driving him, the Master said, "Mang-sun asked me about filial devotion, and I told him: never disobey." Fan Ch'ih said, "What did you mean by it?" The Master said, "While they live, serve your parents by ritual. When they die, bury them by ritual, and sacrifice to them by ritual."

Mang Wu asked about filial devotion. The Master said, "Give your father and mother nothing to worry about except your health."

Tsze-yu asked about filial devotion. The Master said, "Nowadays, filial devotion means keeping one's parents fed. But dogs and horses are kept fed too. Without reverence, where is the difference?"

Tsze-hsia asked about filial devotion. The Master said, "The face is the hard part. When there is work, the young shoulder it; when there is wine and food, the elders are served first — is that all it is to be a devoted son?"

The Master said, "I could talk with Hui all day and he would not once differ with me, as if he were slow. But when he had gone, I watched what he did on his own, and it unfolded what I had taught him. Hui is not slow."

The Master said, "See what a man does. Look into why he does it. Find where he comes to rest. How can a man stay hidden? How can a man stay hidden?"

The Master said, "Keep the old warm and know the new, and you are fit to teach."

The Master said, "A gentleman is not a vessel."

Tsze-kung asked about the gentleman. The Master said, "He acts first. Then his words follow his acts."

The Master said, "The gentleman is broad, not partisan. The small man is partisan, not broad."

The Master said, "Learning without thought is labor lost. Thought without learning is dangerous."

The Master said, "To give your strength to strange doctrines — that is harm indeed."

The Master said, "Yu, shall I teach you what it is to know? When you know a thing, hold that you know it. When you do not, admit that you do not. That is knowing."

Tsze-chang was studying with his eye on a salary. The Master said, "Hear much, set aside what is doubtful, and speak carefully about the rest, and you will seldom be blamed. See much, set aside what is dangerous, and act carefully on the rest, and you will seldom be sorry. Words that draw little blame and acts that leave little regret — the salary lies in that."

Duke Ai asked, "What must I do to make the people submit?" Confucius replied, "Raise the straight and set them above the crooked, and the people will submit. Raise the crooked and set them above the straight, and the people will not submit."

Chi K'ang asked how to make the people reverent and loyal, and eager for virtue. The Master said, "Preside over them with gravity, and they will be reverent. Be a devoted son and a kind master, and they will be loyal. Raise up the good and teach those who fall short, and they will be spurred on."

Someone said to Confucius, "Sir, why do you take no part in government?" The Master said, "The Shu-ching says of filial devotion: 'Be devoted to your parents and a friend to your brothers, and it will carry into government.' That too is governing. Why must a man hold office to govern?"

The Master said, "A man whose word cannot be trusted — I do not know what he is good for. A big cart without a yoke-bar, a small cart without a collar-pin: how do you make them go?"

Tsze-chang asked whether what comes ten generations from now can be known. Confucius said, "The Yin took its rites from the Hsia; what it dropped and what it added can be known. The Chau took its rites from the Yin; what it dropped and what it added can be known. And whoever follows the Chau — though a hundred generations pass, it can be known."

The Master said, "To sacrifice to a spirit that is not your own is flattery. To see what is right and not do it is cowardice."
