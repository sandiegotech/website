The Analects, Book IX

The Passing Stream

Book IX9 of 20

The Master seldom spoke of profit, or of fate, or of human-heartedness.

A man of the village of Ta-hsiang said, "How great the philosopher K'ung is! His learning is vast — and he has made no name in any one thing." The Master heard of it and said to his disciples, "What shall I take up, then? Charioteering? Archery? I will take up charioteering."

The Master said, "Ritual calls for a cap of linen; nowadays it is silk. That is thrifty, and I follow the crowd. Ritual calls for bowing below the hall; nowadays men bow only at the top of the steps. That is arrogant — and though it sets me against the crowd, I still bow below."

There were four things the Master was wholly free of: assumption, insistence, stubbornness, and self.

In K'wang, the Master feared for his life. He said, "King Wan is dead — but does his culture not live on, here, in me? If Heaven had meant this culture to perish, a latecomer like me would never have been given my share in it. And so long as Heaven does not let it perish, what can the men of K'wang do to me?"

A high officer asked Tsze-kung, "Your Master — surely he is a sage? He can do so many things!" Tsze-kung said, "Heaven has certainly given him without stint; he is close to a sage. And besides, he can do many things." The Master heard of it and said, "Does the officer know me? When I was young, I was poor — that is why I can do so many things, and small things they are. Does a gentleman need many skills? He does not." Lao said, "The Master used to say, 'I was never tried in office — so I learned the crafts.'"

The Master said, "Do I possess knowledge? I do not. But let the plainest man ask me a question in earnest, and empty as it may seem, I knock it open at both ends until I have given him everything."

The Master said, "The phoenix does not come; the river sends forth no chart. It is over with me."

When the Master saw a man in mourning dress, or in the full cap and robes of ceremony, or a blind man, he would rise at their approach, though they were younger than he; and if he had to pass them, he quickened his step.

Yen Yuan sighed and said, "I look up at it, and it is higher. I bore into it, and it is harder. I see it before me, and suddenly it is behind. The Master leads a man on step by step, so skillfully — he has broadened me with learning and gathered me in with ritual. I could not stop now if I wished to. And when I have spent every ability I have, something still stands there, sheer above me. I long to follow it — and there is no way up."

The Master was gravely ill, and Tsze-lu set the disciples to act as ministers of a great household. In a remission the Master said, "How long Yu has practiced this fraud! Pretending I have ministers when I have none — whom do I deceive? Do I deceive Heaven? And besides — rather than die in the hands of ministers, would I not sooner die in yours, my friends? A grand burial I may miss; but will I die by the side of the road?"

Tsze-kung said, "Say there is a beautiful gem here. Should I shut it in a case and keep it, or find a good price and sell it?" The Master said, "Sell it! Sell it! But I would wait for the offer."

The Master wanted to go and live among the nine wild tribes of the east. Someone said, "They are crude. How could you?" The Master said, "If a gentleman lived among them, what crudeness would be left?"

The Master said, "Only after I came back from Wei to Lu was the music set right, and the Royal songs and the Praise songs each found their place."

The Master said, "Abroad, to serve the ministers and nobles; at home, to serve father and elder brothers; in the rites of the dead, to spare no effort; and never to be overcome by wine — which of these can I claim?"

Standing by a stream, the Master said, "It all passes like this, does it not? Day and night, it never stops."

The Master said, "I have yet to meet the man who loves virtue the way he loves beauty."

The Master said, "It is like raising a mound: one basket of earth short of done, and if I stop, I am the one who stopped. It is like leveling ground: one basketful tipped in, and if I move forward, I am the one moving."

The Master said, "The one who never flagged while I set a thing before him — that was Hui."

The Master said of Yen Yuan, "What a loss! I watched him advance. I never saw him stop."

The Master said, "There are sprouts that never flower. There are flowers that never fruit."

The Master said, "Stand in awe of the young. How do you know their future will not equal our present? Only when a man is forty or fifty and nothing has been heard of him — then there is nothing left in him to fear."

The Master said, "Words of stern correction — who can refuse to assent to them? But mending your ways is what counts. Words of gentle guidance — who can fail to be pleased by them? But unfolding what they point to is what counts. Pleased, but not unfolding; assenting, but not mending — with such a man there is nothing I can do."

The Master said, "Put loyalty and good faith first. Have no friends who are not your equals. And when you are wrong, do not be afraid to change."

The Master said, "You can carry off the commander of a whole army. You cannot carry off the will of one common man."

The Master said, "To stand in a worn-out, hemp-quilted gown beside men dressed in fox and badger, and feel no shame — that is Yu, isn't it? 'Envying none, coveting nothing — how could he do anything but good?'" Tsze-lu took to chanting the lines constantly, until the Master said, "That much, by itself — how could it be enough to be good?"

The Master said, "Only when the year turns cold do you learn that the pine and the cypress are the last to fade."

The Master said, "The wise are never bewildered; the human-hearted, never anxious; the brave, never afraid."

The Master said, "Some men you can study with, but not walk the Way with. Some you can walk the Way with, but not stand firm with. Some you can stand firm with, but not weigh things with."

"The flowers of the aspen-plum flutter and turn. How could I not be thinking of you? But your house is far away." The Master said, "There was no real longing in it. To one who truly longs, what is far?"
