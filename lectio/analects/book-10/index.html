The Analects, Book X

At Home and at Court

Book X10 of 20

In his own village, Confucius was unassuming and plain, as if words came hard to him. In the ancestral temple of the prince, and at court, he spoke fully on every point, and carefully.

At court, waiting, he talked with the officers of the lower grade freely and directly; with the officers of the higher grade, mildly and precisely. When the ruler was present, he was reverent to the point of unease — grave, and composed.

When the prince called him to receive a guest, his face changed and his legs seemed to catch beneath him. He bowed left and right to the officers beside him as their places required, his robe hanging even before and behind, and hurried forward, his sleeves spread like a bird's wings. When the guest had gone, he always reported back: "The visitor has stopped looking back."

Entering the palace gate, he drew his body in, as if the gate were too small to admit him. He never stood in the middle of the gateway; passing through, he never trod on the threshold. Passing the ruler's empty place, his face changed and his knees bent, and his words came as if breath could barely carry them. Going up to the hall, he gathered his robe in both hands, bent his body, and held his breath as if he dared not breathe. Coming out, when he had descended one step his face eased into relief; from the bottom of the steps he moved quickly forward, sleeves like wings; and back at his own place, the respectful unease returned.

Carrying his ruler's scepter, he bent as if the weight were too much for him, holding it no higher than hands held for a bow, no lower than hands passing a gift; his face changed, as if in fear, and he dragged his feet as if they were tied to the ground. Presenting the gifts of state, his look was placid. At the private audience after, he was glad and at ease.

He wore no deep purple or puce at his collar and cuffs; even his house clothes held no red or reddish shade. In hot weather he wore a single garment of fine or coarse weave, but always over an inner one. Over lamb's fur he wore black; over fawn's fur, white; over fox's fur, yellow. His informal fur robe was cut long, with the right sleeve short. His sleeping-gown had to be half again the length of his body. At home he sat on the thick furs of fox and badger. Out of mourning, he wore all the ornaments of the girdle. Except for court dress, his under-skirt was cut narrow above and full below. Lamb's fur and a black cap were not worn on visits of condolence. And on the first day of the month he put on his court robes and presented himself at court.

Fasting, he put on the bright, clean garment of plain cloth. Fasting, he changed his food, and moved from the place where he usually sat.

He did not object to rice milled fine, nor to meat minced small. Rice soured by heat or damp he did not eat, nor fish or meat that had spoiled; nothing discolored, nothing foul-smelling, nothing badly cooked, nothing out of season. Meat cut wrong he did not eat, nor a dish without its proper sauce. However plentiful the meat, he never let it outweigh the rice. Only in wine he set no measure — but never drank to confusion. Bought wine and market dried meat he did not touch. Ginger was never absent from his table. He did not eat much. After assisting at the prince's sacrifice, he did not keep the meat overnight; the meat of his own family's sacrifices he kept no more than three days — past three days, it was not to be eaten. Eating, he did not converse; in bed, he did not speak. Though the meal were coarse rice and vegetable broth, he would set a little apart in offering, with a grave, respectful face.

If his mat was not straight, he did not sit.

When the villagers drank together, he left only after the elders with their staffs had gone out. When the villagers held the rites to drive out pestilence, he put on his court robes and stood on the eastern steps.

Sending his compliments to someone in another state, he bowed twice as he saw the messenger off. When Chi K'ang sent him a gift of medicine, he bowed and received it — but said, "I do not know this drug. I dare not taste it."

The stable burned down. Coming back from court, the Master said, "Was anyone hurt?" He did not ask about the horses.

When the prince sent him cooked meat, he straightened his mat and tasted it first, then shared it out. When the prince sent raw meat, he had it cooked and offered it to his ancestors. When the prince sent a living animal, he kept it alive. Dining in attendance on the prince, while the prince made the offering, he tasted every dish first. When he was ill and the prince came to see him, he lay with his head to the east, his court robes spread over him and his sash drawn across them. When the prince's summons came, he set out on foot, not waiting for the carriage to be yoked.

Entering the great ancestral temple of the state, he asked about everything.

When a friend died with no family to turn to, he said, "The burial is mine." A friend's gift — though it were a carriage and horses — received no bow. Only one gift did: the flesh of sacrifice.

In bed he did not lie like a corpse. At home he wore no formal air. At the sight of a man in mourning dress, even a familiar friend, his face changed. At the sight of a man in ceremonial cap, or a blind man, even in his house clothes, he showed them the forms of courtesy. To a man in mourning he bowed over the crossbar of his carriage; he bowed the same way to anyone carrying the registers of the people. Where a feast was set in abundance, his face changed and he rose to his feet. At a sudden clap of thunder, or a violent wind, his face changed.

Mounting his carriage, he stood square and took the mounting-cord. Inside it, he did not look sharply about, he did not talk fast, he did not point.

At the look on their faces, the bird rose; it wheeled, and then settled. The Master said, "The hen-pheasant on the hill bridge — she knows her season! She knows her season!" Tsze-lu made a motion toward it. Three times it sniffed the air, and rose.
