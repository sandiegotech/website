# The Analects, Book XII

Yen Yuan

Yen Yuan asked about human-heartedness. The Master said, "To master yourself and return to ritual — that is human-heartedness. Master yourself for one day and return to ritual, and the whole world will come home to human-heartedness. And its practice — does it start from a man himself, or from others?" Yen Yuan said, "May I ask the steps?" The Master said, "Look at nothing against ritual. Listen to nothing against ritual. Say nothing against ritual. Make no move against ritual." Yen Yuan said, "Slow as I am, let me make these words my business."

Chung-kung asked about human-heartedness. The Master said, "Away from home, carry yourself as if receiving a great guest. Employ the people as if attending a great sacrifice. What you do not want done to you, do not do to others. Then there will be no resentment against you in the state, and none in the family." Chung-kung said, "Slow as I am, let me make these words my business."

Sze-ma Niu asked about human-heartedness. The Master said, "The human-hearted man is slow to speak." "Slow to speak — is that all human-heartedness is?" The Master said, "The doing is hard. Can the saying be anything but slow?"

Sze-ma Niu asked about the gentleman. The Master said, "The gentleman has no anxiety and no fear." "No anxiety, no fear — is that all a gentleman is?" The Master said, "He looks inward and finds nothing to be sick over. What is there to be anxious about? What is there to fear?"

Sze-ma Niu said sorrowfully, "Other men all have brothers. I alone have none." Tsze-hsia said to him, "I have heard this: death and life are decreed; wealth and honors rest with Heaven. Let the gentleman be reverent and leave nothing undone, respectful to others and observant of ritual, and everyone within the four seas is his brother. Why should a gentleman grieve that he has no brothers?"

Tsze-chang asked what it is to be clear-sighted. The Master said, "Slander that soaks in like water, accusations that sting like a wound in the flesh — the man on whom they do not work, call him clear-sighted. The man on whom they do not work at all — call him far-sighted."

Tsze-kung asked about government. The Master said, "Enough food, enough arms, and the people's trust in their ruler." Tsze-kung said, "If it cannot be helped, and one of the three must go, which goes first?" "The arms." "And if it cannot be helped, and one of the remaining two must go?" "The food. Death has been with us from the oldest days. But without the people's trust, nothing stands."

Chi Tsze-ch'ang said, "Substance is all a gentleman needs. What is polish for?" Tsze-kung said, "A pity, sir, to hear a gentleman talk so — a team of four horses cannot overtake the tongue. Polish is substance; substance is polish. Take the hair off a tiger's hide or a leopard's, and it might as well be the hide of a dog or a goat."

Duke Ai asked Yu Zo, "The year is lean, and the revenue does not cover the spending. What should I do?" Yu Zo answered, "Why not take the tithe — one part in ten?" "With two parts in ten I have not enough. How would I manage with one?" Yu Zo answered, "When the people have plenty, how can their prince alone be in want? When the people are in want, how can their prince alone have plenty?"

Tsze-chang asked how to raise virtue higher and how to see through delusion. The Master said, "Put loyalty and good faith first, and keep moving toward what is right — that raises virtue. You love a man, and want him to live; you hate him, and want him dead. To want him alive and want him dead — that is delusion. 'Not indeed for her wealth — and yet you make the change.'"

Duke Ching of Ch'i asked Confucius about government. Confucius answered, "Let the prince be a prince, the minister a minister, the father a father, the son a son." "Good!" said the duke. "For truly, if the prince is no prince, the minister no minister, the father no father, and the son no son, then though I have my grain, will I live to eat it?"

The Master said, "The man who could settle a lawsuit on half a word — that is Yu!" Tsze-lu never slept on a promise.

The Master said, "At hearing lawsuits I am no better than the next man. What matters is to bring it about that there are no lawsuits."

Tsze-chang asked about government. The Master said, "Keep it before you without tiring. Carry it out with loyalty."

The Master said, "A gentleman who studies widely and reins himself in with ritual is not likely to go far wrong."

The Master said, "The gentleman helps what is fine in men to completion; he does not help what is ugly in them. The small man does the opposite."

Chi K'ang asked Confucius about government. Confucius answered, "To govern is to set straight. Lead the way straight yourself — then who will dare to be crooked?"

Chi K'ang, troubled by thieves, asked Confucius what to do about them. Confucius answered, "If you yourself did not covet, sir, they would not steal though you paid them for it."

Chi K'ang asked Confucius about government, saying, "Suppose I kill the lawless for the good of the law-abiding — what of that?" Confucius answered, "You are governing, sir. What do you need killing for? Want the good, and the people will be good. The virtue of the gentleman is wind; the virtue of the small man is grass. When the wind crosses the grass, the grass must bend."

Tsze-chang asked, "What must an officer be like to be called a man of distinction?" The Master said, "What do you mean by distinction?" "To be heard of through the state; to be heard of through his clan." "That is renown," said the Master, "not distinction. The man of distinction is straight in his substance and loves what is right. He weighs men's words and reads their faces, and he takes care to put himself below others. Such a man is distinguished in the state, and distinguished in his clan. The man of renown puts on the look of human-heartedness while his conduct runs the other way, and he lives in the pose and never doubts it. Such a man will be heard of through the state — and heard of through his clan."

Fan Ch'ih, walking with the Master below the rain altars, said, "May I ask how to raise virtue higher, how to mend hidden faults, and how to see through delusion?" The Master said, "A good question! Put the work first and the reward after — does that not raise virtue? Attack your own evil, and not the evil in others — does that not mend hidden faults? For one morning's anger to forget yourself, and your parents with you — is that not delusion?"

Fan Ch'ih asked about human-heartedness. The Master said, "Love men." He asked about knowledge. The Master said, "Know men." Fan Ch'ih did not follow. The Master said, "Raise the straight and set them above the crooked, and you can make the crooked straight." Fan Ch'ih withdrew, and finding Tsze-hsia he said, "Just now I was with the Master and asked him about knowledge. He said, 'Raise the straight and set them above the crooked, and you can make the crooked straight.' What did he mean?" Tsze-hsia said, "How rich a saying! When Shun had the kingdom, he chose from among all the people and raised up Kao-yao — and men without human-heartedness went far away. When T'ang had the kingdom, he chose from among all the people and raised up I Yin — and men without human-heartedness went far away."

Tsze-kung asked about friends. The Master said, "Counsel them in good faith and guide them well. If it is no use, stop. Do not disgrace yourself for them."

Master Tsang said, "The gentleman gathers friends by culture, and by his friends builds up his human-heartedness."
