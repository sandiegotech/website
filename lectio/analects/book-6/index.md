# The Analects, Book VI

Knowing, Loving, Delighting

The Master said, "Yung — you could seat him on a throne." Chung-kung asked about Tsze-sang Po-tsze. The Master said, "He will do. He keeps things simple." Chung-kung said, "If a man is exacting with himself and simple in what he asks of his people, that is fine. But to be easy on yourself and easy in everything you do — isn't that carrying simplicity too far?" The Master said, "Yung is right."

The Duke Ai asked which of the disciples loved to learn. Confucius answered, "There was Yen Hui. He loved to learn. He never took his anger out on the wrong man; he never made the same mistake twice. By ill fortune his allotted time was short, and he died. Now there is no one — I have yet to hear of anyone who loves learning the way he did."

When Tsze-hwa was sent on a mission to Ch'i, the disciple Zan asked for grain for Tsze-hwa's mother. The Master said, "Give her a fu." Zan asked for more. "Give her a yu," said the Master. Zan gave her five ping. The Master said, "When Ch'ih set out for Ch'i, he rode behind sleek horses in light furs. And I have always heard: a gentleman helps the desperate; he does not make the rich richer." When the Master made Yuan Sze governor of his town, he gave him nine hundred measures of grain. Sze declined them. The Master said, "Don't. Could you not give it away — to your neighborhood, your hamlet, your village?"

The Master said of Chung-kung, "If the calf of a brindled ox is red and well-horned, men may hesitate to offer it — but will the spirits of the mountains and rivers refuse it?"

The Master said, "Hui could go three months without his heart once straying from human-heartedness. The others manage a day here, a month there, and no more."

Chi K'ang asked whether Chung-yu was fit for office. The Master said, "Yu is decisive. What trouble would office give him?" "And Ts'ze — is he fit for office?" "Ts'ze sees through things. What trouble would office give him?" "And Ch'iu?" "Ch'iu has many talents. What trouble would office give him?"

The head of the Chi family sent for Min Tsze-ch'ien to be governor of Pi. Min Tsze-ch'ien said, "Decline for me — politely. If anyone comes back a second time, they will find me across the Wan."

Po-niu was sick, and the Master went to see him. Taking his hand through the window, he said, "We are losing him. It is Heaven's decree — and yet, that this man should have this disease! That this man should have this disease!"

The Master said, "What a man Hui was! One bowl of rice, one gourd of water, a hovel in a back lane — no one else could have borne the misery, and it never once touched his joy. What a man Hui was!"

Yen Ch'iu said, "It is not that I take no delight in your Way, Master. I lack the strength." The Master said, "Those who lack the strength give out somewhere along the road. You have drawn your line before setting out."

The Master said to Tsze-hsia, "Be a scholar of the gentleman's kind, not the small man's."

When Tsze-yu was governor of Wu-ch'ang, the Master said, "Have you found any good men?" He answered, "There is Tan-t'ai Mieh-ming. He never takes a shortcut, and he has never once come to my office except on public business."

The Master said, "Mang Chih-fan does not boast. When the army broke and ran, he kept the rear; only as they reached the gate did he whip up his horse, saying, 'It was not courage that kept me back. My horse would not go.'"

The Master said, "Without the glib tongue of the litanist T'o or the good looks of prince Chao of Sung, it is hard to get through in the present age."

The Master said, "Who leaves a house except by the door? Why, then, will no one walk by this Way?"

The Master said, "When substance outweighs polish, you get a rustic. When polish outweighs substance, you get a clerk. Polish and substance in balance — that is a gentleman."

The Master said, "A man is born straight. If he loses that and lives on, he is simply lucky to have escaped."

The Master said, "Those who know it are not the equal of those who love it, and those who love it are not the equal of those who delight in it."

The Master said, "To those above the average, you may speak of the highest things. To those below it, you may not."

Fan Ch'ih asked about wisdom. The Master said, "Give yourself to what is owed to men; respect the spirits, but keep your distance from them. Call that wisdom." He asked about human-heartedness. The Master said, "The human-hearted man puts the hard work first and any reward out of mind. Call that human-heartedness."

The Master said, "The wise delight in water; the human-hearted delight in mountains. The wise move; the human-hearted are still. The wise are joyful; the human-hearted live long."

The Master said, "One change, and Ch'i would stand where Lu stands. One change, and Lu would reach the Way."

The Master said, "A cornered cup with no corners — call that a cornered cup? Call that a cornered cup?"

Tsai Wo asked, "Tell a human-hearted man there is a man down the well, and I suppose he jumps in after him?" Confucius said, "Why would he? A gentleman can be sent to the well, but not down it. He can be deceived; he cannot be made a fool of."

The Master said, "A gentleman who studies widely and reins himself in with ritual is not likely to go far wrong."

The Master went to see Nan-tsze, and Tsze-lu was not pleased. The Master swore to him, "If I have done anything wrong, may Heaven throw me aside! May Heaven throw me aside!"

The Master said, "The virtue of the Constant Mean — how complete it is! And how long it has been rare among the people!"

Tsze-kung said, "What about a man who poured out benefits on the people and could help them all — what would you say? Could he be called human-hearted?" The Master said, "Why stop at human-hearted? He would be a sage. Even Yao and Shun fell short of that. The human-hearted man is this: wanting to stand, he helps others to stand; wanting to arrive, he helps others arrive. To take what is near — yourself — as the measure for others: that is the way to human-heartedness."
