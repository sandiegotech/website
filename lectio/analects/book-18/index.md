# The Analects, Book XVIII

The Viscount of Wei

The Viscount of Wei withdrew from the court. The Viscount of Chi became a slave to the tyrant Chau. Pi-kan protested, and died for it. Confucius said, "The Yin had three human-hearted men."

Hui of Liu-hsia was chief judge, and three times he was thrown out of office. Someone said, "Isn't it time, sir, to leave altogether?" He said, "If I serve men by the straight Way, where can I go that they will not throw me out three times? And if I am willing to serve men by the crooked way, why should I have to leave the country of my father and mother at all?" The duke Ching of Ch'i, considering how to receive Confucius, said, "I cannot treat him as I would the head of the Chi family. I will treat him as something between Chi and Mang." Then he said, "I am old. I cannot use him." And Confucius left. The people of Ch'i sent Lu a gift of women musicians. Chi Hwan accepted them, and for three days no court was held. And Confucius left.

Chieh-yu, the madman of Ch'u, went past Confucius singing: "Phoenix! Phoenix! How your virtue has withered! What is gone is past all pleading; what is coming can still be caught. Enough, enough! Danger, now, for those who serve in government!" Confucius got down from his carriage, wanting to speak with him. But Chieh-yu quickened his step and was gone, and Confucius could not speak with him. Ch'ang-tsu and Chieh-ni were plowing together in the same field when Confucius passed by, and he sent Tsze-lu to ask them where the ford was. Ch'ang-tsu said, "Who is that, holding the reins?" "That is K'ung Ch'iu," said Tsze-lu. "K'ung Ch'iu of Lu?" "Yes." "Then he knows the ford."

Tsze-lu asked Chieh-ni instead. "And who are you?" said Chieh-ni. "Chung Yu." "The disciple of K'ung Ch'iu of Lu?" "I am." "The whole world is one rushing flood, and who is there to change it with? You follow a master who runs from this man and that man. Wouldn't you do better following men who have run from the world itself?" And he went on raking earth over the seed without a pause. Tsze-lu went back and reported it. The Master fell quiet a moment, then said, "A man cannot flock with the birds and beasts. If I am not to live among these people — among men — whom am I to live with? If the Way held in the world, I would not be out here trying to change it."

Tsze-lu, traveling behind the Master, had fallen back when he met an old man carrying a weeding basket slung on his staff. Tsze-lu asked him, "Sir, have you seen my master?" The old man said, "You, whose four limbs have never worked, who couldn't tell the five grains apart — whose master?" And he planted his staff and went to weeding. Tsze-lu stood and waited, hands folded in respect. The old man kept him for the night, killed a chicken and cooked millet for him, and presented his two sons. Next day Tsze-lu caught up and told the story. The Master said, "A recluse," and sent him back to see the man again. When Tsze-lu got there, the man had gone.

Tsze-lu said to the household, "It is not right to refuse to serve. The courtesies between old and young cannot be set aside; how then can the duty between ruler and minister be set aside? To keep his own person clean, he lets the great relation fall into ruin. A gentleman takes office because it is right to serve — knowing perfectly well that the Way is not going to prevail." The men who withdrew from the world: Po-i, Shu-ch'i, Yu-chung, I-yi, Chu-chang, Hui of Liu-hsia, Shao-lien. The Master said, "Never bending their wills, never letting their persons be shamed — that was Po-i and Shu-ch'i."

"Of Hui of Liu-hsia and Shao-lien you can say they bent their wills and let their persons be shamed. But their words held the line, and their acts were what men hope for. That much, and no more. Of Yu-chung and I-yi you can say they lived hidden and said whatever they pleased. But they kept their persons clean, and their withdrawal weighed the times exactly. I am different from all of them. Nothing is ruled in for me, and nothing is ruled out." Chih, the grand music master, went to Ch'i. Kan, who played at the second meal, went to Ch'u. Liao, of the third meal, went to Ts'ai. Chueh, of the fourth, went to Ch'in. Fang-shu the drummer crossed north of the River.

Wu, who played the hand drum, crossed the Han. Yang the assistant master and Hsiang, who struck the stone chimes, crossed the sea to the islands. The duke of Chau said to his son, the duke of Lu: "A true prince does not neglect his own kin. He does not leave his great ministers bitter at never being used. He does not cast off men of the old families without grave cause. And he does not demand from any one man a talent for everything."

Chau had its eight officers: Po-ta and Po-kwo, Chung-tu and Chung-hwu, Shu-ya and Shu-hsia, Chi-sui and Chi-kwa.
