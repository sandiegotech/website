The Analects, Book III

Eight Rows of Dancers

Book III3 of 20

Confucius said of the head of the Chi family, who had eight rows of dancers perform in his courtyard, "If he can bear to do this, what can he not bear to do?"

The three families had the Yung ode sung while the sacrificial vessels were cleared. The Master said, "'The princes assist; the son of heaven, deep and grave.' What can those words mean in the hall of the three families?"

The Master said, "A man without human-heartedness — what is ritual to him? A man without human-heartedness — what is music to him?"

Lin Fang asked what comes first in ritual. The Master said, "A great question! In ritual, better spare than lavish. In mourning, better grief than a flawless performance."

The Master said, "The tribes of the east and north still have their princes. They are not like our great land, which goes without."

The head of the Chi family was about to sacrifice to the T'ai mountain. The Master said to Zan Yu, "Can you not save him from this?" He answered, "I cannot." The Master said, "Alas! Will you tell me the T'ai mountain is less discerning than Lin Fang?"

The Master said, "A gentleman has nothing to contend for. If contend he must, let it be at archery: he bows to his rivals and goes up, comes down again and drinks the forfeit. Even in his contending, he is a gentleman."

Tsze-hsia asked, "What do these lines mean — 'The dimples of her artful smile, the clear black and white of her eye, the plain ground for the colors'?" The Master said, "The colors come after the plain ground." "Then ritual comes after?" The Master said, "It is Shang who draws out my meaning. Now I can begin to talk about the odes with him."

The Master said, "I could describe the rites of the Hsia, but Chi cannot bear witness for me. I could describe the rites of the Yin, but Sung cannot bear witness for me. Their records and their wise men are too few. Were they enough, I could call on them to prove my words."

The Master said, "At the great sacrifice, once the opening libation is poured, I have no wish to watch what follows."

Someone asked the meaning of the great sacrifice. The Master said, "I do not know. To the man who knew, the whole kingdom would lie as plain as this" — and he pointed to his palm.

He sacrificed to the dead as if they were present. He sacrificed to the spirits as if the spirits were there. The Master said, "If I am not present at the sacrifice myself, it is as if I had not sacrificed."

Wang-sun Chia asked, "What does the saying mean — 'Better to court the kitchen stove than the southwest corner'?" The Master said, "Not so. A man who sins against Heaven has no one left to pray to."

The Master said, "Chau had two dynasties to look back on. How full, how fine its pattern! I follow Chau."

The Master, entering the grand temple, asked about everything. Someone said, "Who calls the son of the man of Tsau a master of ritual? He enters the grand temple and asks about everything." The Master heard of it and said, "The asking is the ritual."

The Master said, "In archery, piercing the hide is not the point, for men's strength is not equal. That was the old way."

Tsze-kung wanted to do away with the sheep offered at the announcing of each new moon. The Master said, "Ts'ze, you love the sheep. I love the rite."

The Master said, "Serve your prince with the whole of ritual, and men will call it flattery."

Duke Ting asked how a prince should use his ministers, and how ministers should serve their prince. Confucius replied, "The prince uses his ministers according to ritual. The ministers serve their prince with loyalty."

The Master said, "The Kwan Tsu: joy, but never license; grief, but never a wound."

Duke Ai asked Tsai Wo about the altars of the land. Tsai Wo replied, "The sovereigns of Hsia planted them with pine; the men of Yin, with cypress; the men of Chau, with chestnut — to make the people tremble, they said." When the Master heard of it, he said, "What is done, do not discuss. What has run its course, do not contest. What is past, do not blame."

The Master said, "Kwan Chung was a small vessel!" Someone said, "Was Kwan Chung frugal, then?" "Kwan had the San Kwei, and no officer of his did double duty. How is that frugal?" "Then did Kwan Chung know ritual?" The Master said, "The princes of states set a screen before their gates; Kwan set a screen before his too. When two princes met in friendship, they had a stand for the turned-down cups; Kwan had such a stand too. If Kwan knew ritual, who does not know ritual?"

The Master, instructing the grand music-master of Lu, said, "This much of music can be known. It opens with all the parts sounding together. Then, given rein, they run pure and clear, each voice distinct, unbroken — and so to the close."

The border warden at Yi asked to be presented to the Master. "When men of worth have come to this place," he said, "I have never been refused." The followers presented him, and when he came out he said, "Friends, why grieve over your master's fall from office? The world has long been without the Way. Heaven is going to use your master as a bell with a wooden tongue."

The Master said of the Shao music that it was perfectly beautiful and perfectly good, and of the Wu that it was perfectly beautiful, but not perfectly good.

The Master said, "High place without largeness of spirit, ritual without reverence, mourning without grief — what is there for me to look at?"
