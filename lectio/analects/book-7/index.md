# The Analects, Book VII

The Transmitter

The Master said, "I transmit; I do not invent. I trust the ancients and I love them. Quietly, I compare myself to our old P'ang."

The Master said, "To store up knowledge in silence, to learn without tiring, to teach without wearing out — which of these can I claim?"

The Master said, "Virtue left untended; learning left undiscussed; hearing what is right and failing to move toward it; being unable to change what is bad in me — these are the things that worry me."

In his leisure hours the Master was easy and unbent, his face open and pleased.

The Master said, "How far I have sunk! How long it has been since I dreamed of the duke of Chau."

The Master said, "Set your heart on the Way. Hold fast to virtue. Lean on human-heartedness. Take your recreation in the arts."

The Master said, "No one has ever come to me with so much as a bundle of dried meat and gone away untaught."

The Master said, "I do not open the door for a man who is not straining at it, or find the word for one who is not struggling to speak. I hold up one corner; if he cannot come back with the other three, I do not go on."

When the Master ate beside a mourner, he never ate his fill. On a day he had wept, he did not sing.

The Master said to Yen Yuan, "When wanted, to serve; when set aside, to withdraw — only you and I have that in us." Tsze-lu said, "If you had command of the armies of a great state, whom would you take with you?" The Master said, "Not the man who would wrestle a tiger bare-handed or wade a river and die with no regrets. The man I take comes to the thing afraid, loves to plan, and carries the plan through."

The Master said, "If wealth could be had for the seeking, I would take up the whip and hold horses to get it. Since it cannot, I will follow what I love."

The things the Master treated with the greatest care were fasting, war, and sickness.

In Ch'i, the Master heard the Shao, and for three months he did not know the taste of meat. "I never imagined," he said, "that music could be made as fine as this."

Yen Yu said, "Is our Master for the ruler of Wei?" Tsze-kung said, "Well — I will ask him." He went in and said, "What sort of men were Po-i and Shu-ch'i?" "Worthies of the old days," said the Master. "Did they regret how it ended for them?" "They sought human-heartedness and found it. What was there to regret?" Tsze-kung came out and said, "Our Master is not for him."

The Master said, "Coarse rice to eat, water to drink, my bent arm for a pillow — there is joy in these things too. Wealth and rank that come by wrong are to me drifting clouds."

The Master said, "Give me a few more years — fifty for the study of the Yi — and I might come through without great faults."

The Master's constant themes were the Odes, the History, and the keeping of ritual. Of these he spoke again and again.

The Duke of Sheh asked Tsze-lu about Confucius, and Tsze-lu gave him no answer. The Master said, "Why didn't you say: he is a man so eager he forgets to eat, so glad he forgets every sorrow, and who has not noticed old age coming on?"

The Master said, "I was not born knowing. I love what is old, and I go after it in earnest."

The Master did not speak of marvels, feats of strength, rebellion, or the spirits.

The Master said, "Walking with two others, I am sure of a teacher. I pick out what is good in them and follow it; what is bad, and mend it in myself."

The Master said, "Heaven planted this virtue in me. What can Hwan T'ui do to me?"

The Master said, "Do you think I am hiding something, my friends? I hide nothing from you. There is nothing I do that is not laid open before you. That is who I am."

The Master taught four things: letters, conduct, loyalty, and good faith.

The Master said, "A sage I will never get to see; a gentleman would be enough. A good man I will never get to see; a man of constancy would be enough. But men who have nothing and pose as having, who are empty and pose as full, who are pinched and pose as comfortable — constancy is beyond such men."

The Master angled, but never with a net. He shot, but never at a roosting bird.

The Master said, "There may be men who act without knowing why; I am not one of them. To hear much, choose what is good, and follow it; to see much and hold it in memory — that is the second kind of knowledge."

The people of Hu-hsiang were hard to talk with, so when a boy from there was received, the disciples wondered. The Master said, "I take a man at his coming; I do not answer for what he does when he leaves. Why be so hard? If a man cleans himself to come to me, I receive him clean. I do not vouch for his past."

The Master said, "Is human-heartedness so far away? The moment I want it, it is here."

The minister of crime of Ch'an asked whether the duke Chao knew ritual, and Confucius said, "He knew ritual." When Confucius had withdrawn, the minister beckoned Wu-ma Ch'i forward and said, "I have heard that a gentleman takes no side. Does a gentleman take sides after all? The prince took a wife from the house of Wu — his own surname — and covered it by calling her 'The elder Tsze of Wu.' If that prince knew ritual, who does not?" Wu-ma Ch'i passed this on, and the Master said, "I am a fortunate man. If I make a mistake, someone is sure to know it."

When the Master was with a man who sang well, he would have him sing it again, and then join in.

The Master said, "In letters I am perhaps the equal of other men. As for living as a gentleman lives — carrying out in conduct what he professes — I have not yet arrived."

The Master said, "A sage? A man of human-heartedness? How would I dare? Say only this of me: I work toward it without tiring and teach without wearying — that much and no more." Kung-hsi Hwa said, "And that much is exactly what we students cannot learn to do."

The Master was gravely ill, and Tsze-lu asked leave to pray for him. The Master said, "Is that done?" Tsze-lu answered, "It is. The Eulogies say, 'Prayer has been made for you to the spirits above and below.'" The Master said, "Then my praying began long ago."

The Master said, "Extravagance breeds arrogance; thrift breeds shabbiness. Better shabby than arrogant."

The Master said, "The gentleman is level and open; the small man is never out from under his cares."

The Master was mild, yet firm; imposing, yet not fierce; respectful, yet at ease.
