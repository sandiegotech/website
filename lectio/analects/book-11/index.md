# The Analects, Book XI

Men of Former Times

The Master said, "The men who came first to ritual and music were countryfolk, they say; the men who came after are accomplished gentlemen. When I have use for ritual and music, I go with the men who came first."

The Master said, "Of all who were with me in Ch'an and Ts'ai, not one still comes to my door." Distinguished for virtue and conduct: Yen Yuan, Min Tsze-ch'ien, Zan Po-niu, and Chung-kung. For speech: Tsai Wo and Tsze-kung. For the work of government: Zan Yu and Chi Lu. For letters: Tsze-yu and Tsze-hsia.

The Master said, "Hui is no help to me. There is nothing I say that does not delight him."

The Master said, "What a devoted son Min Tsze-ch'ien is! No one disputes what his own parents and brothers say of him."

Nan Yung kept returning to the lines about the white scepter stone. Confucius gave him his elder brother's daughter in marriage.

Chi K'ang asked which of the disciples loved to learn. Confucius answered, "There was Yen Hui — he loved to learn. By ill fortune his allotted time was short, and he died. Now there is no one who loves it as he did."

When Yen Yuan died, his father Yen Lu begged the Master to sell his carriage to buy an outer shell for the coffin. The Master said, "Talented or not, a son is a son. When my own son Li died, he had a coffin and no outer shell. I did not go on foot to buy him one. I have walked behind the great officers, and it was not for me to go on foot."

When Yen Yuan died, the Master cried, "Alas! Heaven is destroying me! Heaven is destroying me!"

When Yen Yuan died, the Master wept for him wildly. His followers said, "Master, this grief is too much." "Too much?" he said. "If I do not grieve past measure for this man, for whom should I grieve?"

When Yen Yuan died, the disciples wanted to give him a grand burial. The Master said, "You may not." They buried him grandly anyway. The Master said, "Hui treated me as his father. I was not allowed to treat him as my son. That was not my doing, my friends. It was yours."

Chi Lu asked about serving the spirits of the dead. The Master said, "You cannot yet serve the living — how will you serve the dead?" He ventured to ask about death. "You do not yet understand life — how will you understand death?"

The disciple Min stood at the Master's side looking mild and precise; Tsze-lu, bold and soldierly; Zan Yu and Tsze-kung, frank and easy. The Master was pleased. "A man like Yu," he said, "will not die a natural death."

Some officials of Lu were going to pull down the Long Treasury and rebuild it. Min Tsze-ch'ien said, "What if it were restored as it stands? Why must it be torn down and made new?" The Master said, "This man seldom speaks. When he does, he hits the mark."

The Master said, "What is Yu's lute doing at my door?" After that the other disciples began to slight Tsze-lu. The Master said, "Yu has climbed to the hall. He has not yet entered the inner rooms."

Tsze-kung asked which was the better man, Shih or Shang. The Master said, "Shih goes too far; Shang does not go far enough." "Then Shih is the better?" "Too far," said the Master, "is no better than not far enough."

The head of the Chi family was richer than the duke of Chau had ever been, and still Ch'iu gathered taxes for him and piled the wealth higher. The Master said, "He is no disciple of mine. Sound the drum, my little ones, and attack him."

Ch'ai is simple. Shan is slow. Shih is all surface. Yu is rough.

The Master said, "There is Hui — almost there, and often with nothing in his bowl. Ts'ze will not accept what Heaven appoints, and his goods multiply; yet when he reckons, he is right more often than not."

Tsze-chang asked about the way of the good man. The Master said, "He does not walk in other men's footprints. But neither does he enter the sage's inner room."

The Master said, "A man's talk is solid and earnest — so we grant him our approval. But is he a gentleman? Or only a man got up to look grave?"

Tsze-lu asked, "When I hear a thing, should I act on it at once?" The Master said, "Your father and elder brothers are alive — how can you act at once on whatever you hear?" Zan Yu asked, "When I hear a thing, should I act on it at once?" The Master said, "Act on it at once." Kung-hsi Hwa said, "Yu asked whether he should act at once on what he hears, and you said, 'Your father and elder brothers are alive.' Ch'iu asked whether he should act at once on what he hears, and you said, 'Act on it at once.' I am confused, and I venture to ask why." The Master said, "Ch'iu hangs back, so I pushed him forward. Yu has drive enough for two men, so I held him back."

When the Master stood in danger in K'wang, Yen Yuan had fallen behind. When he caught up, the Master said, "I thought you were dead." "While you live," said Hui, "how would I dare to die?"

Chi Tsze-zan asked whether Chung Yu and Zan Ch'iu could be called great ministers. The Master said, "I thought you were going to ask about someone remarkable, and it is only Yu and Ch'iu! A great minister serves his prince by the Way, and when he cannot, he resigns. Yu and Ch'iu you may call ministers who fill out the ranks." "Then they will do whatever they are told?" "To kill a father or a prince," said the Master, "— that they would not do."

Tsze-lu got Tsze-kao made governor of Pi. The Master said, "You are ruining another man's son." Tsze-lu said, "There are people there to govern; there are altars of the land and grain. Why must a man read books before anyone calls him learned?" The Master said, "This is why I hate a ready tongue."

Tsze-lu, Tsang Hsi, Zan Yu, and Kung-hsi Hwa were sitting with the Master. He said, "I am a day or so older than you — forget that for now. You are always saying, 'No one knows us.' Suppose some ruler did know you. What would you do?" Tsze-lu answered first, without pausing: "A state of a thousand chariots, squeezed between great powers, armies rolling over it, and famine on top of the war — give it to me, and in three years I could make its people brave, and show them which way to face." The Master smiled at him. "Ch'iu, what about you?" Ch'iu answered, "A territory of sixty or seventy li square, or fifty or sixty — give it to me, and in three years I could give the people plenty. As for ritual and music, that must wait for a gentleman." "And you, Ch'ih?" Ch'ih answered, "I do not say I could do these things; I would want to learn them. At the services of the ancestral temple, and when the princes meet in audience, I should like to put on the dark robe and the black linen cap and serve as a minor assistant." Last of all the Master asked Tsang Hsi: "Tien, what about you?" Tien let the last notes thin away, set his lute aside with a clang of strings, and rose. "My wish is different from anything these three have said." "What harm in that?" said the Master. "Each of us is only telling his wish." Then Tien said, "In the last month of spring, in the new clothes of the season, with five or six young men just capped and six or seven boys, I would bathe in the I, take the breeze at the rain altars, and walk home singing." The Master gave a long sigh and said, "I am with Tien." When the three others had gone out, Tsang Hsi stayed behind and said, "What did you think of what they said?" The Master said, "Each simply told his wish." "Why did you smile at Yu?" "A state is governed by ritual. His words did not yield an inch — that is why I smiled." "But was it not a state that Ch'iu asked for?" "Show me a territory of sixty or seventy li, or fifty or sixty, that is not a state." "And was it not a state that Ch'ih asked for?" "Ancestral temples and audiences of the princes — whose business are they but rulers'? And if Ch'ih is to be a minor assistant there, who could be the major one?"
