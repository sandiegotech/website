Lectio Edition · 2026

The Analects

Confucius

  • 479–221 BCE
  • Eastern tradition
  • Philosophy
  • Lectio Edition 2026

The sayings and conversations of Confucius, written down by his students after his death. Twenty short books on learning, ritual, family, government, and what it takes to become fully human.

Why this editionGoal and purpose

The sayings are short. The Victorian English is not.

James Legge's translation of 1893 is the scholar's standard and it is careful. It is also wordy where the Chinese is brief. Two plain words in the original become "to be true to the principles of our nature and the benevolent exercise of them to others".

Legge also renders the book's central word, ren, as a general "virtue", so a reader cannot follow it. And his "superior man" sounds like rank, which is the opposite of what Confucius meant.

The Lectio Edition keeps each saying about the size the Chinese keeps it, and gives each key term one English word. Legge stays in the app beside it.

The openingFrom the Lectio Edition

How it begins.

Book I · Learning

The Master said, "To learn, and in good time to practice what you have learned — is that not a pleasure? To have friends come from far away — is that not a joy? To go unrecognized and feel no bitterness — is that not a gentleman?"

Master Yu said, "Men who honor their parents and respect their elder brothers rarely have a taste for defying their superiors, and no man without that taste has ever been fond of stirring up revolt. The gentleman tends to the root; when the root is set, the Way grows from it. Devotion to parents and respect for elders — surely these are the root of human-heartedness."

The Master said, "Clever words and a winning face rarely keep company with human-heartedness."

Master Tsang said, "Each day I examine myself on three points. Working for others, was I faithless? With my friends, did I fail to keep my word? What was handed on to me, did I leave unpracticed?"

The first 164 words of the Lectio Edition, as they stand in the bundle the app reads.

The choicesPolicies and divergences

What this edition decided.

The review graded fourteen of twenty books as needing no change. Most of its fixes were to punctuation. The edition's main choices are in its terms.

  • Ren is "human-heartedness" everywhere. "Virtue" is kept for de, moral force, so the reader can watch the two ideas separately.
  • Junzi is "the gentleman": a man defined by conduct, not birth.
  • Li is "ritual", one word throughout.
  • Explanations are not written into the Master's sentences. Glosses the draft had added in Books V, XI and XIV were removed.
  • Proper names follow Legge's romanization, so the edition and the 1893 text can be read side by side.
  • One style of quotation mark across all twenty books. Books VI to X had used a different one.

Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 48 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 8. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

仁 (ren)

Book I and throughout

Older
"true virtue", "perfect virtue", "benevolent actions" (Legge)
Lectio
human-heartedness

Ren is the particular excellence of being fully human toward other people, and it is the book's central word. Legge folds it into "virtue" and it disappears.

君子 (junzi)

Book I and throughout

Older
"the superior man" (Legge)
Lectio
the gentleman

The word once meant a lord's son. Confucius remade it to mean a formed character, open to anyone who does the work. "Gentleman" has made the same journey in English.

禮 (li)

Book III and throughout

Older
"the rules of propriety" (Legge)
Lectio
ritual

Legge makes it sound like a code of etiquette. Li is rite, ceremony and the learned forms respect takes, all as one thing.

忠恕 (zhong shu)

Book IV

Older
"to be true to the principles of our nature and the benevolent exercise of them to others" (Legge)
Lectio
loyalty, and putting yourself in the other man's place

The one thread through the Master's Way is two plain words. The edition keeps it that size.

子不語怪，力，亂，神

Book VII, 20

Older
"extraordinary things, feats of strength, disorder, and spiritual beings" (Legge)
Lectio
The Master did not speak of marvels, feats of strength, rebellion, or the spirits.

Four bare words in the Chinese. Luan in this company is political violence, not untidiness.

過猶不及

Book XI

Older
"To go beyond is as wrong as to fall short" (Legge)
Lectio
Too far is no better than not far enough.

The proverb weighs the same on both sides and uses no moral word. Legge's "wrong" adds a judgment the Chinese leaves out.

己所不欲，勿施於人

Book XII

Older
"not to do to others as you would not wish done to yourself" (Legge)
Lectio
What you do not want done to you, do not do to others.

The golden rule in its negative form, five centuries before the positive one. In the Chinese it is a free-standing command of eight characters, and it returns in Book XV.

君子和而不同

Book XIII

Older
"The superior man is affable, but not adulatory" (Legge)
Lectio
The gentleman is in harmony without being in unison.

These are music words. Harmony is different notes agreeing. Unison is one note doubled. Legge moves the saying into drawing-room manners.
