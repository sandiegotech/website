The Analects, Book XVI

The Chi Family

Book XVI16 of 20

The head of the Chi family was about to attack Chwan-yu. Zan Yu and Chi-lu came to see Confucius and said, "Our chief is going to move against Chwan-yu." Confucius said, "Ch'iu, isn't this your failure? Long ago a former king gave the ruler of Chwan-yu charge of the sacrifices to the eastern Mang. Besides, the place lies inside our own borders, and its lord is a minister of the sovereign himself. What business has your chief attacking it?" Zan Yu said, "Our master wants this. Neither of us, his two ministers, wants it." Confucius said, "Ch'iu, Chau Zan had a saying: 'Show your strength and take your place in the ranks; if you cannot, step down.' What good is a guide who won't steady the blind man when he totters, or lift him when he falls? And besides, what you say is wrong. When the tiger or the rhinoceros breaks out of its cage, when the tortoise-shell or the jade is smashed inside its case — whose fault is that?" Zan Yu said, "But Chwan-yu is strong now, and close to Pi. If our chief does not take it today, it will be a grief to his sons and grandsons." Confucius said, "Ch'iu, a gentleman hates the man who will not say plainly 'I want it,' and has to dress the wanting up in reasons. Here is what I have heard: the lord of a state, the head of a house, does not worry that his people are few; he worries that what they have is shared unevenly. He does not worry about poverty; he worries about unrest. For share evenly, and there is no poverty; live in harmony, and there is no lack of people; keep the peace, and nothing gets overthrown."

"And so, if people far away will not submit, you cultivate learning and virtue to draw them in — and once they have come, you give them peace. Now look at the two of you, Yu and Ch'iu, standing at your chief's side. People far away will not submit, and you cannot draw them in. The state itself is splitting and crumbling, and you cannot hold it together. And in the middle of this, he plans to raise spears and shields inside his own country. I am afraid the trouble of the Chi-sun family is not coming from Chwan-yu. It is waiting inside their own walls."

Confucius said, "When the Way holds in the world, ritual, music, and military campaigns issue from the Son of Heaven. When the Way is lost, they issue from the princes — and once they issue from the princes, few houses last ten generations. Once they issue from the great officers, few last five. Once the stewards of the great officers hold the orders of state in their hands, few last three. When the Way holds in the world, government does not sit with the great officers. When the Way holds in the world, the common people do not argue politics."

Confucius said, "The revenue left the ducal house five generations ago. The government has sat with the great officers for four. That is why the descendants of the three Hwan are fading away." Confucius said, "Three friendships profit a man, and three do him harm. Friendship with the upright, friendship with the faithful, friendship with the widely informed — these profit him. Friendship with the man of studied airs, friendship with the practiced flatterer, friendship with the glib — these do him harm."

Confucius said, "Three pleasures profit a man, and three do him harm. Pleasure in the measured practice of ritual and music, pleasure in speaking well of others, pleasure in having many worthy friends — these profit him. Pleasure in extravagance, pleasure in idle roaming, pleasure in the feast — these do him harm." Confucius said, "In attendance on a gentleman, there are three mistakes. To speak before it is your turn — that is called rashness. To hold back when it is your turn — that is called hiding. To speak without reading his face — that is called blindness."

Confucius said, "There are three things a gentleman guards against. In youth, before the blood has settled, he guards against lust. In his prime, when the blood is at full strength, he guards against fighting. In old age, when the blood has thinned, he guards against greed." Confucius said, "There are three things a gentleman stands in awe of. He stands in awe of Heaven's decrees. He stands in awe of great men. He stands in awe of the words of the sages. The small man does not know Heaven's decrees, and so does not fear them. He takes liberties with great men. He makes jokes of the words of the sages."

Confucius said, "Those born knowing it stand highest. Those who learn it and come to know stand next. Those who find it hard and learn anyway come after them. Those who find it hard and do not learn — they are the lowest of the people." Confucius said, "A gentleman keeps nine things in mind. Seeing, he thinks: is it clear? Hearing: is it exact? His expression: is it warm? His bearing: is it respectful? His words: are they true? His work: is it careful? In doubt, he thinks of asking. In anger, he thinks of the trouble to come. And at the sight of gain, he thinks of what is right."

Confucius said, "'They see the good and chase it as if they could never catch up; they see the bad and flinch as if they had put a hand in boiling water' — I have seen the men, and I have heard the words. 'They live hidden to pursue their aims; they do what is right to carry their Way through' — I have heard the words. I have not seen the men." The duke Ching of Ch'i had a thousand teams of four horses; the day he died, the people could not find one virtue to praise him for. Po-i and Shu-ch'i starved to death at the foot of Shau-yang mountain, and the people praise them to this day. Isn't that what the saying means?

Ch'an K'ang asked Po-yu, "Have you heard anything from your father that the rest of us have not?" Po-yu answered, "No. Once, when he was standing alone and I hurried across the courtyard, he said to me, 'Have you learned the Odes?' 'Not yet,' I said. 'If you do not learn the Odes,' he said, 'you will have nothing to say.' I went away and studied the Odes."

"Another day he was standing alone again, and as I hurried across the courtyard he said to me, 'Have you learned ritual?' 'Not yet,' I said. 'If you do not learn ritual,' he said, 'you will have nothing to stand on.' I went away and studied ritual. Those two things are all I have ever heard from him." Ch'an K'ang came away delighted. "I asked one thing," he said, "and I have gotten three. I have heard about the Odes. I have heard about ritual. And I have heard that a gentleman keeps his own son at arm's length."

The wife of the ruler of a state: the ruler calls her Fu Zan, the Lady. She calls herself Hsiao T'ung, this little child. The people of the state call her Chun Fu Zan, our lord's Lady; to people of other states, they speak of her as K'wa Hsiao Chun, our poor little prince. And the people of other states call her Chun Fu Zan as well.
