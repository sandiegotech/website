# The Analects, Book XVII

Yang Ho

Yang Ho wanted to see Confucius, and Confucius would not see him. So Ho sent him a suckling pig — and Confucius, picking an hour when Ho was out, went to his house to return thanks. But he met him on the road. Ho said to Confucius, "Come here. I want a word with you. A man hides his treasure in his coat while his country loses its way — would you call him human-hearted?" "No," said Confucius. "A man longs to serve, yet over and over lets his moment slip — would you call him wise?" "No," said Confucius. "The days and months are running out. The years do not wait for us." Confucius said, "True. I will take office."

The Master said, "By nature men are close to one another. By habit they end up far apart." The Master said, "Only two kinds never change: the wisest, and the dullest."

The Master came to Wu-ch'ang and heard strings and singing all through the town. He smiled. "Why use an ox-cleaver," he said, "to kill a chicken?" Tsze-yu answered, "Master, I once heard you say, 'When the gentleman studies the Way, he loves men. When the common man studies the Way, he is easy to lead.'" The Master said, "My friends, Yen is right. What I said was a joke." Kung-shan Fu-zao, holding the city of Pi in revolt, sent for the Master, and the Master was inclined to go. Tsze-lu didn't like it. "If there is nowhere to go, then do not go," he said. "Why must it be Kung-shan?"

The Master said, "Would the man have called me for nothing? If someone would only use me — could I not make an eastern Chau?" Tsze-chang asked Confucius about human-heartedness. Confucius said, "To be able to carry five things everywhere under heaven — that is human-heartedness." He asked what they were. "Gravity, generosity, good faith, diligence, kindness. Be grave, and no one will treat you with contempt. Be generous, and you will win everyone. Keep faith, and men will trust you with things. Be diligent, and you will get things done. Be kind, and you will be able to ask men for their work."

Pi Hsi sent for him, and the Master was inclined to go. Tsze-lu said, "Master, I once heard you say, 'A gentleman does not walk into the house of a man who has chosen evil with his own hands.' Pi Hsi is holding Chung-mau in revolt. If you go to him, what will people say?" The Master said, "Yes, I said that. But don't they also say: what is truly hard, grinding cannot thin? What is truly white, black dye cannot darken? Am I a bitter gourd, fit to hang on a string and never be eaten?"

The Master said, "Yu, have you heard of the six words and the six faults that shadow them?" "I have not." "Sit down, and I will tell you. To love human-heartedness without loving learning ends in folly. To love knowledge without loving learning ends in drift. To love good faith without loving learning ends in damage. To love straight speech without loving learning ends in harshness. To love courage without loving learning ends in disorder. To love firmness without loving learning ends in recklessness."

The Master said, "My young friends, why does none of you study the Odes? The Odes can rouse you. They can open your eyes. They can put you in company. They can teach you how to voice a grievance. Near at hand, they teach you to serve your father; further off, to serve your prince. And from them you learn the names of birds and beasts, plants and trees, more than you can count." The Master said to Po-yu, "Have you worked through the Chau-nan and the Shao-nan? A man who has not worked through the Chau-nan and the Shao-nan stands with his face against a wall."

The Master said, "'Ritual! Ritual!' they say. Is it jade and silk they mean? 'Music! Music!' they say. Is it bells and drums?" The Master said, "A stern face over a weak heart — set that man among the small men: isn't he the thief who bores through the wall, or climbs over it?" The Master said, "The village paragon is the thief of virtue." The Master said, "To hear it on the road and tell it on the road is to throw virtue away."

The Master said, "These vulgar men — can you serve a prince beside them? Before they have a thing, their one worry is getting it. Once they have it, their one worry is losing it. And a man in dread of losing his place will do anything. There is nothing he will not do." The Master said, "The ancients had three faults, and even those we may have lost. The wild men of old ignored the small things; the wild men of today just run loose. The proud men of old had sharp corners; the proud men of today have sharp tempers. The fools of old were at least straight; the fools of today are simply liars."

The Master said, "Clever words and a winning face rarely keep company with human-heartedness." The Master said, "I hate purple for stealing the place of vermilion. I hate the tunes of Chang for fouling the music of the Ya. I hate the sharp-tongued for toppling states and houses." The Master said, "I would rather say nothing at all." Tsze-kung said, "But Master, if you say nothing, what will we, your students, have to pass on?" The Master said, "Does Heaven say anything? The four seasons turn, the hundred creatures are born — and does Heaven say a word?"

Zu Pei asked to see Confucius, and Confucius sent word that he was ill. Then, as the messenger was going out the door, he took up his lute and sang — making sure Pei heard it. Tsai Wo asked about the three years' mourning for parents. "One year is long enough," he said. "If a gentleman lets ritual alone for three years, ritual will fall apart. If he lets music alone for three years, music will collapse. Within a year the old grain is gone and the new grain is standing; the fire-drill has gone through its round of woods. A year, and mourning should end." The Master said, "A year on — eating fine rice, wearing brocade — would you be at ease?" "I would," said Wo.

"If you are at ease, then do it. A gentleman in mourning tastes no sweetness in fine food, hears no pleasure in music, finds no rest in his own house. So he does not do these things. But you are at ease — so do them." Tsai Wo went out, and the Master said, "There is no human-heartedness in Yu. A child is three years old before it leaves its parents' arms; the three years' mourning is kept everywhere under heaven. And Yu — did he not get his three years of love from his father and mother?"

The Master said, "Stuffed with food to the end of the day, mind set on nothing — a hard case! Are there no dice players, no players of go? Even that would be better than nothing." Tsze-lu said, "Does the gentleman put courage first?" The Master said, "The gentleman puts rightness first. A great man with courage and no rightness makes a rebel. A small man with courage and no rightness makes a robber." Tsze-kung said, "Does the gentleman have his hatreds too?" The Master said, "He has his hatreds. He hates those who broadcast other men's faults. He hates the man beneath who slanders the men above. He hates courage without ritual. He hates the bold and decisive whose minds are shut."

Then the Master asked, "Ts'ze, do you have your hatreds?" Tsze-kung said, "I hate prying passed off as wisdom. I hate insolence passed off as courage. I hate informing passed off as honesty." The Master said, "Women of the house and servants are the hardest to deal with. Come close, and they lose respect. Keep back, and they resent you." The Master said, "If a man is forty and still disliked, that is how he ends."
