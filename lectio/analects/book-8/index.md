# The Analects, Book VIII

A Heavy Load, a Long Road

The Master said, "T'ai-po may be called virtue at its highest. Three times he gave up an empire, and he hid it so well the people could not even find words to praise him."

The Master said, "Respect without ritual is drudgery; caution without ritual is timidity; courage without ritual is disorder; honesty without ritual is harshness. When those in high place are devoted to their own kin, the people stir toward human-heartedness. When old friends are not dropped, the people do not turn cold."

Master Tsang was ill. He called the disciples of his school and said, "Uncover my feet. Uncover my hands. The Odes say, 'Trembling, watchful, as if at the edge of a deep gulf, as if treading on thin ice' — and that is how I have lived. But from this moment, my little ones, I know that I have come through whole."

Master Tsang was ill, and Meng Chang came to ask after him. Tsang said, "When a bird is about to die, its cry is mournful; when a man is about to die, his words are good. There are three things a gentleman holds precious in the Way: in his bearing, to keep far from violence and carelessness; in his face, to keep close to good faith; in his words and tone, to keep far from anything low or false. As for the sacrificial vessels, there are officers to mind them."

Master Tsang said, "Able, yet asking those less able; rich in knowledge, yet asking those with little; having, yet seeming to lack; full, yet counting himself empty; wronged, yet never striking back — I had a friend once who lived this way."

Master Tsang said, "A man you could trust with an orphaned prince, a man you could charge with a state of a hundred li, a man no crisis however great can bend — is that a gentleman? That is a gentleman indeed."

Master Tsang said, "An officer cannot go without breadth and staying power. The load is heavy and the road is long. Human-heartedness is the load he carries — is that not heavy? Only death ends the road — is that not long?"

The Master said, "The Odes wake us. Ritual sets us on our feet. Music finishes the work."

The Master said, "The people can be made to follow the Way; they cannot be made to understand it."

The Master said, "A man who loves daring and resents his poverty will make disorder. So will a man without human-heartedness, if you press your hatred of him too far."

The Master said, "A man might have gifts as fine as the Duke of Chau's — but if he is proud and stingy, the rest is not worth a look."

The Master said, "Three years of study without a thought of salary — such a man is not easy to find."

The Master said, "Hold fast to good faith, love learning, and keep to the good Way until death. Do not enter a state that is tottering; do not stay in one in chaos. When the Way runs in the world, be seen; when it does not, stay hidden. When the Way runs in your own state, be ashamed of poverty and obscurity. When it does not, be ashamed of wealth and honors."

The Master said, "If the office is not yours, its policies are not your business."

The Master said, "When the music master Chih began, and the finale of the Kwan Tsu swelled — how it filled the ear!"

The Master said, "Wild, yet not straight; simple, yet not honest; dull, yet not to be trusted — I do not know what to make of such men."

The Master said, "Learn as if you were chasing what you cannot catch — and afraid, even so, of losing it."

The Master said, "How majestic they were — Shun and Yu held the whole world, and held it as if it were nothing to them."

The Master said, "How great a ruler Yao was! Majestic! Only Heaven is great, and only Yao measured up to it. So vast was his virtue that the people could find no name for it. How majestic his achievements; how brilliant the culture and the institutions he left!"

Shun had five ministers, and the world was in order. King Wu said, "I have ten able ministers." Confucius said, "Talent is hard to find — is the saying not true? Only the age when T'ang and Yu met was richer in it than this of Chau; and of Chau's ten, one was a woman, so the able ministers were nine men. King Wan held two of the empire's three parts and with them still served the house of Yin. The virtue of Chau — call that virtue at its highest."

The Master said, "In Yu I can find no flaw. His own food and drink were plain, but his offerings to the spirits were rich with devotion. His everyday clothes were poor, but his sacrificial cap and apron were beautiful. He lived in a low, mean house, and poured his strength into the ditches and channels. In Yu I can find no flaw."
