# The Analects, Book XV

One Word to Walk With

Duke Ling of Wei asked Confucius about the ordering of troops. Confucius answered, "Matters of sacrificial vessels I have heard something of. Matters of armies I have never studied." The next day he took his leave. In Ch'an the provisions ran out, and his followers grew too sick to stand. Tsze-lu came to him with his anger showing: "So the gentleman, too, can be brought to this?" The Master said, "A gentleman holds firm in want. A small man in want runs over like flood water."

The Master said, "Ts'ze, you take me for a man who has studied much and holds it all in memory?" "Yes," he answered. "Is it not so?" "No," said the Master. "I have one thread, and everything is strung on it."

The Master said, "Yu, the men who know virtue are few."

The Master said, "If any man governed without governing, it was Shun. What did he do? He composed himself in reverence and sat facing due south — that is all."

Tsze-chang asked how a man should act, that his way be open wherever he goes. The Master said, "Let his words be loyal and true, his conduct earnest and careful, and his way will be open even in the lands of the wild tribes, south or north. Let his words be disloyal and false, his conduct careless and cold — will his way be open even in his own district? Standing, see these two things ranged in front of you. Riding, see them leaning on the yoke. Then your way will be open." Tsze-chang wrote the words on his sash.

The Master said, "Straight — that was the historian Yu! When the state kept the Way, he was an arrow. When the state lost the Way, he was an arrow. And a gentleman — that is Chu Po-yu! When the state keeps the Way, he serves. When the state loses it, he can roll his Way up and put it away inside his coat."

The Master said, "To fail to speak with a man worth speaking to is to waste the man. To speak with a man not worth speaking to is to waste your words. The wise waste neither men nor words."

The Master said, "The scholar with a purpose, the man of human-heartedness — they will not cling to life at the cost of human-heartedness. There are times when they will give up their lives to make human-heartedness whole."

Tsze-kung asked how to work at human-heartedness. The Master said, "A craftsman who wants his work good sharpens his tools first. Whatever state you live in, serve the worthiest of its great officers, and make friends with the most human-hearted of its scholars."

Yen Yuan asked how a state should be run. The Master said, "Keep the calendar of Hsia. Ride the plain state carriage of Yin. Wear the ceremonial cap of Chau. For music, the Shao and its dances. Banish the tunes of Chang, and keep clever talkers at a distance — the tunes of Chang are wanton, and clever talkers are dangerous."

The Master said, "A man who gives no thought to what is far off will find his sorrow near at hand."

The Master said, "It is hopeless! I have yet to meet the man who loves virtue the way he loves beauty."

The Master said, "Did Tsang Wan not hold his post like a thief? He knew the worth of Hui of Liu-hsia, and did not raise him to stand at his side in court."

The Master said, "Demand much of yourself, and little of others, and resentment will keep its distance."

The Master said, "A man who never asks himself, 'What am I to make of this? What am I to make of this?' — I can make nothing of him."

The Master said, "Together the whole day, their talk never once touching what is right, and delighting in little feats of cleverness — hard cases indeed!"

The Master said, "What is right is the gentleman's substance. Ritual is how he carries it out, humility is how he brings it forward, good faith is how he completes it. That is a gentleman."

The Master said, "The gentleman is pained by his own want of ability. He is not pained that men do not know him."

The Master said, "The gentleman dreads leaving the world with his name unspoken."

The Master said, "The gentleman makes his demands of himself. The small man makes his demands of others."

The Master said, "The gentleman holds his ground, and does not wrangle. He keeps company, and joins no faction."

The Master said, "The gentleman does not raise a man up because of his words, and he does not throw away words because of the man."

Tsze-kung asked, "Is there one word a man could walk with all his life?" The Master said, "Would that not be reciprocity? What you do not want done to you, do not do to others."

The Master said, "Of the men I deal with, whom do I blame beyond his due, and whom do I praise beyond it? If I have ever praised a man too high, he had been put to the proof first. It was with this same people that the three dynasties kept to the straight road."

The Master said, "I am old enough to remember when a scribe would leave a blank where he was unsure, and a man with a horse would lend it to a neighbor to ride. Now even that much is gone."

The Master said, "Clever words throw virtue into confusion. Impatience in a small thing throws a great plan into ruin."

The Master said, "When the crowd hates a man, look into it. When the crowd loves a man, look into it."

The Master said, "A man can make the Way great. The Way does not make the man great."

The Master said, "To have a fault and not mend it — that is what deserves to be called a fault."

The Master said, "I have gone a whole day without food and a whole night without sleep, to think. It came to nothing. Better to learn."

The Master said, "The gentleman lays his plans for the Way, not for his food. Plow, and hunger can lie in the middle of it; learn, and pay can lie in the middle of it. The gentleman worries about the Way. He does not worry about being poor."

The Master said, "What wisdom reaches, human-heartedness must hold; otherwise, though a man gains it, he will lose it again. What wisdom reaches and human-heartedness holds, he must preside over with dignity, or the people will not respect him. And what wisdom reaches, human-heartedness holds, and dignity presides over, he must still set in motion by ritual. Short of that, it is not yet good."

The Master said, "The gentleman cannot be read in small things, but he can be trusted with great ones. The small man cannot be trusted with great things, but he can be read in small ones."

The Master said, "Human-heartedness matters more to the people than water and fire. I have seen men die from stepping into water and fire. I have never seen a man die from stepping into human-heartedness."

The Master said, "When it comes to human-heartedness, do not yield place — not even to your teacher."

The Master said, "The gentleman is firm in the right; he is not blindly bound by his word."

The Master said, "In serving your prince, be exacting about the work. Let the pay come after."

The Master said, "In teaching, there are no classes of men."

The Master said, "Men whose Ways are different cannot make plans together."

The Master said, "Words are for reaching the meaning. That is all."

Mien, the music-master, came to call. When they reached the steps, the Master said, "Here are the steps." When they reached the mat, the Master said, "Here is the mat." When everyone was seated, the Master told him, "So-and-so is here. So-and-so is there." After the music-master had gone, Tsze-chang asked, "Is that the way to speak with a music-master?" The Master said, "Yes. That is exactly how one helps the blind."
