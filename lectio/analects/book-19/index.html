The Analects, Book XIX

Tsze-chang

Book XIX19 of 20

Tsze-chang said, "A scholar in service who, seeing danger, will give his life; who, seeing gain, thinks of what is right; who in sacrifice thinks of reverence and in mourning thinks of grief — he will do." Tsze-chang said, "Holding virtue without breadth, trusting the Way without depth — what does such a man add to the world? And what does he take from it?"

The disciples of Tsze-hsia asked Tsze-chang about making friends. Tsze-chang said, "What does Tsze-hsia tell you?" They answered, "Tsze-hsia says: keep company with those who are worth it, and turn away those who are not." Tsze-chang said, "That is not what I was taught. A gentleman honors the worthy and makes room for the many; he praises the good and pities the incapable. If I am greatly worthy, who is there I cannot make room for? If I am unworthy, men will turn me away first — what business have I turning others away?" Tsze-hsia said, "Even the minor paths have things worth seeing along them. But follow them far and you bog down — which is why the gentleman does not take them."

Tsze-hsia said, "A man who learns, day by day, what he still lacks, and month by month does not lose what he has — call him a lover of learning." Tsze-hsia said, "Study widely and hold your purpose fast; question closely and think about what is near at hand. Human-heartedness is in that." Tsze-hsia said, "The hundred craftsmen live in their shops to finish their work. The gentleman learns, to carry his Way to its end."

Tsze-hsia said, "The small man always dresses up his faults." Tsze-hsia said, "The gentleman changes three times. From far off he looks stern. Up close, he is warm. And when you hear him speak, every word is exact." Tsze-hsia said, "The gentleman wins his people's trust, and then he puts them to work; without the trust, they would call it oppression. He wins his prince's trust, and then he speaks against his faults; without the trust, the prince would call it slander." Tsze-hsia said, "In the great virtues a man must not cross the line. In the small ones, a little coming and going is allowed."

Tsze-yu said, "The students of Tsze-hsia can sprinkle and sweep a floor, answer a call, come forward and step back — and do it well. But those are twigs. Of the root they know nothing. What sort of learning is that?" Tsze-hsia heard it and said, "Ah, Yen Yu is wrong. In the gentleman's Way, which lesson do you hand on first? Which do you leave to ripen? Students are like plants: you tend each kind as its kind requires. The gentleman's Way does not cheat its beginners. And to hold it whole, first things and last things in a single grasp — only the sage does that."

Tsze-hsia said, "Strength left over from office should go to learning. Strength left over from learning should go to office." Tsze-hsia said, "Mourning should go all the way to grief, and stop there." Tsze-hsia said, "My friend Chang can do what other men find nearly impossible. Even so, he is not yet human-hearted." Master Tsang said, "How grand Chang is! Hard to work at human-heartedness beside a man like that." Master Tsang said, "I heard this from the Master: no man ever gives the whole of himself — unless it is in mourning his parents."

Master Tsang said, "I heard this from the Master: the filial piety of Mang Chwang — in most of it, other men could match him. But he changed nothing: his father's ministers, his father's way of governing, he kept them all. That is the part hard to match." The head of the Mang family made Yang Fu a criminal judge, and Yang Fu came to Master Tsang for advice. Tsang said, "The men above have lost the Way, and the people have come apart — and it has been so for a long time. When you dig out the truth of a case, grieve for them and pity them. Never congratulate yourself on finding it."

Tsze-kung said, "The tyrant Chau cannot have been as wicked as his name. This is why a gentleman hates to live downstream: everything foul in the world comes washing down to him." Tsze-kung said, "The faults of a gentleman are eclipses of the sun and moon. When he fails, everyone sees it. When he mends it, everyone looks up again." Kung-sun Ch'ao of Wei asked Tsze-kung, "Where did Chung-ni get his learning?" Tsze-kung said, "The Way of Wan and Wu has not fallen to the ground yet. It is alive in people. The worthy remember its great parts; the rest remember its small ones. Nobody is without a piece of the Way of Wan and Wu. Where could our Master go and not be learning? And why should he have needed one fixed teacher?"

Shu-sun Wu-shu said to the great officers at court, "Tsze-kung is a better man than Chung-ni." Tsze-fu Ching-po repeated it to Tsze-kung, who said, "Put it in terms of a house and its wall. My wall comes up to a man's shoulder: anyone walking by can look over and see whatever is good inside. The Master's wall is many fathoms high: unless you find the gate and enter, you will never see the beauty of the ancestral temple or the hundred officers in their splendor. And the ones who find the gate, I think, are few. So the chief's remark — what else would you expect?"

Shu-sun Wu-shu spoke against Chung-ni. Tsze-kung said, "Save your breath. Chung-ni cannot be pulled down. The worth of other men is a hill, a mound — a thing you can still climb over. Chung-ni is the sun and the moon. There is no climbing over them. A man may choose to shut them out, but what harm does that do the sun and the moon? It only shows how badly he has measured himself." Ch'an Tsze-ch'in said to Tsze-kung, "You are being modest. In what way is Chung-ni better than you?" Tsze-kung said, "One word can mark a gentleman wise, and one word can mark him a fool. We cannot afford to be careless in what we say. No one reaches the Master, the way no one climbs a stairway to the sky."

"Had the Master been given a state or a great house, it would have gone as the old words say. He plants the people, and they stand. He leads them, and they walk. He settles them, and they come from every side. He stirs them, and they move as one. Living, he is their glory. Dead, he is their grief. How could anyone reach him?"
