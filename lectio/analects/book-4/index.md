# The Analects, Book IV

Human-Heartedness

The Master said, "It is human-heartedness that makes a neighborhood beautiful. If a man can choose where to live and passes it by, how is he wise?"

The Master said, "Without human-heartedness, a man cannot bear hardship long, and he cannot bear comfort long either. The human-hearted rest in it. The wise know what it is worth."

The Master said, "Only the human-hearted man can rightly love men, and rightly hate them."

The Master said, "Set your will on human-heartedness, and there is no room left for evil."

The Master said, "Riches and rank are what men want; if they cannot be had in the right way, do not hold them. Poverty and low place are what men hate; if they cannot be escaped in the right way, do not escape them. Take human-heartedness from a gentleman, and what is left of the name? A gentleman does not leave it for the length of a meal. In a hurry, he holds to it. In danger, he holds to it."

The Master said, "I have never seen a man who loved human-heartedness, or one who hated its opposite. The lover would set nothing above it. The hater would practice it by letting nothing opposed to it come near him. Is there a man who can give his strength to it for one day? I have never seen one whose strength was too little. If such a man exists, I have not seen him."

The Master said, "A man's faults run true to his kind. Look at the faults, and you know the human-heartedness."

The Master said, "Hear the Way in the morning, and you can die that evening without regret."

The Master said, "A scholar sets his heart on the Way. If he is ashamed of shabby clothes and plain food, he is not worth talking to."

The Master said, "The gentleman moves through the world with nothing fixed for and nothing fixed against. He goes with what is right."

The Master said, "The gentleman thinks of virtue; the small man thinks of comfort. The gentleman thinks of what the law will exact; the small man thinks of what favor he can win."

The Master said, "Act always with an eye to your own advantage, and you will be much resented."

The Master said, "If a man can govern his country by ritual and deference, what difficulty will he have? And if he cannot, what is ritual to him?"

The Master said, "Do not worry that you have no position; worry about what it takes to stand in one. Do not worry that no one knows you; seek to be worth knowing."

The Master said, "Shan, one thread runs through my Way." Master Tsang said, "Yes." The Master went out, and the other disciples asked, "What did he mean?" Master Tsang said, "The Master's Way is loyalty, and putting yourself in the other man's place. That is all."

The Master said, "The gentleman is at home in what is right; the small man is at home in what pays."

The Master said, "When you see a worthy man, think how to equal him. When you see an unworthy one, turn and examine yourself."

The Master said, "In serving your parents, you may reason with them — gently. If you see they will not follow, be more reverent still, and do not turn back. And though it wears you out, do not resent them."

The Master said, "While your parents live, do not travel far. If travel you must, have a fixed place to go."

The Master said, "If for three years a son does not turn from his father's way, he may be called a devoted son."

The Master said, "Your parents' years must never be out of mind — at once a joy and a fear."

The Master said, "The ancients were slow to speak. They were ashamed to have their deeds fall short of their words."

The Master said, "Few go wrong by holding back."

The Master said, "The gentleman wants to be slow in his words and quick in his deeds."

The Master said, "Virtue does not stand alone. It always finds neighbors."

Tsze-yu said, "Press your prince too often, and you will be disgraced. Press your friends too often, and they will fall away."
