The Analects, Book I

Learning

Book I1 of 20

The Master said, "To learn, and in good time to practice what you have learned — is that not a pleasure? To have friends come from far away — is that not a joy? To go unrecognized and feel no bitterness — is that not a gentleman?"

Master Yu said, "Men who honor their parents and respect their elder brothers rarely have a taste for defying their superiors, and no man without that taste has ever been fond of stirring up revolt. The gentleman tends to the root; when the root is set, the Way grows from it. Devotion to parents and respect for elders — surely these are the root of human-heartedness."

The Master said, "Clever words and a winning face rarely keep company with human-heartedness."

Master Tsang said, "Each day I examine myself on three points. Working for others, was I faithless? With my friends, did I fail to keep my word? What was handed on to me, did I leave unpracticed?"

The Master said, "To govern a state of a thousand chariots: attend to the work and keep your word; spend little and love the people; and call on their labor only in the right season."

The Master said, "A young man should be devoted to his parents at home and respectful to his elders away from it. He should be careful and truthful, love everyone, and keep close to the human-hearted. If he has strength left after that, let him spend it on study."

Tsze-hsia said, "A man who prizes worth over beauty, who serves his parents with all his strength, who would give his life for his prince, and whose word to his friends is good — others may say he is unlearned; I will call him learned."

The Master said, "A gentleman without gravity carries no weight, and his learning does not hold. Put loyalty and good faith first. Have no friends who are not your equals. And when you are wrong, do not be afraid to change."

Master Tsang said, "Give the dead their full rites, and keep the long-departed in remembrance, and the virtue of the people will grow deep again."

Tsze-ch'in asked Tsze-kung, "Wherever our master goes, he learns everything about how the place is governed. Does he ask for it, or is it offered?" Tsze-kung said, "Our master gets it by being warm, upright, courteous, temperate, and deferential. His way of asking is not other men's way of asking."

The Master said, "While a man's father lives, watch what he means to do. When his father dies, watch what he does. If for three years he does not turn from his father's way, he may be called a devoted son."

Master Yu said, "In the practice of ritual, ease is the thing to prize. This was the beauty of the way of the ancient kings; in matters small and great, they followed it. But there is a limit. To know the worth of ease and be easy, without ritual to give it shape — that will not do either."

Master Yu said, "When a promise keeps close to what is right, you can make your word good. When respect keeps close to ritual, you stay clear of shame. When the man you lean on is the right man to be close to, you can take him for your guide."

The Master said, "A gentleman does not ask that his food be rich or his home be easy. He is quick in his work and careful in his speech, and he goes where men of the Way are, to be set straight by them. Call such a man a lover of learning."

Tsze-kung said, "Poor, but no flatterer; rich, but not proud — what would you say to that?" The Master said, "Good. But not so good as poor and glad, or rich and in love with ritual." Tsze-kung said, "The Book of Poetry says, 'Cut, then file; carve, then polish.' Is that what you mean?" The Master said, "Now I can begin to talk about the odes with Ts'ze. Tell him what came before, and he knows what comes next."

The Master said, "Do not be troubled that men do not know you. Be troubled that you do not know men."
