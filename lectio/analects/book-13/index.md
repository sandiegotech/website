# The Analects, Book XIII

Setting Names Right

Tsze-lu asked about government. The Master said, "Go ahead of the people, and work hard for them." He asked for more, and was told, "Never tire."

Chung-kung, then steward to the head of the Chi family, asked about government. The Master said, "Put your officers to their proper work first, pardon small faults, and raise up the worthy and able." "How shall I recognize the worthy and able, to raise them?" "Raise the ones you know. The ones you do not know — will other men leave them aside?"

Tsze-lu said, "The ruler of Wei is waiting for you to run his government. What will you do first?" The Master said, "The one thing needed is to set names right." "So that is it!" said Tsze-lu. "How impractical you are! Why should names need setting right?" The Master said, "How uncouth you are, Yu! On what he does not understand, a gentleman keeps a careful silence. If names are not right, what is said does not answer to what is. If what is said does not answer to what is, nothing can be carried through. When nothing can be carried through, ritual and music do not flourish. When ritual and music do not flourish, punishments miss the mark. And when punishments miss the mark, the people have nowhere to set hand or foot. So the gentleman gives no name that cannot be rightly spoken, and says nothing that cannot be carried out. In his words, the gentleman leaves nothing to carelessness. That is all."

Fan Ch'ih asked to be taught farming. The Master said, "An old farmer would serve you better than I." He asked to be taught gardening. "An old gardener would serve you better than I." When Fan Ch'ih had gone out, the Master said, "What a small man Fan Hsu is! Let those above love ritual, and no one among the people will dare to be irreverent. Let them love what is right, and no one will dare to disobey. Let them love good faith, and no one will dare to deal falsely. Do that, and people will come from every quarter with their children strapped to their backs. What does he need to know about farming?"

The Master said, "A man may have the three hundred Odes by heart; but give him a post in government and he flounders, send him abroad on a mission and he cannot answer for himself — then what use is all his learning?"

The Master said, "When the ruler himself is straight, things go forward without an order given. When he himself is not straight, he may give orders, but no one will follow them."

The Master said, "The governments of Lu and Wei are brothers."

The Master said of Ching, a son of the ducal house of Wei, that he knew how to keep a household. When he first had anything, he said, 'It will just about do.' When he had a little more, he said, 'It is just about complete.' When he was rich, he said, 'It is just about fine.'

When the Master traveled to Wei, Zan Yu drove his carriage. The Master said, "How the people have multiplied!" Yu said, "Once they are many, what more should be done for them?" "Make them rich." "And once they are rich, what more?" "Teach them."

The Master said, "If any ruler would employ me, in twelve months I would have something to show. In three years, the thing would be done."

The Master said, "'If good men governed a state for a hundred years running, cruelty would be overcome and killing done away with.' How true that saying is!"

The Master said, "Even with a true king, it would still take a generation before human-heartedness prevailed."

The Master said, "If a man sets his own conduct straight, what difficulty will he have in governing? If he cannot set himself straight, what business has he setting others straight?"

The disciple Zan came back late from court. The Master said, "Why so late?" "There was government business." The Master said, "Family business, more likely. If there had been government business, I would have heard of it — out of office as I am."

Duke Ting asked whether there is a single saying that can make a state prosper. Confucius answered, "No saying can do quite so much. But there is one that men have: 'To be a prince is hard; to be a minister is not easy.' If a ruler knows how hard it is to be a prince, is that not close to one saying making a state prosper?" "And is there a single saying that can ruin a state?" Confucius answered, "No saying can do quite so much. But there is one that men have: 'I take no pleasure in being a prince — except that no one crosses a word I say.' If his words are good and no one crosses them, well and good. But if they are not good and no one crosses them, is that not close to one saying ruining a state?"

The Duke of Sheh asked about government. The Master said, "Make the near glad, and the far will come."

Tsze-hsia, then governor of Chu-fu, asked about government. The Master said, "Do not want it fast. Do not look at small gains. Want it fast, and you never arrive. Look at small gains, and great things go undone."

The Duke of Sheh told Confucius, "Among us there are men upright to the letter. If a father steals a sheep, the son will bear witness against him." Confucius said, "Our upright men are different. The father covers for the son, and the son covers for the father. There is uprightness in that."

Fan Ch'ih asked about human-heartedness. The Master said, "At home, be courteous. At work, be careful. With others, be loyal. Even among the wild tribes, these are not to be set aside."

Tsze-kung asked, "What must a man be before he deserves to be called an officer?" The Master said, "He keeps a sense of shame about his own conduct, and sent to any quarter, he does not disgrace his prince's commission. That deserves the name." "May I ask about the rank below?" "His kinsmen call him a devoted son; his neighbors call him a good brother." "And the rank below that?" "His word is always kept, his acts always carried through — hard little stones of men. Still, they might make the next rank." "And the men in government today?" The Master said, "Pah — peck-measures and rice-baskets. Not worth the counting."

The Master said, "Since I cannot find men of the middle way to work with, I must have the wild and the fastidious. The wild press forward and take hold; the fastidious have things they will not do."

The Master said, "The people of the south have a saying: 'A man without constancy cannot make even a shaman or a doctor.' It is a good one. And the Changes say, 'Inconstant in his virtue — someone will hand him disgrace.'" The Master said, "For such a man, there is no need of the oracle at all."

The Master said, "The gentleman is in harmony without being in unison. The small man is in unison without being in harmony."

Tsze-kung asked, "What of a man whom everyone in his village loves?" The Master said, "Not enough." "What of a man whom everyone in his village hates?" The Master said, "Not enough either. Better the man whom the good of the village love and the bad of the village hate."

The Master said, "The gentleman is easy to serve and hard to please. Try to please him by any crooked path, and he is not pleased; and when he employs men, he measures them to their capacity. The small man is hard to serve and easy to please. Try to please him, crooked path or not, and pleased he is; and when he employs men, he expects each one to be good at everything."

The Master said, "The gentleman is at ease without arrogance. The small man is arrogant without ease."

The Master said, "Firm, steadfast, plain, and slow of speech — that is close to human-heartedness."

Tsze-lu asked, "What must a man be before he deserves to be called a scholar?" The Master said, "Earnest, encouraging, and warm — that deserves the name. With his friends, earnest and encouraging; with his brothers, warm."

The Master said, "Let a good man teach the people for seven years, and they would be fit even to bear arms."

The Master said, "To send people to war untaught is to throw them away."
