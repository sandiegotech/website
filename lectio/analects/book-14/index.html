The Analects, Book XIV

Hsien Asked

Book XIV14 of 20

Hsien asked what is shameful. The Master said, "When the state keeps the Way, draw your pay. When the state has lost the Way, to draw your pay all the same — that is shameful."

"When the love of winning, boasting, resentment, and greed are all held down — may that be called human-heartedness?" The Master said, "It may be called difficult. Whether it is human-heartedness, I do not know."

The Master said, "A scholar who hankers after his comforts does not deserve the name of scholar."

The Master said, "When the state keeps the Way, speak boldly and act boldly. When the state has lost the Way, act boldly still — but let your speech go softly."

The Master said, "A man of virtue will always have something to say. A man with something to say does not always have virtue. A human-hearted man will always have courage. A brave man does not always have human-heartedness."

Nan-kung Kwo put a question to Confucius: "The archer I and Ao, who could drag a boat over dry land — neither of them died a natural death. Yu and Chi worked the fields with their own hands — and the kingdom came to them." The Master gave no answer. When Nan-kung Kwo had gone out, the Master said, "There is a gentleman! There is a man who prizes virtue!"

The Master said, "Gentlemen who lack human-heartedness — those there have been. But a small man with human-heartedness — never."

The Master said, "Can you love a man and not make him labor? Can you be loyal to a man and not teach him?"

The Master said, "In the drawing up of a state paper, P'i Shan made the rough draft, Shi-shu examined and discussed it, Tsze-yu, the master of embassies, polished it, and Tsze-ch'an of Tung-li gave it its finish."

Someone asked about Tsze-ch'an. The Master said, "A generous man." He asked about Tsze-hsi. "That man! That man!" He asked about Kwan Chung. The Master said, "He was a man: Pien, a town of three hundred households, was taken from the head of the Po family for him, and the Po chief, brought down to coarse rice, never let one resentful word be heard to the end of his life."

The Master said, "To be poor without resentment is hard. To be rich without arrogance is easy."

The Master said, "Mang Kung-ch'o would make a fine elder for the house of Chao or Wei; but he could not serve as a great officer of Tang or Hsieh."

Tsze-lu asked what makes a complete man. The Master said, "Take the knowledge of Tsang Wu-chung, the freedom from greed of Kung-ch'o, the bravery of Chwang of Pien, and the many talents of Zan Ch'iu, and finish them with ritual and music — that might be called a complete man." Then he said, "But need a complete man of these days have all that? A man who at the sight of gain thinks of what is right; who at the sight of danger is ready to give his life; who does not forget the promise of years ago, however long the years — he too may be called a complete man."

The Master asked Kung-ming Chia about Kung-shu Wan: "Is it true that your master does not speak, does not laugh, and does not take?" Kung-ming Chia answered, "Whoever told you that told you too much. My master speaks when it is time to speak, so no one tires of his speaking. He laughs when there is something to be glad of, so no one tires of his laughing. He takes when taking is right, so no one tires of his taking." The Master said, "So? Can it really be so?"

The Master said, "Tsang Wu-chung held the city of Fang while he asked the duke of Lu to appoint his heir. Say what you like about his not coercing his prince — I do not believe it."

The Master said, "Duke Wan of Tsin was crafty and not straight. Duke Hwan of Ch'i was straight and not crafty."

Tsze-lu said, "When Duke Hwan had his brother Chiu killed, Shao Hu died with his master. Kwan Chung did not. Was that not a failure of human-heartedness?" The Master said, "That Duke Hwan could gather all the princes together, and not by war-chariots and weapons — that was Kwan Chung's strength. Where is the human-heartedness to match his! Where is the human-heartedness to match his!"

Tsze-kung said, "Kwan Chung was no human-hearted man, surely? When Duke Hwan had his brother Chiu killed, Kwan Chung could not even die with him — he went on to serve as Hwan's prime minister." The Master said, "Kwan Chung served as minister to Duke Hwan, made him chief of all the princes, and set the whole kingdom in order — and the people are living on his gifts to this day. But for Kwan Chung, we would be wearing our hair loose and buttoning our coats on the left. Would you have him keep the small good faith of a common man and his wife — strangled in some ditch, and no one ever the wiser?"

Kung-shu Wan's own steward, the great officer Hsien, rose to the prince's court at Wan's side. When the Master heard of it, he said, "He deserved to be called Wan."

The Master was speaking of the misrule of Duke Ling of Wei, and Chi K'ang said, "If he is such a man, how is it he does not lose his state?" Confucius said, "Chung-shu Yu looks after his guests and envoys, the litanist T'o keeps his ancestral temple, and Wang-sun Chia commands his army. With officers like those, how should he lose his state?"

The Master said, "Say a thing without a blush, and you will find it hard to make the thing good."

Chan Ch'ang murdered Duke Chien of Ch'i. Confucius bathed, went to court, and told Duke Ai, "Chan Hang has slain his sovereign. I ask you to punish him." The duke said, "Tell the chiefs of the three families." Confucius withdrew, saying, "Because I walk behind the great officers, I did not dare not to speak. And my prince says, 'Tell the chiefs of the three families.'" He went and told the chiefs, and they refused. Confucius said, "Because I walk behind the great officers, I did not dare not to speak."

Tsze-lu asked how to serve a ruler. The Master said, "Never deceive him. And stand up to his face."

The Master said, "The gentleman works his way upward. The small man works his way down."

The Master said, "The men of old learned for their own sake. The men of today learn for the eyes of others."

Chu Po-yu sent a man to Confucius with his greetings. Confucius sat with him and asked, "What is your master working at?" The messenger answered, "My master wants to make his faults fewer, and has not yet managed it." When he had gone out, the Master said, "There is a messenger! There is a messenger!"

The Master said, "If the office is not yours, its policies are not your business."

Master Tsang said, "The gentleman's thoughts do not wander from his post."

The Master said, "The gentleman is ashamed to let his words outrun his deeds."

The Master said, "The Way of the gentleman has three parts, and I can manage none of them: human-hearted, he is never anxious; wise, never bewildered; brave, never afraid." Tsze-kung said, "Master, you have just described yourself."

Tsze-kung was always measuring men against one another. The Master said, "Ts'ze must be a worthy man indeed. I have no time to spare for that."

The Master said, "I am not troubled that men do not know me. I am troubled by my own want of ability."

The Master said, "Not to expect deceit, not to imagine bad faith — and yet to be the first to sense them when they come: is that not a worthy man?"

Wei-shang Mau said to Confucius, "Ch'iu, what is all this perching here and roosting there? Have you become a smooth talker?" Confucius said, "I would not dare to be a smooth talker. But I hate a man who will not budge."

The Master said, "A ch'i is not called a great horse for its strength, but for its virtue."

Someone said, "What do you say of repaying injury with kindness?" The Master said, "Then what will you repay kindness with? Repay injury with straight justice. Repay kindness with kindness."

The Master said, "No one knows me — alas!" Tsze-kung said, "Why do you say no one knows you?" The Master said, "I do not rail at Heaven. I do not blame men. I learn from what is lowest and reach toward what is highest. And the one who knows me — is that not Heaven?"

Kung-po Liao slandered Tsze-lu to Chi-sun. Tsze-fu Ching-po brought word of it, saying, "My master's mind is certainly being poisoned by Kung-po Liao — but I still have power enough to see Liao's corpse exposed in the market and the court." The Master said, "If the Way is to go forward, that is the decree. If the Way is to fall, that is the decree too. What can Kung-po Liao do against the decree?"

The Master said, "Some worthy men withdraw from the world. Some withdraw from a state. Some withdraw at a look. Some withdraw at a word."

The Master said, "Seven men have done so."

Tsze-lu passed a night in Shih-man. The gatekeeper asked, "From whom do you come?" "From Mr. K'ung." "Ah," said the gatekeeper, "the one who knows it cannot be done and keeps on doing it?"

One day in Wei the Master was playing the stone chimes, when a man carrying a straw basket passed the door and said, "There is a heart still full of the world, in that beating of stones!" Then he added, "But how petty, that stubborn clang-clang! If no one knows you, give it up, and be done. 'Where the water is deep, wade through in your clothes; where it is shallow, hitch them up.'" The Master said, "How resolute! Giving up — there is nothing hard in that."

Tsze-chang said, "The Book of History says that Kao-tsung kept the royal mourning three years without speaking. What does that mean?" The Master said, "Why single out Kao-tsung? The ancients all did the same. When a sovereign died, the officers of state gathered their duties and answered to the prime minister for three years."

The Master said, "When those above love ritual, the people are easy to lead."

Tsze-lu asked about the gentleman. The Master said, "He cultivates himself with reverent care." "Is that all?" "He cultivates himself, and others find rest in him." "Is that all?" "He cultivates himself, and the whole people find rest in him. He cultivates himself, and the whole people find rest — even Yao and Shun found that past their strength."

Yuan Zang sat sprawled on his heels, waiting for the Master, who said to him, "Young, and never modest; grown, and nothing to your name; old, and refusing to die — you are a pest." And he rapped him on the shin with his staff.

A boy of the village of Ch'ueh used to carry messages between the Master and his visitors. Someone asked about him: "Coming along well, I suppose?" The Master said, "I have seen him take a grown man's seat, and I have seen him walk abreast of his elders. He is not after progress. He is in a hurry to be finished."
