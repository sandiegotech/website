Aristotle · Lectio Edition · 2026

# Nicomachean Ethics, Book I

The Good for Man

Every craft and every line of inquiry, and likewise every action and deliberate choice, seems to aim at some good. That is why people have rightly described the good as "what everything aims at." But the aims clearly differ. In some cases the aim is the activity itself; in others it is some product over and above the activity. And where there are products beyond the activities, the products are by their nature better than the activities. Since there are many actions, crafts, and sciences, there are also many aims: health is the aim of medicine, a ship of shipbuilding, victory of generalship, wealth of household management.

Wherever a number of such pursuits fall under a single master art — as bridle-making and all the other trades that produce a horse's equipment fall under horsemanship, and horsemanship together with every military action falls under generalship, and so on — in every case the aim of the master art is more worth choosing than the aims of the arts beneath it, since those lower aims are pursued for the sake of the higher. And it makes no difference whether the activities themselves are the aims, or something further beyond them, as in the sciences just mentioned.

Now if there is some aim of everything we do that we want for its own sake, while we want everything else for the sake of it — and if we do not choose absolutely everything for the sake of something further (for then the chain would run on without end, and our desiring would be empty and pointless) — then plainly this aim will be the good, in fact the highest good. Surely, then, knowing it carries real weight for the conduct of our lives: like archers with a target to aim at, we are more likely to hit what is right. If that is so, we should try to grasp, at least in outline, what this good is and which of the sciences or capacities it belongs to.

One would naturally say it belongs to the most authoritative and most comprehensive of them — and that is plainly the science of the community, the art of statecraft. For statecraft lays down which sciences should exist in a community, which ones each class of citizen should learn, and to what level. We see the most esteemed capacities falling under it — generalship, household management, rhetoric. And since statecraft makes use of all the other practical sciences, and further legislates what people must do and refrain from doing, its aim must take in the aims of the rest, so that this aim will be the human good. For even if the good is the same for an individual and for a community, the good of a community is plainly a greater and more complete thing to attain and preserve. To secure it for one person is worth having; to secure it for a whole people and for communities is nobler and more godlike.

Such, then, is what this inquiry aims at; it is a kind of political inquiry. And we will have treated the subject well enough if we make it as clear as its material allows. Precision is not to be looked for in all discussions alike, any more than in all the products of a craft. What is noble and what is just — the things statecraft examines — vary and waver so much that they are sometimes thought to exist only by convention and not by nature. And even the things agreed to be good show a similar wavering, since harm has come to many people through them: some have been destroyed by their wealth, others by their courage. So we must be content, in speaking about such things and from such starting points, to indicate the truth roughly and in outline. Since we are speaking about what holds for the most part and arguing from premises of the same kind, our conclusions will be of the same sort. Each claim should be received in the same spirit. It is the mark of an educated person to look for exactness in each subject only so far as the nature of the thing allows — it is just as foolish to accept merely probable reasoning from a mathematician as to demand strict proofs from an orator.

Each person judges well the things he knows, and is a good judge of those. So a person educated in a particular subject is a good judge in that subject, and the person broadly educated is a good judge generally. This is why a young person is not a fit student of statecraft. He has no experience of the actions of life, and the whole discussion proceeds from such experience and is about it. And because he tends to follow his feelings, he will listen pointlessly and without benefit, since the goal here is not knowledge but action. It makes no difference whether he is young in years or merely young in character; the defect does not come from age but from living by feeling and chasing each thing as it comes. For such people, like those who lack self-control, knowledge brings no benefit; but for those who shape their desires and act according to reason, knowing these things is of great value. Let this serve as a preface — on the student, on how our remarks should be received, and on what we have set out to do.

Let us pick up the thread. Since all knowledge and every choice reaches for some good, what is the good we say statecraft aims at — the highest of all the goods achievable by action? As far as the name goes, almost everyone agrees: ordinary people and refined people alike call it happiness, and they take living well and doing well to be the same as being happy. But when it comes to what happiness actually is, they disagree, and ordinary people do not give the same account as the wise. Some say it is one of the plain, obvious things — pleasure, or wealth, or honor — and different people say different things; often the same person says different things at different times, calling it health when he is sick and wealth when he is poor. And aware of their own ignorance, people admire anyone who talks grandly over their heads. Others have held that beyond all these many goods there is some single good, existing in itself, which is the cause of all the rest being good.

To sift through every opinion would probably be a waste of effort; it is enough to examine the ones most widely held, or that seem to have something to them. We should not overlook the difference between arguing from first principles and arguing toward them. Plato, too, was rightly puzzled by this, and asked whether the right road runs from first principles or toward them — like the question, on a racecourse, of whether you run from the judges to the far post or back. We must start from what is known. But "known" means two things: known to us, and known without qualification. For us, presumably, we must start from what is known to us. This is why anyone who is going to study what is noble and just, and statecraft generally, with any real profit, must first have been brought up well in good habits. The starting point is the fact that something is so, and if that is clear enough, there is no further need to know why it is so. A person raised this way either already has the right starting points or can take them in easily. As for the person who has neither and cannot acquire them, let him hear Hesiod's verdict: "Best of all is the man who works everything out for himself; good too is the one who takes a good suggestion to heart; but the man who neither thinks for himself nor listens and learns from another — that man is useless."

To return from the digression. People seem, reasonably enough, to form their picture of the good and of happiness from the kinds of lives they lead. The many, the more vulgar sort, take it to be pleasure, and so are content with a life of enjoyment. There are three lives that stand out: this life of pleasure, the life of public engagement, and, third, the life of contemplation. Now the many plainly show themselves to be slavish in choosing a life fit for cattle; yet they get some hearing, because many people in power share the tastes of Sardanapalus. The cultivated and active sort take the good to be honor, which is more or less the aim of public life. But honor seems too shallow to be what we are looking for. It seems to depend more on those who give it than on the person who receives it, whereas we sense that the good must be something of our own, not easily taken away. Besides, people seem to pursue honor in order to convince themselves that they are good: they want to be honored by people of sense, by those who know them, and on the grounds of their virtue. Clearly, then, in their own eyes virtue ranks higher than honor.

So one might suppose virtue is more the aim of public life. Yet even virtue turns out to be not complete enough. It seems possible to possess virtue while asleep, or inactive throughout one's life, or — worse — while suffering the greatest misfortunes; and no one would call a person living like that happy, except to defend a thesis. Enough on these lives; they have been discussed sufficiently elsewhere. The life of contemplation we will examine later. As for the moneymaker's life, it is a life under compulsion, and wealth is plainly not the good we are seeking, since it is merely useful, a means to something else. One would sooner take the aims mentioned before to be the good, since at least people value them for their own sake — though they too fall short. Much has been said about them; let us leave them.

We had better also examine the notion of a single universal good, and ask what it could mean, even though the inquiry is awkward because the people who introduced this theory of Forms are friends of ours. Still, it is surely better — indeed a duty, where the truth is at stake — to give up even our own views, especially since we are lovers of wisdom. Both our friends and the truth are dear to us, but it is right to honor the truth first. Now those who put forward this theory did not posit Forms for classes where they spoke of before and after, which is why they did not make a Form of numbers. But good is spoken of in the category of substance, in quality, and in relation; and what exists in itself — substance — is by nature prior to what is relative, which is a kind of offshoot of being. So on their own principles there could be no single common Form covering all these.

Furthermore, good is spoken of in as many ways as being itself: in the category of substance (the god, or intellect), in quality (the virtues), in quantity (the right amount), in relation (the useful), in time (the right moment), in place (a habitation), and so on. So good plainly cannot be some single common thing, universal and one; otherwise it would not have been spoken of in every category but in one only. And third: since the things falling under a single Form are studied by a single science, there would be just one science of all goods together — whereas in fact there are many sciences even of the goods in a single category. The right moment, for instance, is studied in war by generalship, in illness by medicine; the right amount, in food by medicine, in exercise by athletics. One might also ask what on earth they mean by "the thing itself," since the account of humanity is one and the same in "man himself" and in any particular man. Insofar as both are man, they do not differ at all; and if so, then "good itself" and any particular good will not differ insofar as both are good. Nor is good made any more good by lasting forever, since something white for a long time is no whiter than something white for a day.

The Pythagoreans seem to give a more convincing account when they place "the One" in their column of goods, in their double list of goods and bads — and Speusippus seems to have followed them in this. But these matters belong to another occasion. There is an obvious objection to what I have said: the theory was not meant to apply to all goods, but only those pursued and valued for their own sake belong under one Form, while whatever tends to produce or preserve these, or to prevent their opposites, is called good only because of them, in a derivative sense. Clearly, then, things are called good in two ways: some in themselves, others for the sake of these. So let us separate the goods that are good in themselves from the merely useful ones, and ask whether the former are spoken of under a single Form. But which goods should we count as good in themselves? Should it be all those pursued even in isolation — like understanding, sight, certain pleasures and honors, which we do pursue with some further end in view but would still count among the goods worth having in themselves? Or is nothing good in itself except the Form, in which case the Form would amount to nothing?

If, on the other hand, these things are goods in themselves, then the account of what makes them good should be the same in all of them, as the account of whiteness is the same in snow and in white lead. But it is not: the accounts of what makes honor, understanding, and pleasure good are distinct and different. So the good is not some common thing answering to a single Form. Then how does the one name come to be shared, since it does not look like mere coincidence? Are different things called good because they all derive from one source, or all contribute to one end — or rather by analogy, so that as sight is to the body, intellect is to the soul, and so on? But perhaps we should drop these questions for now; pursuing them precisely belongs to another branch of philosophy. The same goes for the Form. Even if there is some single good predicated in common of all good things, or some good existing separately and on its own, it plainly could not be acted on or attained by a human being — and what we are looking for is exactly that. Someone might think it would still help to know the universal good as a model, so as to know better which goods are within our reach and, knowing them, to attain them. The argument has some plausibility, but it is contradicted by how the crafts actually work. Every craft aims at some good and seeks to supply what is missing, yet they get along without any knowledge of the good itself. It is hardly likely that all craftsmen alike should be ignorant of so great an aid and not even look for it. And it is hard to see how a weaver or a carpenter would be helped in his own work by knowing this good itself, or how someone would be a better healer or general for having gazed on the Form. The doctor does not even study health in this abstract way, but the health of human beings — or rather of this or that patient, since he treats individuals. So much for these matters.

Let us return to the good we are seeking. What can it be? It plainly differs from one action and craft to another: it is one thing in medicine, another in generalship, and so on. What is the good in each? Surely it is that for the sake of which everything else is done — health in medicine, victory in generalship, a house in building, and in each case something else; in every action and choice it is the end, since for the sake of this people do everything else. So if there is some single end of everything we do, this will be the good attainable by action; and if there are several, then these will be. The argument, after circling back, has reached the same point as before, and we must try to make it still clearer. The ends are evidently many, and some of them — wealth, flutes, instruments generally — we choose for the sake of something else; so plainly not all ends are complete. But the highest good is clearly something complete. So if there is only one complete end, that is what we are seeking; and if there are several, the most complete of them.

We call an end that is pursued for its own sake more complete than one pursued for the sake of something else; and we call an end never chosen for the sake of anything else more complete than ends that are chosen both in themselves and for the sake of that further end. So what is always chosen for itself and never for the sake of anything else is complete without qualification. Happiness above all seems to be of this kind, since we always choose it for itself and never for the sake of anything else. Honor, pleasure, intelligence, and every excellence we do choose for themselves — we would choose each even if nothing came of it — but we also choose them for the sake of happiness, supposing that through them we will be happy. No one, though, chooses happiness for the sake of these, or for the sake of anything else at all.

The same conclusion follows from the notion of self-sufficiency, which is thought to belong to the complete good. By self-sufficient we do not mean sufficient for a person living a solitary life, but for his parents and children and wife as well, and in general his friends and fellow citizens — since a human being is by nature a political animal, made for life in a community. Some limit must be set here, of course, for if we extend it to parents and descendants and friends of friends it will go on without end; but this we can leave for later. For now we take the self-sufficient to be that which, all by itself, makes life worth choosing and lacking in nothing. And such, we hold, is happiness. Moreover, we take happiness to be the most choiceworthy of all things — and not as one good counted alongside others. For if it were so counted, then adding even the smallest further good would plainly make it more choiceworthy than before; the addition would be that much more good, and of two goods the greater is always more choiceworthy. So happiness is plainly something complete and self-sufficient, the end of everything we do.

But to call happiness the highest good is perhaps to say something everyone accepts, and what we still want is a clearer account of what it actually is. We can get this by grasping the function of a human being. For a flute player, a sculptor, any craftsman — anyone, in general, who has a function and an activity — the good and the doing-well are thought to lie in that function. The same should hold for a human being, if there is a function that belongs to a human being as such. Are we to say that a carpenter and a cobbler have their functions and activities, but a human being as such has none and is left by nature with no work to do? Surely, just as the eye, the hand, the foot, and each part has its own function, we should suppose a human being too has some function beyond all of these. What could it be? Not merely being alive — that is shared even with plants, and we are looking for what is peculiar to us. So we set aside the life of nutrition and growth. Next would come the life of perception, but this too is shared, with horses and oxen and every animal. What remains is a life of action belonging to the part of us that has reason. Of this, one part has reason in the sense of obeying it, the other in the sense of having it and exercising thought. And since "the life of reason" itself can mean either the capacity or its actual exercise, we should take it as the active exercise, since that is more properly called living.

If, then, the function of a human being is an activity of the soul in accordance with reason, or not without reason; and if we say that the function of a given thing and of a good one of that kind are the same in type — a harpist and a good harpist, and so on across the board, adding to the function its excellence (the harpist's function is to play the harp, the good harpist's to play it well) — if all this holds, then we take the human good to be the function performed well: an activity of the soul in accordance with excellence, or virtue, and if there are several, in accordance with the best and most complete. And we must add: in a complete life. For one swallow does not make a spring, nor does one fine day; and in the same way one day, or a short time, does not make a person blessed and happy. Let this stand as a rough sketch of the good. Probably the right way is to draw the outline first and fill it in afterward, and anyone could carry forward and complete what has been well sketched; time is a good discoverer and collaborator in such things. That is how the arts have advanced — anyone can supply what is missing.

Remember, too, what was said before: do not demand the same exactness in everything, but in each thing the kind that suits its subject and is proper to the inquiry. A carpenter and a geometer ask about the straight line in different ways: the carpenter only so far as it serves his work, the geometer asking what it is and what its properties are, since he is after the truth. We should proceed the same way in other matters, so that side issues do not overgrow the main ones. And do not demand the reason why in everything alike; in some cases it is enough that the fact has been well established, as with first principles, where the fact comes first and is itself the starting point. Some first principles are grasped by induction, some by perception, some by a kind of habituation, others in other ways. We should try to approach each according to its nature and take pains to define them well, since they carry great weight for what follows. The starting point seems to be more than half the whole, and many of the things we are seeking come to light once it is clear.

We should examine happiness not only from our conclusion and the premises of our argument, but also in light of what people commonly say about it — for with the truth all the facts harmonize, while the false soon clashes with how things really are. Goods are commonly divided into three kinds: external goods, goods of the soul, and goods of the body, and we call the goods of the soul good in the fullest and truest sense. Now we take the actions and activities of the soul to be where happiness lies, and these belong to the soul. So our account agrees with this opinion, which is an old one and accepted by philosophers. We are also right to say that the end consists in certain actions and activities, since this puts it among the goods of the soul and not the external ones. Our account fits the common view that the happy person lives well and does well, since we have made happiness more or less a kind of living well and doing well.

All the things people look for in happiness seem to be present in our account. Some think happiness is virtue, some practical wisdom, some a kind of philosophic wisdom; some think it is these together with pleasure, or at least not without pleasure; others throw in external prosperity as well. Some of these views are held by many people or are very old, others by a few but distinguished men, and it is unlikely that either group is wholly wrong; more likely each is right on at least one point, or on most. Our account agrees with those who say happiness is virtue, or some virtue, since activity in accordance with virtue belongs to virtue. But there is an important difference between locating the good in a possession and locating it in a use — between a settled state and an activity. A state can be present in someone without producing any good — in a person asleep, say, or otherwise inactive — but an activity cannot, for it will necessarily act, and act well. And just as at the Olympic games it is not the finest and strongest men who are crowned but those who actually compete (the winners come from among them), so in life it is those who act rightly who carry off the noble and good things.

Their life is also pleasant in itself. Feeling pleasure is something internal to the soul, and each person finds pleasant the things he is said to love: a horse to the horse lover, a show to the lover of shows, and in the same way just acts to the lover of justice, and in general the things that accord with virtue to the lover of virtue. For most people the things they find pleasant conflict, because they are not pleasant by nature; but to those who love what is noble, the things pleasant by nature are pleasant — and actions in accordance with virtue are such, so that they are pleasant both to such people and in themselves. So the good person's life has no need of pleasure as some added ornament; it contains its pleasure within itself. For, besides what was just said, a person who takes no pleasure in noble actions is not even good — no one would call a person just who takes no pleasure in acting justly, or generous who takes none in generous acts, and so on. If that is right, then actions in accordance with virtue must be pleasant in themselves. They are also good and noble, and supremely so, if the good person judges rightly about them — and he judges as we have said.

So happiness is the best, the noblest, and the most pleasant thing, and these are not separated as in the inscription at Delos: "Justice is noblest, health is best, but to get what you long for is sweetest." For all three belong together in the best activities, and we say that happiness is these activities, or the one that is best. Still, happiness plainly also needs external goods, as we said. It is impossible, or at least not easy, to do noble actions without resources: many things are done by means of friends, wealth, and political power, as if by instruments. And the lack of some things spoils our blessedness — good birth, fine children, good looks. A person who is repulsive in appearance, or low-born, or solitary and childless is not really capable of happiness, and still less, perhaps, one whose children or friends are thoroughly bad, or who has lost good ones to death. So, as we said, happiness seems to need this kind of prosperity as well; which is why some identify it with good fortune, while others identify it with virtue.

This raises a question: is happiness something that can be learned, or acquired by habit or some other training, or does it come by some divine allotment, or even by chance? Now if the gods give anything to human beings, it is reasonable that happiness is a gift of theirs, especially since it is the best of human things. But this may belong to another inquiry. Even so, it is clear that even if happiness is not sent directly by the gods but comes through virtue and some kind of learning or training, it is still among the most godlike things; for the prize and end of virtue is plainly the best thing there is, something divine and blessed. On this view it would also be widely shared, since it could belong, through learning and effort, to anyone not crippled for virtue. And if it is better to be happy this way than by chance, that itself is a reason to think it is so, since whatever is by nature is naturally as fine as it can be — and likewise whatever is by art or by any cause, above all the best cause. To leave the greatest and noblest thing to chance would strike a badly false note. The answer is also clear from our definition: happiness is an activity of the soul in accordance with a certain kind of excellence. Of the other goods, some must be present as preconditions, and others are by nature useful as instruments.

This agrees with what we said at the start: we took the end of statecraft to be the best thing, and statecraft devotes its greatest care to making the citizens people of a certain kind — good, and apt to do what is noble. With good reason, then, we do not call an ox or a horse or any other animal happy, since none of them can take part in such activity. For the same reason a child is not happy either: because of his age he cannot yet do such things, and when we call a child happy we are predicting on the basis of his promise. For happiness requires, as we said, complete virtue and a complete life. Many reversals and all kinds of chances occur over a lifetime, and the most prosperous person may fall into great misfortunes in old age, as the heroic poems tell of Priam; and no one calls a person happy who has met such a fate and died in misery.

Are we then to call no one happy while he lives, and, as Solon advised, look to the end? And if we hold to that, is a person actually happy once he is dead? Or is that completely absurd, especially for us, who say happiness is a kind of activity? But suppose we do not call the dead person happy, and Solon did not mean that either, but only that one can safely pronounce a person happy once he is finally beyond evils and misfortunes — even this is open to dispute. For it is thought that something good or bad can reach a person who is dead, just as it can reach someone alive who is unaware of it: honor and dishonor, and the good or bad fortune of his children and descendants generally. This raises a further difficulty: a person may live in blessedness to old age and die accordingly, and yet many changes may come about through his descendants — some of them may flourish and get the life they deserve, others the reverse, and clearly they may stand in all sorts of relations to their ancestor. It would be strange for the dead person to shift along with them and become now happy, now wretched; but it would also be strange if the fortunes of descendants made no difference at all, for no time, to their ancestors.

But we should return to the earlier question, since its answer will settle the present one. If we are to look to the end and only then call a person blessed — not as being so but as having been so earlier — surely it is absurd that, at the very time when he is happy, the truth of it cannot be asserted, just because we are unwilling to call the living happy on account of their exposure to change, and because we have conceived of happiness as something stable and not easily altered, whereas fortune keeps revolving around the same people. For clearly, if we follow people's fortunes, we will often call the same person happy and a little later wretched, presenting our happy person as a kind of chameleon, built on no firm ground. Surely this is not right — to track our verdict to the swings of fortune. For our doing well or badly does not rest in fortune; human life merely needs it as an accessory, as we granted, while it is the activities in accordance with virtue that decide our happiness, and their opposites that decide the reverse.

And the question we just discussed bears witness to our account. For nothing in human affairs has the stability of the activities in accordance with virtue; they are thought to be more lasting even than the sciences, and the most valued of them are the most lasting, because the blessed live in them most fully and most continuously — which seems to be why such activities are not forgotten. So the happy person will have the stability we are after, and will keep it throughout his life. He will always, or above all, be doing and contemplating the things that accord with virtue, and he will bear the chances of life most nobly and gracefully in every way, being truly good and "foursquare without reproach." Many things happen by chance, differing in size; the small strokes of good or bad fortune plainly do not tip the balance of a life, but the great and repeated ones, if they go well, will make life more blessed, since by their nature they add grace and the use of them is noble and good; and if they go badly, they crush and maim our blessedness, bringing pain and obstructing many activities. Yet even here nobility shines through, when a person bears many great misfortunes with good grace — not from any dullness to pain, but because he is noble and great-souled.

And if the activities are what govern a life, as we said, then no one among the blessed can become wretched, since he will never do what is hateful and base. The truly good and sensible person, we believe, bears all his fortunes becomingly and always does the noblest thing the circumstances allow — just as a good general makes the best use of the army he has, and a good cobbler makes the finest shoe from the leather given him, and so with every other craftsman. If that is right, the happy person can never become wretched — though he will not be blessed, of course, if he meets a fate like Priam's. Nor is he unsteady and easily turned. He will not be dislodged from his happiness lightly, nor by ordinary misfortunes, but only by great and repeated ones; and after such blows he will not recover his happiness in a short time, but, if at all, over a long and complete stretch in which he has won great and noble things. Why, then, should we not call happy the person who is active in accordance with complete virtue and is adequately furnished with external goods — not for some passing period, but over a complete life? Or should we add that he must also go on living so and die accordingly, since the future is hidden from us, and we take happiness to be in every way an end and complete? If so, we will call those among the living blessed who have and will have the things we have described — blessed, but as human beings are blessed.

Let this much settle these points. Now to hold that the fortunes of descendants and of friends in general contribute nothing at all to the condition of the dead seems too cold a view, and against what people commonly believe. But since the things that happen are many and differ in all sorts of ways, some touching us nearly and others lightly, to go into every distinction would be a long and endless task; it is enough to speak in outline. As with a person's own misfortunes — some have weight and turn the balance of a life, others are lighter — so it is with those that befall his friends. And it makes a great difference whether those affected are alive or dead, far more than whether the crimes and horrors in a tragedy are merely assumed in the background or actually carried out before us. We should take this difference into account, and still more, perhaps, the open question whether the dead really share in any good or evil at all. From all this it seems that if anything does reach them, good or bad, it must be something faint and small, either in itself or to them — at any rate not great or strong enough to make happy those who are not, or to take away the blessedness of those who are. So the good or bad fortunes of friends do touch the dead somewhat, but not in such a kind or degree as to turn the happy unhappy, or to do anything like that.

With these points settled, let us examine whether happiness belongs among the things we praise or among the things we honor as precious — for it plainly is not a mere capacity. Whatever we praise, we praise for being of a certain kind and standing in a certain relation to something else. We praise the just person, the brave person, the good person generally, and virtue itself, because of their actions and results; and we praise the strong man and the fast runner for being of a certain nature and standing in a certain relation to something good and fine. This is clear from the praise of the gods, which comes out ridiculous when they are measured against our standard — and that happens because all praise, as we said, involves reference to a standard. But if praise is for such things, then what applies to the best things is not praise but something higher and better. And so it is: we call the gods blessed and happy, and we call those people blessed who come nearest the gods. The same holds for goods: no one praises happiness the way he praises justice; he calls it blessed, as something more godlike and better.

Eudoxus, too, seems to have argued well for pleasure's claim to the highest prize. The fact that pleasure, though one of the goods, is not praised he took as a sign that it is superior to the things that are praised — a superiority he assigned also to a god and to the good, since these are the standard to which everything else is referred. For praise belongs to virtue, since virtue makes us apt to do noble things, while celebration in words belongs to definite achievements of body or mind. But pursuing this precisely belongs more to a treatise on praise; for us it is enough that what has been said shows happiness to belong among the things that are precious and complete. And it seems to be so also because it is a starting point: it is for the sake of happiness that we all do everything else, and we take the source and cause of good things to be something precious and divine.

Since happiness is an activity of the soul in accordance with complete excellence, we should examine excellence; for that will probably give us a clearer view of happiness as well. The true statesman, moreover, is thought to have taken the most pains over this, since he wants to make the citizens good and obedient to the laws — the lawgivers of the Cretans and the Spartans are examples, and any others like them. If this inquiry belongs to statecraft, then clearly pursuing it is in keeping with our original plan. The excellence we are to examine is, of course, human excellence, since the good and the happiness we were seeking were a human being's. And by human excellence we mean not that of the body but that of the soul, since we say happiness is an activity of the soul. If so, the statesman plainly must know something about the soul, just as the eye doctor must know about the body as a whole — and all the more, since statecraft is more precious and higher than medicine; indeed the better doctors take great pains over knowledge of the body. So the statesman too must study the soul, but with his own ends in view and only so far as his inquiry requires; to carry the study to greater precision is more laborious than his purpose demands.

A few things have been said about the soul well enough in popular discussions, and we may use them here: that the soul has two parts, one without reason and one having reason. (Whether these are actually separate, like the parts of the body and everything divisible, or are two only in account while being by nature inseparable, like the convex and concave of a curve, makes no difference for our purpose.) Of the part without reason, one element seems shared with all living things and is vegetative — the cause of nutrition and growth; one would assign such a power of the soul to everything that takes nourishment, even to embryos, and the same power to full-grown creatures, which is more likely than that it should be a different one. The excellence of this part is plainly not peculiar to human beings but shared; for this part and this power are thought to be most active in sleep, when the good person and the bad are least distinguishable — whence the saying that for half their lives the happy are no different from the wretched. And reasonably so, since sleep is an inactivity of the soul in the respect in which it is called good or bad — except that to some small degree some of its movements get through, so that the dreams of decent people are better than those of ordinary ones. But enough of this; we may set the nutritive part aside, since by its nature it has no share in human excellence.

There seems to be another part of the soul without reason, which nonetheless shares in reason in a way. In the self-controlled person and in the one who lacks self-control alike, we praise the reasoning part of the soul, the part that has reason, because it urges them rightly toward the best. But there is plainly in them, alongside reason, something else by nature, which fights and strains against reason. Just as paralyzed limbs, when a person means to move them to the right, swerve off to the left, so it is with the soul: the impulses of people who lack self-control run counter to reason. The difference is that in the body we see the part that swerves, while in the soul we do not. Still, we should suppose there is in the soul too something besides reason, opposing it and running against it — how it is distinct does not matter here. This part, too, plainly shares in reason, as we said: in the self-controlled person it obeys reason, and in the person of real temperance and in the brave person it is still more obedient, since in them it speaks entirely with the same voice as reason.

So the part without reason is itself twofold. The vegetative part has no share in reason at all; but the appetitive part, desire in general, shares in it in a way, insofar as it listens to reason and can submit to its rule. This is the sense in which we say a person "listens to reason" from a father or friends — not the sense in which we speak of the reason, the account, of a mathematical truth. That the part without reason is in some way won over by reason is shown by correction and by every kind of rebuke and encouragement. And if we are to say this part too has reason, then the part that has reason will itself be twofold: one part has reason strictly and in itself, the other has it in the way a child heeds its father. Excellence, too, is divided along this line. We call some excellences intellectual and others of character: philosophic wisdom, understanding, and practical wisdom are intellectual; generosity and temperance are excellences of character. When we speak of a person's character we do not call him wise or quick-witted, but even-tempered or temperate; yet we do praise the wise person too, for his state of mind — and the praiseworthy states are the ones we call excellences.
