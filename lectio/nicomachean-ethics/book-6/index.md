# Nicomachean Ethics, Book VI

Practical Wisdom

We have already said that one should choose the mean, and avoid both the excess and the deficiency, and that the mean is fixed by right reason. It is time to spell out what right reason is. In every settled state of character we have described, and in all the others too, there is a kind of target the person who has reason keeps his eye on as he tightens or slackens his effort; and there is a standard that marks off the mean states, which lie between an excess and a deficiency and accord with right reason. To say this much is true, but it is not yet definite enough. In any field that calls for care and method — anywhere skill or knowledge is brought to bear — it is true to say that we should neither work nor rest too much or too little, but in the right measure, as right reason directs. Yet a person who had only this would be none the wiser, the way he would be none the wiser if he asked what to apply to his body and were told, "Whatever medicine prescribes, and in the way the doctor directs." So with the states of the soul: it is not enough to say truly that the mean lies where right reason says it does; we have to lay down what right reason actually is, and how to define it.

When we sorted the excellences of the soul, we said there are two kinds: those of character and those of the intellect. We have gone through the excellences of character; now we turn to the intellectual ones, after a few words about the soul itself. We said before that the soul has two parts, one that has reason and one that does not. Now we must divide the part that has reason in the same way. Let us suppose it has two parts: one by which we grasp the kind of things whose first causes cannot be otherwise than they are, and one by which we grasp the kind of things that can be otherwise. For when objects differ in kind, the parts of the soul that are naturally suited to each must differ in kind too, since it is by some likeness and kinship to their objects that these parts know them. Call the first part the part that knows, and the second the part that calculates — for to deliberate and to calculate are the same thing, and no one deliberates about what cannot be otherwise. So the calculating part is one part of the soul that has reason. We have to find the best state of each of these two parts, for that best state is the excellence of each — and the excellence of each is relative to the work it has to do.

There are three things in the soul that govern action and truth: perception, intellect, and desire. Perception originates no action; we can see this from the fact that animals have perception but take no part in action. What affirmation and denial are in thought, pursuit and avoidance are in desire. So, since excellence of character is a settled state that issues in choice, and choice is desire that has been deliberated, the reasoning must be true and the desire right if the choice is to be good, and what reason affirms the desire must pursue. This is thinking and truth that bear on action. For the part of the intellect that only contemplates, and is concerned with neither action nor production, doing well or badly is just hitting the truth or missing it — that is the work of any intellectual faculty. But for the part that is both practical and intellectual, doing well is reaching the truth that answers to right desire. Now the origin of action — the thing that sets it going, not the goal it aims at — is choice, and the origin of choice is desire together with reasoning aimed at some end. This is why there is no choice without intellect and thought, and none without a certain moral character, since acting well and its opposite require both thought and character. Thought by itself moves nothing; only thought aimed at some end and bearing on action moves anything. (Thought that governs production is a separate matter, since everyone who makes something makes it for the sake of something further; what is made is not an end in itself but only a means to something else and for someone. What is done, though, is an end in itself, since acting well is itself the end, and that is what desire reaches for.) So choice is either desiring thought or thinking desire, and a being capable of that origin is a human being. Nothing already done and past can be chosen; no one chooses to have sacked Troy. For no one deliberates about the past, only about what is still to come and can be otherwise, whereas what has happened cannot be made not to have happened. Agathon was right: "This alone is denied even to a god — to make undone whatever has been done." So truth is the work of both intellectual parts of the soul, and the excellence of each is the state in which it best reaches the truth.

Let us start again, then, and speak of these excellences. Suppose there are five states by which the soul reaches truth, in affirming or denying — five and no more: craft, scientific knowledge, practical wisdom, philosophic wisdom, and intuitive understanding. (Judgment by supposition and opinion are left out, since by them we can be wrong.) What scientific knowledge is becomes clear if we are exact about it and not misled by loose resemblances. We all assume that what we know in the strict sense cannot be otherwise than it is; with things that can be otherwise, the moment they pass out of our sight we cannot tell whether they still hold or not. So the object of scientific knowledge holds of necessity, and is therefore eternal, since whatever exists of strict necessity is eternal — and eternal things have no beginning and no end. Again, all scientific knowledge can be taught, and what it covers can be learned. And all teaching starts from what is already known, as we also say in the Analytics — sometimes by working out the consequences (deduction), sometimes by gathering the universal from instances (induction). Induction is the source of first universals, while deduction reasons from them. So there are starting points that deduction reasons from but cannot itself prove, and these are taken in by induction. Scientific knowledge, then, is a settled capacity to demonstrate — with all the further marks we set out in the Analytics. A person knows in the strict sense when he is confident in a certain way and the starting points are clear to him. If they are not clearer to him than his conclusion, his knowledge will be only incidental. Let that stand as our account of scientific knowledge.

What can be otherwise than it is comes in two kinds: things to be made, and things to be done. Making and doing are different (we have shown this elsewhere), so the reasoned state apt for doing is distinct from the reasoned state apt for making, and neither falls under the other — doing is not making, and making is not doing. Since building is a craft, and is essentially a reasoned state apt for making, and since there is no craft that is not such a state and no such state that is not a craft, craft is the same as a state apt for making that involves true reasoning. Every craft is concerned with bringing something into being — with the know-how of making some thing that may or may not come to be, and whose origin lies in the maker and not in the thing made. Craft has nothing to do with things that are or come to be by necessity, or with things that arise by nature, since these have their origin in themselves. And since making and doing are different, craft belongs to making, not to doing. In a way craft and luck deal with the same matters, as Agathon says: "Craft loves luck, and luck loves craft." So craft, as we said, is a settled state, conjoined with true reason, that is apt for making; and its opposite, lack of craft, is a settled state conjoined with false reasoning — both bearing on what can be otherwise.

We can get at the nature of practical wisdom by looking at the kind of person we call practically wise. It seems to belong to the practically wise person to be able to deliberate well about what is good and useful for himself — not in some narrow line, like what makes for health or strength, but about what makes for living well as a whole. A sign of this is that we call people wise about some particular thing when they calculate well toward some worthwhile end in a matter where there is no fixed rule. So, broadly, the person who is good at deliberating will be practically wise. But no one deliberates about what cannot be otherwise, or about things that are not within the reach of his own action. So, since scientific knowledge requires strict demonstration, and the matters practical wisdom deals with admit of being otherwise — for all matters of deliberation can be otherwise, and there is no deliberating about what holds by necessity — practical wisdom is neither scientific knowledge nor craft. Not knowledge, because what is done can be otherwise; not craft, because doing and making differ in kind. What is left is that it is a true and reasoned state apt for action, concerned with what is good and bad for a human being. For the end of making is always something beyond the making, but the end of doing cannot be, since acting well is itself the end. This is why we count Pericles and people like him as practically wise: they can see what is good for themselves and for people in general — and that, we think, is the mark of someone able to run a household or a city. It is also why temperance has the name it does in Greek, a word that means "what preserves practical wisdom" — for what temperance preserves is exactly this kind of judgment about one's own true good. Pleasure and pain do not destroy or distort every judgment — not the judgment that the angles of a triangle add up to two right angles — but only judgments about action. For the starting points of action are the ends they are done for, and to a person corrupted by pleasure or pain the starting point at once goes dark: he no longer sees that he should choose and do everything for the sake of that end and because of it. Vice destroys the starting point. So practical wisdom must be a true and reasoned state apt for action, having to do with what is good for a human being.

There is more. A craft can be done better or worse, but practical wisdom admits of no such degrees. And in a craft, a person who goes wrong on purpose is the better craftsman; in practical wisdom, as in the excellences of character, he is the worse. So plainly practical wisdom is an excellence of a certain kind and not a craft. Of the two parts of the soul that have reason, it must be the excellence of the part that forms opinion — the part we earlier called calculating or deliberative — since opinion, like practical wisdom, deals with what can be otherwise. And it is not merely a reasoned state, as is shown by this: a reasoned state of that kind can be forgotten, while practical wisdom cannot.

Scientific knowledge is a grasp of universals and of what holds by necessity, and every chain of demonstration, and so all such knowledge, rests on first principles. The faculty that takes in the first principles of what falls under scientific knowledge cannot itself be scientific knowledge, or craft, or practical wisdom. Not scientific knowledge, since the object of knowledge is reached by demonstration; not either of the other two, since they deal only with what can be otherwise. Nor can it be philosophic wisdom, since the wise person must in some matters rely on demonstration. So if the states by which we reach truth and are never deceived, in dealing with what is necessary or even with what can be otherwise, are scientific knowledge, practical wisdom, philosophic wisdom, and intuitive understanding — and the first three cannot grasp first principles — then it must be intuitive understanding that does so.

We use the word for philosophic wisdom mainly in two ways. First, in the crafts, we ascribe it to those who have carried their craft to the highest finish — we call Phidias a wise sculptor, Polyclitus a wise statue-maker, meaning by "wisdom" here nothing but excellence in a craft. Second, we think some people wise in general, not in any one line or about any one thing, the way Homer says in the Margites: "The gods had made him neither a digger nor a plowman, nor wise in anything else." So it is clear that philosophic wisdom must be the most exact form of knowledge. The wise person must not only know what follows from first principles but also have the truth about the first principles themselves. So philosophic wisdom is intuitive understanding combined with scientific knowledge — knowledge, with its head on, of the highest things. I say the highest things, because it would be absurd to suppose that statecraft, or practical wisdom, is the best kind of knowledge, unless a human being were the best thing in the universe. Now "healthy" and "good" mean different things for people and for fish, but "white" and "straight" are always the same; so everyone would grant that wisdom is always the same, while what counts as practical wisdom shifts. For we give the name to whatever looks well after its own interest — which is why people even call some animals practically wise, the ones that plainly have a power of foresight about their own survival. And it is plain that philosophic wisdom and statecraft cannot be the same thing. If we called the faculty that looks after our own interest "wisdom," there would be many wisdoms, one for each kind of creature, since there is no single faculty that looks after the good of all animals at once — any more than there is one art of medicine for every living thing. It is no answer to say that human beings are superior to the other animals, since there are many things more divine in their nature than a human being — the heavenly bodies that make up the universe, most obviously. So philosophic wisdom is intuitive understanding and scientific knowledge together, trained on the highest things by nature. This is why people call Anaxagoras, Thales, and men of that stamp wise, but not practically wise: they can see them ignorant of their own good, and they say that what these men know is extraordinary, marvelous, hard, and even superhuman, but useless — because it is not the human good they are after.

Practical wisdom, by contrast, is concerned with human affairs and with what can be deliberated about. We say the chief work of the practically wise person is to deliberate well, and no one deliberates about what cannot be otherwise, or about anything that does not lead to an end — an end that is some good attainable by action. The person who is good at deliberation without qualification is the one who, by calculation, can hit on the best of the goods attainable by action for a human being. Nor does practical wisdom deal with universals alone; it must also recognize particulars, since it is concerned with action, and action is about particulars. This is why some people who lack the general knowledge are more effective in action than others who have it — above all those who go by experience. If a man knows that light meats are easy to digest and healthy, but not which meats are light, he will not produce health; the man who knows that chicken is light and healthy is far likelier to. Since practical wisdom is concerned with action, it should have both kinds of knowledge — or, if only one, the knowledge of particulars rather than of universals. Still, here too there will be a governing kind and a subordinate kind. Statecraft and practical wisdom are the same state of mind, but they are not the same in their point of view. Of practical wisdom exercised over a city, the master form is legislation; the subordinate form, dealing with particulars, keeps the common name "statecraft," and its business is action and deliberation, since a decree is something to be acted on, the last step in this kind of deliberation. That is why people say only these men are really governing, since they alone act — standing to the lawgivers as workmen do to a master builder. But practical wisdom is thought to be most properly the kind concerned with oneself, the individual, and this kind takes over the common name; the others are called household management, legislation, and civic government, the last divided into the deliberative and the judicial. Now knowing one's own good is one kind of knowledge, but it varies widely; and people commonly think the man who knows and minds his own affairs is practically wise, while those who busy themselves with the public are meddlers. Euripides puts the sentiment in a character's mouth: "How am I wise, who might have lived at ease, an idle number in the ranks, sharing the common lot? For Zeus hates the busy and the over-clever." People generally seek their own good and think that is their proper business — and from this opinion comes the idea that such men are the practically wise. Yet it may well be that a person's own good cannot be secured apart from his household and his city. And how a man should manage even his own affairs is sometimes far from clear, and has to be worked out.

What I have said is borne out by the fact that the young can become geometers and mathematicians, wise in such matters, but are not thought capable of practical wisdom. The reason is that practical wisdom is concerned with particulars, which become known through experience, and a young person has no experience, since experience takes time to acquire. One might also ask why a boy can become a mathematician but not a natural scientist or a philosopher. Is it not because mathematics works by abstraction, while the starting points of those other fields come from experience? The young can repeat the words of natural science but do not really grasp them, whereas the objects of mathematics are clear and plain. Again, in matters of action, error can lie in the general rule, in the course of deliberation, or in the particular fact — for instance, the general rule "all heavy water is bad," or the particular fact "this water is heavy." And it is clear that practical wisdom is not scientific knowledge, since it bears on the last particular, as we said, which is the object of action. It stands opposite to intuitive understanding, which takes in the principles that no reasoning can prove, while practical wisdom is concerned with the last particular, which no scientific knowledge can grasp but only perception — not perception by one of the five senses, but the same kind by which we perceive that the last figure in geometry is the triangle, where too we have to stop. But that is perception more than practical wisdom, or perception of a different sort.

Inquiring and deliberating are different, though deliberating is a kind of inquiring. We should work out what good deliberation is — whether a kind of scientific knowledge, or opinion, or a knack for a lucky guess, or something else again. It is not scientific knowledge, since people do not inquire about what they already know, and good deliberation is a kind of deliberation, and the person deliberating is inquiring and calculating. Nor is it a lucky guess, since a lucky guess comes without reasoning and in a flash, while people deliberate a long time — as the saying goes, you should carry out quickly what you have resolved on, but deliberate slowly. Quick wit is yet another faculty, a species of lucky guess, and good deliberation is not opinion of any kind. Since the person who deliberates badly goes wrong and the one who deliberates well does it rightly, good deliberation is plainly a kind of rightness — but not rightness of knowledge, nor of opinion. Knowledge cannot be called right, since it cannot be wrong; and rightness of opinion is just truth, and what opinion is about is already settled and definite. Yet good deliberation is not without reason. So it must be rightness of thinking, since thinking is not yet an assertion. Opinion is not an inquiry but an assertion already made, while the person deliberating, whether well or badly, is still inquiring and calculating. Good deliberation is a kind of rightness in deliberation, so we have to start from what deliberation is and what it is about. Now "rightness" has several senses, and we plainly do not mean every kind. The person who lacks self-control, or the bad one, will by calculation reach what he set out to reach, so in a sense he has deliberated rightly — but what he gets is a great evil. To have deliberated well, though, seems to be a good thing, since the rightness of deliberation that counts as good deliberation is the kind that hits on something good. But even this can be reached by false reasoning — you can land on the right result through the wrong means, by a faulty middle term — so this is still not good deliberation, getting what you should but not by the proper route. Again, one man reaches a result after long deliberation, another quickly, and that quickness is not yet good deliberation either; good deliberation is rightness measured against what is beneficial, reaching the right end, in the right way, at the right time. Finally, one can deliberate well in general or toward a particular end. Good deliberation in general is the kind that goes right toward the end without qualification; good deliberation in a particular case is the kind that goes right toward a particular end. So, since deliberating well is the mark of the practically wise, good deliberation is rightness about what serves the end that practical wisdom truly grasps.

There is also the faculty of understanding, and its absence, by which we call people men of understanding or not. Understanding is not the same as scientific knowledge or opinion in general — if it were, everyone would have it — nor is it any one special science, like medicine, which is about health, or geometry, which is about magnitudes. It is not about what always exists and cannot change, nor about whatever happens to come to be, but about the things a person might be in doubt about and deliberate over. So it deals with the same matters as practical wisdom; yet the two are not the same. Practical wisdom issues commands — its end is what one should do or not do — while understanding only judges. (When we put "good" in front of it in Greek, "good understanding" means exactly the same as plain "understanding.") Understanding is neither the having of practical wisdom nor the getting of it. Just as we use the word for "grasping" when a learner puts his knowledge to use, so understanding is using the opinion-forming part of the soul to judge the matters practical wisdom deals with, when someone else states them — and judging not just any way, but judging well. The faculty we call good judgment, by which we call people sympathetic and fair-minded, is the correct discernment of the decent person. A sign of this is that we say the decent person above all is one who makes allowances, and making allowances in some cases is decent. Indeed the word for forgiveness is sympathetic judgment that discerns rightly what is fair — and by "rightly" I mean the judgment of a truthful person.

All these states naturally point in one direction, just as ordinary speech leads us to expect. We speak of good judgment, understanding, practical wisdom, and intuitive understanding, and we credit the same people with good judgment and intuitive understanding whom we call practically wise and men of understanding. For all these faculties are concerned with the last things — with particulars. A person has understanding, and good judgment, in virtue of being able to decide the matters that fall to the practically wise, since fairness is common to all good people in their dealings with others. And all matters of action belong to the particulars, the last things; the practically wise person has to recognize them, and understanding and good judgment are likewise about actions, which are particulars. Intuitive understanding takes in the last things at both ends, since both the first terms and the last are grasped by intuitive understanding, not by reasoning. In demonstration, intuitive understanding takes in the unchanging first terms; in action, it takes in the last term, the one that can be otherwise — the minor premise. These are the starting points of the end aimed at, for the universal is built up out of particulars. To grasp these we must have perception, and that perception is intuitive understanding. This is why these states are thought to be natural gifts. No one is held to be wise by nature, but people are held to have good judgment, understanding, and intuitive understanding by nature — a sign of which is that we think they come with certain times of life, and that a given age brings intuitive understanding and good judgment, as if nature were the cause. So intuitive understanding is both a beginning and an end, since demonstrations start from these and are about these. We should therefore pay as much attention to the undemonstrated sayings and opinions of experienced and older people, and of the practically wise, as to demonstrations — for experience has given them an eye to see rightly.

We have now said what practical wisdom and philosophic wisdom are, what each is concerned with, and that each is the excellence of a different part of the soul. But someone might ask what use they are. Philosophic wisdom, he could say, studies none of the things that make a human being happy, since it produces nothing. Practical wisdom does have that to recommend it — but why do we need it? Its province is what is just and noble and good for a human being, and these are the things the good person does as a matter of course; we are no more apt to do them for knowing them, since the excellences of character are settled states — just as we are no healthier or fitter for knowing what medicine and training say, since these terms describe what shows health, not what produces it. And if we say it is by becoming good, not by knowing what is good, that one becomes practically wise, then practical wisdom will be no use even to those who are already good, and none to those who are not — since it makes no difference whether they have it themselves or follow others who have it. We might be content to do here as we do with health: we want to be healthy, but we do not therefore set about learning medicine. On top of this, it would be strange if practical wisdom, lower in rank than philosophic wisdom, were to govern it — and govern it it does, since whatever produces a result rules and directs in its own field. These are the questions we must now take up; so far we have only raised them.

First, then, we say that both of these are choiceworthy in themselves, simply because each is the excellence of a part of the soul — even if neither produced any result at all. Next, they do in fact produce a result: philosophic wisdom produces happiness, not the way medicine produces health, but the way health itself does. For wisdom is a part of excellence as a whole, and by being possessed and exercised it makes a person happy. Further, a human being's work is fully done in virtue of practical wisdom together with excellence of character: excellence of character makes the aim right, and practical wisdom makes the means to it right. (The fourth part of the soul, the merely nutritive one, has no such excellence, since nothing is in its power to do or leave undone.) As for our not being more apt to do noble and just things by having practical wisdom, we must go a little further back and take this as our starting point. We say that people can do things that are in themselves just without being just — when they do what the law requires unwillingly, or out of ignorance, or for some other motive, and not for the sake of the things themselves — and yet they do what they ought, all that the good person should. So it seems that to be a good person one must do each act in a certain frame of mind: from choice, and for the sake of the acts themselves. Now it is excellence of character that makes the choice right, but carrying out whatever that choice naturally requires belongs not to excellence but to another faculty. We must pause and speak about this more plainly.

There is a faculty people call cleverness, of such a nature that it can do whatever serves a given aim and hit it. If the aim is noble, the cleverness is praiseworthy; if it is base, the same faculty is mere unscrupulousness. This is why we call both the practically wise and the unscrupulous clever. Practical wisdom is not the same as this faculty, but it does not exist without it. This eye of the soul does not reach its proper state without excellence of character, as we have said and as is plain. For the reasonings that govern action have a starting point of the form "since the end, the chief good, is such-and-such" — fill in the blank with whatever you like, since we only want to show the shape — and what should fill that blank is seen only by the good person, because vice distorts the moral vision and deceives us about the starting points of action. So it is clear that a person cannot be practically wise without being good.

We must look again, then, at excellence of character, since here too there is a close parallel: just as cleverness stands to practical wisdom, so natural virtue stands to full, mature virtue — a relation of resemblance, not identity. People think each of the moral dispositions belongs to us somehow from birth: we are inclined toward justice, temperance, and courage straight from the start. Yet we look for goodness in the full sense as something beyond these, and we expect these dispositions to belong to us in a different way. Children and even animals have these natural states, but without intelligence they are plainly harmful. We can see this much by experience: a powerful body deprived of sight stumbles badly when it moves, just because it cannot see, and it is the same here. But once intelligence is added, it transforms the way a person acts; and the disposition, which only resembled virtue before, will then be virtue in the full sense. So just as the opinion-forming part of the soul has two forms, cleverness and practical wisdom, the part concerned with character has two, natural virtue and full virtue — and the second of these cannot be formed without practical wisdom. This is why some say that all the virtues are just forms of practical wisdom. Socrates was partly right and partly wrong: wrong to think the virtues simply were practical wisdom, but right that they do not exist without it. A sign of this is that everyone now, in defining virtue, after naming the state and what it is about, adds "in accordance with right reason" — and right reason is the one that accords with practical wisdom. So everyone seems to sense that the state in accordance with practical wisdom is virtue. But we must change their formula slightly: virtue is the state that not merely accords with right reason but actually involves it — and right reason in such matters is practical wisdom. Socrates thought the virtues were forms of reasoning, since he took them all to be knowledge; we say they involve reason. From all this it is clear that one cannot be good in the strict sense without practical wisdom, nor practically wise without goodness of character.

This also answers the argument that the virtues can be had separately, on the grounds that one and the same person is not by nature equally disposed to all of them at once, so that he will have acquired one before another. With the natural virtues this is possible; but with the virtues that make a person good without qualification it is not, since they all come together with the single faculty of practical wisdom. It is plain, too, that even if practical wisdom were not concerned with action, we would still need it, because it is the excellence of a part of the soul — and that no choice can be right without practical wisdom and goodness of character, since goodness of character supplies the right end, and practical wisdom makes us do what leads to it. And practical wisdom is not the master of philosophic wisdom, the higher part of the soul — any more than medicine is the master of health. Medicine does not use health but looks to how it may come about; it gives orders for the sake of health, not to health. To say otherwise would be like saying that statecraft governs the gods because it gives orders about everything in the city.
