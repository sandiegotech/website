Nicomachean Ethics, Book III

Choice, Courage, and Temperance

Book III3 of 10

Virtue has to do with how we manage our feelings and our actions, and praise and blame attach to actions that are voluntary, while for the involuntary we make allowances and sometimes even feel pity. So anyone trying to understand virtue has to draw the line between what is voluntary and what is not. The distinction matters for lawmakers too, when they decide whom to honor and whom to punish.

Involuntary actions seem to come in two kinds: those done under compulsion, and those done out of ignorance. An action is compulsory, strictly speaking, when its source lies outside the agent and the agent contributes nothing to it — better, the person undergoes it rather than does it. Picture a wind carrying you off somewhere, or men who have you in their power hauling you along. But there are harder cases. Suppose you do something out of fear of something worse, or for some noble reason — say a tyrant who holds your parents and children orders you to do something shameful, and they live if you comply and die if you refuse. Here it is genuinely open to question whether the act is voluntary or involuntary. The same goes for jettisoning cargo in a storm: in the abstract, no one throws away his own property; but to save himself and everyone aboard, anyone with sense would.

The truth is that such acts are mixed, but they are closer to voluntary than to involuntary. At the moment they are done, they are chosen, and the point of an action has to be judged against the actual circumstances. We should call an act voluntary or involuntary by reference to the moment of acting. And in these cases the person acts voluntarily, because the source of the movement — the working of his own limbs — lies within him; and where the source is in him, it is up to him whether to act or not. So such acts are voluntary, even if, considered in the abstract, they are involuntary, since no one would choose any such thing for its own sake. For acts like these people are sometimes even praised, when they put up with something disgraceful or painful to win something great and noble. But if it is the other way around — disgrace endured for nothing noble, or for some trifle — they are blamed, since only a low character endures real disgrace for no noble end. And for some acts there is no praise, only forgiveness: when a person does what he should not because the pressure overstrains human nature and goes past what anyone could bear.

But some acts, perhaps, can never be excused by compulsion; a person should die first rather than do them. The pleas of compulsion that Euripides puts in the mouth of Alcmaeon, to excuse killing his own mother, are simply absurd. Still, it is sometimes hard to judge what should be chosen at what cost, and what endured to avoid what, and harder still to stand by the judgment once made — because usually the alternatives are painful and the act demanded is base, and so we get praise or blame depending on whether a person gave in or held out.

So which actions should we call compulsory? Simply those whose cause is external and to which the agent contributes nothing. As for actions that are in themselves the kind no one would want, but are chosen here and now over some worse alternative, and whose source lies in the agent — these are involuntary in the abstract but voluntary here and now, in preference to the alternative. And they are more like voluntary actions, because action is made of particular concrete steps, and those steps are voluntary. (Exactly what should be chosen over what is not easy to lay down, since the particular cases differ so much.)

Someone might object that pleasant and noble things compel us too, since they are external and they do pull us; but then every action would be compulsory, since these are what move all of us in everything we do. That is wrong. People who act under real compulsion, against their will, act with pain; people who act for the sake of something pleasant or noble act with pleasure. It is absurd to blame the outside thing instead of yourself for being so easily hooked by it — or to take credit for the noble acts and pin the base ones on pleasure. So compulsion is this: when the source is outside, and the person compelled adds nothing.

Now, not everything done out of ignorance is, strictly, involuntary. An act is involuntary only when it is followed by pain and regret. Someone who did something out of ignorance but is not bothered by it did not act with his will, since he did not know what he was doing — but he did not act against it either, since he is not sorry. So within the class of those who act out of ignorance, the one who regrets it afterward we count as having acted involuntarily; the one who feels nothing, since his case is plainly different, we will call a non-voluntary agent. The two deserve different names because they really are different.

There is also a difference between acting because of ignorance and acting in a state of ignorance. A man who is drunk or angry is thought to act not because of ignorance but because of his drink or his anger; yet he acts not knowingly, but in ignorance. And of course every bad person is ignorant of what he ought to do and avoid — that very failure is what makes people unjust and worthless. But we do not call an act involuntary just because someone was ignorant of what was good for him. Ignorance in our choices does not make us excusable; it makes us depraved. Nor does ignorance of the general principle excuse us, since for that we are blamed. What excuses is ignorance of the particulars — the specific circumstances the action is made of and concerns. These are where pity and forgiveness come in, because the person who is ignorant of one of them is acting involuntarily in the full sense.

It may help, then, to spell out these particulars: who is acting, what he is doing, about what or in what, sometimes with what (an instrument, say), to what end (rescuing someone, for example), and in what manner (gently or hard). No one in his right mind could be ignorant of every one of these — least of all of who is acting, since that is himself. But what he is doing he can be ignorant of, as when people say something "slipped out," or as Aeschylus said of the Mysteries, that he had not known it was forbidden to speak of them; or as in that recent catapult accident, where the man said he was only demonstrating how it works. Or someone might take his own son for an enemy, as Merope did; or think the pointed spear had a button on it, or that the stone was pumice; or kill a man with a drink meant to save him; or land a real blow while only meaning, as in sparring, to show someone a move. Since ignorance is possible about any of these particulars that make up the action, the person ignorant of one of them is held to have acted involuntarily — and most clearly so when he was ignorant of the most important ones, which are taken to be the circumstances of the act and its result. But for ignorance to make an act involuntary in this way, the act must also be followed by distress and regret.

Since an involuntary action is one done under compulsion or out of ignorance, a voluntary action looks to be one whose source is in the agent himself, who knows the particular circumstances the action is made of. It is a mistake, by the way, to call actions done from anger or appetite involuntary. If that were so, then first of all no animal but a human being would ever act voluntarily, and neither would children. Next, are we to say that nothing we do from appetite or anger is voluntary — or only that the noble things we do that way are voluntary and the shameful ones are not? That last is ridiculous, since the cause is one and the same. And the first is strange too: we are not supposed to call involuntary the very things we ought to want. There are things it is right to be angry about, and things we ought to desire — health, for instance, and learning. Besides, what is genuinely involuntary is felt as painful, while acting on appetite is felt as pleasant. And why should it matter, for the voluntariness, whether a wrong is done by cold calculation or in the heat of anger? Both are to be avoided, and the non-rational feelings are no less part of a human being than reason is — so the actions that spring from anger and appetite are no less the person's own. Treating them as involuntary makes no sense.

Having marked off voluntary from involuntary action, the next thing to examine is choice — prohairesis — since it seems more bound up with virtue than action is, and a surer test of character. Choice is plainly voluntary, but the two are not the same: voluntary is the wider term. Children and animals share in voluntary action but not in choice, and we call sudden acts voluntary without crediting them to choice. Choice is not the same as appetite or anger or wish or any kind of opinion either. It is not appetite or anger, because animals share in those but not in choice. The man who lacks self-control acts from appetite, not from choice; the self-controlled man, the reverse. Appetite can run against choice, but not against appetite. And appetite is about the pleasant and the painful, while choice is about neither as such. Still less is it anger, since acts of anger seem the least connected of all to choice.

Nor is choice the same as wish, close as the two are. We cannot choose what is impossible — anyone who said he was choosing it would seem a fool — but we can wish for the impossible, for immortality, say. We can wish for outcomes we ourselves could never bring about, like a particular runner or actor winning; but no one chooses such things, only what he thinks he can do something about himself. And wish is aimed more at the end, choice at the means: we wish to be healthy, but we choose the things that will make us healthy; we wish to be happy and say as much, but it sounds wrong to say we choose to be happy. In short, choice seems to be about what lies in our own power. Nor is it opinion. Opinion ranges over everything — the eternal and the impossible as much as what is in our power — and opinions are true or false, not good or bad the way choices are. Probably no one identifies choice with opinion outright; the claim is that it is the same as a certain kind of opinion. But it is not, because it is by choosing good and bad things that we become a certain kind of person, not by holding opinions about them. We choose to take a thing or avoid it; we form an opinion about what a thing is, or what it is good for, or how — but we do not form an opinion to take or avoid. Choice is praised more for aiming at the right object, opinion for being true. We choose things we are fairly sure are good; we form opinions about things we do not really know. And the people best at choosing are not always the ones best at judging: some see the better course and yet, through some flaw of character, choose against it. Whether opinion comes before choice or alongside it does not matter; that is not the question. The question is whether choice is a kind of opinion, and it is not.

So if choice is none of these, what is it? It is clearly voluntary, but not everything voluntary is chosen. Then is it this: the voluntary that has been thought through beforehand? For choice involves reasoning and weighing things out. The very Greek word for it points this way — pro-hairesis, what is taken before, chosen ahead of the alternatives.

So: do we deliberate about everything — is anything at all a possible object of deliberation — or are there things we do not deliberate about? (By "object of deliberation" let us mean what a sensible person would weigh, not what some fool or madman might.) No one deliberates about the eternal — the order of the universe, say, or the fact that the diagonal and the side of a square cannot be measured by a common unit. Nor about things that move but always come out the same way, by necessity or by nature or by some other steady cause, like the turning of the seasons or the rising of the sun. Nor about what varies at random, like droughts and rains, nor about chance events, like stumbling on buried treasure. Not even about all human affairs: no Spartan deliberates about the best constitution for the Scythians, because none of that is within his power. We deliberate about the practical things that are in our own power — which is what is left after all those exclusions.

The causes of things seem to be nature, necessity, chance, and on top of these our own minds and everything in our power. And just as people in general deliberate about what people in general can do, each of us deliberates about what each of us can do. We do not deliberate about the exact, self-contained sciences — how to form the letters of the alphabet, say, where there is no doubt how it is done. We deliberate about the things that depend on us but do not always come out the same way: medicine, for instance, or making money; more about navigation than about athletics, since navigation has been worked out less precisely; and more about the arts than the sciences, since we are less sure of the arts. Deliberation, then, belongs where things fall under general rules but it is still uncertain how a particular case will turn out — wherever there is some open question. For the big decisions we bring in others to help, distrusting our own judgment to settle them alone.

We deliberate not about ends but about the means to them. A doctor does not deliberate about whether to cure, nor a speaker about whether to persuade, nor a statesman about whether to make good laws; no one deliberates about his end. They set down the end and then ask how, by what means, it can be reached. If it can be reached several ways, they work out which is easiest and best; if only one way, then how to do it by that means, and that means by what further means, until they arrive at the first cause — which is the last thing found. Someone deliberating seems to search and analyze the way you would analyze a geometric figure to solve it. Not every search is deliberation — the mathematician's is not — but every deliberation is a search, and the last step in the analysis is the first in the doing. If in searching they hit something impossible, they give it up — if money is needed, say, and cannot be had; if it looks possible, they set about it. By "possible" I mean what can be done through ourselves — including what is done through our friends, since in a sense the source of that still lies with us.

So a person is the source of his own actions, and deliberation is about what he himself can bring about, and actions are done for the sake of other things. Hence it is not the end we deliberate about, but the means. Nor is it the particular facts of the moment — whether this here is bread, or whether it has been baked properly; those are matters for perception, and if you had to deliberate about everything you would go on forever. The object of deliberation and the object of choice are the same, except that the object of choice has already been settled — singled out as the thing preferred after deliberation. For each of us stops asking how to act once he has traced the starting point back to himself, to the part of him that governs; that is the part that does the choosing. (The old kingships Homer wrote of show this nicely: the kings would announce to the people what they had already decided.) Since the object of choice is something in our power that we want and have deliberated about, choice is a deliberate desire for something in our power: we deliberate, then settle on it, then desire it in line with what we deliberated. Let that stand as a sketch of choice and its object — the means to our ends.

Wish, as we said, is for the end. Some hold its object is the genuine good; others, whatever strikes a person as good. Those who say its object is the real good run into this: that what a person wishes for when he chooses wrongly is then not really an object of wish at all (since on their view, if it is wished for it must be good, and in this case it is bad). Those who say its object is whatever strikes one as good run into the opposite trouble: that nothing is naturally an object of wish, only whatever seems good to each person — and different people are struck differently, sometimes by opposite things. If neither view satisfies us, perhaps we should say: in plain truth, the object of wish is the genuine good, but for each person it is whatever strikes him as good. For the good person, then, the object of wish is what is truly good; for the bad person, it can be anything at all — just as, in the body, what is healthy for the healthy is what is truly healthy, while other things are "healthy" for the sick. So too with what is bitter or sweet, heavy or hot, and the rest. The good person judges each case rightly, and in each the truth appears to him. Each character has its own range of noble and pleasant things, and what most sets the good person apart may be just that he sees the truth in every case, being himself a kind of standard and measure of it. Most people are led astray by pleasure: it is not really good, but it strikes them as good, so they go after the pleasant as good and shun pain as bad.

Now the end is the object of wish, and the means are the objects of deliberation and choice; so the actions concerning the means are matters of choice, and therefore voluntary. The activities that exercise the virtues concern the means — so virtue is up to us. And so is vice. Wherever it is in our power to act, it is in our power not to act, and the reverse; so if doing the noble thing is in our power, so is not doing it, and if not doing the base thing is in our power, so is doing it. If doing and not doing the noble or base thing is up to us, and that is what being good or bad consists in, then being a good person or a bad one is up to us.

The old saying that "no one is wicked willingly, or blessed against his will" is half right and half wrong. No one is blessed against his will, true; but wickedness is voluntary. Otherwise we would have to deny what we just argued — that a person is the originator and begetter of his actions as much as of his children. And if that is plainly so, and we cannot trace our actions back to any sources beyond the ones in us, then the things whose sources are in us are themselves in our power and voluntary.

This is borne out by what people actually do — by private individuals and by lawmakers, who punish and chastise wrongdoers (unless they acted under compulsion or out of ignorance they did not cause) and honor those who do well, on the theory that this encourages the one and restrains the other. No one is encouraged to do what is not in his power — it is useless to talk someone out of feeling hot, or pain, or hunger; he will feel it all the same. And there is a stronger point: lawmakers punish the ignorance itself, when a person caused it. Drunks pay a double penalty, because the source was in the man himself — he could have not gotten drunk, and the drunkenness caused the ignorance. They also punish people who are ignorant of laws they ought to know and easily could; and likewise in every case where the ignorance is thought to come from carelessness, on the grounds that it was in the person's power not to be ignorant, since he could have paid attention.

Suppose someone is the kind of person who cannot pay attention. But people make themselves that kind of person by living carelessly, and they make themselves unjust or self-indulgent by doing wrong or by spending their days drinking and the like — because the particular things we do shape the kind of person we become. You can see this in anyone training for a contest or a skill: they keep doing the thing. To plead that you did not know habits are built out of repeated acts is the mark of someone hopelessly dull. And it is beside the point to say that the unjust or the self-indulgent man does not wish to have those vices. If a man knowingly does the things that make him unjust, he is unjust voluntarily, even though he cannot simply wish himself back to being just. The sick man cannot wish himself well — yet he may be ill voluntarily, if his illness came from loose living and ignoring his doctors. There was a time he could have not been ill; now that he has let himself go, he cannot. Once you have thrown a stone, you cannot call it back — and yet the throwing was up to you, since the source was in your hand. In the same way the unjust man and the self-indulgent man could have avoided becoming what they are, so they are what they are voluntarily; but now that they have become it, they can no longer be otherwise.

It is not only diseases of the soul that are voluntary; bodily ones are too, in some people, and we blame them accordingly. No one blames a man for ugliness he was born with, only for the kind that comes from neglect and never exercising. The same with weakness and disability: no one would reproach a man blind from birth or from illness or an accident — we would pity him — but everyone blames the man whose blindness came from too much wine or some other excess. So among bodily defects, the ones in our power are blamed and the others are not; and if that is right, then among defects of the soul too, the ones we blame must be the ones in our power.

But here is an objection. Everyone, you grant, aims at what strikes him as good, and no one controls how things strike him; the end appears to each according to the kind of person he is. If a man is somehow responsible for his own character, then he is responsible too for how things strike him. But if he is not, then no one is the cause of his own wrongdoing; he does wrong out of ignorance of the true end, thinking that this is how he will secure what is best. Aiming at the end, the objection goes, is no one's own choice; you have to be born with a kind of inner eyesight that judges rightly and chooses the truly good, and the person who has this by nature, well and fully, is the one truly favored — for it is the greatest and noblest thing, something you cannot get or learn from anyone else but have just as nature gave it. If all this holds, how is virtue any more voluntary than vice? For both the good man and the bad, the end appears and is fixed by nature, or however you want to put it, and they refer everything else to it.

But take it either way. Say the end does not appear to each by nature alone, but something also depends on the person himself — then vice is as voluntary as virtue, since the bad man too has a hand in how the end appears to him. Or say the end is fixed by nature, but virtue is still voluntary because the good man does everything toward it voluntarily — then vice is just as voluntary, since the bad man's own agency is in his actions just as much, even if not in the setting of the end. So if, as people say, the virtues are voluntary (we are at least partly responsible for our characters, and it is because we are a certain kind of person that we take the end to be a certain kind of thing), then the vices are voluntary too. The cases are exactly alike.

So we have covered the moral virtues in general — that in outline they are mean states and settled dispositions, how they are formed, that they are naturally exercised on the same circumstances that formed them, that they are in our power and voluntary, and that they follow what right reason directs. Note, though, that actions and dispositions are not voluntary in the same way. We are in command of our actions from start to finish, knowing the particulars. With dispositions we command only the beginning; the steady accumulation that builds them is no more noticeable than the slow advance of a disease. Still, they are voluntary, since it was up to us to use our circumstances one way rather than another.

Now let us take up the moral virtues one at a time — what each is, what it deals with, and how. Their number will come clear in the process. Start with courage. We have already said it is a mean state regarding fear and confidence. What we fear is, obviously, the fearful — broadly, evils — which is why fear gets defined as the expectation of evil. We fear all sorts of evils: disgrace, poverty, illness, friendlessness, death. But not all of these are the brave man's concern. Some things it is right and noble to fear and base not to fear — disgrace, for one. The man who fears disgrace is decent and has a sense of shame; the man who does not is shameless. (Some call that man brave by a loose analogy, since he resembles the brave man in being unafraid.) But poverty and illness — anything that does not come from our own vice or fault — these a man should not fear; and yet being unafraid of them does not make him brave in the strict sense. We use the word for him too, by resemblance: some men are cowards in the dangers of war but generous with money and steady in the face of losing it. Nor is a man a coward for fearing insult to his wife and children, or fearing envy; nor is he brave for being unflinching about a flogging.

What sort of fearful things, then, is the brave man concerned with? Surely the greatest, since no one stands up to the terrible better than he does. And the most terrible thing is death, because it is the end of everything, and the dead man is thought to have no share in good or evil any longer. Yet even death is not the brave man's concern in every form — not death at sea or from disease. In what form, then? Surely the noblest. And that is death in war, since it is death in the greatest and noblest danger — confirmed by the honors cities and rulers give it. So the man most properly called brave is the one who is fearless in the face of a noble death and the sudden threats of death that war brings. Not that he will not be steady at sea or in sickness too — but not in the way sailors are. The brave man may give himself up for lost there and recoil at that kind of end, while sailors stay cheerful and hopeful out of experience. Courage shows where there is something a man can do to save himself, or where death would be noble — and neither holds for drowning or disease.

What is fearful is not the same to everyone. There is a kind of terror, as we say, beyond human endurance — fearful to anyone with sense. The fearful things on a human scale differ in size and degree, and so do the things that inspire confidence. The brave man is as undaunted as a human being can be. He will fear such things, but he will stand up to them as he should, as reason directs, for the sake of what is noble, since that is the end of the virtue. It is possible to fear these things too much, or too little, or to fear what is not really fearful as if it were. The brave man goes wrong in none of these ways. He stands his ground, fears, and is confident toward the right objects, for the right reason, in the right way, at the right time — for he feels and acts as he should and as reason directs. The end of each activity is whatever accords with the disposition behind it; for the brave man, that is courage, and courage is noble — so the end is noble too, since the end takes its character from the disposition. It is for the sake of the noble, then, that the brave man stands fast against terror and does what courage demands.

On the side of too much: the man who fears nothing at all has no proper name (many of these states do not), but if he feared nothing — not earthquake, not the high seas, as they say of the Celts — he would be a madman, or numb to pain. The man with too much confidence toward the fearful is reckless. He tends to be a boaster, faking the courage he does not have: he wants to seem to stand toward danger the way the brave man really does, and copies him where he can. So most of the reckless turn out to be a strange mix of bravado and cowardice — loud beforehand, but failing to hold when the real danger comes. The man who feels too much fear is the coward: he fears the wrong things in the wrong ways, and so on. He is short on confidence, but it is in the experience of pain that he is most exposed — a despairing sort, afraid of everything. The brave man is the opposite, since confidence belongs to the hopeful. So the coward, the reckless man, and the brave man all face the same things but stand differently toward them: the first two overshoot and fall short, the last hits the mean and acts as he should. The reckless are rash, eager before the danger and gone once it is on them; the brave are sharp in the moment but quiet and steady before it.

Courage, then, is a mean state regarding the things that inspire confidence and fear, in the circumstances described, and the brave man chooses his course and holds his ground because doing so is noble, or because failing to is base. But to die in order to escape poverty or heartbreak or anything merely painful is not the act of a brave man but of a coward. Running from what is hard is softness, and the suicide faces death not because it is noble but to get clear of an evil.

True courage is roughly what we have described. But five other dispositions also get called courage in ordinary talk. First, and closest to the real thing, the courage of the citizen. Citizens face danger, it is thought, because of the penalties and disgrace the laws fix on cowardice and the honors they give the brave — which is why the bravest peoples are the ones where cowards are shamed and the brave are honored. This is the courage Homer gives Diomedes and Hector. Hector says, "Polydamas will be the first to heap disgrace on me"; Diomedes, that Hector will boast in Troy of beating him. This comes nearest to real courage, because it springs from a virtue — from shame, from longing for what is noble (that is, for honor), and from avoiding disgrace, which is base.

You might put in the same rank men who stand firm under their commanders' compulsion. But they are lower, because what moves them is fear, not a sense of shame, and what they flee is pain, not disgrace. Commanders do compel their men. As Hector says, "Any man I find skulking far from the fight will have no hope of escaping the dogs." Commanders who post steady troops behind doubtful ones, or beat men who flinch, or draw their line up against a trench or some other barrier, are all doing the same thing: using force. But a man should be brave because it is noble, not under compulsion.

Second, experience and skill pass for courage — which is why Socrates even thought courage was knowledge. In war this shows up in seasoned soldiers. There are many things in war that are not really dangerous, and these men have seen them so often that they look brave to people who do not know how harmless they are. Their skill also lets them strike without being struck, since they handle their weapons well and carry the best for attack and defense — like armed men against unarmed, or trained athletes against amateurs, where the best fighters are not the bravest but the strongest and fittest. But the regulars turn coward the moment the danger outruns their advantage — when they are outnumbered and outsupplied. Then they are the first to run, while the citizen militia stand and die where they are, as actually happened at the temple of Hermes: to the militia, flight was disgraceful and death better than safety bought that way, while the regulars had gone in trusting their edge and ran once they saw they had lost it, fearing death more than disgrace. That is not the brave man's way.

Third, plain animal spirit gets called courage. Men driven by raw spirit are thought brave, the way a wounded beast charges — and the truly brave do have plenty of spirit, since nothing drives at danger like it. Hence those phrases all through Homer: "he put strength in his spirit," "roused his strength and his spirit," "and the keen strength flared in his nostrils," "his blood boiled" — all of them naming the way animal spirit rouses and surges. But the truly brave act because it is noble, with spirit as their ally; beasts act from pain — because they have been hurt or scared. Leave them alone in their forest or marsh and they do not attack. They are not brave for rushing at danger when goaded by pain and spirit, with no eye to the risk — by that measure a hungry donkey would be brave, since you can beat it and it will not leave the feed; and adulterers do plenty of bold things out of lust. So men goaded into danger by pain and raw spirit are not brave. Still, this spirited temper is the most natural of the lot, and would be real courage if you added deliberate choice and the right motive. Men feel pain at an insult and pleasure in revenge, and those who fight from that may be good fighters, but they are not brave — they act on the feeling of the moment, not because it is noble and not as reason directs. Yet they resemble the brave.

Fourth, the merely confident are not brave either. Their boldness in danger comes from a string of wins over weak opposition. They resemble the brave in being confident, but the brave are confident for the reasons given, while these are confident from settled conviction that they are stronger and will not be hurt — much as drunk men grow bold. When things turn out otherwise, they run. But it was the brave man's mark to stand against what is, or seems, fearful to a human being, because it is noble to do so and base not to. So it counts as greater proof of courage to be unshaken in sudden terror than in danger you saw coming, since the steadiness then comes from a fixed character rather than from preparation. Foreseen dangers you can face by calculation; the sudden ones you face from the kind of person you have become.

Fifth and last, those acting in ignorance look brave, and are not far from the merely confident — but they are lower, since they have nothing real behind them. The confident at least think well of themselves and so hold the field a while, whereas men who have been deceived bolt the instant they learn the truth, as the Argives did when they ran into the Spartans, taking them for the men of Sicyon.

So we have said what kind of men are brave, and what kind only seem to be. One more point: though courage has to do with both confidence and fear, it is far more about fear. The man who keeps his head under what is painful, and stands toward it as he should, has a better claim to be called brave than the man who is right about what inspires confidence. So it is for enduring painful things that men are called brave. Courage involves pain, and is rightly praised for it, since standing up to pain is harder than holding back from pleasure.

It is not that the end of courage is not pleasant — it is, but the surrounding circumstances bury it. Take the games: for the boxers the end is pleasant — the crown, the honor — but the blows they take hurt flesh and blood, and all the training is hard, and with so many such drawbacks the small prize starts to look like no pleasure at all. So with courage: death and wounds will be painful to the brave man, and against his will. But he endures them because it is noble to, or base not to. And the more complete his virtue and his happiness, the more it will pain him to die — for life is most worth living to a man like that, and he knows he is being robbed of the greatest goods, which is a bitter thing. He is no less brave for feeling it; he may be more so, for choosing the honor of war over keeping those goods safe. Acting with pleasure is not true of every virtue, except so far as a man is reaching his end. And maybe the best soldiers are not men like this, but men who are less truly brave and have nothing else worth keeping — men quick to meet danger and ready to trade their lives for a small gain. Let that be enough about courage; from what has been said, its nature is not hard to grasp at least in outline.

Next, temperance, which seems to come right after courage, since these two are the virtues of the non-rational part of the soul. We have already said it is a mean state concerning pleasures (it has to do with pains too, but less, and differently), and self-indulgence has the same field. So let us settle which pleasures. Divide them into the mind's and the body's. Loving honor or learning are pleasures of the mind: a man takes pleasure in whichever of these he is inclined to love, with his body untouched and only his intellect engaged. We do not call people temperate or self-indulgent over pleasures of that kind, or over any that are not bodily. People who love telling long-winded stories and gossiping their days away over nothing we call chatterboxes, not self-indulgent; nor do we call a man self-indulgent for grieving over lost money or friends.

So temperance is about bodily pleasures — but not even all of those. People who delight in what they see — colors, shapes, a painting — are not called temperate or self-indulgent, though here too one can take the right amount of pleasure, or too much, or too little. So too with hearing: no one calls a man self-indulgent for being wild about music or theater, or temperate for enjoying them rightly. Nor for pleasures of smell, except by association. We do not fault a man for enjoying the scent of fruit or roses or incense; we fault him for loving the smell of perfumes and rich cooking, since these recall to the self-indulgent man the objects of his cravings. (You can watch other men enjoy the smell of food when they are hungry too — but to enjoy it in that craving way is the mark of the self-indulgent, for whom these are objects of desire.) Even animals do not take pleasure through these senses except by association: a dog enjoys not the scent of a hare but the eating of it, and only smells out how near it is; the lion likes not the lowing of the ox but the meal, and the lowing only tells him it is close. He takes no pleasure in merely seeing or finding a deer or a wild goat — only in the prospect of the meal.

Temperance and self-indulgence, then, are about the pleasures animals also share — which is why they look slavish and brutish. These are the pleasures of touch and taste. And men barely use taste at all: taste is for telling flavors apart, the way wine-tasters and cooks judging a dish do, and the self-indulgent take little or no pleasure in that. Their pleasure is in the enjoyment itself, which comes entirely through touch — in eating, in drinking, in sex. Hence the wish a certain glutton is said to have made, that his throat were longer than a crane's, since his pleasure was in the touch. So the sense behind self-indulgence is the most widely shared of all the senses, and the vice looks rightly shameful, because it belongs to us not as human beings but as animals. To delight in such things and love them above all is brutish. (The most respectable touch-pleasures are set aside here — the rub-down and warm bath of the gym — since the self-indulgent man's craving touch is not for the whole body, only certain parts.)

Of cravings, some are common to everyone, others particular and acquired. The desire for food is natural: anyone who needs it craves food, solid or liquid or both, and a young man in his prime, as Homer says, craves sex. But when it comes to this or that particular food or person, not everyone craves the same thing, and the craving is not universal. Still, there is something natural in it, since different things please different men, and preferring certain things to whatever is at hand is universal. In the strictly natural desires few men go wrong, and all in one direction — too much. To eat or drink whatever is in front of you until you are stuffed is to overshoot the natural limit, since natural desire is just the filling of a real lack. That is why such men are called belly-mad, stuffing it past what they should; the slavish sort end up this way. But in the particular pleasures many men go wrong, and in many ways. To be "a lover of" something means taking pleasure in the wrong objects, or too intensely, or in the common vulgar way, or in the wrong manner — and the self-indulgent overshoot in all of these: they enjoy some things they should not enjoy at all, things that ought to be loathed, and the things it is right to enjoy they enjoy too much, in the common way.

So excess regarding pleasures is self-indulgence, and is blameworthy. Looking at these dispositions on the side of pains, though, we find that a man is not called temperate for enduring pains (as the brave man is) or self-indulgent for failing to. The self-indulgent man is so because he is pained more than he should be at not getting the pleasant thing — so his very pleasure causes him pain — while the temperate man is so because he is not pained by its absence, by having to go without. The self-indulgent man craves all pleasant things, or the most pleasant, and his craving drives him to choose them over everything else. So he is pained both when he misses them and in the very craving, since all craving is painful — a strange thing, to be pained by reason of pleasure.

As for men deficient on the side of pleasure, who take less pleasure than they should, they hardly exist, since that kind of numbness is not human: even the other animals tell foods apart and like some and dislike others. A man to whom nothing was pleasant and everything was alike would scarcely be human at all. There is no name for such a person, because he is barely real. The temperate man holds the mean. He takes no pleasure in what most delights the self-indulgent — he rather dislikes it — and none in the wrong things, none to excess in anything of the kind; he is not pained by their absence and does not crave them, or only in moderation, never more than he should, never at the wrong time, and so on. The pleasant things that make for health and a good condition of body he will go after in moderation, as he should, along with other pleasant things that do not get in the way of those and are not unseemly or beyond his means. The man who went after these past that point would be loving such pleasures more than he should — and that is just what the temperate man is not. He keeps his desires in line with what reason directs.

Self-indulgence seems more truly voluntary than cowardice. Pleasure is the cause of the one, pain of the other, and we go toward pleasure and away from pain. Pain twists and unsettles a man's nature, while pleasure does nothing of the kind — so it is more voluntary and more justly blamed. It is also easier to train yourself against pleasures: life is full of them and the practice carries no danger, whereas with fearful things it is the reverse. And cowardice as a settled habit seems voluntary in a different way from the particular acts that build it. The habit itself is painless, but the particular moments unhinge a man with pain, so that he throws down his arms and disgraces himself — which is why some even think those moments carry a kind of compulsion. With the self-indulgent man it is the other way: the particular acts are fully voluntary, done out of craving and desire, but the resulting habit is less so, since no one craves to become self-indulgent.

We give this vice a name — akolasia, which by its roots means "un-chastened-ness" — that we also use for the misbehavior of children, since the two cases are alike. (Which came first and lent the name to the other does not matter here, though plainly the later sense borrowed from the earlier.) The image is a good one: whatever reaches after base things and is apt to grow large needs chastening, and that fits the appetite and the child best of all, since children too live by craving, and the reach for pleasure is strongest in them. Unless the appetite is made obedient and put under the ruling part, it will swell out of bounds — in a fool the reach for pleasure is bottomless and indiscriminate, every indulgence feeds the habit, and if the cravings grow great and violent they will drive out reason altogether. So they should be moderate and few, and never set against reason. An appetite in that state we call obedient and chastened. As a child should live by his tutor's orders, the appetite should live by reason's; and in the temperate man the appetite is in tune with reason, since both aim at the same mark — what is noble. The temperate man craves the right things, in the right way, at the right time, which is exactly what reason directs. Let that be our account of temperance.
