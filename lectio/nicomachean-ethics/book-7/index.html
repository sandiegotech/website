Nicomachean Ethics, Book VII

Self-Control and Pleasure

Book VII7 of 10

Let us start again from a fresh point. There are three states of character to avoid: vice, lack of self-control, and brutishness. The opposites of the first two are clear enough — virtue answers to vice, self-control to its lack. As the opposite of brutishness it makes sense to set down a virtue beyond the human, a heroic and godlike excellence. So Homer has Priam say of Hector that he was so outstanding "he seemed not the son of a mortal man, but of a god." And if, as people say, men become gods through some surpassing height of excellence, then the state opposed to the brutish would plainly be of that order. For just as a beast has neither virtue nor vice, neither does a god; a god's condition is something more precious than virtue, and the brute's is something different in kind from vice.

Now a godlike man is rare — the Spartans, when they admire someone past all measure, call him seîos anēr, "a divine man" — and the brutish man is rare too. You find brutishness mostly among barbarians, though some cases come from disease or mutilation; and we also fling the word at men whose vice runs past every ordinary bound. We will come back to that condition later. Vice we have already treated. For now the task is lack of self-control, along with its near relations softness and luxuriousness, set against self-control and endurance on the other side. We should not take any of these to be the very same states as virtue and vice, nor yet as wholly different in kind.

We will proceed as before: lay out the common opinions, work through the puzzles they raise, and then, if we can, leave standing the most credible of the views people hold — or at least the greater and weightier part of them. For we will have done enough if the difficulties are untangled and the respected opinions survive. Roughly, the common opinions come to this. Self-control and endurance are counted good and praiseworthy; lack of self-control and softness, low and blameworthy. The self-controlled man is taken to be the man who stands by what he has reasoned out, the man who lacks self-control the one who abandons it. The man who lacks self-control acts at the urging of his feelings, knowing it is wrong, while the self-controlled man, knowing his cravings are wrong, refuses on the strength of reason to follow them. The temperate man is thought to have both self-control and endurance — though some say anyone with these is temperate and others deny it. Some people run together the self-indulgent man and the man who lacks self-control — the weak-willed man; others keep them apart. The man of practical wisdom, it is sometimes said, cannot be weak-willed, and sometimes that even shrewd, capable men can be. And finally, people are called weak-willed not just flatly but with a qualification — weak-willed about anger, say, or honor, or money. Those, roughly, are the common opinions.

Here is the first puzzle. When a man lacks self-control, what is the state of the knowledge he violates? Some say he cannot fail while genuinely knowing, in the strict sense, what is right. It seemed absurd to Socrates that knowledge could be present in a man and yet something else master it and drag it around like a slave. Socrates denied weakness of will outright: no one, he held, acts against what he judges best — failures come only from ignorance of what is best. But this collides head-on with the plain facts, and we have to ask, if the failure does involve ignorance, what kind of ignorance it is. For it is obvious the man does not think his act right before the feeling takes hold of him.

Some agree with Socrates in part. They grant that nothing can overpower knowledge, but deny that no one acts against his own conviction of what is better; so they say it is not knowledge the weak-willed man yields against, but only opinion. But if it is mere opinion and not knowledge — if the conviction opposing the craving is faint, a matter of half-belief, as in someone who is genuinely unsure — then we might excuse him for not holding out against strong desire. Yet real wickedness is not to be excused, nor is anything that deserves blame. Is it practical wisdom, then, that does the opposing, since that is the firmest principle of all? Absurd: the same man would then be both practically wise and weak-willed, and no one would say it belongs to practical wisdom to do, of one's own accord, the gravely wrong thing. Besides, we have shown that the practically wise man is marked precisely by acting, not merely knowing, since he is at home in the particulars and possesses the other virtues as well.

More puzzles. If the self-controlled man must have strong and bad cravings, then he is not the same as the temperate man, who has no such cravings — yet the self-controlled man must, for if his desires were good, the state restraining him from acting on them would be bad, and self-control would not always be good; and if they were weak and harmless, there would be nothing impressive in resisting them. Again, if self-control made a man stand by every opinion whatsoever, it could be a bad thing — by a false opinion, say; and if weakness made him drop every opinion, there would be cases where weakness is good. Think of Neoptolemus in Sophocles' Philoctetes: he is praised for not standing by the lie Odysseus talked him into, because it pained him to be false. Sophistical reasoning makes trouble too: a man can find himself fettered by an argument he cannot refute yet cannot accept, since the conclusion offends his judgment. And on the "mere opinion" view, foolishness joined to weakness might come out as a kind of goodness: a man's settled view is that the good thing is bad and must not be done, and his weakness then makes him act against his view — so he ends up doing the good after all. These tangles tell us the account needs fixing.

So let us examine, first, whether the weak-willed man acts knowing what is right; and if he does, in what sense. The real starting point is this. To say it is true opinion, not knowledge, that he violates changes nothing, since some people hold opinions with no doubt at all and take themselves to know — Heraclitus believed his own views as firmly as anyone believes what he strictly knows. Here is the better answer: "knowing" has two senses. We say a man knows both when he has the knowledge but is not using it and when he is actively using it. There is all the difference between doing wrong while holding knowledge you are not drawing on, and doing wrong while actually exercising it; the second would be strange, the first is no anomaly at all.

Push further. Action involves two kinds of premises, a universal and a particular, and a man may well have both yet act against his knowledge by using the universal without the particular — and it is the particulars that action is about. The universal itself can split in two: "dry food is good for every man" and "I am a man," with the further premise "this food here is dry" — and whether this food is dry he may either not know or not be attending to. Across these senses the difference is enormous, so that knowing in one sense and acting wrongly is nothing to wonder at, while in another sense it would be astonishing. And men can hold knowledge in yet another way. We see people who have knowledge and do not use it — who in a sense have it and in a sense do not — when asleep, or mad, or drunk. The man in the grip of passion is in just that condition. Anger, lust, and the like visibly alter even the body, and in some they bring on a kind of madness; so the weak-willed man is in a state like the drunk's or the sleeper's. That he recites the right principle proves nothing about his using it: men under passion can reel off proofs and quote Empedocles' verses the way beginners string words together before they grasp the sense, or the way an actor speaks his lines. They are saying the moral truth, not exercising it.

Look at the mechanics of action. Every action follows from a kind of syllogism, with a universal premise and a particular one that perception takes in; and where the two combine, the mind must affirm the conclusion, and in matters of doing, the man must act on it at once. Suppose the universal is "everything sweet should be tasted" and the particular "this is sweet": a man who can act, and is not stopped, must straightaway taste. Now suppose there is in him one universal forbidding him to taste, and at the same time the universal "everything sweet is pleasant" with its particular "this is sweet" — and that this last is the live one, while desire is present. The one principle tells him to keep away; desire drives him on, since desire can set each of the body's parts in motion. So he fails, and in a sense under the direction of reason and opinion — opinion not in itself opposed to right reason, but only by accident, since it is the desire, not the opinion, that runs counter to right reason. This is also why beasts are never called weak-willed: they have no grasp of universals, only of particular images and impressions.

How the ignorance lifts, and the weak-willed man recovers his knowledge, is the same story as for the drunk man and the sleeper, and belongs to the natural scientists to explain. But since the particular premise is an opinion about something perceived, and it is this that governs action — the man under passion either does not have this premise, or has it in a way that amounts not to knowing but to merely mouthing it, like the drunk reciting Empedocles. And because the particular term is not universal, and is not thought to yield knowledge the way the universal does, the result Socrates was after does, in a way, come out true: it is not knowledge in the strict and proper sense that gets dragged about by the passion — that knowledge stays untouched — but the knowledge that comes through perception. So much for whether the weak-willed man acts with knowledge, and how.

Next: is there such a thing as being weak-willed without qualification, or is everyone weak-willed only about some particular thing — and if there is the unqualified kind, what is it about? Plainly self-control and its lack, like endurance and softness, are about pleasures and pains. Now things that give pleasure are of two sorts: those that are necessary, and those choiceworthy in themselves but admitting of excess. The necessary ones are the bodily pleasures — food, drink, sex, the rest of touch and taste. The others are not necessary but are worth choosing for their own sake: victory, honor, wealth, and such fine or pleasant things. When men go too far after these, against the right reason within them, we do not call them weak-willed flatly, but with the addition — weak about money, honor, anger — because they are really a different sort of character, called weak-willed only by a likeness, the way we tack "Olympic victor" onto a man's name and his account shifts only slightly. And a sign of the real difference: weakness about the bodily pleasures is blamed not as mere error but as a vice, while these other cases are not.

It is the man whose object is bodily pleasure — who chases the pleasures and shrinks from the pains of touch and taste, not by deliberate choice but against his own choice and judgment — who is called weak-willed without any qualifier, just as "soft" is used only of these and not of the others. We put this man, the self-indulgent man, the self-controlled man, and the temperate man together in one class, because they share the same field of pleasures and pains; they differ in how they stand to it — two of them act on deliberate choice, two against it. So we would sooner call self-indulgent the man who chases excessive pleasures and flees moderate pains with little or no desire, than the man who does so from violent desire — for what would the first do with the added goad of youth and the violent pain of going without?

Some desires and pleasures are noble and good in kind — recall that pleasant things divide into the naturally choiceworthy, the naturally to-be-avoided, and the indifferent in between, like money and gain and honor. For all of these, and for the indifferent ones, men are blamed not for feeling or wanting or liking them, but for overdoing it in any way — for being more eager than they should about honor, or even about their own children or parents. These are good objects, and men are praised for caring about them; but the caring can run to excess, as when someone, like Niobe, would even fight the gods, or doted on his father like Satyrus, nicknamed "father-lover" for the foolishness of it. There is no real depravity here, since each object is by nature worth choosing; only the excess is wrong and blamable. And there is no weakness of will here in the strict sense, since that is not merely something to avoid but something blameworthy. Because the condition resembles weakness of will, the word gets used with the object attached, the way we call a man a bad doctor or a bad actor whom we would never call simply bad. So we reserve the bare terms — self-control and its lack — for the field that the temperate man and the self-indulgent man share, and use them of anger and the rest only by resemblance.

Some things are naturally pleasant — some to everyone, some to particular kinds of creature or person — while others become pleasant only through injury, or habit, or a corrupted nature; and there are states of character to match each. Take the brutish: the woman they say would rip open pregnant women and devour the unborn; the savage tribes near the Black Sea, some eating raw flesh, some eating human flesh, some lending their children for one another's feasts; or the stories told of Phalaris. These are brutish states. Others come from disease or madness — the man who sacrificed and ate his mother, the slave who devoured his fellow's liver. Still others come from disease or from habit: pulling out one's hair, gnawing one's nails, eating coal or dirt, and sex between men — in some by nature, in others from habit, as in those abused from childhood. Where nature is the cause, no one would call such people weak-willed, any more than one would call women so because in sex they are passive rather than active; nor those made morbid by habit. They are simply outside that frame. To have any of these urges is something apart from vice, as brutishness is apart from it; mastering them is not self-control in the proper sense, nor being mastered by them weakness of will, but only by resemblance. So there is brutish and there is morbid weakness of will, but only the human kind earns the name without qualification.

Now to the claim that weakness about anger is less disgraceful than weakness about appetite. Anger, it seems, does listen to reason after a fashion, but mishears it — like a hasty servant who runs off before he has heard the whole order and gets it wrong, or a dog that barks at any noise before checking whether it is friend or foe. Reason or some impression signals an insult or a slight, and anger, reasoning in its rough way that it must fight such a thing, flares up at once. Appetite is different: let reason or perception merely say "this is sweet," and appetite rushes off to enjoy it. So anger follows reason in a way, and appetite not at all; the appetite-driven man is the more disgraceful, since the angry man yields, after a fashion, to reason, while the other yields to nothing but his craving. And we more readily forgive a man for following natural desires, the ones common to all — and anger and irritability are more natural than appetites for what is excessive and unnecessary. (Recall the man who beat his father and pleaded, "My father beat his, and his beat his, and this boy here will beat me when he grows up — it runs in the family"; and the old man being dragged along who told his son to stop at the door, since that was as far as he had once dragged his own father.) Anger, besides, is not sly — it is out in the open — whereas appetite is the schemer; they call Aphrodite "weaver of deceits" and her girdle the thing that "cheats even the shrewd mind." So weakness about appetite, being the more devious and unjust, is the more disgraceful, and is weakness of will in the full sense, and a kind of vice; whereas the angry man acts in pain, and no one is insolent in pain.

So weakness about appetite is more disgraceful than weakness about anger, and the field of self-control and its lack is bodily appetites and pleasures — but among these we must mark the differences already drawn: some proper to mankind and natural in kind and degree, some brutish, some born of injury and disease. Only the first are the field of temperance and self-indulgence, which is why we never ascribe either to beasts, except by metaphor when one whole species outdoes another in insolence or destructiveness or greed; beasts have no deliberate choice, no power to reason things out, and stand apart from us as madmen do. Brutishness is not as low in the scale as vice, but it is more to be feared — not because the highest principle has been corrupted, as in a man, but because the creature has no such principle at all. It is like asking which is worse, a thing without life or a thing with it: the harm done by what has no source of action in it is always the smaller, and intellect is exactly such a source. A bad man can do ten thousand times the harm of a bad beast.

Turn now to the pleasures and pains of touch and taste, and to standing firm against them or giving way. A man may yield where most people would hold out, or hold out where most would yield: in pleasures, that makes him weak-willed or self-controlled; in pains, soft or enduring. Most men fall somewhere between, leaning a little to the worse side. The man who chases excessive pleasures by deliberate choice, for their own sake and nothing further, is the self-indulgent man — incapable of regret, and so incurable, since a man who feels no remorse cannot be cured. (The man who takes too little pleasure is his opposite; the temperate man holds the mean.) Of those who go wrong without choosing it, one is led on by pleasure, the other gives way because he flees the pain of denying his craving — and these are not the same. Everyone would call a man worse for doing something base with little or no desire than under a violent one, just as we judge a man worse for striking in cold blood than in anger: "What would he do if passion had hold of him?" Of these two, the one is really softness, the other weakness of will proper.

Self-control is opposed to weakness of will, endurance to softness; for enduring is holding out under strain, self-control is outright mastery, and holding out is to mastering as not being beaten is to winning — so self-control is the more worth having. The man who caves where ordinary people easily stand firm is soft and pampered — the sort who lets his cloak trail in the mud rather than take the trouble to lift it, who plays the invalid without thinking himself wretched though he acts the part. If a man gives way to violent, extreme pleasures or pains, that is no wonder, and we forgive him if he put up what fight he could — like Philoctetes bitten by the snake in Theodectes' play, or men straining to hold in laughter who burst out helplessly. The wonder is when a man caves to what ordinary people can resist, unless his failure comes from his birth or a disease (some Scythian royal lines are soft by constitution, and there is the natural difference between the sexes). The man enslaved to amusement, by the way, is usually thought to lack self-control, but he is really soft — amusement is relaxation, a kind of rest, and he overdoes the resting.

Weakness of will comes in two forms, the impulsive and the weak. In the weak kind a man makes resolutions and fails to keep them under the pull of feeling; in the impulsive kind he is swept off by feeling because he never formed a resolution at all — whereas some men, like people who tickle first and so are not tickled themselves, see the temptation coming, brace themselves and their reason, and do not give way. The impulsive form runs strongest in the quick-tempered and the moody, the one because his passions are too fast, the other because they are too violent, to wait on reason; both lurch after whatever notion strikes them. And the self-indulgent man, as we said, does not feel regret, since he stands by his choice; the weak-willed man is all regret. So the earlier puzzle resolves: the first is incurable, the second curable. Settled vice is like a chronic disease, dropsy or consumption; weakness of will is like an acute attack. Vice is continuous, and its victim does not notice it; weakness is not, and he does. As Demodocus said of the Milesians, "They are not fools, but they do the kind of things fools do" — so the weak-willed are not unjust, but they do unjust things.

To sum the comparison: the weak-willed man chases bodily pleasure to excess against right reason, but holds no settled conviction that he should; the self-indulgent man does hold such a conviction, since chasing pleasure is, to his mind, exactly his line. So the first is easy to talk round, the second is not. For virtue and vice respectively save and destroy the governing principle, and in action the end we aim at is the principle, as the axioms are in mathematics; in neither field does argument teach us the starting point — it is excellence, native or trained, that holds the right view of it. The man who holds it rightly is the temperate man; the self-indulgent man is his contrary. There is also the man swept off against right reason by passion — mastered so far that he cannot act on reason, but not so far as to be convinced he ought to pursue such pleasures without limit. That is the weak-willed man, better than the self-indulgent man and not simply bad, because in him the best part, the principle, is preserved. Set against him stands the man who keeps to his resolutions and is not shaken from them by passion. From all this it is plain that self-control is a good state and its lack a bad one.

Is a man self-controlled for standing by any conclusion and choice whatever, or only by the right one? And weak-willed for abandoning any conclusion and choice, or only a true one? Surely it is true conclusions and right choice the two are essentially defined by, and any old conclusion only incidentally. If a man pursues one thing for the sake of another, it is the second he pursues in his own right, the first only along the way; so it is the man who holds to, or lets go of, the true opinion who is self-controlled or weak-willed in the full sense. There are also people with a knack for clinging to their own opinions — the stubborn, hard to budge, not easily turned. They look a bit like the self-controlled, the way a spendthrift looks like the generous man or the reckless like the brave, but they differ on many counts. The self-controlled man does not shift for passion or craving, yet will gladly be persuaded when there is reason to; the stubborn man will not be moved by reason, though plenty of his kind are in fact led by their pleasures. The opinionated, the ignorant, and the boorish are all stubborn — the opinionated from pleasure and pain, since they relish a kind of victory in never being talked round and resent having their verdicts overturned. So they actually resemble the weak-willed more than the self-controlled. And some men depart from their resolutions not from weakness but for a noble reason, like Neoptolemus, who broke his resolution to lie because being truthful was, in his eyes, the nobler thing. Not everyone who acts for pleasure's sake lacks self-control or is base or weak-willed — only the man who acts for a base pleasure.

Since there is also a man who takes less pleasure than he ought in bodily things and fails to stand by his reasoning, the self-controlled man is the mean between him and the weak-willed man: the weak-willed man falls away through a little too much, this man through a little too little, while the self-controlled man stands firm and shifts for nothing but his own conclusions. Self-control being good, both contrary states are bad; but because the deficient one is rarely seen, self-control gets treated as the opposite of weakness alone, just as temperance is treated as the opposite of self-indulgence alone. Many terms are used by likeness, and so people speak of the temperate man's "self-control": the two share the trait of never acting against right reason for bodily pleasure, but the self-controlled man has bad desires and the temperate man does not, and the temperate man is so made that he feels no pleasure against his reason at all, while the self-controlled man feels it but does not give in. Likewise the weak-willed man and the self-indulgent man resemble each other though they are distinct: both pursue bodily pleasures, but the latter thinks it the proper thing to do and the former does not.

The same man cannot be both practically wise and weak-willed, since practical wisdom, as we showed, includes goodness of character, and is not knowledge only but the readiness to act — which the weak-willed man lacks. The merely shrewd man, though, can well be weak-willed; and the reason some are now and then taken for practically wise yet weak-willed is that shrewdness sits very close to practical wisdom in its intellectual side and parts from it only in the choice. The weak-willed man is not like the man who knows and is using his knowledge, but like the man who has it and is overpowered by sleep or wine. He acts of his own will, since in a sense he knows what he is doing and where it leads, but he is not a settled bad man, since his choice is sound — he is, at most, half bad. Nor is he unjust, for he does not act by design: of the two forms, one does not keep to his resolutions and the other, the man of violent passions, never forms any. So he is like a city that passes all the right laws and has excellent statutes but acts on none of them — proving Anaxandrides' gibe, "the city willed it, that cares nothing for laws" — whereas the bad man is like a city that does act on its laws, but its laws are bad.

Both self-control and its lack are conditions beyond the run of men: the one keeps to reason more, the other less, than most people can manage. And of the two forms of weakness, the kind found in men of violent passion is more easily cured than the kind found in men who resolve and break their resolutions; those weak by habit are easier to cure than those weak by nature, since habit is easier to change than nature — and the very closeness of habit to nature is what makes it hard, as Evenus says: "Long practice, friend, in the end becomes a man's own nature." We have now said what self-control is, and weakness of will, and endurance, and softness, and how these states stand to one another.

The next subject is pleasure and pain — and it belongs to the student of human affairs to examine them, since he is the one who fixes the end against which we call each thing good or bad. We cannot pass them by. We said that virtue and vice of character are both bound up with pains and pleasures, and most people hold that happiness must include pleasure — which is why they call the blessed man makarios, from a root meaning to rejoice. There are three views to confront. Some say no pleasure is good, in itself or by result, since good and pleasure are not the same. Others say some pleasures are good but most are bad. A third party grants that every pleasure is good, but denies that pleasure could be the chief good.

The case that pleasure is simply not good runs like this. Every pleasure is a perceived process toward a natural state, and no process is of the same order as the end it aims at, just as building is not of the same order as the finished house. The temperate man, besides, stays clear of pleasures; the practically wise man pursues freedom from pain, not the getting of pleasure; pleasures get in the way of thought, the more so the more intensely they are felt; pleasure answers to no art, though every good is the product of some art; and children and beasts chase pleasures. The case that not all pleasures are good points out that some are shameful and reproached, and some are positively harmful, since some pleasant things bring on disease. And the case that pleasure is at any rate not the chief good says it is no end but a process toward one. That is a fair report of the views in circulation.

But none of these arguments shows that pleasure is not good, or not the chief good, as the following will make plain. First, good is of two kinds, the unqualified and the relative, and so too are the natures and states that embody it — and likewise the movements and processes. Among those reckoned bad, some will be bad without qualification yet not bad relative to a given person, perhaps even worth his choosing; some not even worth choosing for him except at certain times or briefly, though not in themselves. And some are not pleasures at all, only seem so — all those bound up with pain and undertaken as a cure, like the "pleasures" of the sick. Next, since good is either an activity or a state, the processes that restore us to our natural state are pleasant only incidentally, by way of that restoration; the real activity lies in the desires of the part of us that is already sound. For there are pleasures with no admixture of pain or desire at all — the activity of contemplation, for one, where nothing in our nature is lacking to be filled. A sign of this: the same thing is not pleasant while the natural state is being refilled and once it is whole. When we are whole, what pleases us is what is pleasant in itself; while we are being restored, even the contrary things please — sharp and bitter things that no one would call pleasant by nature, so that the pleasures from them are not natural pleasures either.

Next, it does not follow, because (as some argue) the end must be better than the process that produces it, that there is always something better than a given pleasure. For not all pleasures are processes or even come with a process — some are activities, and are themselves ends; they arise not from our coming to be something but from our using what we already are. And the end is not in every case distinct from the process; that holds only for processes that complete a natural state. This is why it is wrong to call pleasure "a perceived process." Better to say "the unimpeded activity of a natural state" — "activity" for "process," and "unimpeded" for "perceived." People take it for a process because they think it good in the fullest sense, confusing activity with process, though the two are distinct. As for the objection that some pleasures are bad because some pleasant things harm the health — that is like saying some healthy things are bad for business. In that sense both are "bad," but not bad without qualification: even hard thinking can damage the health. What harms practical wisdom, or any state, is not the pleasure proper to it but some foreign pleasure; the pleasure of thinking and of learning only makes us think and learn the more.

Next: "no pleasure is the work of any art." What else would you expect? No activity is the work of an art — only the capacity for the activity is — though the perfumer's and the cook's arts are in fact taken to serve pleasure. As for "the temperate man avoids pleasures," "the practically wise man pursues freedom from pain," and "children and beasts pursue pleasures" — one answer covers all three. We have already said in what sense all pleasures are good in themselves and in what sense not all are; it is the latter that beasts and children pursue, the ones tied to desire and pain, the bodily pleasures and their excesses, the very ones the self-indulgent man is given over to. And it is freedom from the pain that goes with these that the practically wise man seeks, and these that the temperate man avoids — for plainly the temperate man has pleasures of his own. Further, pain is agreed to be an evil and to be avoided — some of it bad in itself, some as a hindrance — and the contrary of what is to be avoided as evil is good; so pleasure must be a good. Speusippus' attempted answer, that pleasure is the contrary of pain the way the greater part of a quantity is the contrary of the lesser, both being opposed to the equal, does not hold; he cannot mean that pleasure is a species of evil.

And even granting that some pleasures are low, there is no reason a particular pleasure should not be very good, just as a particular kind of knowledge can be very good even though some kinds are low. Indeed it may follow — since every state has its unimpeded activity, and happiness is the unimpeded activity of all of them or of some one — that this activity, when unimpeded, must be worth choosing for its own sake; and pleasure is exactly that. So the chief good could be a pleasure of some kind, even if most pleasures are, let us grant, low in themselves. That is why everyone thinks the happy life pleasant and weaves pleasure into happiness — and reasonably, since no impeded activity is complete, and happiness is something complete. This is why the happy man needs, in addition, the goods of the body, external goods, and good fortune, so that on these counts he is not hampered. Those who say a man is happy on the rack, or fallen into great disasters, so long as he is good, are talking nonsense, whether they mean to or not. And because fortune is needed besides, some take good fortune itself to be happiness — wrongly, for fortune in excess is itself a hindrance, and perhaps no longer deserves the name of good, being good only so far as it serves happiness.

That every creature, beast and man alike, pursues pleasure is some sign that it is in a way the chief good — "there is something in what all men say." But since no single nature or state is, or is thought, best for all, men do not all pursue the same pleasure, though pleasure they all pursue. Perhaps what they pursue is not what they think or would say, but really one and the same thing, since in everything there is something divine. The bodily pleasures, though, have taken the name to themselves, because they are the kind we meet most often and all of us share; and because to many people these are the only ones they know, they suppose these the only ones there are.

It is plain, too, that unless pleasure and its activity are good, it cannot be true that the happy man's life is pleasant: why would he want pleasure, if it is not good, when he could live even with pain? For if pleasure is not good, then pain is neither good nor evil, and there would be no reason to avoid it. And the good man's life is no more pleasant than any other unless his activities are. We must look as well at the bodily pleasures, for the sake of those who say that some pleasures — the good ones — are highly worth choosing, but not the bodily ones, the field of the self-indulgent man. If so, why are the contrary pains bad? The contrary of bad is good. Perhaps the necessary bodily pleasures are good in the sense in which what is not bad is good — or good only up to a point. States and movements that admit no excess of the better admit no excess of pleasure either; those that admit excess of the one admit it of the other. The bodily pleasures do admit excess, and it is by pursuing the excess, rather than the necessary measure, that the bad man becomes bad. Food, drink, and sex give pleasure to everyone, but not to everyone in the right way and degree. With pain it is the reverse: he avoids not excessive pain but pain at all — for it is only relative to excessive pleasure that pain at all becomes its contrary.

It is not enough to state the truth; we should show how the false view arises, since that strengthens conviction — when we can give a plausible reason why what is not true strikes people as true, they hold the truth more firmly. So we must say why the bodily pleasures appear more worth choosing than the rest. The first reason is that bodily pleasure drives out pain; men feel pain in excess and so pursue pleasure in excess, generally bodily pleasure, as a remedy. These remedies grow violent — which is the very reason they are pursued, since they make their impression by being set against their opposite. And there are two reasons, already given, why bodily pleasure is thought not good. Some such pleasures belong to a low nature, whether inborn, as in beasts, or acquired by habit, as in bad men; and others are cures of some deficiency, and of course it is better to be in the healthy state to begin with than to have it come on later. (Some pleasures do accompany a nature being brought to completion, and these are good by result.)

Their very violence, again, makes the bodily pleasures sought by men who can relish no others — men who manufacture their own thirsts. When the thirsts are harmless we do not blame it; when harmful, it is base. They have nothing else to take pleasure in, and to many people the neutral state, free of both pleasure and pain, is in itself distasteful, for living is some kind of effort, as the natural scientists testify when they tell us that seeing and hearing are painful, only we have grown used to it. In youth the constant growth produces a state like a mild drunkenness, and youth is pleasant; the melancholic by temperament constantly need a cure, since the body is forever fretting them, and live in chronic violent craving. Pleasure drives out pain — not only the directly contrary pleasure but any strong one — and this is how men become self-indulgent and bad. But the pleasures with no admixture of pain admit no excess; these belong to things pleasant by nature and not merely by result. By things pleasant "by result" I mean the curative ones, thought pleasant because the cure is worked by whatever part of us is still sound; by "pleasant by nature" I mean what sets a healthy nature in action.

No single thing is invariably pleasant to us because our nature is not simple but composite, so that we are perishable beings with something in us at war with the rest: when one part acts, that action is against the other part's nature, and only when the two are in balance does what is done feel neither pleasant nor painful. For a being whose nature was simple, one and the same activity would always be the most pleasant. This is why the god enjoys a single, simple pleasure forever — for there is an activity not only of motion but of rest, and pleasure lies more in stillness than in movement. "Change is the sweetest of all things," the poet says, and it is true only because of some flaw in us: the man easily changed is a bad man, so the nature that craves change is bad, being neither simple nor good. We have now had our say about self-control and its opposite, and about pleasure and pain — what each is, and how the one set is good and the other bad. It remains to speak of friendship.
