Nicomachean Ethics, Book IV

The Virtues of Character

Book IV4 of 10

Let us take up generosity next. It is held to be the mean concerning money: the generous person is praised not in war, nor in the matters of temperance, nor in the courtroom, but in the giving and receiving of money — and chiefly in the giving. By money I mean everything whose worth can be measured in coin.

The excess and the deficiency about money are wastefulness and stinginess. We always pin "stingy" on people who care too much about money; "wasteful" we sometimes use in a looser, mixed sense, for people who lack self-control and pour money into their own runaway appetites. These are thought the worst sort, because they have several vices at once. But that is not the word's proper use. Strictly, the wasteful person is the one who has a single fault — ruining his own resources — for to be wasteful, in the root of the word, is to be one who destroys himself by his own doing. And the man who burns through his substance does in a way destroy himself, since his substance is what he lives on. That, then, is how we will use the word.

Anything that can be used can be used well or badly, and money is among the things that can be used. The person who uses a given thing best is the one who has the excellence concerned with it; so money will be used best by the person who has the excellence concerned with money — and that is the generous person. Now spending and giving are using money, while taking it in and holding onto it are simply possessing it. So giving to the right people is more the mark of the generous person than taking from the right sources and refusing to take from the wrong ones. In general, doing good to others is more the mark of excellence than having good done to you, and doing what is noble more than merely refraining from what is base — and plainly the doing of good and the doing of noble things belong to giving, while taking belongs only to receiving good or not behaving badly. Besides, we thank the giver, not the man who simply declines to take, and we praise him more. And refusing to take is easier than giving, since people part with their own less readily than they refrain from taking what is another's. It is the givers, too, who get called generous; those who refuse to take are commended for fair dealing rather than for generosity, and for taking, people are not praised at all. And the generous are about the best-liked of all who have excellence, because they are useful — and their usefulness lies in the giving.

Every action that accords with excellence is noble and done for the sake of what is noble. So the generous person, too, will give for the sake of what is noble, and give rightly — to the right people, in the right amounts, at the right times, and with everything else that right giving involves — and he will do it gladly, or at least without pain, since whatever accords with excellence is pleasant, or at any rate not painful, and certainly not done with reluctance. The man who gives to the wrong people, or for some motive other than what is noble, should not be called generous but something else. Nor should the one who gives with pain — that shows he would rather have kept the money than done the noble thing, and no generous person feels that way. He will not take from the wrong sources either, since a man who sets no great store by money does not grab at it; and he is not one to ask favors, since a person who does others good does not readily take it from them. From the right sources — his own property, say — he will take, but as something necessary rather than noble, so that he has something to give. He will not neglect his own resources, since he wants the means to help others; and he will not give to just anyone, so that he has enough left for those he ought to give to, at the right times, and where giving is noble. It is very much in the generous person's grain even to overshoot in giving, leaving too little for himself; for it is like him to give no thought to his own interest.

Generosity is measured against a person's means, for it is not the size of the gift that makes the giving generous but the disposition of the giver, who gives in proportion to what he has. So nothing prevents the man who gives less from being the more generous, if he is giving out of less. People who have inherited their wealth rather than earned it are thought more generous, since they have never known want, and since everyone is fondest of what he himself has made — as parents are of their children, and poets of their poems. It is not easy for the generous person to be rich, since he is neither inclined to take nor to keep but to lay out, and he values money not for its own sake but for the sake of giving it away. This is why people complain that fortune leaves the most deserving the least rich. But it makes sense: you cannot have money if you take no trouble to have it, here as anywhere else. Still, he will not give to the wrong people, at the wrong times, and so on, since to do that would no longer be acting with generosity, and if he spent on such things he would have nothing left for what he ought. For, as I said, the generous person is the one who spends in proportion to his means and on the right objects, while the one who exceeds is wasteful. This is why we never call tyrants wasteful: it does not seem easy for their giving and spending to outrun their enormous holdings.

To sum up: since generosity is a mean concerning the giving and taking of money, the generous person will give and spend on the right objects, in the right proportion, in matters great and small alike, and do it gladly; and he will take from the right sources, in the right proportion. Because the excellence is a mean in both, he will do both as he ought, and right taking goes hand in hand with right giving, while taking that is not right runs against it. Should he happen to spend more than he should, or otherwise than he should, he will be vexed — but moderately, and as he ought, since feeling pleasure and pain at the right objects and in the right way is a feature of excellence. The generous person is also an easy partner where money is concerned: he is easily cheated, since he does not value money, and he is more pained at having failed to spend where he should than at having spent where he should not. He has no taste for the saying of Simonides.

The wasteful person goes wrong in these respects too: he feels neither pleasure nor pain at the right objects or in the right way, as will grow clearer as we go on. We have already said that wastefulness and stinginess are excess and deficiency, and in two things, giving and taking — for we class spending under giving. Wastefulness exceeds in giving and in refusing to take, and falls short in taking; stinginess falls short in giving and exceeds in taking, though only in small ways. Now the two sides of wastefulness do not usually go together: it is not easy to give to everyone while taking from no one, since a private person who gives like that soon runs through his means. People of this kind are the ones really thought wasteful, and such a man would seem a good deal better than the stingy one. He is easily cured — by age, and by running short of money — and he can come round to the mean, since he already has the makings of the generous person: he gives and refuses to take, only he does neither well or rightly. Train him in this, or change him some other way, and he becomes truly generous, giving to those he should and refusing to take where he should not. This is why he is not thought low in character; to overshoot in giving and in refusing to take is no sign of badness or meanness, only of folly.

So the man who is wasteful in this way is held to be far better than the stingy one, both for the reasons given and because he does good to many, while the stingy person does good to no one, not even himself. But most wasteful people, as we said, also take from the wrong sources, and on that side they are really stingy. They become grasping because they want to spend and cannot do it easily — their means soon give out, so they are forced to draw from elsewhere — and because they do not care about what is noble, they take recklessly and from any source at all, since their one wish is to give, and they are indifferent to how or from where. So their gifts are not even generous, being neither noble nor disinterested nor given in the right way. Often they make rich the people who ought to be poor: to the steady, decent sort they will give nothing, while to flatterers, or anyone who serves their pleasures, they hand over a great deal. That is why most of them are self-indulgent: free with their money, they lavish it on their own unbridled appetites and slide off toward their pleasures, because they do not live with an eye to what is noble. So the wasteful person, left to himself, drifts into these faults; but with some care taken of him he could reach the mean and what is right.

Stinginess, on the other hand, is incurable — old age and any kind of incapacity are thought to make people stingy — and it is more deeply rooted in human nature than wastefulness, since most people are fonder of getting money than of giving it. It also reaches far and takes many forms, since it has two parts, falling short in giving and exceeding in taking, and not everyone has it whole; sometimes it splits, and one set of people overshoot in taking while another fall short in giving. The names — close-fisted, tight, pinching, and the like — all belong to those deficient in giving; but they neither want nor will take what belongs to others, some from a real decency and a shrinking from anything base. Some keep their own out of a motive, real or alleged, of never being driven to do anything shameful — the skinflint belongs here, and everyone of that stamp, named from never letting anything go. Others hold off from their neighbors' goods out of fear, on the theory that you cannot take other people's things without their taking yours, so they would rather neither take nor give. The other set, stingy in taking, will accept anything from any source: people in degrading trades, pimps and the like, and loan sharks who lend small sums at steep interest. All of these take from the wrong sources and in the wrong amounts. What they have in common is shameful gain, since they all swallow disgrace for the sake of profit, and small profit at that. The people who take great things from the wrong sources and of the wrong kind — tyrants who sack cities and loot temples — we call wicked, impious, unjust, but not stingy. The cheat at dice, the petty thief who steals clothes, the highway robber — these are stingy, since they go after shameful gain; both kinds put themselves to trouble and take on disgrace for profit, the one running the worst risks for plunder, the other making gain off the very friends they ought to be giving to. So both, in trying to profit from the wrong sources, go after shameful gain, and every such taking is stingy. And stinginess is rightly called the opposite of generosity: it is a greater evil than wastefulness, and people go wrong on its side more than on the side of the wastefulness we described. So much for generosity and the vices opposed to it.

Next would seem to come magnificence. This too is an excellence concerning money, but unlike generosity it does not extend to every transaction — only to expensive ones, and there it outruns generosity in sheer scale. For magnificence is, as the Greek word itself hints, fitting expenditure on a grand scale. But "grand" is relative: outfitting and captaining a warship is not the same expense as funding a public festival, and "fitting" too is relative to the man, to the occasion, and to what he is spending on. A person is not called magnificent for spending as he should on small or ordinary things — "often I gave to the wandering beggar" — but for doing it in great matters. The magnificent person is generous, then, but the generous person is not thereby magnificent. The deficiency here is called shabbiness, the excess gaudiness, tastelessness, and the like — faults not because the scale is too large for the right objects, but because they make a show in the wrong objects and in the wrong way. We will come to them shortly. The magnificent person is like a man of skill: he can see what fits and spend lavishly with taste. For, as we said at the start, a settled disposition is defined by its activities and by what it concerns.

The magnificent person's expenses, then, are large and fitting, and so are his results — for that secures spending that is not merely large but suited to the work. The work should match the expense, and the expense the work, or even exceed it. And the magnificent person will spend such sums for the sake of what is noble, which is common to all the excellences, and he will do it gladly and without stinting, since fussing over the figures is small. He asks how a thing may be done most beautifully and fittingly rather than how cheaply, and at what least cost. So the magnificent person must also be generous, since the generous person too will spend the right amount in the right way; but it is the grand scale that marks off the magnificent one, the object being the same as generosity's. And without spending more than the next man, he will make the result more magnificent. For the excellence of a possession and of a work are not the same: as a possession, the most valuable thing is the one worth most, gold for instance; but as a work, it is the one that is great and beautiful, since to behold such a thing is wonderful — and that is what magnificence is, excellence in a work on a grand scale. There are kinds of spending we call honorable: offerings to the gods, the furnishing of their temples, sacrifices, everything to do with the divine, and all the public matters people compete to do finely — funding a chorus splendidly, say, or fitting out a warship, or hosting a feast for the whole city. In all these, as we said, regard is paid to the doer's standing and means: the spending should be proportionate to these and should befit not only the work but the man doing it. This is why a poor man cannot be magnificent — he has not the means to spend largely and yet becomingly, and if he tries, he is a fool, since he is out of proportion and against propriety, whereas to accord with excellence a thing must be done rightly. Such spending suits people who have the resources to begin with, through themselves or their forebears or their connections, and those who are high-born or highly regarded, since all of this carries greatness and reputation.

So the magnificent person is roughly as I have described, and magnificence shows itself in expenditures of this kind, the greatest and most honorable, and among private ones in those that come only once, like a wedding, and in anything that engages the whole community or its leading people, and in receiving and sending off foreign guests, and in gifts and the returning of gifts. For the magnificent person spends not on himself but on the common good, and gifts have something of the character of offerings to the gods. It is like him, too, to furnish his house in keeping with his wealth, since even that brings a kind of credit, and to spend rather on works that last, since these are the most honorable — and to observe what fits in each case, since the same things do not suit gods and men, or a temple and a tomb. And each expense should be great of its kind, and great spending on a great object is most magnificent — though within any given field it is whatever is great there. There is a difference, too, between greatness in the work and greatness in the outlay: a very beautiful ball or flask makes a magnificent gift to a child, though its price is small and almost mean. So it is the mark of the magnificent person to do whatever he does magnificently, since work of that kind is hard to outdo and stands in fitting proportion to the cost. Such, then, is the magnificent person.

The man in excess — call it gaudiness — overspends because he spends improperly, as we said. Where small expense is called for, he lavishes much and makes a tasteless show: he throws his dinner club a wedding feast, or, when he has to fund the chorus for a comedy, brings the chorus on in purple at their entrance, as the Megarians did. And all of this he does not for the sake of what is noble but to flaunt his wealth and because he thinks he will be admired for it — spending little where he should spend much, and much where he should spend little. The shabby man, by contrast, comes up short in everything: even after spending the most, he ruins the whole effect for the want of some trifle. He dithers over every move, schemes how to spend the least, and then groans about even that, sure he is doing everything on a grander scale than he ought. Both states are faults, but they bring no disgrace, since they neither harm others nor look especially ugly.

The very name greatness of soul tells us that great things are its concern; let us first settle what sort. It makes no difference whether we look at the disposition in the abstract or at the person who has it. The great-souled person, then, is one who thinks himself worthy of great things and is — for a man who thinks it without grounds is a fool, and no one with excellence is a fool. The person who thinks himself worthy of little and is, is modest, but not great-souled, since greatness of soul implies bigness, the way beauty implies a sizable frame: small people can be neat and well-proportioned, but not beautiful. The man who thinks himself worthy of great things without being so is vain, though not everyone who overrates himself deserves the name. The one who thinks himself worthy of less than he is is little-souled, whether his real worth is great, middling, or small, since his estimate always falls short of it. The most striking case is the man of real worth, for what would he do if his worth were less? So in the size of what he claims the great-souled person is at the top, but in claiming what fits he is in the mean, since he rates himself at his true value, while the others overshoot and fall short.

Now since he rightly rates himself at a high, or rather the highest, value, his concern will be above all with one thing — and "value" here refers to external goods. We should take the greatest of these to be the one we assign to the gods, the one the powerful most desire, and the prize set on the noblest deeds. That is honor; honor is the greatest of external goods. So it is in matters of honor and dishonor that the great-souled person bears himself as he should. Even without argument it is plain that the great-souled are concerned with honor, since honor is what the great think themselves chiefly worthy of, and worthy of in proportion to their worth. The little-souled person falls short, both as regards himself and as regards what the great-souled person is worth; the vain person overreaches as regards himself, but does not reach beyond the great-souled person. And since the great-souled person, by definition, is worthy of the greatest things, he must be the best of men, for the better a man is the more he is worth, and the best is worth the most. So the truly great-souled person must be good, and whatever is great in each excellence would seem to belong to him. It would not at all suit the great-souled person to run away flailing, or to wrong anyone — for with what aim would he do anything base, in whose eyes nothing is great? Go through the particulars and the great-souled person looks downright absurd if he is not good. He would not even deserve honor if he were bad, since honor is the prize of excellence and is given to the good. Greatness of soul, then, seems to be a kind of crown on the other excellences: it makes them greater, and cannot exist without them. This is why it is so hard to be truly great-souled — it is impossible without full goodness and nobility of character.

So honor and dishonor are above all the great-souled person's concern. At great honor from good people he will be pleased, but only moderately, as getting his due, or even a little less — for no honor can quite measure up to complete excellence; still, he will accept it, since they have nothing greater to give. But honor from ordinary people and on trifling grounds he will utterly despise, since it falls short of his deserts, and dishonor likewise, since in his case there can be no just ground for it. And though honor is his chief concern, he will also bear himself with moderation toward wealth, power, and every turn of fortune, good or bad, however it falls — neither overjoyed in good times nor unduly pained in bad. He does not even behave so about honor, and honor is the greatest of these things, since it is what makes power and wealth worth choosing, those who have them wanting honor through them. To the man who counts even honor a small thing, everything else is small too — which is why such men are thought arrogant. Strokes of good fortune are thought to feed greatness of soul: the well-born, the influential, the wealthy are held worthy of honor, since they stand higher, and whatever is superior in some good is held more worthy of honor. So such advantages do dispose people toward greatness of soul, since they bring honor from some quarters. But in truth only the good man deserves honor, though the man who has both goodness and these advantages is held still more worthy of it. People who have the advantages without the excellence are not justified in rating themselves high and are not rightly called great-souled, since complete excellence is one of the conditions of that character. Such men become arrogant and insolent — it is not easy to carry good fortune well without goodness — and, unable to carry it, and convinced of their own superiority, they look down on others and do whatever they please. They are imitating the great-souled person without being like him, copying him where they can: they skip the deeds that only real goodness produces and just despise other people. The great-souled person despises on good grounds, since his judgments are true, but most people do it at random.

He is not one to run small risks, nor does he go courting danger, since there are few things he prizes; but he will face great dangers, and when he does, he is lavish with his life, knowing there are terms on which life is not worth keeping. He is the sort to do favors, but ashamed to receive them, the one putting a man above, the other below. He will more than repay a favor done him, so that the original giver ends up the one in his debt and on the receiving end. Such men are thought to remember the favors they have done but not the ones done to them, since the receiver is below the giver, and the great-souled person wants to be above. He likes to hear of his own good turns but not of those done to him — which is why, in Homer, Thetis does not bring up her kindnesses to Zeus, nor the Spartans theirs to the Athenians, but only the benefits they had received. It is like the great-souled person to ask favors not at all, or very reluctantly, but to do a service readily; to bear himself loftily toward the great and the fortunate, but easily toward people of middling station — since to be above the first is hard and grand, but above the second is easy, and standing tall over the great is not ill-bred, while doing it to humble folk would be crude, like flexing your strength against the weak. He does not throw himself in honor's way, or go where others take the lead; he is slow and holds back, except where some great honor or work is at stake; he concerns himself with few things, but great and famous ones. He is open in his hatreds and his loves, since hiding them is a mark of fear, and he cares more for the truth than for appearances. He speaks and acts in the open — his contempt for others makes him blunt, so he is given to telling the truth, except when he is being self-deprecating with ordinary people — and he is reserved toward most. He cannot shape his life to please anyone but a friend, since that is servile; this is why all flatterers are lowly and all lowly men are flatterers. He is not easily impressed, since nothing is great in his eyes, nor does he bear grudges, for it is not like the great-souled person to dwell on things, least of all on wrongs — better to overlook them. He is no gossip: he will not talk about himself or anyone else, since he cares neither to be praised himself nor to have others blamed, nor again is he free with praise; and so he will not speak ill even of his enemies, except to show his contempt. He is the last man to lament over things that cannot be helped, or to ask for trifles, since that belongs to a person who takes such things seriously. He is the sort to own beautiful and useless things rather than profitable and productive ones, this being more the mark of a self-sufficient man. A slow gait, a deep voice, a measured way of speaking are thought to suit the great-souled person, since a man earnest about few things is not one to hurry, and one who counts nothing great is not one to strain — and it is anxiety that breeds the shrill voice and the quick step.

Such, then, is the great-souled person. The one in deficiency is little-souled, the one in excess is vain. These too are not thought vicious, exactly, only mistaken, since they do no harm. The little-souled person, though worthy of good things, cheats himself of his deserts; there is something wrong with him in his failing to think himself worthy of what is good, and it comes from not knowing himself, for otherwise he would reach for what he really merits, since that is good. Still, such people are thought not foolish so much as timid. But thinking so little of themselves seems to make them worse, since everyone's aims are set by what he thinks he deserves, and these men, convinced they deserve nothing, hold back from noble actions and pursuits, and from external goods as well. The vain, on the other hand, are fools, and plainly self-ignorant: they attempt honorable things as though worthy of them, and then get found out. They deck themselves out in dress and bearing and the like, and want their good fortune on display, and talk about it expecting to be honored for it. But little-souledness is more opposed to greatness of soul than vanity is, since it is the commoner failing and the worse.

Greatness of soul, then, is concerned with great honor, as we said. There seems to be an excellence concerned with honor too — as we mentioned earlier — which stands to greatness of soul roughly as generosity stands to magnificence: both keep clear of the great and dispose us rightly toward moderate and small matters. Just as there is a mean, an excess, and a deficiency in the giving and taking of money, so in reaching for honor there is the more and the less than one ought, and also the reaching from the right sources and in the right way. We blame the ambitious person for going after honor more than he should and from the wrong sources, and we blame the unambitious person for not even wanting honor for noble things. But sometimes we praise the ambitious person as manly and a lover of what is noble, and the unambitious one as moderate and temperate — as we noted before. Plainly, since "lover of so-and-so" can mean several things, we do not always mean the same thing by "lover of honor": as praise it means more than most people are, as blame more than a man should be. Since the mean here has no name, the extremes seem to fight over the empty ground between them. But where there is excess and deficiency there is a mean, and in fact people do reach for honor more than they should, and less, and sometimes just as they ought. This last state is the one praised, a mean concerning honor with no name of its own. Set beside ambition it looks like indifference to honor, and beside indifference it looks like ambition, and beside both it looks like both — which is not unique to this case. The extremes seem opposed to each other just because the mean has no name.

Even temper is the mean concerning anger. Since the person in the mean has no name, and the extremes nearly none either, we give the name even temper to the one in the mean, leaning toward the deficiency, which also has no name. The excess might be called hot temper, since the feeling is anger and its causes are many and various. The person who is angry at the right things and with the right people, and in the right way, at the right time, and for the right length of time, is praised — so this person is even-tempered, since even temper is praised. The even-tempered person means to be unruffled, not carried off by feeling, but angry only in the way, and at the things, and for the time that reason directs. He is thought to err rather toward the deficiency, since he is not quick to take revenge but more inclined to forgive. The deficiency — call it lack of spirit, or what you like — is blamed: people who do not get angry at what they should are thought fools, as are those who get angry in the wrong way, at the wrong time, or with the wrong people. A man in this state seems to feel nothing and to take no offense, and since he feels no anger, he is in no shape to defend himself. To put up with insults to yourself, and to stand by while your friends are insulted, is servile.

The excess comes in every form: people get angry with the wrong people, at the wrong things, more than they should, too quickly, and for too long. Not all of these are found in one man, of course — that would be impossible, since the evil at full strength would destroy itself and become unbearable. The hot-tempered get angry fast, at the wrong people and the wrong things and to excess, but they cool off fast too, which is their best feature: they do not bottle it up, but pay their offenders back, since their vehemence makes them show what they feel, and then it is over. The choleric are excessively quick and angry at everything, on every occasion — which is just what their Greek name means. The bitter hold their anger a long time, because they keep it pressed down, and only when they have had their revenge does it subside, since the revenge replaces pain with pleasure and so drains the anger off. If that does not happen, they carry the weight inside: because it does not show, no one can talk them out of it, and digesting anger by oneself takes time. Such people are a great burden to themselves and to their closest friends. We call those bad-tempered who get angry at the wrong objects, too much, and too long, and who are not satisfied without revenge or at least punishment. To even temper we oppose the excess rather than the deficiency, since it is the commoner — human nature being readier to take revenge than to let it go — and bad-tempered people are harder to live with. What I said before is plain here too: it is no easy thing to say how, and with whom, and at what, and how long one ought to be angry, and at what point one is right or wrong. The man who strays a little, either way, is not blamed; sometimes we praise people deficient in anger and call them even-tempered, and sometimes we call the irritable spirited, as fit to lead. So it is hard to lay down in words just what degree or kind of lapse is blameworthy, since the decision lies in the particulars and rests with perception. But this much is clear: the mean is praiseworthy — being angry with the right people, at the right things, in the right way, and so on — while the excesses and deficiencies are blameworthy, slightly so if slight, more so if greater, and very much so when great. So we should plainly hold to the mean. Let that be our account of the states concerned with anger.

In social life — the give-and-take of words and deeds — some people are thought obsequious: out to please at all costs, they agree to everything and oppose nothing, and take it as their job never to cause anyone pain. The opposite sort are quarrelsome and contrary, opposing everything and not caring in the least whether they cause pain. Plainly both states are blameworthy, and the mean between them is praiseworthy — accepting what one should as one should, and objecting in the same spirit. This state has no name, but it is very like friendliness, since the person who has it is just the sort we would call a good friend, only with affection added. Where it differs from friendship is exactly there: it carries no feeling or affection for the people the man deals with. He takes everything as he should not out of love or hatred but simply because that is his character, and he treats strangers and acquaintances alike, intimates and outsiders alike — though fittingly in each case, since it is not right to be equally considerate of intimates and strangers, or to pain them equally. In general, then, his dealings are governed by what is fitting: he aims to avoid giving pain and to add to people's pleasure, but always with an eye to what is noble and beneficial. His concern is the pleasures and pains of social life; and wherever contributing to someone's pleasure would be ignoble, or even harmful to himself, he will refuse and choose to cause pain instead. If a thing would bring real disgrace, or some harm, to the man doing it, while his objecting would cause only a little pain, he will not go along but hold out against it. He deals differently with the great and the ordinary, and with people according to how well he knows them, allowing for whatever differences there are, giving each his due — by himself preferring to give pleasure and wary of giving pain, but guided in the end by the consequences, by what is noble and beneficial, whichever weighs more. And he will inflict a small pain for the sake of a greater pleasure to come. So the person in the mean is roughly as I have described, but he has no name. Of those who set out to please, the one who simply wants to be agreeable, with no further motive, is obsequious; the one who does it to get something — money or what money buys — is a flatterer. And the man who is contrary in everything, as I said, is quarrelsome. Here too the extremes look opposed to each other because the mean has no name.

The mean that steers clear of boastfulness concerns much the same things and likewise has no name. Still, it is worth going over these states: by treating each separately we come to know moral character better, and we are the more convinced that the excellences are means by seeing that this holds across the board. We have already spoken of those whose social dealings aim at pleasure and pain; now let us turn to people who are truthful or false, in word and deed and in the claims they make. The boaster is thought to claim things that bring him credit when he does not have them at all, or to a greater degree than he does, while the self-deprecating person, on the contrary, denies what he has or plays it down. The one in the mean is a straightforward sort, truthful in life and word, owning what is his and making it neither more nor less than it is. Any of these lines can be taken with or without a further motive, but in general a person speaks and acts and lives according to his character, unless he has some special end in view. Now since falsehood is in itself low and blameworthy, and truth noble and praiseworthy, the truthful person, who is also in the mean, is praiseworthy, while both who depart from the truth are blameworthy — the boaster more so.

Let us take each in turn, and the truthful person first. I do not mean the man who keeps faith in his agreements, or in matters that touch on justice and injustice — that belongs to another excellence — but, in matters where nothing so serious is at stake, the man who is truthful in life and word simply because that is his settled state. Such a person must be counted good: someone who loves the truth as such, and is truthful where it makes no difference, will be all the more truthful where it does, since he will shun falsehood as base, having shunned it even for its own sake. And such a person is praiseworthy. Still, he leans rather toward understating than overstating, since that seems to be in better taste — exaggeration being so tiresome. The man who lays claim to more than he has with no further motive looks like a base sort, since otherwise he would take no pleasure in falsehood; but he comes off as a fool rather than a scoundrel. The one who does it with a motive — for honor or glory, like the braggart — is not so very blameworthy; but the one who does it, directly or indirectly, for money is uglier. The braggart is a braggart not by his ability but by his choice, by his settled state and the kind of man he is — just as one liar takes pleasure in falsehood for its own sake while another lies out of a hunger for reputation or gain. Those who exaggerate for reputation claim the sort of qualities that win praise and congratulation; those who do it for gain claim qualities their neighbors can make use of, and whose absence can be hidden — being a skilled seer, say, or a good doctor. So most people pretend in this direction and exaggerate here, since these are the qualities that pay.

The self-deprecating, who play down their own qualities, come across as the more refined, since they are thought to speak not for gain but to avoid grandstanding. One very common trait in them is to disown the things people generally admire — as Socrates used to do. Those who lay false claim to small things, and to things whose falseness is obvious, are called poseurs and are contemptible. Sometimes this very modesty looks like boasting, as with the studied plainness of dress the Spartans go in for; both excess and the extreme of deficiency have something of boasting in them. But people who practice understatement in moderation, and about things not too plainly false, give an impression of refinement. Here it is the boaster who seems opposed to the truthful person, as the worse character.

Since life has its breaks and in them makes room for amusement with a touch of humor, it seems there is a fitting way to behave here too — a sense of the kind of thing to say and how to say it, and likewise of what to listen to. There is a difference between saying such things and hearing them. Here too there is an excess, a deficiency, and a mean. People who overdo the laughable are thought buffoons and vulgar, grabbing at a laugh any way they can, at any cost, bent on raising one rather than on saying what is seemly and sparing the target of the joke. Those who would never crack a joke themselves and are annoyed by anyone who does are thought boorish and stiff. The ones who joke in good taste are called witty — "nimble," really, since their wit is a kind of nimbleness of character, and just as we judge bodies by their movements, so we judge characters. But because the laughable lies near at hand and most people enjoy fun and joking more than they should, the buffoons too get called witty, as if they were charming. That they differ, and by a good deal, is plain from what has been said. Another thing belonging to the mean is tact: it is the tactful person who says and listens to the things that befit a decent and well-bred man, since there are things it suits such a man to say and hear by way of humor, and the humor of a gentleman differs from that of a vulgarian, as the educated man's does from the boor's. You can see this by comparing the old comedy with the new: in the old, the fun came from obscenity; in the new, more from innuendo — and that is no small difference where decency is concerned. Should we then define the man who jokes well by his saying what befits a gentleman, or by his not paining the target, or even by his giving pleasure? Or is any such definition vague, since different things are hateful and pleasant to different people? Whatever he says, he will also listen to, since a man is thought to do what he can stand to hear — though there is a limit; he will not do quite everything he will listen to, since a joke is a kind of abuse, and lawgivers forbid certain kinds of abuse and perhaps should have forbidden certain jokes too. So the refined and gentlemanly man is, as it were, a law to himself. Such is the person in the mean, whether you call him tactful or witty. The buffoon cannot resist the laughable, sparing neither himself nor anyone else so long as he gets his laugh, saying things no man of refinement would say, and some he would not even put up with hearing. The boor is useless for such company: contributing no fun of his own, he is sour with everyone who does. Yet some rest and amusement in life are generally agreed to be necessary. So the three means we have described all occur in life, and all concern the give-and-take of words and deeds. They differ in that one is concerned with truth, and the other two with what is pleasant: of these two, one deals with the humor of life, the other with the rest of social life.

To call shame an excellence is not right, since it is more like a feeling than a settled state. It is defined as a kind of fear of disgrace, and its effects are much like those of the fear of danger: people who feel shame turn red, and people who fear death turn pale. Both, then, are plainly bodily in some way, which is thought a mark of a feeling rather than a settled state. The feeling, moreover, does not suit every age, only youth. We think the young should be capable of shame, since living by feeling as they do, they go wrong often, and shame holds them back. We praise the young for being modest, but no one would praise an older man for being prone to shame, since we think he ought not to do anything that calls for it. For shame, arising at base actions, does not belong to a good man at all, since such actions should not be done in the first place — and it makes no difference whether some things are disgraceful in truth and others only in opinion, since neither should be done, so that one ought not to be in a position to feel shame at all. To be the kind of man who does something disgraceful is the mark of a bad character. To be the kind who would feel shame if he did, and to count that as being good, is absurd, since shame is felt only at voluntary actions, and a good man will never willingly do anything base. Shame may be good on a condition — "if he were to do such a thing, he would feel shame" — but the excellences are good without conditions, not just in supposed cases. And granting that shamelessness, not blushing to do what is disgraceful, is base, it still does not follow that it is good to do such things and then feel shame. Self-control, likewise, is not properly an excellence but a kind of mixed state — but all that will be set out in a later book.
