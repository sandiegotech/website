# Nicomachean Ethics, Book IX

Self-Love and the Need for Friends

In every friendship between unequal partners, it is proportion that keeps things even and holds the bond together, as we have already said. In a civic friendship, for instance, the shoemaker is paid for his shoes at a certain rate, and so is the weaver, and everyone else; here money has been supplied as a common measure, and everything is referred to it and measured by it. But in the friendship of lovers the complaint sometimes comes from the lover that for all his devotion his love is not returned — though he may have nothing in him worth loving; and just as often it comes from the one who is loved, that the suitor who once promised the world now delivers nothing. These quarrels happen because the lover's friendship rests on pleasure and the beloved's on advantage, and the quality each wants is not there in the other. Since these were the grounds of the friendship, the friendship dissolves once the grounds give out: the two never loved each other but only loved qualities in each other, and those qualities do not last, so neither does the bond. Friendship grounded in character, by contrast, exists for its own sake and is not after anything, and so it lasts — as we have already said.

Quarrels also break out when the partners get a different return than the one they wanted, because getting something other than what you were after feels like getting nothing at all. There is the well-known story of a man who promised a musician more and more in proportion to how well he played; but when the musician came the next morning to claim what he was owed, the man said he had already paid pleasure for pleasure. If both had meant that to be the bargain, all would have been well. But if one wanted entertainment and the other wanted money, and one got his object while the other did not, the exchange is not square — because each fixes his mind on what he happens to want, and is willing to pay for that specific thing.

So who should set the rate — the one who gives first, or the one who receives? At first glance the one who gives first seems to leave it up to the other. This, they say, is exactly what Protagoras did: when he had taught a man something, he would have the learner judge for himself what the knowledge was worth, and then he took that much. In such cases some people follow the rule, "a friend should be content with the agreed reward."

People are rightly blamed, though, who take the money up front and then do nothing they said they would, having promised far past their power. The Sophists are perhaps forced into this, since no one would pay anything for what they know once they had it; but if they have already taken the fee for a job and not done it, the blame is fair.

Where no terms are fixed in advance for the mutual services, those who give the first service freely will not raise the question — as we said before — because it belongs to friendship grounded in goodness to take its measure from the other's intention, and that intention is the mark of a true friend and of goodness itself.

The same rule seems to hold for the relationship between teachers and students of philosophy: here the value of what is given cannot be measured in money, and no equivalent price can be set on it, so perhaps it is enough to repay what one can — as with the gods or with one's parents.

But where the first gift was made openly for some return, the best course is for the repayment to be one both sides agree is proportionate; and where that cannot be reached, then for the receiver to set the value is not only necessary but fair. For when the giver gets back what matches the benefit he conferred — or what he would have paid for the pleasure he got — then he has had his worth from the other. We see this is the rule in buying and selling, too; and in some places the law allows no suit on voluntary agreements, on the principle that once a man has trusted another he must be content to settle the debt in the same spirit he made it. The thinking is that the trusted party should fix the value rather than the trusting one. For people who own a thing and people who want it do not value it the same: what is mine, and what I am giving up, always looks to me worth a great deal. Still, since the return is set by the receiver's estimate, he should perhaps measure what he received not by what he values it at now that he has it, but by what he valued it at before he got it.

Other questions arise as well — for instance: Does a father have an unlimited claim on your obedience and service, or should you obey your doctor when you are sick? And in electing a general, should fighting ability be the only thing considered?

Likewise: should you do a favor for a friend, or for a good man? Should you repay a benefactor, or give to a comrade, when you cannot do both?

Surely the honest answer is that none of these can be settled with precision, because they vary in every direction — in how much is at stake, in what is noble, in what is necessary. It is obvious, at least, that no single person can have every claim on us at once. As a general rule, returning a kindness is a higher duty than doing an unasked favor for a comrade: paying a debt binds you more than making a gift. And yet even this admits exceptions. Which is the higher duty — for a man who has been ransomed out of the hands of robbers to ransom his ransomer in turn, whoever he is, or to repay him in cash on demand though he was never himself captured; or, instead, to ransom his own father? It would seem a man should ransom his father even before himself.

So, as we said, the debt should generally be paid; but if in a particular case the gift weighs far heavier — as nobler or more urgently needed — then we must let that decide. Sometimes, too, repaying an existing debt would not even be a fair exchange: suppose the original benefactor helped a good man knowing he was good, while this good man has to repay someone he now believes to be a scoundrel. And in some cases there is no obligation to lend back to a man who lent to you: suppose a bad man lent to you taking you for good, expecting to be repaid, while you, being good, have no hope of repayment from him, since he is bad. Either the case really is as we have described, and then the claim is not equal; or it only seems so — and even then, in acting this way, people are not to be judged as doing wrong. As we have often said, all statements about feelings and actions can be exact only so far as their subject is exact. It is obvious enough that not everyone has the same claim on us, and that a father's claims are not unlimited — just as not every kind of sacrifice is offered to Zeus. Since the claims of parents, brothers, comrades, and benefactors all differ, we must render to each what belongs to and befits each.

And this is in fact what people do. To weddings they invite their relatives, because they share a common stock, and so share in everything bound up with it; and to funerals they think relatives ought to gather before others, for the same reason. As for support, it seems our first duty is to our parents, since we are in their debt, and it is nobler to help the authors of our existence in this than to help ourselves. Honor we owe our parents as we owe it to the gods — but not every kind of honor, and not the same to a father as to a mother; nor the honor due a man of learning or a general to a father, but the honor that is a father's due, and likewise for a mother. To all our elders we owe the honor that suits their age: rising when they enter, giving up our seat, and the rest. To comrades and brothers we owe frankness and a free share in all we have. And to kin, fellow tribesmen, fellow citizens, and everyone otherwise tied to us, we should keep trying to render what is due, weighing what belongs to each by closeness, goodness, or familiarity. With people of the same kind the weighing is easier; with people of different kinds it takes more effort. But that is no reason to give up the attempt; we must draw the distinctions as far as we can.

There is also the question of whether or not to dissolve friendships when the partners no longer remain what they were when the bond was formed. Where the motive was advantage or pleasure, there is surely nothing wrong in breaking off once those qualities are gone, since it was the qualities the two were friends of, and when these fail it is only natural the feeling should fail too.

A man does have grounds for complaint, though, if the other was really attached to him for advantage or pleasure but pretended it was for his character. As we said at the start, the most common source of quarrels between friends is precisely this — that they are not friends on the grounds they suppose. When a man has deceived himself, imagining he was loved for his character while the other gave no sign of it, he has only himself to blame. But when he has been deceived by the other's pretense, he has every right to complain of the man who deceived him — more right, even, than against a counterfeiter, in proportion as what was counterfeited here is more precious.

But suppose a man takes up another as good, and the other turns out, and is found out, to be a scoundrel — is he still bound to be his friend? Surely not; it is impossible. Not everything can be an object of friendship, but only what is good; and so there is no obligation to befriend a bad man, and indeed one should not, for one ought not to love what is evil or grow like what is base — which is what would happen, since, as we said before, like is friend to like.

Should the break, then, come at once? Not in every case — only where the friend is incurably corrupt. Where there is a chance of reform, we are bound to come to the rescue of a friend's character even more than his money, in proportion as character is the better thing and the closer to friendship. Still, a man who does break off is not to be judged as acting wrongly; he was never the friend of a person like the one his friend has now become, and since the man has changed and cannot be brought back, he withdraws.

Take the reverse case. Suppose one partner stays as he was when the friendship formed, while the other grows in goodness and far outstrips him. Should the improved man treat the other as a friend? Surely he cannot. It is clearest where the gap is widest, as in the friendships of boys: if one of two boyhood friends stays a boy in mind while the other becomes a man of the highest character, how can they be friends, when they no longer enjoy the same things or love and hate the same things? They will not share these toward each other, and without that, as we assumed, they cannot be friends, since they cannot live a shared life — and we have already spoken of those who cannot. Should the improved man, then, treat his old friend exactly as if the friendship had never existed? Surely he ought to remember their old closeness, and, just as we think ourselves bound to do favors for friends before strangers, so to those who were friends and are no longer we should grant something on the score of the past — as long as the cause of the break was not sheer depravity on their part.

Now the friendly feelings we show toward our friends, the feelings by which we recognize friendship, seem to have grown out of the feelings we have toward ourselves. People define a friend as one who wishes and does good — or what he takes to be good — to another for that other's sake; or as one who wishes his friend to exist and to live for the friend's own sake, which is what mothers feel toward their children, and what estranged friends feel toward each other; or as one who shares a life with another and chooses the same things; or as one who grieves and rejoices along with his friend, which again is true above all of mothers. By one or another of these marks people generally identify friendship — and every one of them the good man has toward himself, and the rest of us have insofar as we take ourselves to be good. (For, as we said, goodness, the good man, is the measure for everyone else.)

For the good man is at one with himself, and with every part of his soul he desires the same things. He wishes for himself what is good, and what he believes to be good, and he acts on it — since it is the mark of a good man to work at what is good — and he does it for his own sake, that is, for the sake of the thinking part of him, which is generally held to be a man's true self. He wishes himself to live and be kept alive, and above all that thinking part by which he is what he is, because existence is a good to a man who is good. It is for himself that each man wishes good things; no one, imagining he might become someone other than he is, would choose for that new self to have everything — a god already has the chief good, but he has it as the being he actually is — and the thinking part must be judged to be each man's self, or at least most truly so. And the good man wishes to go on living with himself, because he can do it with pleasure: his memories of past actions are full of delight, his hopes for the future are good, and good hopes are pleasant. His mind is well stocked with things to contemplate. And he grieves and rejoices most of all with himself, because what gives him pain and pleasure stays the same from day to day, not one thing today and another tomorrow — for he is not, so to speak, a man of regrets. Since each of these feelings is something the good man has toward himself, and since a friend feels toward a friend as toward himself — a friend being, in fact, another self — friendship is held to be one of these feelings, and those in whom they are found are reckoned friends. Whether or not there can really be friendship of a man toward himself is a question we will set aside; there can be thought to be, insofar as two or more of these conditions are met, and because the highest friendship, as the word is ordinarily used, resembles the feeling a man has toward himself.

It might be objected that these same conditions seem to show up even in ordinary, low men. The answer is that they share in them only so far as they please themselves and take themselves to be good. In the thoroughly corrupt and wicked these feelings are not found, really or even in appearance — one might almost say they are not found in bad men at all. For bad men are at war with themselves, craving one set of things while wishing, in cool reason, for another, exactly like men who lack self-control: they choose what is harmful but pleasant over what they themselves believe to be good; or, out of cowardice and laziness, they shrink from doing what they are convinced is best for them; and those who have done many terrible things, driven by their own depravity, come to hate and flee their own lives, and so kill themselves. The wicked seek out others to spend their days with precisely so as to flee themselves, since alone they are haunted by ugly memories and can look forward only to more of the same, but in company they drown it out. Having nothing in themselves to love, they feel no friendship toward themselves.

Nor can men of this stamp rejoice and grieve along with themselves, because their soul is torn by faction: one part, on account of the corruption in it, is pained at being denied certain things, while the other and better part is pleased; the one drags them this way and the other that, as if tearing them apart. They cannot literally feel pleasure and pain at once, but a little later the man is sorry he was ever pleased, and wishes those things had never pleased him at all — for the wicked are full of regret. It is plain, then, that the wicked man cannot stand even to himself as a friend, since he has nothing in him to be a friend of. And if to be like this is utterly miserable, then a man should flee wickedness with all his strength and strive to be good, for so he can be a friend to himself and come to be a friend to another.

Goodwill resembles friendship but is not the same thing. It can be felt toward people we do not know, and without their being aware of it, which friendship cannot. (This too we have said before.) And it is not even affection, since it carries no intensity or longing, which affection brings with it. Affection takes time and familiarity, but goodwill can spring up all at once — as it does toward athletes in a contest: spectators come to wish them well and share their hopes, though they would not lift a finger to help, because, as we said, the feeling arose suddenly and is only a surface liking.

Goodwill seems, rather, to be where friendship starts — the way pleasure taken through the eyes is where love starts. No one falls in love without first being pleased by the look of the beloved, yet being pleased by the look does not by itself amount to loving; that comes only when he aches for the beloved in absence and longs for the beloved's presence. In the same way, men cannot become friends without first passing through goodwill, but those who feel goodwill do not necessarily go on to friendship: they have only an inert wish for the good of the people they feel it toward, and would not act with them or put themselves out for them. So, speaking loosely, one might call goodwill a dormant friendship, which, once it has lasted a while and ripened into familiarity, becomes friendship in fact — though not the kind whose object is advantage or pleasure, since those motives cannot produce even goodwill. The man who has received a kindness returns it with goodwill toward his benefactor, and rightly so; but the man who wishes another well because he hopes to get something through him has goodwill not toward that person but toward himself, just as he is no real friend if he courts him for his own ends. In general, goodwill arises on the ground of some goodness and worth, when one man strikes another as noble, or brave, or the like — as we said happens with athletes in a contest.

Concord, too, is plainly a feature of friendship, and so is not the same as mere agreement of opinion, which can hold even between people who do not know each other. Nor do we usually say people are in concord just because they agree on any old point — say, on a question of astronomy, where agreement has nothing to do with friendship. We say a community is in concord when its people agree about what is to their advantage, settle on the same policy, and carry out what they decided together. So concord is about matters of action — and about the important ones, those that affect both parties, or, in a single state, everyone. A community is in concord when all agree, say, that the offices should be elective, or that they should ally with Sparta, or that Pittacus should rule (assuming he was willing). But when each man wants the power for himself, as the brothers do in the Phoenician Women, they fall out and form factions. Concord is not merely each man holding the same idea, whatever it may be, but holding it about the same object — as when both the common people and the men of sense want the best men in office, so that everyone gets what he is after. Concord, then, is a civic friendship, just as it is said to be, since its subject is what is advantageous and bears on the conduct of life.

Concord exists among the good. They are in concord with themselves and with one another, standing, if I may put it so, on the same ground: the wishes of such men hold steady and do not ebb and flow like the tide in the strait, and they wish for what is just and advantageous and pursue it in common. The bad can no more be in concord than they can be true friends, except to the slightest degree; they grab for more than their share of what is profitable while shirking the work and service the common good demands. Each wanting these things for himself, each is jealous of his neighbor and gets in his way; and since no one watches over the common good, it is lost. They end up quarreling, each insisting the other do his part while refusing to do his own.

Benefactors are generally thought to feel more friendship for those they help than the helped feel for them — a fact that gets debated as running against what one would expect. Most people explain it by saying the one party are debtors and the other creditors: just as with actual loans, where debtors wish their creditors gone while creditors are anxious to keep their debtors alive, so those who have done kindnesses want the people they helped to go on living, in hopes of a return, while those people are in no hurry to repay. Epicharmus would probably say that the people who give this explanation are judging from their own meanness — and yet it is true to human nature, since most people do have short memories on this score and would rather receive a kindness than do one.

But the real cause seems to lie in nature, and the case is not parallel to the creditor's. There is no affection in the creditor for the debtor's person, only a wish to keep him around for the sake of the return; whereas those who have done kindnesses genuinely feel friendship and love for the people they have helped, even when those people neither are nor ever could be of any use to them. The same is true of craftsmen: every one of them loves his own work more than that work, if it came alive, could ever love him back. It is most of all true of poets, who are devoted to their poems and love them like their own children. The benefactor's case is like this: the person he has helped is his own handiwork, and so he loves him more than the handiwork loves its maker. The reason is that existence is, to everyone, something worth choosing and worth loving; and we exist by activity — by living and acting; so a man who has made something exists, in a manner of speaking, by his act of making it. He loves his work, then, because he loves existing — which is only natural, since the work makes actual, out in the open, what was there before only as a possibility.

There is more. The benefactor has something noble in his deed, so that he can take pleasure in the man in whom that noble thing now resides; but the one who received the benefit has nothing noble in his relation to his benefactor, only something advantageous — which is both less pleasant and less an object of friendship. Pleasure comes from carrying out a present action, from looking forward to a future one, and from remembering a past one; but the keenest pleasure, and the one we love most, attends the action while it is being done. Now the benefactor's deed endures, since what is noble is lasting, while the advantage to the one helped passes away. And there is pleasure in recalling noble deeds, but little or none in recalling advantageous ones — though, oddly enough, the reverse holds for looking forward to them. Besides, to feel friendship is like acting on someone, while to be its object is like being acted upon; so the friendly feeling, and everything that goes with it, belong to the party who is, in the benefaction, the more active. One more thing: everyone loves most what has cost him effort to produce — people who made their own money are fonder of it than those who inherited it. Receiving a kindness costs no effort; doing one costs a great deal. This is also why mothers love their children more, since their share in producing them costs the most pain, and they know more surely that the children are theirs — and this feeling, too, would seem to belong to benefactors.

There is also the question of whether a man should love himself most, or someone else. People find fault with those who love themselves above all, and call them self-lovers as a reproach. The bad man, it is thought, does everything for his own sake, and the worse he is the more thoroughly he does so — people accuse him of never doing a thing that is not in his own interest. The good man, by contrast, acts from a sense of the noble, and the better he is the more he does, and he acts for his friend's sake, careless of his own interest. But the facts do not square with these theories — and no wonder. We also say a man should love most the one who is most his friend, and the one who is most a friend is the one who wishes another good for that other's own sake even where no one will ever know. Now these conditions — and all the rest by which we recognize a friend — belong first and foremost to a man in relation to himself, since, as we said, all the friendly feelings flow out to others from feelings that have the self as their first object. And all the proverbs back this up: "one soul," "friends hold all in common," "friendship is equality," "the knee is nearer than the shin." Every one of these is most fully true of a man in relation to himself, since he is most of all a friend to himself, and so is bound to love himself most.

It is reasonable to ask which of the two views to follow, since both have something to be said for them. With theories of this kind the right course is probably to sort out how far each is true, and in what sense. If we could pin down what each side means by "self-loving," the matter would clear up.

Those who use the word as a reproach give the name to people who assign themselves the larger share of money, honors, and bodily pleasures. For most people grasp after these things and take them in earnest as the best things there are, which is why they are fought over. Those who are greedy for them gratify their appetites and their passions in general — the irrational part of the soul. Most people are like this, and so the word "self-lover" took its meaning from the majority, who are low and bad; and people of that kind are justly reproached. It is plain that the crowd usually applies the term to those who give themselves the larger share of such things. For suppose a man were eager, more than anyone else, to do what is just, or temperate, or in any way virtuous — to secure for himself, in short, what is noble: no one would call him a self-lover, or blame him for it.

And yet such a man might be judged the truer self-lover. He assigns himself the noblest and best things; he gratifies the part of his nature that has the rightful authority, and obeys it in everything. Just as a city, or any other system, is most truly identified with its sovereign part, so is a man; and so the man who loves and gratifies this part is most truly a self-lover. We also call a man self-controlled or not according to whether his intellect is in command, which plainly implies that this part is what each man is; and the actions we most credit a man with having done himself, of his own will, are the ones done with reason. So it is clear that this part either wholly or chiefly is the man, and that the good man loves it above all. He is therefore a self-lover in the highest degree — but of a kind other than the one we reproach, as far above it as living by reason is above living at the beck of passion, and as aiming at the truly noble is above aiming at apparent advantage.

Everyone approves and praises those who throw themselves into noble action; and if everyone competed for the noble and strained to do what is noblest, the community as a whole would have all it needs, and each individual would have the greatest of goods — virtue. So the good man should be a self-lover, since by doing what is noble he will both benefit himself and do good to others; but the bad man should not be, since by following his low and evil passions he will harm both himself and his neighbors. In the bad man, what he does and what he ought to do pull apart, while the good man does what he ought, because intellect always chooses what is best for itself, and the good man obeys his intellect.

It is true of the good man, too, that he does many things for the sake of his friends and his country, even to the point of dying for them if need be. He will throw away money, honors, and all the goods men compete for, while clutching the noble for himself. He would rather have one year of intense joy than many tame ones, one great and noble life than a long ordinary one, one great and noble act than many small ones. This is perhaps what happens to those who die for their country and their friends: they choose great glory for themselves. They will give away their own money so that a friend may receive more — the friend gets the money, but the man himself gets the noble, and so assigns himself the greater good. It is the same with honors and offices: he will hand them all to his friend, because doing so reflects honor and praise back on himself; and with good reason he is held a fine character, since he chooses the noble before everything else. He may even surrender the chance to act to his friend, and to have been the cause of a friend's doing something may be nobler than to have done it himself. In short, in everything we praise, the good man plainly assigns himself the larger share of the noble. In this sense a man ought to be a self-lover; in the vulgar sense, he ought not.

There is also a question about the happy man: will he need friends or not? Some say that the blessed and self-sufficient have no need of friends, since they already have everything good, and, being self-sufficient, want nothing more — whereas a friend's whole office, they say, is to be a kind of second self and supply what a man cannot get for himself. Hence the line, "when fortune favors us, what need of friends?" On the other hand, it looks absurd to hand the happy man every other good and yet deny him friends, who are reckoned the greatest of external goods. And if it is more the mark of a friend to do kindnesses than to receive them, and if doing good belongs to the good man and to virtue, and if it is nobler to do kindnesses for friends than for strangers, then the good man will need people to be the objects of his kindness — which raises the further question of whether friends are needed more in good fortune or in bad, since the unlucky man needs people to help him and the fortunate man needs people to help. And it would be strange, besides, to make the happy man a solitary, since no one would choose to have every good in the world on the condition of being alone. Man is a political animal, made by nature to live with others; the happy man has this nature too, since he has everything that is good by nature; and plainly the company of friends and good men is better than the company of strangers and ordinary people. So the happy man does need friends.

What, then, did the first speakers mean, and in what way are they right? Surely it is that most people mean by "friends" those who are useful — and of course the happy man will not need those, since he already has every good; nor will he need friends for pleasure, or only slightly, since his life is already pleasant and wants no pleasure imported from outside. And because he needs no friends of these kinds, he is thought to need none at all. But this is probably not true. As we said at the start, happiness is a kind of activity, and an activity is plainly something that has to come into being, not something already in hand like a piece of property. If being happy lies in living and being active, and the good man's activity is in itself excellent and pleasant, as we said early on; and if what is our own is among the things that please us; and if we can see our neighbors better than ourselves and their actions better than our own — then the actions of good men who are our friends are pleasant to the good, since they hold both of the things naturally pleasant. So the man at the height of happiness will need friends of this kind, since he wants to contemplate actions that are good, and that are in a sense his own — which the actions of a friend, being a good man, are.

Besides, the common view requires that the happy man live a life pleasant to himself. Life is hard for the man who lives alone, since it is hard to keep working continuously by oneself, but easier in company and in relation to others; so the activity, being pleasant in itself, will be more continuous — which is just what the happy man's should be. For the good man, in that he is good, takes pleasure in actions that accord with virtue and is pained by those that spring from vice, exactly as a musical man delights in fine music and is grated on by bad. And, as Theognis says, virtue itself can be improved by practice, from living with the good.

On further and more searching grounds it will appear that the good friend is naturally a thing the good man should choose. We have said that whatever is naturally good is also in itself good and pleasant to the good man. Now living, in animals generally, is defined by the power of perception, and in man by the power of perception or of thought — and the power is always to be referred to its actual exercise, since that is the main thing — so that to live is, above all, to perceive or to think. Living is in itself one of the things that are good and pleasant, for it is something definite and complete, and whatever is such belongs to the nature of the good; and what is naturally good is good to the good man, which is why it seems pleasant to everyone. (We must not bring in a life that is corrupt and depraved, or one spent in pain, for such a life is something indefinite, like the qualities running through it; what is to be said about pain will be clearer later.)

If living is in itself good and pleasant — and it appears to be, since all desire it, and the good and the deeply happy above all, their lives being the most worth choosing and their existence likewise — then consider this. The one who sees perceives that he sees; the one who hears perceives that he hears; the one who walks perceives that he walks; and in every other case there is some power that perceives the fact that we are active, so that when we perceive, we perceive that we perceive, and when we think, we perceive that we think. But to perceive that we perceive or think is to perceive that we exist, since existence was defined as perceiving or thinking. And to perceive that one is alive is in itself a pleasant thing, since life is by nature good, and to perceive that something good is present in us is pleasant. So living is worth choosing, and to the good especially, since existence is to them good and pleasant: they take pleasure in their inner awareness of what is in itself good.

But the good man stands toward his friend as toward himself, since a friend is another self; and so, just as his own existence is worth choosing to each man, his friend's existence is worth choosing too, or nearly so. We said that one's existence is worth choosing because one perceives oneself to be good, and any such perception is pleasant in itself. So a man should be fully aware of his friend's existence as well, which comes about through living with him — sharing in his words and his thought; for this is what "living together" means for human beings, not merely feeding in the same place, as it does for cattle. If, then, for the deeply happy man his own existence is in itself worth choosing, being naturally good and pleasant, and his friend's existence is nearly as much so, then a friend too must be among the things worth choosing. And whatever is worth choosing a man must have, or else he will be deficient in just this respect. So the man who is to measure up to our idea of the happy man will need good friends.

Should we, then, make our friends as many as possible? Or, just as it has been well said of acquaintances, "have not many, yet do not be without," so with friendship might we adopt the same rule — that a man should be neither without friends nor burdened with a great many? For friends meant for use, the rule fits perfectly well, since repaying the services of many is hard work, and a whole life would not be long enough to do it. More of them, then, than your own life can handle become a nuisance and a hindrance to living well, and we do not want them. And of friends meant for pleasure, a few are quite enough, like a little seasoning in food. But of good friends, should we make as many as we possibly can? Or is there a limit to the number, as there is to the number that makes a city? You cannot make a city out of ten men, and if you swell the number to a hundred thousand it is no longer a city; though the right number is perhaps not some single figure but anything between certain bounds. So with friends too there is a limit — perhaps the largest number with whom one could keep up a shared life, since that is reckoned among the surest marks of friendship, and it is plain you cannot share your life with many, cannot divide yourself among many. And they must also be friends to one another, if they are all to live a common life, which is a hard thing to find in many men at once. It is hard, too, to enter into the joys and sorrows of many people, since one would likely have to rejoice with this one and grieve with that one at the same moment.

So perhaps the thing is not to seek as many friends as possible, but only as many as are enough for a shared life — since it would seem impossible to be much of a friend to many at once. For the same reason one cannot be in love with many at once: love is a kind of excess of friendship, and that points at a single object; and all strong feelings must be limited in the number they are felt toward. The facts seem to bear this out. Not many become friends in the way of close companionship; all the famous friendships of that kind are between two people. Those who have many friends and meet everyone on intimate terms seem to be no one's friend, except in the way of ordinary fellow-citizens — the people we call ingratiating. In the ordinary way of fellowship a man can indeed be a friend to many without being ingratiating, by being genuinely good; but one cannot be a friend to many for their virtue and for their own sakes. It is much to be content with even a few such.

Again: are friends needed more in good fortune or in bad? They are wanted in both. The unfortunate need help, and the fortunate need people to live with and to do kindnesses to, since they have a desire to act well toward someone. Having friends is more necessary in misfortune, which is why useful friends are wanted then; and having them in good fortune is nobler, which is why the fortunate seek out good men, since it is better to do kindnesses for these and to live with them. Even in misfortune the very presence of friends is pleasant, for the grieving are comforted by their friends' sympathy.

This raises the question whether friends actually take up part of the weight of our troubles, or whether it is only that their presence is pleasant, and the awareness of their sympathy, that lessens the sufferer's pain. We need not settle which of these, or what other cause, produces the relief; at any rate the relief is a plain fact. But the presence of friends probably has a mixed effect. Simply to see friends is pleasant, especially to a man in misfortune, and it helps lighten his grief, since a friend with any tact comforts by his look and his words, knowing the sufferer's temper and what gives him pleasure and pain. But to see a friend grieved at one's misfortunes is itself painful, since everyone shrinks from being a cause of pain to his friends. That is why men of a manly nature are careful not to drag their friends into their suffering, and, unless a man is utterly hardened to others' pain, he cannot bear the pain he thus causes his friends; in short, he does not let others wail with him, not being given to wailing himself. Women, and the men who are like them, like having others groan along with them, and love such people as friends and fellow-mourners. But plainly it is our duty in all things to imitate the better character.

The advantage of friends in good fortune, on the other hand, is the pleasant company and the awareness that they are glad at our success. It would seem, then, that we should call friends in readily when fortune is good, since it is noble to be ready to do others good, but reluctantly when fortune is bad, since we should make others share our troubles as little as possible — which is the point of the saying, "my own misfortune is enough." The best occasion to call them in is when, at little trouble to themselves, they can be of great use to the one who needs them. Conversely, it is perhaps fitting to go to friends in their misfortunes unasked and eagerly, since kindness is a friend's office, above all toward those in need who do not demand it as a right — which is both more creditable and more pleasant for both. And in their good fortune we should go readily if we can help it along in any way, since men need their friends for this too; but we should be slow to share in the benefits, since eagerness to receive an advantage is not creditable. One should perhaps take care, though, not to seem sullen in turning down a friend's sympathy or help, as occasionally happens. So the presence of friends turns out to be, in every circumstance, worth choosing.

May we not say, then, that just as the sight of the beloved is what lovers prize most, choosing that sense above all the others because it is chiefly through sight that love comes to be and lives, so for friends a shared life is what is most worth choosing — friendship being a communion? Again, as a man is to himself, so he is to his friend; and toward himself the perception of his own existence is worth choosing, so it is so toward his friend as well. Their friendship, besides, is acted out in a shared life, and so with good reason they desire it. Whatever each man takes existence to be, or whatever it is for the sake of which he chooses to live, that is what he wishes to do alongside his friends. And so some men drink together, others gamble, others train or hunt together, others do philosophy together — each spending his days with his friends in whatever he loves most in life; for wishing to share their lives with their friends, they do and take part in the things they think will bring that about. So the friendship of the wicked turns out depraved: being unstable, they share in bad things, and grow depraved by being made alike. But the friendship of the good is good, and grows by their company; they even improve, it seems, by acting together and correcting one another, for each takes an imprint from the other in the things that please them — whence the poet's line, "from the good you will learn good things."
