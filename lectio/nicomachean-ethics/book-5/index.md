Aristotle · Lectio Edition · 2026

# Nicomachean Ethics, Book V

Justice

Our subject now is justice and injustice: what kinds of action they are concerned with, what sort of mean justice is, and between which extremes the just itself stands as a middle. We will go about it the same way we have gone about everything else in this inquiry.

Everyone uses the word justice to mean a settled state of character — the state that makes people capable of doing just things, and that makes them actually do them and want them. Injustice is the matching state, the one that makes people act unjustly and want what is unjust. Let that rough sketch stand as our starting point. I put it that way deliberately, because a state of character does not work the way a science or a skill does. We think a single science or skill covers both members of a pair of opposites — medicine knows both health and disease — but a state of character does not. Health produces only healthy actions, not their opposites; we say a man is walking in a healthy way when he walks the way a healthy man would. Still, you can often read one of two opposite states off the other, and read the states off the things they apply to. If it is clear what good physical condition is, it becomes clear what bad condition is too, and the good condition shows itself in the things that go with it, and the reverse. If good condition is firm flesh, then bad condition is flabby flesh, and whatever produces firm flesh goes with the good condition.

As a rule, if one of two opposite terms is used in more than one sense, so is the other. If "the just" has several senses, so does "the unjust." And justice and injustice do in fact seem to be used in several senses each — but because the line between the senses is so fine, it usually goes unnoticed that the word is doing more than one job. It is not obvious, the way it is when the senses of a word are wildly far apart and the eye catches the difference at once — the way the Greek word kleis names both the collarbone under an animal's neck and the bar you use to lock a door. So let us pin down how many senses the word "unjust man" carries. We call a man unjust if he breaks the law, and we also call him unjust if he grasps for more than his share and takes unequal advantage. Plainly, then, the just man will be the one who keeps the law and the one who takes only his fair, equal share. So "the just" means the lawful and the equal, and "the unjust" means the unlawful and the unequal.

Since the unjust man is also a grasping man, he grasps after good things — though not after every kind of good. He grasps after the goods that are the stuff of good and bad fortune: things that are good in the abstract, always, but not always good for the particular person who has them. People still pray for these things and chase them, when what they should do is pray that the things good in the abstract may also be good for them, and choose what is actually good for themselves. The unjust man does not always grab the larger amount, though. Sometimes he takes the smaller — when what is on offer is simply bad. But since the lesser evil counts in a way as a good, and grasping is always a grasping after good, even here he is reckoned a grasping man: a man who reaches for more good than his fair share. He is also an unequal man, which is the wider term that covers both cases.

We said the lawbreaker is unjust and the law-keeper just. Clearly, then, everything lawful is in some sense just, since by "lawful" we mean what the lawmaking power has laid down, and every such thing we call just. And the laws speak to everything, aiming either at the common good of all, or at the good of the best, or at the good of those in power — taking real excellence as the standard, or some other measure. So in one sense we call "just" whatever tends to produce and preserve happiness, and the parts of happiness, for the political community. The law also commands the actions of a brave man — not deserting your post, not fleeing, not throwing away your weapons — and the actions of the temperate man, like keeping clear of adultery and wanton excess, and the actions of the even-tempered man, like not striking others or hurling abuse. It does the same across the whole range of the virtues and vices, commanding the one and forbidding the other — rightly, if it is a good law, less well if it was thrown together carelessly.

Justice in this sense is complete virtue — though not just in itself, but as exercised toward other people. This is why justice is often thought the best of the virtues, finer than the evening star or the morning star, and why the proverb says that in justice every virtue is summed up. It is complete virtue in a special way, because to have it is to be able to exercise virtue toward others, not only on yourself. Plenty of people can manage virtue in their own private affairs but cannot pull it off in dealings with others. There is wisdom in the saying of Bias, that office will show the man — because a man who holds office is necessarily in contact with others, bound up in a community. For this same reason justice alone among the virtues is thought to be a good that belongs to someone else, since it always bears on another person: the just man does what serves another, whether a ruler or a fellow citizen. The worst man is the one whose vice infects not only himself but his friends; the best is the one whose virtue reaches not only himself but his neighbor — for that is hard. Justice in this broad sense, then, is not one part of virtue but the whole of virtue in action, and the injustice that answers to it is not one part of vice but vice entire. How this justice differs from virtue as such is clear from what we have said: it is the very same thing, looked at from a different angle. Insofar as it bears on your neighbor, it is justice; insofar as it is simply this kind of settled character, it is virtue.

But what we are really after is justice as a part of virtue — for there is such a thing, as we ordinarily speak — and likewise injustice as a part of vice. Here is the proof that this particular injustice exists. A man can act unjustly through any number of vices without grasping for more than his share: he throws away his shield out of cowardice, or hurls abuse out of bad temper, or refuses a friend money out of stinginess. But sometimes a man does a grasping thing that traces to none of those vices, certainly not to all of them — and yet to some vice, since we do blame him — and that vice is injustice. So there is a kind of injustice distinct from the injustice that is coextensive with vice as a whole, related to it as a part to the whole. And there is a corresponding "unjust" related to the unlawful, again as part to whole. Take two cases. One man sleeps with another's wife for profit and actually comes out ahead by it; another does the same thing out of sheer lust, at the cost of money and to his own harm. We would call the second man self-indulgent rather than grasping, and the first unjust but not self-indulgent. Why? Plainly because the first man gained. Every other unjust act we trace back to some particular vice — adultery to runaway appetite, deserting a comrade to cowardice, throwing a punch to anger. But if a man gains by the act, we trace it to no vice except injustice. So there is clearly a kind of injustice, distinct from and alongside the injustice that takes in all vice, sharing the same name because its definition falls in the same family. Both have their force in dealings with others; but the one operates on honor, money, safety — whatever single name might cover all of these — and is driven by the pleasure that comes with gain, while the other operates across everything the good man's conduct touches.

That there is more than one kind of justice, then — that there is one distinct from and alongside the justice coextensive with virtue — is clear. We must next find out what it is and what it is like. We divided the unjust into the unlawful and the unequal, and the just into the lawful and the equal. The broad injustice already discussed runs along the unlawful. But the unequal and the merely-more are not the same; they differ as part to whole, since everything more is unequal, but not everything unequal is more. So this unjust, and this injustice, are not the same as the earlier ones but distinct — the part as against the whole. This particular injustice is a part of the injustice coextensive with vice, and this justice a part of the justice coextensive with virtue. So what we have to discuss now is particular justice and injustice, and the particular just and unjust. Let us set aside the justice that ranks as complete virtue — virtue practiced toward others in all its forms — and the injustice that answers to it. It is plain enough, too, how to mark off the just and unjust that belong to it: most lawful acts are simply the acts that naturally flow from virtue in its full sense, since the law commands living by each virtue and forbids living by each vice, and the things that produce virtue across the board are the measures laid down for a citizen's education. (Whether the education that makes a man simply good, without reference to others, belongs to political science or some other science, we can settle later — for it may be that being a good man and being a good citizen are not always the same thing.)

Of this particular justice, and the just that goes with it, there are two kinds. One has to do with the distribution of honor, money, or anything else divisible among the members of the community — for here one man's share, set beside another's, can be equal or unequal. The other is the corrective justice that sets right the dealings between one person and another. And this second kind has two parts, because some dealings are voluntary and some involuntary. The voluntary ones include selling, buying, lending at interest, putting up security, lending without interest, leaving goods on deposit, and hiring — called voluntary because the parties enter into them by their own choice. The involuntary ones either work in secret — theft, adultery, poisoning, pandering, luring away slaves, killing by stealth, bearing false witness — or are done by open force: assault, imprisonment, murder, robbery, maiming, insult, public abuse.

We said the unjust man is the unequal man, and the unjust thing the unequal thing. Plainly, then, there is a mean of the unequal — namely the equal, the exact midpoint; for wherever there is a more and a less, there is also an equal. So if the unjust is the unequal, the just is the equal — which everyone grants without needing it proved. And since the equal is a mean, the just is a mean too. Now the equal requires at least two terms. So the just turns out to be both a mean and an equal, and to be these for certain people: a mean between certain amounts — the greater and the less — equal between two parties, and just toward certain persons. The just, then, involves at least four terms: two persons for whom it is just, and two things being shared. And the equality must hold between the persons as it holds between the things, since as the things stand to one another, so must the persons. If the persons are not equal, they must not get equal shares. This is the very root of all the quarreling and grievance in the world — when equals are awarded unequal shares, or unequals are awarded equal ones. The need for this matching of ratios is plain from the common phrase "according to merit." Everyone agrees that just distribution must go by some standard of merit, even if they do not agree on what the standard is: democrats say free birth, oligarchs wealth, others noble descent, aristocrats excellence.

So the just, in distribution, is a proportional thing. Proportion is not a feature of numbers alone but of quantity in general — it is equality of ratios, and it requires at least four terms. (This is obvious in what we may call separated proportion, with four distinct terms; but it holds even in continuous proportion, where one term is used twice and counted as two, as when A is to B as B is to C — there B does double duty.) The just likewise requires at least four terms, and the ratio between the two pairs is the same, because the persons and the things are divided alike. As person A stands to person B, so thing C stands to thing D; combine them and the share that joins the first person to his thing, set against the share that joins the second person to his, keeps the same ratio. The distribution is just exactly when it joins the terms so as to preserve this ratio. Mathematicians call this kind of proportion geometric, since in geometric proportion the whole stands to the whole as each part stands to its corresponding part. So the just, in distribution, is this proportional thing, and the unjust is what breaks the proportion. When the proportion is broken, one share grows greater and the other smaller — which is exactly what happens in real life: the man who acts unjustly ends up with more of the good, and the man wronged with less. With bad things it runs the other way, since the lesser evil, set against the greater, counts as a good — the lesser evil being more worth choosing, and what is worth choosing is a good, the more so the greater the good. This, then, is one kind of the just.

The remaining kind is the corrective, which arises in dealings both voluntary and involuntary. This just has a different form from the last. The justice that governs the sharing of common property always follows the proportion just described — shares come out in the same ratio as what each person put in. The injustice opposed to it is the breaking of that proportion. But the justice in private dealings, though it is still a kind of equality, follows a different proportion — not the geometric one but the arithmetic. Here it makes no difference whether a good man has robbed a bad one or a bad man a good one, whether the adulterer is a decent man or a scoundrel. The law looks only at the harm done; it treats the parties as equals and asks merely who did the wrong and who suffered it, who inflicted the damage and who took it. So when this injustice — this inequality — has arisen, the judge tries to even it out again. When one man has been struck and the other has struck him, or one has killed and the other lies dead, the suffering and the doing have been split into unequal shares; the judge tries to restore equality by means of the penalty, subtracting from the gain. (We do apply the words "gain" and "loss" to these cases, even where they do not strictly fit — calling what the man who threw the punch got a "gain," and what the man who took it suffered a "loss" — but once the harm has been measured, the one side is called loss and the other gain.) So the equal is a mean between the more and the less, and gain and loss are the more and the less running in opposite directions: more good and less evil is gain, less good and more evil is loss. The equal, the mean between them, is the just. So the corrective just is the mean between loss and gain.

This is why people in a dispute turn to a judge. To go to the judge is to go to justice itself, for the judge is meant to be justice come alive. People look for a judge as a man in the middle; some even call judges "middlemen," on the idea that if they can hit the middle they will hit the just. So the just is a mean, since the judge is. The judge's job is to make things equal. It is as though a line had been divided unevenly: he takes from the longer part the amount by which it overshoots the half, and adds that to the shorter. When the whole has been split into two equal parts, then people say they have what is their own — they have gotten the equal. The equal here is the arithmetic mean between the greater and the less. This, by the way, is where our Greek words for "just" and "judge" come from: dikaion, the just, is as if to say dichaion, "in two halves," and the judge, dikastes, is the dichastes, "the halver." When you take a piece from one of two equal amounts and add it to the other, the second now exceeds the first by twice that piece; the excess of the larger over the mean must be taken from the larger, and the excess of the mean over the smaller must be added to the smaller. The words "loss" and "gain" themselves come from voluntary exchange: to have more than you started with is to gain, to have less is to lose — as in buying and selling and the other dealings the law leaves to private agreement. When the outcome leaves you with neither more nor less but exactly what you began with, people say they have their own, and neither lose nor gain. So the corrective just is a mean between loss and gain in involuntary dealings: it is having the same after the transaction as you had before.

Some people think that justice is simply reciprocity — being done to as you have done. The Pythagoreans held this; they defined the just, flatly and without qualification, as "suffering in return what you have done to another." But bare reciprocity does not fit either distributive or corrective justice — though people read it into the saying of Rhadamanthus: "If a man should suffer what he did, that would be straight justice." In plenty of cases the two come apart. If an official strikes a man, the official must not be struck back; but if a man strikes an official, he must not only be struck but punished as well. And whether the act was willing or unwilling makes a great difference. Still, in dealings of exchange it is exactly this kind of justice — reciprocity — that binds people together, but reciprocity by proportion, not by flat equality. A community holds together by proportionate give-and-take. People want either to return evil for evil — and where they cannot, they feel themselves no better than slaves — or to return good for good; and where they cannot, no exchange happens, and it is exchange that binds them. This is the point of putting the temple of the Graces in the public square: to remind people to return a favor, for that is what belongs to grace. A man who has received a kindness should repay it, and then begin a fresh round of grace by doing a kindness himself.

Exchange in due proportion can be pictured by the diagonals of a figure. Set the parties at two corners and their goods at the other two, so that each party is linked along one side to his own goods, and the line joining the parties runs opposite the line joining the goods. The builder is to take from the shoemaker the shoemaker's product and give the shoemaker his own in return. If there is first a proportional equality, and reciprocity follows, the just result we are after comes about. If not, the deal is not equal and will not hold — for there is no reason one man's product should not be worth more than the other's, and so the products must be made equal before the exchange. The same holds in every craft. Crafts would be wiped out altogether if there were no matching of what the producer makes to what the consumer takes, in amount and in quality. No exchange arises between two of the same kind — two doctors do not trade with each other — but between a doctor and a farmer, between people who are different and unequal; and these must be brought to equality before they can trade.

So everything that is to be exchanged must somehow be comparable. This is why money came in. Money is a kind of middle term: it measures everything, and so measures excess and shortfall — how many shoes are worth a house, or worth a given amount of food. As builder is to shoemaker, so a certain number of shoes must be to the house (or to the food, if a farmer takes the builder's place). Without this proportion there is no exchange and no dealing, and there is no proportion unless the goods are in some way made equal. Hence the need, as we said, for one single measure of all things. And that measure is, in truth, demand — the need people have, which holds all dealings together. If people needed nothing, or did not need each other's goods, there would be no exchange, or not the same exchange. By common agreement, money has come to stand in for demand. That is why it has the name it does: the Greek for money, nomisma, comes from nomos, custom or law — because money is what it is not by nature but by custom, and it lies with us to change its value or to make it worthless. There will be reciprocity, then, once the goods have been made equal — so that as farmer is to shoemaker, so the shoemaker's goods are to the farmer's. They must be brought into that proportion before they trade. Otherwise one side will run away with both extremes — too much profit at the other's expense. But when each has exactly his own, the parties are equal and can deal, because the same equality can hold for both. What links people in their dealings is demand, as a single common thing — shown by the fact that when one party does not want what the other has, or neither wants the other's, they do not trade at all; they trade only when one wants what the other holds, giving grain, say, for wine.

Money also serves as a kind of guarantee for future exchange: if we need nothing now, money assures us we can get goods when we do. The principle of money is that whoever brings it can take goods in exchange. Money too loses value, of course — its buying power is not always the same — but it holds steadier than the goods it stands for. This is why everything should be priced: so that exchange is always possible, and where there is exchange there is dealing. So money, like a measuring rod, makes all things commensurable and brings them to equality. Without exchange there would be no dealing, without equality no exchange, and without commensurability no equality. Strictly, things so wildly different cannot be made truly commensurable, but in reference to demand we can come close enough for all practical purposes. The common measure must be a single thing, and one fixed by agreement — which is why it is called nomisma — for that is what makes everything commensurable. In the end, all things are measured by money. Let a house be worth five minae and a bed one mina; then it is clear how many beds equal a house — five. Plainly this is how exchange worked before money existed: it makes no difference whether you give five beds for a house or the price of five beds.

We have now said what the just and the unjust are in the abstract. With those defined, it is clear that acting justly is a mean between acting unjustly and being treated unjustly — the first being to have more than your share, the second to have less. But justice is a mean in a different way from the other virtues. It is not itself poised between two vices; rather, it aims at the mean, while injustice occupies both extremes. Justice is the settled state that makes a man able, by deliberate choice, to do what is just — to divide things between himself and another, or between two other people, so as to give himself not the larger and his neighbor the smaller share of what is worth having (and the reverse with what is harmful), but a proportionally equal share, and to do the same when judging between two others. Injustice is the matching state with respect to the unjust. Since the unjust is an excess or a deficiency of what is beneficial or harmful, in breach of proportion, injustice is both excess and deficiency, because it aims at producing both: excess, in one's own case, of what is simply beneficial, and deficiency of what is harmful; and in the case of others much the same, except that the proportion may be broken in either direction, however it happens to fall. Of the unjust act, the lesser part is being treated unjustly, and the greater part is treating others so. Let this stand as an adequate account of the nature of justice and injustice, and of the just and the unjust in general.

A man may do unjust things without yet having a settled unjust character — so we may ask whether a man becomes unjust in a given form, a thief or an adulterer, simply by doing an act of that kind. The answer, I think, is that the act alone does not settle it. A man might sleep with another's wife, knowing full well whose wife she was, and yet do it not by deliberate choice but on a surge of passion. He acts unjustly, certainly, but he has not thereby formed an unjust character. He may have stolen without being a thief, committed adultery without being an adulterer, and so on. We have already spoken of how reciprocity stands to the just. Here we should add that the justice we are looking into is both justice in the abstract and justice as it appears in political life — and political justice holds among people who share a life with a view to self-sufficiency, who are free and equal, whether equal by proportion or by number. So among people not in this position there is no political justice between them, only justice of a kind, by resemblance. For justice exists only where there is law to govern the relations of people, and law only where injustice is possible, since the giving of judgment is the act of marking off the just from the unjust. And wherever injustice is possible, acting unjustly is possible too — though not the reverse: not everyone capable of acting unjustly is therefore unjust by character; for to be unjust by character is to give oneself the larger share of what is good in the abstract and the smaller share of what is bad.

This is why we do not let a man rule, but let reason rule — because a man rules in his own interest and turns into a tyrant. A ruler is meant to be the guardian of the just, and so of the equal. If he is a just man, he seems to get no special advantage of his own, since he does not allot himself the larger share of what is good in the abstract, except as it falls to him by proportion. So he really rules for others — which is why people say justice is a good belonging to someone else. Some compensation, then, is owed him, and it comes in the form of honor and privilege; where these fall short, rulers turn into tyrants. The justice between master and slave, and between father and child, is not the same as political justice but only resembles it. There can be no injustice, strictly, toward what is simply your own; and a slave, or a child while still young and not yet broken off into a separate person, is as it were a part of yourself — and no one chooses to harm himself. So there is no injustice toward oneself, and therefore none of the political just or unjust here, which we said holds by law and among those whose nature it is to live under law — and those are people who share equally in ruling and being ruled. This is also why there is more room for justice between husband and wife than between a man and his children or his slaves: that is household justice, and it too differs from political justice.

Political justice itself comes in two kinds, natural and conventional. The natural is what has the same force everywhere and does not depend on people's accepting it or not. The conventional is what could just as well have been set up one way or the other before it was fixed, but is no longer indifferent once it has been laid down — like fixing a ransom at one mina, or sacrificing one goat rather than two sheep, or any rule passed for a particular occasion, such as the sacrifice in honor of Brasidas; in short, anything settled by specific decree. Some people think all justice is conventional, on the grounds that what exists by nature is unchangeable and has the same force everywhere — fire burns the same here as it does in Persia — whereas they see what is counted just shifting from place to place. That is not quite right, though there is something to it. Among the gods, perhaps, nothing of the just changes at all; but among us there is something that exists by nature, even granting that all of it is subject to change. Even so, we can mark off, well enough in practice, what among changeable things exists by nature and what does not but rests on convention and agreement — granting that both alike can be altered. The same distinction applies elsewhere: the right hand is naturally the stronger, yet some people can become equally skilled with both. Conventional just things can be compared to measures: the measures for wine and grain are not the same everywhere — larger where the goods are bought wholesale, smaller where they are sold. In the same way, the just things that are not natural but humanly devised are not everywhere the same, just as constitutions are not the same everywhere — though only one constitution is everywhere, by nature, the best.

Every just and lawful thing stands to the acts that embody it as a universal stands to its particulars: the acts are many, but each principle is single, since each is universal. So there is a difference between an unjust act and the unjust in the abstract, and between a just act and the just in the abstract. A thing is unjust in itself, by nature or by ordinance; once that thing has been carried out in an action, there is an unjust act — but not before; before that there is only an unjust thing. The same goes for a just act. A man is said to act unjustly or justly when he embodies these abstracts in voluntary actions; when he embodies them involuntarily, he acts neither justly nor unjustly except by accident. Whether an act counts as just or unjust depends on its being voluntary or not: when the embodying of the unjust is voluntary, it is blamed and is at the same time an unjust act; without voluntariness there is something unjust in itself but not yet an unjust act. By voluntary I mean, as before, whatever a man does that is in his own power, knowingly — not in ignorance of whom he is acting on, with what, and to what end. He must know whom he is striking, with what, and with what likely result, and none of this by accident or under compulsion. If someone seized his hand and used it to strike a third party, he does not act voluntarily, since it was not up to him. Or he may know he is striking a man, even one of those present, yet not know it is his father. Apply the same to the result and to the whole of any action. In short, what is done through ignorance, or not through ignorance but not in the agent's control or under compulsion, is involuntary. I mention this because there are many natural things we do and undergo knowingly that are still neither voluntary nor involuntary — growing old, for instance, or dying.

Accident can attach to unjust acts just as it can to just ones. A man might return a deposit, but against his will and only from fear of the consequences of refusing; we should not say he does what is just, or acts justly, except by accident. Likewise the man who fails through compulsion and against his will to return a deposit acts unjustly, or does the unjust, only by accident. Voluntary actions we do either from deliberate choice or without it — from choice when we have first deliberated, without choice when we have not. There are three kinds of harm that can be done in dealings between people. When the harm is done with ignorance, it is a mere mistake: the agent did the thing to a person he did not mean to, or did not the thing he meant, or not with the instrument or to the result he intended — he did not think he would hit the man at all, or not with this, or not this man, or did not expect this result; he meant to prick, not to wound. When the harm comes about against all reasonable expectation, it is a misfortune. When it is not against expectation but carries no malice, it is a blunder — a man blunders when the source of the error lies in himself, and meets misfortune when the source lies outside him. When he acts knowingly but without prior deliberation, it is an unjust act — for instance, whatever men do in anger or under other passions that are unavoidable or natural. In doing such harms they do act unjustly, and their acts are unjust, but they are not yet confirmed unjust or wicked men on that account, because the harm did not spring from depravity. When it does spring from deliberate choice, then the man is a confirmed unjust and depraved character.

On this principle, acts done in anger are rightly judged not to be done with malice aforethought, since the man who acts in anger is not the real instigator — the one who provoked his anger is. And the dispute in such cases is not about the facts but about who is in the right, the anger having been roused by a sense of injury. Unlike disputes over a contract, where one party must be lying unless he can plead genuine forgetfulness, here both sides agree on what happened and quarrel over which of them has justice on his side — the real aggressor, the one who laid the plot, cannot be ignorant of what he did. So one side thinks a wrong was done and the other does not. A man acts unjustly, then, if he harms another on purpose; and a man who commits such acts of injustice, in breach of proportion or of equality, is by that very fact an unjust character. In the same way a man is a just character when he acts justly by deliberate choice — and he acts justly if he acts voluntarily. As for involuntary harms, some are pardonable and some are not. Those done not merely in ignorance but from ignorance are pardonable; those done not from ignorance but in ignorance, where the ignorance is caused by some passion that is neither natural nor a fair part of human weakness, are not.

We may ask whether we have said enough about being treated unjustly and treating others unjustly. First, is the case possible that Euripides puts, strangely, when he writes: "My mother he has slain; the tale is short — he willing, and she willing; or she willing, and he against his will"? In other words, can a person be treated unjustly with his own consent, or must every case of being treated unjustly be against the sufferer's will, just as every act of treating someone unjustly is voluntary? And next, are all cases of being treated unjustly of one kind — involuntary — or are some voluntary and some not? The same questions arise for being treated justly. All acting justly is voluntary, so it is reasonable to expect the two — being treated unjustly and being treated justly — to line up the same way, each either voluntary or involuntary. Yet it would be odd to say that every case of being treated justly is voluntary, since some people are treated justly against their will.

One might also fairly ask whether everyone who has suffered something unjust has thereby been treated unjustly, or whether suffering works like acting: in both, a man can take part in what is just or unjust only by accident. Plainly it is the same with the unjust. Doing things that are unjust in themselves is not the same as acting unjustly, and suffering them is not the same as being treated unjustly. The same holds for acting justly and being treated justly, since no one can be treated unjustly unless someone else acts unjustly, and no one treated justly unless someone else acts justly. Now if acting unjustly is simply harming someone voluntarily — knowing whom you harm, with what, and how — and if the man who lacks self-control harms himself voluntarily, then it would seem a man could be treated unjustly with his own consent, and could treat himself unjustly. (This is in fact one of the questions raised: whether a man can treat himself unjustly.) Or again, a man might, through his own lack of self-control, be harmed by another acting voluntarily — another case, it seems, of being treated unjustly with one's consent. The solution, I think, is that our definition of being treated unjustly is incomplete. We must add, to harming with knowledge of the person, the instrument, and the manner, the further condition that it is against the wish of the man harmed. So a man can be harmed and can suffer what is in itself unjust voluntarily — but treated unjustly voluntarily no one can be, since no one wishes to be harmed, not even the man who lacks self-control. He acts against his own wish, for no one wishes for what he does not think good, and the man who lacks self-control does not do what he thinks he ought. And a man who gives away his own property — as Homer says Glaucus gave Diomedes "gold armor for bronze, the worth of a hundred oxen for the worth of nine" — is not treated unjustly, since the giving rests entirely with him, whereas being treated unjustly does not: there must be someone else doing the wrong. So it is clear that being treated unjustly is not voluntary.

Two points remain that we set out to discuss. First, in a faulty distribution, is the man who acts unjustly the one who awarded the larger share against proper merit, or the one who got it? And second, can a man treat himself unjustly? On the first: if it is the distributor who acts unjustly and not the man with the larger share, then when a person knowingly and willingly gives more to another than to himself, he is treating himself unjustly — which is what decent, moderate men are thought to do, since it is a mark of the fair-minded man to take less than his due. But the case is not quite as stated. The man who gave away the larger share did get more of some other good — reputation, say, or the simple honor of the thing. And the difficulty dissolves by the definition of unjust treatment: the man suffers nothing against his own wish, so on that score at least he is not treated unjustly; at most he is harmed. It is also clear that the one who acts unjustly is the distributor, not the man who ends up with the larger share. For what makes an act unjust is not merely that the unjust attaches to what a man does, but that he does it voluntarily — and the source of the action lies with the distributor, not the receiver. Besides, "doing" is said in several senses: in one sense lifeless things kill, or a hand does, or a slave at his master's command; the receiver in our case does not act unjustly but does things that are in themselves unjust. Again, if a man makes a wrong award in ignorance, he does not act unjustly in the eye of the law, nor is his judgment unjust — though it is unjust in a sense, for the legally just and the primary, natural just are not the same. But if he gave the wrong judgment knowingly, then he too grasps at a larger share — of gratitude from the one party, or of revenge against the other. So the man who judges wrongly from such motives gets more, in exactly the way a man does who takes a cut of the unjust gain itself: the judge who wrongly awarded a field did not get land but money for his unjust verdict.

People suppose that acting unjustly is entirely up to them, and conclude that acting justly must be just as easy. It is not. To sleep with a neighbor's wife, or strike a neighbor, or pass money from your hand — that is easy and up to you. But to do these things from a certain inner disposition is neither easy nor wholly up to you. In the same way, people think there is nothing clever in knowing what is just and unjust, since it is no great feat to grasp what the laws talk about. They forget that these are just acts only by accident: to be truly just, the acts must be done and apportioned in a certain way, and that is a harder task than knowing what is good for health. There it is easy enough to know about honey, wine, hellebore, cautery, and the knife — but knowing how to apply them for health, to whom, and when, is the whole work of being a doctor. The same mistake makes people think the just man is no less able to act unjustly — that he can do every one of these acts just as readily, indeed more readily, than the unjust man: he too can lie with a woman or strike a man, and the brave man can throw away his shield and turn and run any direction he likes. True. But cowardice and injustice are not the mere doing of these things, except by accident; they are the doing of them from a certain inner state — just as practicing medicine and healing are not the mere using of the knife or the drug, but the using of them in a certain way. The principles of justice operate among those who share in what is good in the abstract and who can have too much or too little of it. Some beings can never have too much — the gods, perhaps. For others, the incurably wicked, no amount of these goods does any good at all; everything harms them. For others still they are good up to a point. So justice has its place among human beings.

We have next to speak of equity and the equitable — how equity stands to justice, and the equitable to the just. Looked at closely, they are neither flatly identical nor different in kind. Sometimes we praise the equitable, and the man who embodies it, and we even transfer the word to other things as a term of praise in place of "good," showing that the more equitable a thing is, the better. At other times, following the argument out, we reach a puzzle: if the equitable is something apart from the just and yet praiseworthy, then either the just is not good, or the equitable is not just — since by assumption they differ; or if both are good, they are the same thing. That is a fair statement of the difficulty. But it all reconciles, and there is no real contradiction. The equitable is just; it is better than one kind of just, but it is not better than the just as though it were a different kind of thing. The just and the equitable are the same, and both being good, the equitable is the better of the two.

What creates the difficulty is this: the equitable is just, but not the just that goes by the letter of the law — rather, it is a correction of that. And the reason is that every law is framed in universal terms, while there are some things about which it is not possible to lay down a correct universal rule. So where a universal rule is needed but cannot fit every case, the law takes the general run of cases, fully aware that it does not capture them all. And it is no less right for that, since the fault lies not in the law, nor in the lawmaker, but in the nature of the case — for that is just how the material of conduct is. So when the law states a general rule, and a case turns up that the rule does not fit, then it is right, where the lawmaker has fallen short and erred by speaking without qualification, to correct the gap — to rule as the lawmaker himself would rule if he were present, and as he would have written into the law had he foreseen the case. This is why the equitable is just, and better than one kind of just — not better than the just in the abstract, but better than the error that comes of speaking in unqualified terms. The very nature of the equitable is this: a correction of law where law falls short by reason of its generality. It is also why not everything can be settled by law: there are matters about which no law can possibly be laid down, so that we need a special ruling for the particular case. For the measure of what is indeterminate must itself be indeterminate — like the leaden rule the builders of Lesbos use, which bends to the shape of each stone instead of staying rigid; just so, the special ruling bends to the facts of the case. It is clear, then, what the equitable is: it is the just, and better than one kind of just. And it is clear who the equitable man is — the man inclined to choose and do such things, who does not stand on his rights to a fault but is willing to take less than his share even when the law is on his side. This settled state is equity, a species of justice and not some separate state of character.

The answer to the other question we raised — whether a man can treat himself unjustly — follows from what we have said. One class of just things is what the law commands in accordance with virtue in its fullest sense: the law, for instance, does not bid a man kill himself, and whatever it does not bid, it forbids. So whenever a man does harm in violation of the law, voluntarily — knowing whom he harms and with what — and not in return for a harm done to him, he acts unjustly. The man who kills himself in a rage does this voluntarily, against right reason, which the law does not allow. So he acts unjustly — but toward whom? Toward the city, not toward himself, since he suffers it with his own consent, and no one can be treated unjustly with his own consent. This is why the city punishes him: a certain dishonor attaches to the suicide, as to one who has wronged the city. A man cannot treat himself unjustly in the other sense either — the sense in which a man is unjust who does unjust acts without being thoroughly bad. (These are different: the unjust man is bad in a way, as the coward is, without being charged with badness in the full sense, so he does not act unjustly in that full sense.) For if he could, the same thing would have been taken from and added to the same person at once — which is impossible; the just and the unjust always require more than one person. Besides, an unjust act must be voluntary, deliberately chosen, and unprovoked — for the man who harms in return for harm first suffered is not thought to act unjustly — whereas here the man would be doing and suffering the same thing at the same moment. It would also imply that a man could be treated unjustly with his own consent. And on top of all this, no man can act unjustly without his act falling under some specific wrong: a man cannot seduce his own wife, burgle his own house, or steal his own property. In the end the whole question is answered by what we settled about being treated unjustly with one's own consent.

It is plain, too, that being treated unjustly and treating others unjustly are both bad — the one is having less than the mean, the other more — the mean being here what health is in medicine and fitness is in training. Still, treating others unjustly is the worse of the two, for it involves wickedness and deserves blame — wickedness either complete, or close to it, since not every voluntary wrong amounts to injustice — whereas being treated unjustly involves no wickedness or injustice on the sufferer's part. In itself, then, being treated unjustly is the lesser evil, though by accident it may turn out the greater. Such accidents are beyond the reach of any precise statement: a doctor calls pleurisy a greater bodily evil than a bruise, yet by accident the bruise may prove the greater, if a man takes a fall, is bruised, and so is captured by the enemy and killed. Finally, there can be justice of a sort toward oneself — not in the strict sense, between a man and himself, but by metaphor and likeness, between the parts of his nature; and not justice of every kind, only the kind that holds between master and slave, or between the head of a household and his dependents. For throughout this inquiry we have treated the rational part of the soul as distinct from the irrational. Looking to these parts, people suppose there can be injustice toward oneself, since a man can suffer, in one part, against the impulses of another part that are truly his own — and so they think there is a kind of justice between these parts, as between ruler and ruled. Let this stand as our account of justice and the other moral virtues.
