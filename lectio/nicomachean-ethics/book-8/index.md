# Nicomachean Ethics, Book VIII

Friendship

After the virtues, friendship is the natural next subject, for friendship is either a virtue itself or something bound up with virtue, and beyond that it is one of the things life most needs. No one would choose to live without friends even if he had every other good thing in the world. In fact the rich, and people who hold power and office, are thought to need friends most of all. What good is such prosperity if you are stripped of the chance to do good to others, which is done above all, and most admirably, for friends? And how could prosperity be kept safe without friends? The greater it is, the more precarious. In poverty too, and in every other kind of trouble, people think their friends are the one refuge they have.

Friendship helps the young to keep from going wrong, and the old to be cared for and to make up for what their failing strength leaves undone, and those in their prime to act nobly — "two going together," as Homer says, for two are better able to think and to act than one. It seems, moreover, to be planted in us by nature: a parent feels it toward its offspring and the offspring toward the parent, and not only in human beings but in birds and most animals; members of the same race feel it toward one another, and human beings toward other human beings especially, which is why we praise those who love their fellow men. You can see in traveling how every man is kin and friend to every other.

Friendship also seems to be what holds communities together, and lawgivers seem to care about it even more than about justice; for concord is something close to friendship, and concord is what they aim at most, while what they most try to drive out is faction, which is its enemy. Where people are friends, there is no need of justice besides, but where they are merely just, they still need friendship; and the truest form of justice is thought to be a kind of friendly thing. Friendship is not only necessary but admirable. We praise those who love their friends, and having many friends is counted to a man's credit. Some even hold that to be a good man and to be a friend come to the same thing.

Yet there is no shortage of disputed points about it. Some lay it down that friendship is a kind of likeness, and that like is friend to like — hence the sayings "like to like" and "birds of a feather." Others say the opposite: that two of a trade never agree. Some push the question higher and answer it in terms of nature. Euripides says the parched earth loves the rain, and the heavy sky, swollen with rain, loves to fall upon the earth. Heraclitus holds that what opposes helps, that the finest harmony comes out of differences, and that everything comes to be through strife. Empedocles, against them, says that like reaches for like. These questions about nature we may set aside; they belong to another inquiry. We will look at what is properly human — what concerns character and feeling.

Here, for instance, is one such question: does friendship arise among all people without distinction, or is it impossible for bad men to be friends? And another: is there only one kind of friendship, or several? People who think there is only one, on the ground that friendship admits of degrees, are arguing from too little; for things that differ in kind can also admit of degrees. (We have discussed that elsewhere.)

These points will come clear once we have settled what it is that is the proper object of friendship. For it is thought that not just anything is loved, but only what is lovable — which is to say, what is good, or pleasant, or useful. And the useful, it would seem, is whatever brings about some good or some pleasure; so that the good and the pleasant are the lovable things as ends in themselves. There is a question here: do men feel friendship for what is good without qualification, or for what is good to themselves? The two sometimes come apart — and the same goes for the pleasant. It seems that each man feels it toward what is good to himself; that without qualification the real good is lovable, and to each man what is good to him. So it comes to this: each man feels friendship not for what is good, but for what strikes him as good to himself. This makes no real difference, though, since what is truly lovable will also strike him that way.

There are, then, three grounds on which men feel friendship. But we do not use the word for fondness toward lifeless things, since there is no return of the feeling, and no wishing of good to the object. It would be absurd to say a man wishes his wine well; if anything, he wishes it kept sound — for his own use. Toward a friend, they say, one should wish good for the friend's own sake. When someone wishes good to another this way and it is not returned, we call him merely well-disposed; for friendship, as they define it, is mutual goodwill. But should we not add that the goodwill must be known to both? Many people feel goodwill toward those they have never met but believe to be decent or useful — and the other might feel just the same toward them. They are plainly well-disposed to one another, then; but how can we call them friends when neither knows how the other feels? To be friends, then, they must have goodwill toward each other, must wish each other good on one of the grounds named, and must each know the other feels it.

Since the grounds of friendship differ in kind, so do the feelings and the friendships. There are three kinds of friendship, equal in number to the things that can be loved; for in each there can be mutual goodwill that both are aware of. Now those who are friends wish each other good on the ground of their friendship. So those whose ground is usefulness do not love each other for themselves, but only for the good that comes to them from each other. The same holds for those whose ground is pleasure: they love witty people not for their character but because they find them pleasant. Friends of utility, then, love what is good to themselves; friends of pleasure, what is pleasant to themselves — not loving the friend for being who he is, but for being useful or pleasant.

These friendships, then, are matters of incidental result, since the man is not loved for being who he is but for supplying some advantage or some pleasure. Friendships of this kind are easily dissolved when the parties stop being what they were; for once the others are no longer pleasant or useful, the feeling stops. And usefulness is not a lasting thing but shifts from moment to moment; so when the ground of the friendship is gone, the friendship dissolves with it, since it existed only for the sake of that ground. This kind of friendship is found mostly among the old (who at that age pursue not what is pleasant but what is profitable), and among such of the young and the prime-aged as chase advantage. People like this do not even live much in each other's company; sometimes they are not even pleasant to each other, and they have no wish for such company unless there is profit in it, since they please each other only so far as there is hope of gain. With these we usually rank the friendship of host and guest.

The friendship of the young is thought to rest on pleasure, since they live by feeling and chase above all what is pleasant to them and present to the moment; and as their age changes, so do their pleasures. This is why they form and break friendships fast — the friendship shifts as the pleasant object shifts, and that kind of pleasure shifts quickly. The young are also much given to love, which is largely a matter of feeling and rests on pleasure; so they fall into friendships and drop them quickly, changing often within a single day. But they do want to spend their days and live in close company with their friends, for that is how they get what they want from the friendship.

The complete friendship is the one between people who are good and alike in their goodness. These wish each other good in the same way, just insofar as they are good — and good is what they are in themselves. Those who wish good to their friends for the friends' own sake are friends most of all, for they feel this on their friends' own account and not as some incidental result. So this friendship lasts as long as they are good, and goodness is a lasting thing. Each is good without qualification and good for his friend as well, since good men are both good in themselves and useful to one another. They are pleasant to each other too, for good men are pleasant both in themselves and to one another; each takes pleasure in the actions that suit his own nature, and good men's actions are of one kind, or at least alike.

A friendship like this, then, is naturally lasting, since it brings together everything friends should have. Every friendship is for the sake of good or pleasure — either without qualification or relative to the one who feels it — and rests on some likeness; and in this kind all these belong to the friends themselves, for here they are alike, and the rest follows. In it there is the good without qualification and the pleasant without qualification, and since these are most of all the objects of friendship, the feeling and the state of friendship are at their strongest and best in people of this sort. Such friendships are likely to be rare, since such men are rare. Besides, they take time and familiarity; for as the proverb says, men cannot know each other until they have eaten the salt together, nor can they admit one another to intimacy, much less be friends, until each has shown himself to the other to be worthy of friendship. People who strike up friendly exchanges quickly may wish to be friends, but they are not friends unless they are also lovable and know it of each other. A wish for friendship comes quickly; friendship itself does not.

This friendship, then, is complete both in time and in every other respect, and each gets from the other the same or nearly the same — which is how it should be between friends. The friendship based on pleasure is a copy of this one, since good men are pleasant to each other; and so is the friendship based on usefulness, since good men are useful to each other. Among such people friendships last longest when the same thing comes to each from the other — pleasure, say — and not merely the same thing but from the same source, as with two witty people, and not as with a lover and the one he loves. These two do not draw their pleasure from the same thing: the lover from looking at the beloved, the beloved from the attentions paid him. So when the bloom of youth fades the friendship sometimes ends too, since the lover no longer enjoys the sight and the beloved no longer gets the attentions. But many do stay friends, if from long company they have come to love each other's character, being alike in temper.

Where lovers exchange not pleasure but profit, the friendship is both weaker and shorter-lived. People who are friends for advantage break apart when the advantage ends; they were never really friends of each other but of the gain. So it turns out that bad men can be friends to each other for pleasure or profit, and good men to bad, and a neutral character to anyone at all; but for each other's own sake, plainly, only the good can be friends — for bad men take no pleasure even in each other except where there is some advantage in it. The friendship of the good is also the only kind proof against slander, since it is not easy to believe a third party against someone you have long tried and tested. Between good men there is mutual trust, the conviction that a friend would never wrong you, and all the other things expected in a friendship worthy of the name. In the other kinds there is nothing to stop such suspicions.

I call these friendships, because people commonly give the name of friends to those tied by profit (as political language allows, since alliances between states are thought to be made for advantage) and to those tied by pleasure (as children are). So we may perhaps be allowed to call such people friends too, and to say there are several kinds of friendship: first and foremost that of the good, insofar as they are good, and the rest by resemblance to it. The others are friends only so far as there is something good in their bond, and some likeness to the true kind (remember that the pleasant is good to those who love it). But these secondary friendships do not often combine; the same people do not become friends both by advantage and by pleasure, for these incidental things rarely come together.

Just as men are called good in respect of an inner state in one case and of activity in another, so it is with friendship. Those who live together take pleasure in each other and do each other good; but those who are asleep or far apart are not exercising the friendship, though they are in the kind of state from which they would act in a friendly way if they did act. Distance has no direct effect on the friendship itself; it only blocks its exercise. Still, if the absence runs long, it is thought to make even the friendship forgotten — hence the saying that lack of contact has dissolved many a friendship. Neither the old nor the sour-tempered seem suited to friendship, for there is little pleasure in them, and no one can spend his days with what is painful, or even not pleasant, since to avoid pain and reach for pleasure is one of the most obvious facts of our nature.

People who think well of each other but are not in the habit of each other's company are more like men of goodwill than friends; for nothing is so characteristic of friends as living together. Those in need want help, and even the fortunate want company — they are the last people made for a solitary life. But you cannot pass your time together unless you are pleasant to each other and take pleasure in the same things, which is thought to belong to the friendship of companions.

The friendship between the good, then, is friendship in the fullest sense, as has been said often. For what is good or pleasant without qualification is thought to be lovable and worth choosing, and to each man whatever is such to him; and the good man is lovable to the good man for both reasons at once. (Loving is like a feeling, friendship like a settled state; for the feeling can have even lifeless things for its object, but returning friendship involves choice, which comes from a settled state. And men wish good to those they love for the friends' own sake, not as a mere feeling but as a matter of character.) In loving his friend the good man loves his own good, since the good man, once in this relation, becomes a good to the one he is joined with. Each loves his own good and pays his friend back in equal measure, in wishing well and in pleasure — for equality is said to be a bond of friendship. All this belongs most of all to the friendship between good men.

Among the sour or the elderly, friendship is less apt to spring up, since they are harder to deal with and take less pleasure in society and company, which are thought to be especially friendly and to breed friendship. That is why the young make friends quickly and the old do not (no one becomes a friend to those he takes no pleasure in), and likewise the sour-tempered. Such people may have goodwill toward one another — they wish each other well and help each other in need — but they are not quite friends, since they neither spend their time together nor take pleasure in each other, which are thought the marks of friendship most of all.

It is not possible to be a friend to many people in the complete way, any more than to be in love with many at once. That is a kind of excess, and its nature is to have a single object; it is not easy for one man to be very pleased with many people at the same time, nor perhaps to find many who are really good. And one needs experience and close familiarity, which is very hard. But it is quite possible to please many on the score of advantage and pleasure, since there are many such people and the services take little time. Of the two lesser kinds, the one that most resembles the complete friendship is the one based on pleasure, where the same thing comes from both and they take pleasure in each other or in the same things — as in the friendships of the young, since these have the most generosity of spirit in them. The friendship for advantage is the bond of tradesmen.

The very fortunate have no need of useful friends, but they do need pleasant ones, since they want people to share their lives with; what is painful they can bear for a short time, but no one could endure it without break — not even the supreme Good itself, if it were painful to him in particular. So they look for pleasant friends; though perhaps they ought to require that these be good as well, and good to themselves in particular, since then they would have all that friendship needs. Men in power are often seen to keep several distinct friends — some useful to them, some pleasant — but rarely both in one person; for they do not actually look for friends who are at once pleasant and good, nor useful for noble ends. To get what is pleasant they look for witty men, and again for men clever at carrying out any task put to them; and these two skills are seldom found in the same man. We have already said that the good man is at once pleasant and useful. But such a man will not be a friend to someone above him in rank unless he is also above him in goodness; otherwise, being outdone in rank, he cannot make things even by a proportionate return of friendship. And men who unite high station with goodness are not common.

Now all the kinds of friendship mentioned so far rest on equality, since either the same thing comes to both and they wish the same things to each other, or else they trade one thing for another — pleasure, say, for profit. (We have said that this last kind is weaker and shorter-lived, and that it is their likeness and unlikeness to the same thing — the friendship based on virtue — that makes them seem now to be friendships and now not. They look like friendships through their likeness to that one, the one having the pleasant and the other the useful, both of which belong to it as well; and they look unlike it through their unlikeness, which is that the true kind is above slander and lasting, while these change quickly and differ in many other ways.)

But there is another form of friendship, the kind in which one party stands above the other: father and son, older and younger, husband and wife, ruler and ruled. These differ among themselves; the friendship of parents toward children is not the same as that of ruler toward ruled, nor is the father's toward the son the same as the son's toward the father, nor the husband's toward the wife the same as hers toward him. For the function, and so the excellence, of each of these is different, and so are the grounds on which they feel friendship; their feelings and their friendships, then, are distinct. The same things do not come to each from the other, nor should they be looked for; but when children render to their parents what is owed to those who gave them life, and parents to their children what is owed to their offspring, the friendship between them will be lasting and fair.

In all friendships between unequals the affection should be proportionate: the better, or the more useful, and so on, should be loved more than he loves in return; for when the affection matches the merit, a kind of equality results, and equality is thought to belong to friendship. (Equality in friendship is not the same as in justice, though. In justice the proportionate equal comes first and the numerically equal second; in friendship it is the reverse.) That equality is required is shown plainly when a wide gap opens — in goodness, or in vice, or in wealth, or anything else; for then people are no longer friends, and do not even think they have a right to be. The clearest case is the gods, who are most superior in every good thing. It is plain too with kings, for those far beneath them do not feel entitled to be their friends, and very insignificant men do not feel entitled to be friends with men of great excellence or wisdom.

There is no fixing the exact point up to which such friends can remain friends; many points of agreement can fall away and the friendship still hold, but when one party is set very far off — as a god from men — it can no longer continue. This has raised a puzzle: do friends really wish their friends the very greatest goods, such as to become gods? For if the wish came true, they would no longer have them for friends — and so would lose a good, since friends are a good. If, then, it was rightly said that a friend wishes good to his friend for the friend's own sake, the friend must be understood to stay the kind of being he now is; so one wishes him the greatest goods of which, as a human being, he is capable. And perhaps not even all of these, since each man wishes good most of all for himself.

Most people, out of love of honor, seem to want to be loved rather than to love, which is why they are fond of flatterers — a flatterer being an inferior friend, or one who pretends to be a friend and to love more than he is loved; for to be loved is thought to be close to being honored, which is what most people are after. Yet men seem to choose honor not for its own sake but incidentally. The common run delight to be honored by those in power because of the hope it raises — they think they will get from them whatever they happen to want — so they enjoy the honor as a token of benefits to come. Those who crave honor from good men, men who really know their worth, want to confirm their own opinion of themselves; they take pleasure in the conviction that they are good, resting on the verdict of those who say so. But men delight in being loved for its own sake, and so it may be judged higher than being honored, and friendship choiceworthy in itself.

Friendship seems to consist in loving rather than in being loved, as is shown by the joy mothers take in loving. Some give their children to others to raise and, knowing them, go on loving them without ever seeking it returned, if both cannot be had; they seem content to see their children doing well and to keep on loving them, even though the children, out of ignorance, never give them any of a child's regard or love in return. Since friendship lies more in loving than in being loved, and those who love their friends are the ones we praise, loving seems to be the excellence of friends. So in those in whom it is present in proper measure, the friends are steady and the friendship lasting. This is the way unequals can best be friends, since loving in proportion can make them equal.

Equality and likeness are a bond of friendship, especially likeness in goodness; for good men, being steady in themselves, are steady toward others too, and neither ask degrading favors nor do them — rather, they hold them off. It belongs to the good neither to go wrong themselves nor to let their friends go wrong. The bad have no steadiness in them; they do not even stay like themselves for long, and become friends only for a short while, taking delight in each other's badness. Friends for profit or pleasure hold together somewhat longer — as long as they can give each other pleasure or gain. The friendship of profit seems to form most of all out of opposites: the poor man becomes the friend of the rich, the ignorant of the well-informed, each wanting what he lacks and giving something else in exchange. Here too we might rank the lover and the beloved, the beautiful and the plain. This is why lovers sometimes look ridiculous, demanding to be loved as intensely as they love; if they were equally lovable they might have the right, but when they have nothing of the kind it is absurd. Perhaps, too, the opposite does not reach for its opposite for its own sake, but only incidentally; what it really wants is the mean, since it is good for the dry not to become wet but to reach the mean, and so with the hot, and the rest. But let us set these questions aside; they are somewhat off our path.

As was said at the start, friendship and justice seem to have the same field and to hold between the same people; for in every community there is thought to be some justice, and some friendship too. People call those friends whom they share a voyage or a campaign with, and likewise those joined with them in other ways. The friendship reaches just as far as the community does, and so does the justice. This bears out the proverb that friends' goods are shared, since friendship rests on community. Brothers and close companions share everything, while others share their property only in part — some more, some less, since friendships too differ in degree. The claims of justice differ as well: they are not the same between parents and children as between brothers, nor between companions as between mere fellow-citizens, and so on through all the kinds of friendship.

The kinds of injustice differ accordingly, and wrongs grow worse when done to friends. It is worse to rob a companion than a fellow-citizen, worse to refuse help to a brother than to a stranger, worse to strike your father than anyone else. Justice naturally increases with the closeness of the friendship, since both hold between the same people and reach equally far. All communities are like parts of the political community, since in them men band together for some advantage and to procure some of what life needs; and the political community itself is thought to have come together, and to last, for the sake of advantage. This is what lawgivers aim at, calling just what is in the common interest. The other communities aim at advantage in particular areas: a ship's crew at what comes from the voyage, undertaken to make money or the like; comrades in arms at what comes from war, whether wealth or victory or political standing; members of a tribe or deme in the same way.

Some communities seem to form for the sake of pleasure — religious societies, say, or dining clubs, which exist for sacrifice or company. But all of these fall under the political community, since its aim is not the advantage of the moment but advantage for the whole of life; with this in view, members hold sacrifices and the gatherings that go with them, to render honor to the gods and to win themselves a rest from toil along with pleasure. For it seems that the old sacrifices and assemblies came after the harvest as a kind of offering of first fruits, since that was the season of most leisure. So all the kinds of community are parts of the political one, and the corresponding kinds of friendship will follow upon these communities.

There are three kinds of political constitution, and an equal number of deviations from them — corruptions, so to speak. The constitutions are kingship, aristocracy, and a third based on a property qualification, which it seems fitting to call timocracy, though most people simply call it a polity. The best of these is kingship, the worst timocracy. The deviation from kingship is tyranny: both are rule by one, but they differ enormously, since the tyrant looks to his own advantage and the king to his subjects'. For a man is no true king unless he is self-sufficient and superior in all good things; and such a man, needing nothing, will not look to his own advantage but to his subjects', while one who is not in this position is a king chosen by lot for the occasion. Tyranny is the very opposite of this kingship, for the tyrant pursues his own good. Here the inferiority is clearest, and what is worst is the opposite of what is best. The slide to tyranny comes from kingship, tyranny being the corruption of monarchy — the bad king turns tyrant.

From aristocracy the slide is to oligarchy, through the vice of the rulers, who distribute the city's goods against right proportion, giving themselves all the good things or the greater share, and keeping the offices always in the same hands, making wealth their idol; so a few rule, and bad men instead of the best. From timocracy the slide is to democracy, since they border on each other: timocracy too is meant to be rule by the many, and all in the same property class are equal. Democracy is the least bad of the deviations, since it departs least from the form of its constitution. These, then, are roughly the changes the constitutions undergo, these being the smallest in degree and the easiest to make.

You can find likenesses and, as it were, models of these even in the household. The bond between a father and his sons has the shape of kingship, since the father cares for his children; this is why Homer calls Zeus father, kingship being meant to be a fatherly rule. Among the Persians, though, the father's rule is tyrannical, for they treat their sons as slaves. (The rule of master over slaves is tyranny in kind, since here it is the master's interest that is regarded.) This last seems as it should be, but the Persian custom mistaken, since different subjects call for different rule. The bond of husband and wife has the shape of aristocracy, for the husband rules by merit and in matters proper to a husband, leaving to the wife what befits her. When the husband lords it over everything, he turns the relation into oligarchy, doing so against right proportion and not as the better of the two. Sometimes the wives rule, being heiresses; here rule goes not by merit but by wealth and power, as in oligarchies. Timocracy finds its likeness in the bond of brothers, who are equal except as age sets them apart — so if they are very different in age, the friendship is no longer a brotherly one. Democracy shows up most in households without a head, where all are equal, or where the proper head is weak and everyone does as he pleases.

For each form of constitution there is plainly a friendship, reaching just as far as the justice does. The friendship of a king toward his subjects is one of superiority in benefit, since he does his subjects good — assuming he is a good king and looks after their welfare, as a shepherd tends his flock; hence Homer calls Agamemnon shepherd of the people. The fatherly friendship is of this same kind, only that it surpasses the king's in the greatness of the benefits, since the father is the cause of his children's being — thought the greatest benefit — and of their nurture and upbringing. (These are credited to ancestors generally too.) By nature a father rules his sons, ancestors their descendants, a king his subjects. These friendships hold between superior and inferior, which is why parents are not only loved but honored. And the justice between these parties is not equal but proportionate, just as the friendship is.

Between husband and wife there is the same friendship as in an aristocracy, for the relation goes by merit: the better person has the greater good, and each has what befits — and so too the justice between them. The brotherly friendship is like that of companions, since brothers are equal and close in age, and such people generally share like feelings and like characters. Like this too is the friendship of a timocracy, where the citizens are meant to be equal and fair, so that rule passes from hand to hand and is shared on equal terms — and the friendship matches. In the deviant forms, just as the justice is small, so is the friendship, and least of all in the most perverted; in tyranny there is little or no friendship. For wherever ruler and ruled have nothing in common, there is no friendship, because there is no justice; the case is rather like that of a craftsman and his tool, or soul and body, or master and slave. All these are benefited by those who use them, but toward lifeless things there is neither friendship nor justice — nor toward a horse or an ox, nor toward a slave as a slave, since they have nothing in common. A slave as such is a living tool, a tool a lifeless slave.

As a slave, then, there is no friendship toward him; only as a man. For there is thought to be some justice between any man and any other who can share in law and be party to an agreement, and so some friendship too, insofar as he is a man. So in tyrannies friendship and justice reach only a little way, but in democracies they reach furthest, since those who are equal have much in common.

Now all friendship rests on community, as has been said; but one might set apart from the rest the friendship of kindred and that of companions, while the friendships of fellow-citizens, fellow-tribesmen, shipmates, and the like seem more peculiarly to rest on community, since they plainly exist by an agreement, stated or implied. Among these we may rank the friendship of host and guest. The friendship of kindred is itself of many kinds, and all of them seem to depend on the parental: parents love their children as a part of themselves, and children love their parents as deriving from them. But parents know that their children are theirs more surely than children know they come from their parents, and the source is more closely bound to what it produces than the offspring to what made it. For whatever comes from oneself is one's own — a tooth, a hair, anything at all, to the one it belongs to — while the source is not its own, or less so. Then there is the length of time: parents love their children from the moment they exist, but children love their parents only after time has passed and they have gained understanding or perception. These same points show why mothers love more than fathers.

Parents, then, love their children as themselves, since what comes from them becomes a kind of second self by being separated off; and children love their parents as having sprung from them. Brothers love one another from having sprung from the same source, for their sameness with that source makes them the same with each other — hence the phrases "same blood," "same root," and the like. In a sense they are the same thing even in distinct individuals. Being raised together and being near in age are a great help to friendship, for a man likes one of his own age, and people used to each other become companions; this is why the friendship of brothers resembles that of companions. Cousins and other relatives get their bond from these — from their common origin — and the bond is stronger or weaker according to how close they stand to the common ancestor.

The friendship of children toward parents, and of men toward the gods, is friendship toward something good and above us; for these have done the greatest benefits, being the cause of our existence and nurture, and of our upbringing once we were born. And a friendship of this kind has more of the pleasant and the useful in it than friendship between people unrelated by blood, in proportion as their life is more shared. In the brotherly friendship there is everything there is in that of companions, and more in the case of the good, and of those who are alike — more in proportion as they are more closely tied, having from birth a feeling for one another to start with, and being more alike in character, since they spring from the same stock, have grown up together, and have been brought up alike; and they have had the fullest chance, over time, to test one another, and so can rely on the proof most securely.

Between husband and wife there seems to be a friendship by nature; for man is by nature more a pairing animal than a political one, inasmuch as the household comes before the city in time and is more necessary, and procreation is something he shares with the other animals. The other animals come together only this far, but human beings live together not only to have children but for life in general; for from the start the tasks are divided, some belonging to the man, others to the woman, so that they supply each other's needs, each putting what is its own into the common stock. For these reasons this friendship is thought to combine the useful and the pleasant; and it will rest on virtue too, if they are good, since each has a virtue and they can delight in this in each other. Children too are thought to be a bond, which is why the childless part more easily; for children are a good shared by both, and what is shared holds people together. How a man should live with his wife — or, in general, one friend with another — seems to be just the question of how it is just for them to live; for plainly the justice between friend and friend is not the same as between strangers, or companions, or chance fellow-travelers.

There are, then, as was said at the start of this book, three kinds of friendship, and in each there can be friends on equal terms and friends in a relation of superior and inferior; people alike in goodness become friends, and the better with the worse, and likewise the pleasant; and again people become friends for advantage, either balancing their usefulness exactly or differing in it. Those who are equal should be made equal in the friendship and the rest by their very equality, and those who are unequal by rendering friendship in proportion to the other's superiority.

Complaint and blame arise either only, or chiefly, in the friendship of utility. For those who are friends through goodness are eager to do each other good, since that is what goodness and friendship produce; and when men vie with each other in this, there can be no complaint or quarrel, since no one is annoyed at someone who feels friendship for him and does him good — if he has any refinement, he repays him with kind acts. And even if one outdoes the other, he will not blame his friend, since he is getting what he aimed at, for what each aims at is the good. Nor can there be much quarreling between friends for pleasure's sake; for if they enjoy living together, both get what they want, and a man would look ridiculous to blame another for not pleasing him, when it is in his power to stop spending time with him.

But the friendship of utility is full of complaint. Since the parties use each other for advantage, they always want more, and think they are getting less than their due, and complain that they are not getting as much as they deserve; while those who do the good can never come up to what the receivers want. It seems that, just as the just is of two kinds — the unwritten and the written — so the friendship of utility is of two kinds, the moral and the legal. The richest source of complaints is when people take on an obligation in one spirit and settle it in another. The legal kind is on stated terms — either purely commercial, hand to hand, or more gentlemanly as to time, but still by agreement a fixed return for a return. In the legal kind the obligation is clear and beyond dispute; the friendly element is the delay in calling it in. (For this reason, in some places no suit is allowed for such debts, on the view that those who have dealt on trust must live with the result.)

What may be called the moral kind is not on stated terms; a man gives as to a friend, and so on. But he still expects to get an equivalent back, or even more, as though he had not given but lent; and he too will complain, because he does not get it settled in the same spirit in which he entered into it. This happens because all men, or most, wish for what is noble but, when it comes to the test, choose what is profitable; and doing good disinterestedly is noble, while receiving benefit is profitable. In such cases one should, if able, return something proportionate to the good received, and do it willingly; for one ought not to make a man a disinterested friend against his will. You should behave as if you had made a mistake at the start, by accepting a kindness from someone you should not have taken it from — someone who was no friend and was not acting for your sake — and settle the matter as if the kindness had been given on stated terms. A man would agree, if he could, to repay it, and if he could not, even the doer would not expect it; so if he can, he ought to repay. But one ought at the start to find out from whom one is receiving the kindness, and on what understanding, so that one can accept it on those terms or not.

A disputable question is whether one should measure a kindness by the good done to the receiver, and make that the standard of repayment, or by the kind intention of the doer. Those who have received kindnesses often plead, to play them down, that they got from their benefactors things that were small for them to give, or things they could have got from others; while the doers insist they gave the best they had, what could not be got elsewhere, and in time of danger or other straits. May we not say that, since utility is the ground of the friendship, the advantage to the receiver must be the standard? For he is the one who asked the kindness, and the other helps him in his need on the understanding that he will get an equivalent; so the help is exactly proportionate to the advantage the receiver gained, and he should repay as much as he gained, or even more, that being nobler. In friendships based on goodness this question is never raised; here the intention of the doer seems the proper standard, since virtue and character depend above all on intention.

Quarrels also arise in friendships between unequals, because each thinks himself entitled to the greater share — and when this happens, the friendship breaks up. The better man thinks the greater share belongs to him by right, since more is always awarded to the good man; and likewise the more useful one. "A useless man," they say, "should not share equally; it becomes a tax, not a friendship, unless the fruits of the friendship are reaped in proportion to the work done." Their idea is that, as in a money partnership those who contribute more take more, so it should be in friendship. The needy man and the less good make the opposite claim: it is the very business of a good friend, they say, to help those in need — else what use is a good or powerful friend, if one is to reap no advantage from him?

Each seems to make a right claim, and each is entitled to get more out of the connection than the other — only not more of the same thing. The superior man should get more honor, the needy man more profit; for honor is the reward of goodness and of doing good, while profit is the relief of need. This is plainly the principle in political communities: a man who contributes no good to the common stock gets no honor, since the public's property goes to the man who does the public good, and honor is the public's property. One cannot both make money out of the public and receive honor from it as well, for no one will take the smaller share in everything. So to the man who loses out in money they award honor, and money to the man who can be paid by gifts; for, as has been said, observing due proportion equalizes and preserves the friendship.

The same rules, then, should govern dealings between unequal friends: to the man who does another good in money or in goodness, that other should repay honor, returning what he can; for friendship asks what is possible, not what is strictly owed. Strict repayment is not possible in every case, as in the honors paid to gods and to parents — no one could ever make the due return — and a man is thought good if he pays respect according to his means. For this reason it would seem never to be allowable for a son to disown his father, though a father may disown a son. The debtor is bound to pay, and a son can never, by anything he does, fully repay the benefits first conferred on him, so he is always in debt; but the one to whom something is owed may release his debtor, and so the father may release the son. At the same time, it must perhaps be granted that no father would ever cut himself off from a son utterly, except in a case of extreme depravity; for, quite apart from the natural friendship, it is human not to push away the help a son might give. But for the son, if depraved, helping his father is a thing to be shunned, or at least one he will not be eager to do; most men are willing enough to receive a kindness but reluctant to do one, as unprofitable. Let this be enough on these matters.
