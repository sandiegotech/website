# Nicomachean Ethics, Book II

Virtue as a Mean

Excellence comes in two kinds: excellence of intellect and excellence of character. The intellectual kind owes both its origin and its growth mostly to teaching, which is why it needs experience and time. Excellence of character, by contrast, comes from habit — and that is reflected even in its name, since the Greek word for it is only a slight inflection of the word for habit.

This makes one thing clear at once: none of the virtues of character arises in us by nature. Nothing that exists by nature can be trained into a different way by habit. A stone, which by nature falls downward, could never be trained to rise, not if you threw it up ten thousand times to accustom it; fire cannot be habituated to burn downward; and in general nothing that behaves one way by nature can be made by habit to behave another. So the virtues come about in us neither by nature nor against nature. Rather, nature gives us the capacity to receive them, and habit brings that capacity to completion.

Consider further: in everything we have by nature, we are equipped with the capacity first and put it to use afterward. The senses make this plain. We did not acquire sight or hearing by seeing or hearing repeatedly; the reverse is true — we had the senses and then used them, we did not get them by using them. But the virtues we acquire by first putting them into practice, exactly as we acquire any skill. The things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them: people become builders by building and harpists by playing the harp. In the same way, we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, and brave by doing brave acts.

What happens in communities bears this out. Lawgivers make the citizens good by habituating them, and this is the aim of every lawgiver; those who fail to do it well miss their mark — and it is just here that a good constitution differs from a bad one.

Moreover, every virtue is both produced and destroyed by the very same kind of acts — and so is every skill. It is by playing the harp that both good and bad harpists are made, and likewise builders and all the rest: build well and you become a good builder, build badly and you become a bad one. If this were not so, there would be no need for teachers at all; everyone would have been born already good or bad at his craft. It is the same with the virtues. By how we conduct ourselves in our dealings with others, some of us become just and others unjust; by how we conduct ourselves in the face of danger, growing accustomed to fear or to confidence, some become brave and others cowards. The same goes for situations involving appetite and anger: some people become temperate and even-tempered, others self-indulgent and hot-tempered, depending on how they behave in such situations. In a word, our settled states are produced by acts of the same kind as themselves. That is why we must give our acts a certain quality, since our states take their character from the differences in our acts. So it makes no small difference whether we are habituated one way or another straight from childhood — it makes a very great difference, or rather it makes all the difference.

Now the point of the present inquiry is not, as it is in some others, mere knowledge. We are not investigating what virtue is just in order to know it, but in order to become good — otherwise the inquiry would be useless. So we have to look at how to perform our actions, since, as we have said, our actions decide what sort of states we form. That we should act in accordance with right reason is common ground, and we will take it for granted here; later we will say what right reason is and how it stands to the other virtues.

But let one thing be agreed between us from the start: any account of how to act can only be given in outline, not with precision. As we said at the very beginning, the kind of treatment demanded must suit the subject, and matters of action and of what is good for us have nothing fixed about them, any more than matters of health do. And if the general account is as inexact as this, the account of particular cases is even less precise, for such cases fall under no craft and no set of rules. The agents themselves must, in each instance, look to what the occasion demands — just as happens in medicine and in navigation. Still, even though our subject is like this, we must try to offer what help we can.

First, then, notice this: states like these are by their nature destroyed by deficiency and by excess. We can see it in the cases of strength and health (since for things we cannot observe we have to use evidence we can): too much exercise ruins strength, and so does too little; likewise too much food and drink, or too little, ruins health, while the right amount produces it, increases it, and preserves it. It is the same with temperance and courage and the other virtues. The man who runs from everything and fears everything and stands his ground against nothing becomes a coward; the man who fears nothing at all and marches against everything becomes reckless. In the same way, the man who indulges in every pleasure and abstains from none becomes self-indulgent, while the man who shuns all pleasure, like a boor, becomes insensible, as it were. So temperance and courage are destroyed by excess and by deficiency, and preserved by the mean.

And there is more. Not only are these states produced, increased, and ruined by the same kinds of acts; their exercise, once they are formed, takes place in the same kinds of acts as well. This holds for more observable things too, like strength: strength comes from eating plenty and working hard, and the strong man is the one best able to do just that. It is the same with the virtues. By abstaining from pleasures we become temperate, and once we are temperate we are best able to abstain from them. And so with courage: by training ourselves to make light of frightening things and to stand up to them we become brave, and once brave we are best able to stand up to them.

We should treat the pleasure or pain that follows on our acts as a sign of the state we have formed. The man who abstains from bodily pleasures and is glad to do so is temperate; the man who abstains but resents it is self-indulgent. The man who stands up to danger and either enjoys it or at least feels no pain is brave; the man who does it in distress is not. For excellence of character is concerned with pleasures and pains: it is because of pleasure that we do what is base, and because of pain that we shrink from what is noble. This is why, as Plato says, we should have been trained from childhood to feel pleasure and pain at the right things; this is what right education means.

There is further evidence. Since the virtues have to do with actions and feelings, and every action and feeling is attended by pleasure or pain, here too virtue turns out to be concerned with pleasure and pain. The fact that we use punishments to correct people points the same way, for punishments are a kind of cure, and cures by their nature work through opposites. Recall, too, what we said before: every state of the soul has its nature and exercises itself in relation to the very things that naturally make it better or worse — and it is through pleasures and pains that states become bad, namely by pursuing or avoiding the wrong ones, or at the wrong times, or in the wrong way, and so on. This is why some people actually define the virtues as a kind of freedom from feeling, a state of calm. But they are wrong to put it without qualification, instead of adding "in the right way" and "in the wrong way," "at the right time," and the rest. We may take it, then, that virtue is the kind of state, with respect to pleasures and pains, that does what is best, and that vice is the opposite.

The following will make the same point clear. There are three things that move us to choose and three that move us to avoid: the noble, the advantageous, and the pleasant, and their three opposites — the shameful, the harmful, and the painful. About all of these the good man tends to go right and the bad man to go wrong, but most of all about pleasure. For pleasure is something we share with all animals, and it is bound up with everything we choose, since both the noble and the advantageous strike us as pleasant. What is more, the feeling for pleasure has grown up with us all since infancy, which makes it hard to scrape away, dyed as it is into our very lives. And, to a greater or lesser degree, we even use pleasure and pain as the measure of our actions. For all these reasons, our whole business has to be with pleasure and pain, since getting our pleasures and pains right or wrong matters enormously for how we act. As Heraclitus says, it is harder to fight pleasure than to fight anger — and skill and virtue alike always concern themselves with what is harder, since in what is hard, doing well counts for more. So this is one more reason why virtue, and the study of conduct generally, must devote itself entirely to pleasures and pains: handle these well and you will be good; handle them badly and you will be bad.

Let us take it, then, as established that virtue is concerned with pleasures and pains; that it is increased or ruined by the same kinds of acts that bring it into being, used in the right or the wrong way; and that it exercises itself in the same kinds of acts out of which it arose.

Now someone might be puzzled by our claim that to become just we must do just acts, and to become temperate we must do temperate acts. "For," he would say, "if people are already doing just and temperate acts, then they are already just and temperate — just as someone who does what is grammatical or musical is already a grammarian or a musician." But the case is not even like that with skills. A person may do something grammatically correct by chance, or because someone else prompted him; he is a grammarian only when he both produces something grammatical and produces it as a grammarian does — that is, out of grammatical knowledge of his own.

And in any case skills and virtues are not parallel. The products of a skill have their merit in themselves, so it is enough that they turn out a certain way. But acts done in accordance with virtue are not done justly or temperately merely because they themselves are a certain way; the doer must also be in a certain condition when he does them. First, he must act knowingly; second, he must choose the act, and choose it for its own sake; and third, he must do it from a firm and unshakable character. For having a skill, these conditions do not count — only the knowledge does. For having a virtue, the knowledge counts for little or nothing, while the other conditions count for everything — and it is precisely by doing just and temperate acts again and again that we come to meet them. So acts are called just and temperate when they are the kind of acts a just or temperate person would do; but the person who does them is not yet just or temperate unless he also does them as the just and temperate do. We are right, then, to say that a person becomes just by doing just acts and temperate by doing temperate ones; without doing them, no one has even a chance of becoming good. But most people do not do them. They take refuge in talk and suppose that they are doing philosophy and that this will make them good — behaving rather like patients who listen carefully to the doctor and then do nothing he prescribes. Such patients will never be healthy in body; and people who philosophize like that will never be healthy in soul.

Next we must ask what virtue is. The things that arise in the soul are of three kinds — feelings, capacities, and states — so virtue must be one of these. By feelings I mean appetite, anger, fear, confidence, envy, joy, love, hatred, longing, emulation, pity — in general, whatever is attended by pleasure or pain. By capacities I mean what we have in virtue of which we are able to have these feelings: the capacity, for instance, by which we are able to feel anger or pain or pity. By states I mean what we have in virtue of which we stand well or badly toward these feelings — toward anger, for example, we stand badly if we feel it too violently or too weakly, and well if we feel it in the balanced way; and so on for the rest.

Now the virtues and vices are not feelings, because we are not called good or bad on account of our feelings, but we are on account of our virtues and vices; and because we are neither praised nor blamed for our feelings as such — a man is not praised simply for being afraid or angry, nor blamed simply for being angry, but only for being so in a particular way — whereas we are praised and blamed for our virtues and vices. Again, we feel anger and fear without choosing to, whereas the virtues are a kind of choice, or at any rate involve choice. And besides, we are said to be "moved" by our feelings, but as for the virtues and vices, we are not said to be moved but to be in a certain condition. For these same reasons, the virtues are not capacities either, since we are not called good or bad, nor praised or blamed, simply for being able to have feelings. And we have our capacities by nature, but we do not become good or bad by nature, as we said earlier. So if the virtues are neither feelings nor capacities, the only thing left is that they are states.

That settles what kind of thing virtue is, in its genus. But we must not stop at saying it is a state; we must say what sort of state it is. Here is the point. Any excellence both brings the thing that has it into good condition and makes it perform its work well. The excellence of an eye makes both the eye good and its work good, since it is by the eye's excellence that we see well. The excellence of a horse makes a horse good — good at running, at carrying its rider, at standing up to the enemy. If this holds in every case, then the excellence of a human being will be the state that makes a person good and makes him perform his own work well. We have already said how this comes about, but it will be clearer still if we look at the particular character that virtue has.

In anything continuous and divisible, you can take a larger part, a smaller part, or an equal part — and this either in the thing itself or relative to us; and the equal is a mean between excess and deficiency. By the mean of the thing I mean the point equidistant from each extreme, which is one and the same for everyone. By the mean relative to us I mean the amount that is neither too much nor too little — and this is not one and the same for everyone. Take a quantity: if ten is too much and two too little, then as a matter of arithmetic six is the mean of the thing, since it exceeds two by the same amount that ten exceeds it. But the mean relative to us cannot be found that way. It does not follow that if ten pounds of food is too much to eat and two too little, the trainer will prescribe six; for even six may be too much or too little for the particular person — too little for Milo the wrestler, too much for a beginner. The same goes for how much running or wrestling to do. In this way every expert avoids excess and deficiency and looks for the mean — not the mean of the thing, but the mean relative to us — and chooses that.

If, then, every skill does its work well by keeping its eye on the mean and steering its products toward it — which is why people say of a thing in good condition that you could not add anything to it or take anything away, on the assumption that excess and deficiency destroy what is good while the mean preserves it; and if good craftsmen, as we say, work with their eye on the mean; and if excellence, like nature, is more precise and better than any craft — then excellence too must aim at the mean. I mean excellence of character, since that is what is concerned with feelings and actions, and these admit of excess and deficiency and the mean. It is possible, for instance, to feel fear, confidence, appetite, anger, pity, and pleasure and pain in general too much or too little, and both are wrong. But to feel these things when we ought, at the things we ought, toward the people we ought, for the reasons we ought, and in the way we ought — that is the mean, and the best, and the mark of virtue. In the same way, actions too admit of excess, deficiency, and the mean. Virtue is concerned with feelings and actions, and in these excess is wrong and deficiency is blamed, while the mean is praised and gets it right — and praise and rightness both belong to virtue. So virtue is a kind of mean, in that it aims at what is intermediate.

Consider, besides, that there are many ways to go wrong (since, as the Pythagoreans put it, the bad belongs to the unlimited and the good to the limited), but only one way to go right. That is why going wrong is easy and going right is hard — easy to miss the target, hard to hit it. So for this reason too excess and deficiency belong to vice, and the mean to virtue: "Good in one way only, bad in all sorts of ways."

Virtue, then, is a settled state that issues in choice, lying in a mean relative to us, a mean defined by reason — the reason by which the person of practical wisdom would define it. It is a mean between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency. And it is a mean in this further sense: the vices fall short of, or overshoot, what is right in both feelings and actions, while virtue finds the middle and chooses it. So in respect of its essence and the account that states what it is, virtue is a mean; but in respect of what is best and right, it is an extreme — the peak.

Not every action or feeling, however, admits of a mean. Some have names that already imply badness — spite, shamelessness, envy; and among actions, adultery, theft, murder. These and their like are condemned because they are bad in themselves, not because there is too much or too little of them. With them you can never be right; you are always wrong. Rightness and wrongness in such cases do not depend on doing them to the right person, at the right time, in the right way; simply to do any of them at all is to be wrong. You might as well demand a mean, an excess, and a deficiency in acting unjustly, or in cowardice, or in self-indulgence — for then there would be a mean of excess and a mean of deficiency, an excess of excess and a deficiency of deficiency. But just as there is no excess or deficiency of temperance or courage, because the mean is in a way the peak, so there is no mean of these bad things, and no excess or deficiency of them either; however they are done, they are wrong. In short, there is no mean of an excess or a deficiency, and no excess or deficiency of a mean.

It is not enough, though, to state this in general; we have to fit it to particular cases. In discussions of conduct, general statements cover more ground but say less that is true, while statements about particulars are truer to life — because action deals with particulars, and our account has to square with those. So let us take our particulars from the following table.

With fear and confidence: the mean is courage. Of those who go too far, the man who exceeds in fearlessness has no name (many of these states do not), and the man who exceeds in confidence is reckless; the man who has too much fear and too little confidence is a coward.

With pleasures and pains — though not all of them, and with fewer pains than pleasures: the mean is temperance, and the excess is self-indulgence. People deficient in the pursuit of pleasure hardly occur, so this character too has no name; but since such a person is conceivable, let us call him insensible.

With giving and taking money, first on the ordinary scale: the mean is generosity, the excess wastefulness, the deficiency stinginess. Here the two extremes go to excess and deficiency in opposite respects: the wasteful man gives out too much and takes in too little, the stingy man takes in too much and gives out too little. (We are giving only an outline and summary here, on purpose; we will draw these distinctions more exactly later.)

With money on the grand scale: there is a mean called magnificence — the magnificent man differs from the merely generous one, since magnificence deals with large sums and generosity with small. The excess here is called tastelessness or vulgar display, the deficiency pettiness; these too differ from the extremes that go with generosity, and we will say later how they differ.

With honor and dishonor, on the great scale: the mean is greatness of soul, the excess is what we might call vanity, the deficiency smallness of soul.

With honor on the small scale: there is a state related to greatness of soul as generosity is related to magnificence — concerned with small honor rather than great. A man may reach for honor more than he should or less than he should; the one who reaches too far is called ambitious, the one who falls short unambitious, and the one who is just right has no proper name. The states have no names either, except that the disposition of the ambitious man is called ambition. This is why both extremes lay claim to the middle ground as contested territory, and why we ourselves sometimes call the right character ambitious and sometimes unambitious, and praise now the one and now the other. Why we do this will be explained later; for now let us go through the rest of the virtues on the plan we have laid down.

With anger: here too there is excess, deficiency, and a mean. They have no settled names, but since we call the right character even-tempered, let us call the mean even temper. Of the extremes, let the man who goes too far be called hot-tempered and his fault hot temper, and the man who falls short spiritless, and his fault a lack of spirit.

There are also three further means that resemble one another but differ. All three have to do with how we behave in words and deeds when we are with others. They differ in that one is concerned with truth in such things, the other two with what is pleasant — one of these in relaxation and amusement, the other across the whole of social life. We should say a word about these too, so as to see more clearly that in everything the mean is praiseworthy, while the extremes are neither right nor praiseworthy but blameworthy. Most of these also lack proper names, but we will try, as before, to coin some, for clarity's sake.

With truth about oneself: the man in the mean we may call truthful, and his state truthfulness. As for misrepresenting oneself, if it tends toward exaggeration it is boastfulness and the man a boaster; if toward understatement it is irony, or self-deprecation, and the man self-deprecating.

With what is pleasant in relaxation and amusement: the mean is wit, and the man witty; the excess is buffoonery, and the man a buffoon; the man who falls short is a boor, and his state boorishness.

With what is pleasant in the rest of social life: the man who is as he should be is friendly, and his mean friendliness; the man who overdoes it, if from no motive of his own, is obsequious, and if to get something out of it, a flatterer; the man who falls short and is unpleasant in everything is contrary and quarrelsome.

There are means in our feelings and in matters involving feeling as well. Modesty, for instance, is not a virtue, but the modest person is praised. Here too one person is said to be in the mean, another in the excess — like the bashful man who is overcome with shame at everything — while the person who feels no shame at all is shameless; the one in between is modest. And righteous indignation is a mean between envy and spite. All three have to do with the pleasure or pain we feel at what happens to our neighbors: the man with righteous indignation is pained at undeserved good fortune; the envious man overshoots him and is pained at anyone's good fortune; and the spiteful man falls so far short of being pained that he actually rejoices at others' misfortune. But there will be another occasion to discuss these, and justice too, since justice is spoken of in more than one sense; afterward we will treat each precisely and show how they are means. We will do the same for the intellectual excellences.

Since there are three dispositions in each case — two of them faults, one in the way of excess and one in the way of deficiency, and one of them right, the mean — they are all in a way opposed to one another. The extremes are opposed both to the mean and to each other, and the mean is opposed to the extremes. Just as the equal is larger compared to the smaller and smaller compared to the larger, so the mean states are excessive compared to the deficient ones and deficient compared to the excessive ones, in feelings and in actions alike. The brave man looks reckless next to the coward and cowardly next to the reckless man; the temperate man looks self-indulgent next to the insensible man and insensible next to the self-indulgent; the generous man looks wasteful next to the stingy man and stingy next to the wasteful. So the extreme characters each push the man in the middle toward the other side: the coward calls the brave man reckless, the reckless man calls him a coward, and so on. And because of this mutual opposition, the extremes are more contrary to each other than either is to the mean, since they stand farther from each other than from the middle — just as the larger and the smaller stand farther from each other than either does from the equal.

There is this too: some extremes bear a certain resemblance to the mean — recklessness to courage, wastefulness to generosity — but the extremes are utterly unlike each other. Now things that stand farthest apart are defined as contraries, so the farther apart, the more contrary they are. In some cases it is the deficiency that is more opposed to the mean, in others the excess. Cowardice, the deficiency, is more opposed to courage than recklessness, the excess; but self-indulgence, the excess, is more opposed to temperance than insensibility, the deficiency. There are two reasons for this. One comes from the nature of the thing itself: when one extreme is nearer and more like the mean, we set not it but the other against the mean as the true opposite. Since recklessness seems nearer and more like courage, and cowardice less like it, we set cowardice rather than recklessness against courage, because what stands farther from the mean seems more contrary to it. The other reason comes from ourselves. The things we are naturally more inclined toward seem more opposed to the mean. We are naturally inclined more toward pleasures, and so we slide more readily into self-indulgence than into decorum. We call "more contrary," then, the extreme we tend to drift into — which is why self-indulgence, the excess, is more contrary than insensibility to temperance.

So we have now said enough about how excellence of character is a mean; how it lies between two faults, one of excess and one of deficiency; and how it is such because it aims at the middle in both feelings and actions. This is also why being good is hard work. In each case finding the middle is hard, just as finding the center of a circle is not something anyone can do, but only the one who knows how. Anyone can get angry — that is easy — and anyone can give away money or spend it; but to do these to the right person, in the right amount, at the right time, for the right reason, and in the right way — that is not something anyone can do, and it is not easy. That is why doing it well is rare, and praiseworthy, and noble.

So the man aiming at the mean should first steer clear of the extreme more opposed to it — as Calypso advises in Homer, "Keep the ship clear of this spray and surge." For of the two extremes one is always more of a mistake than the other; and since hitting the mean exactly is so hard, the next-best course, as they say, is to take the least of the evils. We will do this best by the method just described. We should also watch for our own natural bias, which differs from person to person and shows itself in the pleasures and pains we feel. And we should drag ourselves off in the opposite direction, for by pulling well clear of where we go wrong we will arrive at the middle — the way people straighten warped timber. In everything, we should be most on guard against what is pleasant, and against pleasure itself, since we are not impartial judges of it. We ought to feel toward pleasure the way the elders of Troy felt toward Helen, and say of it what they said of her — for if we send it away, we will go wrong less often.

In short, by taking these precautions we will best be able to hit the mean. Still, this is no doubt hard, especially in particular cases. It is not easy to determine exactly how, with whom, for what, and for how long one should be angry; we ourselves sometimes praise people who fall short and call them even-tempered, and at other times call the hot-tempered manly. And the person who deviates a little from what is right, whether toward too much or too little, is not blamed; only the one who deviates a good deal is, since he cannot go unnoticed. But just how far and how much someone has to go to be blameworthy is not easy to fix in words — no easier than anything else that is a matter of perception. Such things lie among the particulars, and the judgment rests with perception. This much, at least, is clear: the mean state is praiseworthy everywhere, but we have to lean sometimes toward excess and sometimes toward deficiency, since that is the easiest way to hit the mean — and so to get it right.
