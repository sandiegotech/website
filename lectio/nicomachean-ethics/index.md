# Nicomachean Ethics

Aristotle

- 340 BCE
- Western tradition
- Philosophy
- Lectio Edition 2026

Aristotle's inquiry into how to live well. Happiness is the activity of a whole life lived with excellence. Virtue is a mean, formed by habit. Friendship and contemplation complete a human life. The book is his lecture notes, in ten books.

## An argument cannot be followed if its terms keep changing.

The Ethics is compressed and technical, and it doubles back. That is hard enough. D. P. Chase's translation, the public-domain version in the bundle, adds Victorian terms of art: "Perfected Self-Mastery", "Imperfect Self-Control", "Great-mindedness".

The first Lectio draft had the opposite fault. It was plain, but each book was drafted alone, and the key terms drifted. The review found four English words for temperance.

The edition now keeps every distinction Aristotle makes and gives each term one English word in all ten books. Chase stays in the app beside it.

## How it begins.

> **Book I · The Good for Man**

> Every craft and every line of inquiry, and likewise every action and deliberate choice, seems to aim at some good. That is why people have rightly described the good as "what everything aims at." But the aims clearly differ. In some cases the aim is the activity itself; in others it is some product over and above the activity. And where there are products beyond the activities, the products are by their nature better than the activities. Since there are many actions, crafts, and sciences, there are also many aims: health is the aim of medicine, a ship of shipbuilding, victory of generalship, wealth of household management.

> Wherever a number of such pursuits fall under a single master art — as bridle-making and all the other trades that produce a horse's equipment fall under horsemanship, and horsemanship together with every military action falls under generalship, and so on — in every case the aim of the master art is more worth choosing than the aims of the arts beneath it, since those lower aims are pursued for the sake of the higher. And it makes no difference whether the activities themselves are the aims, or something further beyond them, as in the sciences just mentioned.

The first 205 words of the Lectio Edition, as they stand in the bundle the app reads.

## What this edition decided.

This edition took more correction than any other: 206 fixes, most of them to make the vocabulary hold. The review wrote a glossary and applied it.

- One English word for each Greek term, in every book. *Sophrosyne* is "temperance". *Akolasia* is "self-indulgence". *Enkrateia* is "self-control". *Akrasia* is "lack of self-control". These are four different conditions and Aristotle keeps them apart.
- *To kalon*, as a motive for action, is "the noble". The draft had "fine", "honor" and "honorable". "Honor" is now kept for *time* alone.
- *Eudaimonia* stays "happiness", and Aristotle's own argument is left to say what he means by it.
- *Arete* is "virtue" in the moral sense and "excellence" where he means being good at a function.
- *Nous* is "intuitive understanding" as the faculty that grasps first principles, and "intellect" as the highest part of us.
- No contractions. Book III had 189 and read in a different voice from the rest.
- Chase's own insertions came out, including a line of Shakespeare in Book IX. Two examples in Book VII that Chase had silently cut were restored.

### Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 30 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 8. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

### εὐδαιμονία (*eudaimonia*)

Book I and throughout

**Older:** "happiness" (Chase)

**Lectio:** happiness

Here the edition keeps the old word. *Eudaimonia* is a whole life that has gone well, not a mood, and some translators prefer "flourishing". The edition keeps the word readers expect and lets the argument redefine it.

### ἀρετή (*arete*)

Book I, chapter 7

**Older:** "virtue" (Chase)

**Lectio:** excellence

A good knife cuts well. A good harpist plays well. *Arete* is that, before it is anything moral. "Excellence" lets the function argument read as an argument.

### σωφροσύνη, ἀκολασία

Book III, chapters 10 to 12

**Older:** "Perfected Self-Mastery", "absence of self-control" (Chase)

**Lectio:** temperance

Chase's second term suggests the man who knows better and fails, which is a different condition treated in Book VII. The self-indulgent man does not think he is failing.

### μεγαλοψυχία (*megalopsychia*)

Book IV, chapter 3

**Older:** "Great-mindedness" (Chase)

**Lectio:** greatness of soul

The Greek is built from the words for great and soul, not mind. It is the soul Aristotle is measuring.

### φρόνησις (*phronesis*)

Book VI

**Older:** "Practical Wisdom" (Chase), "prudence"

**Lectio:** practical wisdom

"Prudence" has shrunk to caution. *Phronesis* is the settled ability to work out what is good to do and to do it.

### ἀκρασία (*akrasia*)

Book VII

**Older:** "Imperfect Self-Control" (Chase), "incontinence"

**Lectio:** lack of self-control

The state of a person who knows the better course and takes the worse. "Incontinence" now means something medical.

### φιλία (*philia*)

Book VIII

**Older:** "friendship" (Chase)

**Lectio:** friendship

Kept, with a caution. *Philia* covers parent and child, husband and wife, fellow citizens, host and guest. It is wider than the English word, and the edition says so in its notes instead of inventing a term.

### θεωρία (*theoria*)

Book X, chapters 7 and 8

**Older:** "Contemplative Speculation" (Chase)

**Lectio:** contemplation

Chase makes it sound mystical. *Theoria* is active looking: the mind fully at work on the highest things.

### Still open

What the first review left for the editor to decide or restore.

- The editor's own read of all ten books.
- Book IV still says "excellence" 26 times for the moral virtues, where Books III and V say "virtue". Bringing it into line is a separate pass of about twenty changes.
- Book VII, chapter 5: the examples Chase had cut are restored. They are faithful and plainly worded. Because they add content readers of Chase never saw, the editor should read the passage himself.
- One sentence in Book VII uses "weak" alone for the man who lacks self-control, against the edition's own rule.
