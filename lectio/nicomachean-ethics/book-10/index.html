Nicomachean Ethics, Book X

Pleasure and the Contemplative Life

Book X10 of 10

What comes next, it seems, is pleasure. For nothing is bound up more tightly with our kind than this: it is by the two rudders of pleasure and pain that we steer the young, and learning to enjoy and to hate the right things is held to count for more than almost anything in the shaping of a good character — for these feelings run the whole length of a life, pulling us toward virtue and a life that goes well, since men reach for what is pleasant and shrink from what hurts. A subject this large we cannot simply walk past, the more so because people disagree about it so sharply. Some call pleasure the chief good outright; others go to the far end and call it thoroughly bad — a few because they honestly believe it, but others, I suspect, on a theory that even if pleasure is not bad it is useful to say it is, since the crowd is its slave, and dragging men toward the opposite extreme may land them somewhere near the middle.

I doubt the wisdom of that. When it comes to what men feel and do, theories carry less weight than facts, and the moment a theory clashes with how people actually live it is not merely scorned — it drags the truth down with it. Let the man who runs pleasure down be caught once reaching for it, and the crowd, unable to make fine distinctions, takes his lapse as proof that pleasure is exactly the universal good he denied. So honest talk about these things is the useful kind, not only for knowing but for living, because what squares with the facts is believed, and what is believed moves the wise to live by it. Enough of that, though; let us turn to what people actually say about pleasure.

Eudoxus held that pleasure is the chief good, on the grounds that everything reaches for it, rational and irrational alike. In each case, he argued, what a thing chooses is good for it, and what it chooses most is best; so the fact that all are drawn to one thing shows that one thing is best for all. "Each finds its own good," he said, "the way it finds its own food, and what is good for all, the target of every aim, is the chief good." His arguments were trusted less for themselves than for the man behind them, for he was thought a man of exceptional temperance, so no one supposed he praised pleasure because he loved it — he was simply saying what he believed. He thought the point was made just as well from the opposite side: since pain is something everything avoids, its contrary must be something everything chooses. And he urged that what we choose for nothing beyond itself is the most choiceworthy thing of all, and that pleasure is plainly of this kind, since no one ever asks to what end a man is pleased — taking pleasure to be worth having in itself. Add it to any other good, he said — to a just act, to a temperate one — and you make that good more worth choosing; and a thing can only be increased by what is good.

But that last argument seems to prove only that pleasure belongs among the goods, not that it stands above the rest, since every good is more choiceworthy paired with another than taken alone. In fact Plato uses just this argument the other way, to show pleasure is not the chief good: the life of pleasure is more choiceworthy combined with wisdom than without it, but if the mixture is better, then pleasure by itself is not the chief good — for the chief good is the one thing that cannot be made more choiceworthy by any addition. So whatever can be improved by combination with some other good is ruled out. As for those who object that "what everything aims at need not be good," I cannot see much in it. What everyone believes, we say is so; and the man who pulls that ground from under us has nothing steadier to put in its place. If only the brute animals desired pleasure he might have a point — but the rational desire it too, and even in the lower animals there may be some natural good above themselves that aims at the good proper to them.

Nor does the worry hold that pleasure cannot be good because pain is bad — that evil can be opposed to evil, and both to what is neither. True in general, but not here. If both pleasure and pain were bad, both would be things to avoid; if neither, neither would be — yet plainly men avoid the one as bad and choose the other as good, so the opposition is complete. And it is no objection that pleasure is not a quality: neither are the activities of virtue, neither is happiness, and these are surely goods. Some say the chief good is bounded but pleasure unbounded, because it admits of more and less. But if they mean the feeling itself, the same could be said of justice and the other virtues, in respect of which a man is plainly called more or less just, more or less brave. And if they mean the pleasures themselves, perhaps they have missed the real cause — that some are pure and some are mixed. Health is bounded and still admits of degrees; why not pleasure?

Then comes the charge that the chief good is complete while every movement and coming-to-be is incomplete, and that pleasure is a movement or a coming-to-be. But it is not even a movement. Every movement has a speed, fast or slow — if not in itself then relative to something — yet pleasure has neither. A man may become pleased quickly, as he may grow angry quickly, but he cannot be pleased quickly, nor quickly relative to anyone else, though he can certainly walk or grow fast or slow. And how could pleasure be a coming-to-be? A thing comes to be from what it later dissolves back into — but pleasure has no such partner, for what pain destroys is not what pleasure builds up. They say pain is a falling-short of what nature requires and pleasure the filling of it; but those are states of the body, and if pleasure were really a refilling, the thing being refilled would feel the pleasure — the body — which no one believes. The notion grew, I think, out of the pleasures and pains of eating: men feel a lack, suffer for it, and then enjoy the filling. But not every pleasure is like that. The pleasures of mathematics involve no prior pain at all; nor do many of the pleasures of smell, or many sounds, sights, memories, hopes. Of what could these be a filling, when there was no emptiness to fill?

What of the disgraceful pleasures people bring up? We can answer that these are not really pleasant — not pleasant simply because they are pleasant to the corrupt, any more than what tastes sweet to the sick is really sweet, or what looks white to the man with diseased eyes is really white. Or we can say the pleasures are choiceworthy, only not from those sources — as wealth is good, but not as the wages of treason; or health, but not at the price of eating something vile. Or, better still, that pleasures differ in kind: the pleasure that comes from noble things is a different thing from the pleasure that comes from base ones, and you cannot have the just man's pleasure without being just, or the musician's without being musical. The very difference everyone draws between a friend and a flatterer shows as much — the friend aims at your good in the friendship, the flatterer only at your pleasure, and we reproach the one and praise the other, as men with different ends. No one, after all, would choose to live his whole life with a child's mind, however much he enjoyed what children enjoy, or to take pleasure in doing something utterly shameful, even on the guarantee of never being pained for it. And there are many things we would work hard for even if no pleasure came of them at all — seeing, remembering, knowing, having the virtues. That pleasure follows on these by nature changes nothing; we would choose them with no pleasure attached. So this much is plain: pleasure is not the chief good, and not every pleasure is worth choosing, but some are worth choosing in themselves, differing in kind by the source they spring from. So much for the common talk about pleasure and pain.

What pleasure actually is, and what sort of thing, comes clearer if we start again from the beginning. Seeing seems to be complete at any moment of it; it lacks nothing that could be added later to round out its nature. Pleasure is like that. It is a whole, and you could not find, at any instant, a pleasure whose nature would be completed by lasting longer. This is why it is not a movement. Every movement takes time and aims at some end — the building of a house is finished only when the builder has made what he set out to make, either in the whole stretch of time or in a given part of it. The lesser movements inside it are each incomplete in their own stretch, and different in kind from the whole and from one another: setting the stones is one movement, fluting a column another, raising the temple a third, and only the last is complete, lacking nothing for its end — laying the foundation and cutting a single triglyph are incomplete, each being the movement of a mere part. So with walking and the rest: motion from here to there comes in kinds — flying, walking, leaping — and even within walking the where-from and where-to differ across the whole course and within any part of it, for a man is not just crossing a line but crossing a line in a particular place, and this place is not that one.

I have dealt with movement exactly elsewhere; here it is enough to say that it is not complete at any given moment, and that most movements are incomplete and differ in kind, since their whence and whither make different species of them. But pleasure is complete in its whole nature at any moment whatever. So pleasure and movement must be two different things, and pleasure belongs among things that are whole and complete. The same follows from this: a thing cannot move except over some stretch of time, but pleasure needs no stretch — what is there at the very present instant is a kind of whole. So men are wrong to call pleasure a movement or a coming-to-be. Those words fit only what is divisible and not a whole: there is no coming-to-be of an act of seeing, none of a point, none of a unit, and none of these is a movement either. And so there is none of pleasure, for it is, one might say, a whole.

Now every faculty of perception is set to work on the object that answers to it, and works best when the faculty is in good condition and the object is the finest within its range — for that is about what we mean by perfect activity, whether we credit the faculty itself or the thing the faculty resides in. In each case, then, the best activity is that of the best-conditioned faculty upon the best of its proper objects; and this activity will also be the most complete and the most pleasant. For pleasure goes with every perceiving faculty, and likewise with every act of thought and contemplation; and the most pleasant is the most complete, and the most complete is the work of the best faculty on the finest of its objects. Pleasure completes the activity — but not the way the faculty and the object complete it, by being its causes, any more than health and the physician are causes of being well in the same sense.

Pleasure completes the activity not as a settled state already in it, but as an end that supervenes on it, like the bloom of youth on those in their prime. So long as the object of thought or perception is what it should be, and the faculty that judges or takes it in is what it should be, there will be pleasure in the activity; for when the thing apt to act and the thing apt to be acted on are alike and stand in the right relation, the same result follows of its own nature. Why, then, does no one feel pleasure without a break? Because we tire — no human faculty can keep up unbroken exertion, and so the pleasure flags too, since it rides on the activity. It is the same reason some things delight us when new and less so afterward: at first the mind is roused and works at full stretch, the way men stare hard at a thing they have just caught sight of; later the working slackens into something careless, and the pleasure dulls with it. One might conclude that everyone reaches for pleasure because everyone reaches for life, and life is an activity, and each man works at and with the things he loves best — the musical man works at music with his hearing, the studious man at problems with his mind, and so on. Pleasure completes the activities, and so completes the life men reach for; no wonder they reach for pleasure too. (Whether we choose life for the sake of pleasure or pleasure for the sake of life we may leave aside — the two are yoked together and will not come apart, since there is no pleasure without activity, and every activity pleasure completes.)

This is part of why pleasures seem to differ in kind: we suppose that things differing in kind are completed by things that differ in kind — as we plainly see in the works of nature and of craft, animals and trees, paintings and statues, houses and furniture. So activities differing in kind, we think, are completed by pleasures that differ likewise. And the activities of thought differ in kind from those of the senses, and the senses from one another; so do the pleasures that complete them. You can see it too in how tightly each pleasure is bound to the activity it completes. The pleasure proper to an activity sharpens it — those who work with pleasure judge more finely and carry things through with more precision. Men become geometers by taking pleasure in geometry and so grasp its points more fully; lovers of music, of building, of anything, each get better at their own pursuit because they enjoy it. The pleasures help the activities grow, and what helps a thing grow is proper to it; and what is proper to things that differ in kind itself differs in kind.

It shows even more clearly in this: the pleasure that belongs to one activity gets in the way of another. People fond of the flute cannot keep their minds on a conversation once they catch the sound of it, because they take more pleasure in flute-playing than in the thing they were doing — the pleasure of the flute wrecks the activity of talk. It is the same whenever a man does two things at once: the more pleasant of the two crowds out the other, and if the gap in pleasantness is wide enough, crowds it out altogether, till he stops doing it at all. This is why, when something pleases us greatly, we do nothing else; and only when one occupation pleases us mildly do we mix in another — the way men nibble sweets in the theater most when the show is poor. Since, then, the proper pleasure makes an activity sharper, longer-lasting, and better of its kind, while a foreign pleasure spoils it, the two are plainly far apart. In fact a foreign pleasure does to an activity nearly what the pain proper to it does — and that pain destroys the activity. If a man dislikes writing, or another dislikes adding sums, the one will not write and the other will not reckon, because the activity comes with pain. So an activity's proper pleasures and proper pains pull it opposite ways; and a foreign pleasure, as we said, works rather like the proper pain — it destroys the activity, only not in the same manner.

Now since activities differ in goodness and badness — some to be chosen, some to be avoided, some neither — their pleasures differ the same way, each activity having its own proper pleasure. The pleasure proper to a good activity is good, that proper to a bad one bad; for even our desires are praised when they reach for what is noble and blamed when they reach for what is base. And the pleasures are even more tightly knit to the activities than the desires are. The desires stand apart from the activity in time and in nature, but the pleasures lie so close to it as to raise the question whether activity and pleasure are one and the same. Still, pleasure is surely not the act of thought or the act of perceiving — that would be absurd; it only looks the same because it is never found apart from them. As the activities differ, so do their pleasures. Sight is purer than touch, hearing and smell purer than taste, and their pleasures rank accordingly; and the pleasures of thought are purer than these pleasures of sense, and the kinds within each rank differ among themselves.

Each animal, it seems, has a pleasure of its own, as it has a work of its own — the pleasure that rides on that work. Look case by case and you see it: horse, dog, and man take different pleasures; as Heraclitus says, an ass would sooner have its fodder than gold, since chaff is sweeter to an ass than gold is. So animals that differ in kind have pleasures that differ in kind, while we may reasonably suppose those of one kind do not differ. Among human beings, though, they differ no little: the very same things delight one man and pain another, are hateful to one and dear to another. As with sweet things — they will not seem the same to a man in a fever and a man in health — and with warmth, which the sick and the strong judge differently, and so with much else. In all such cases what really is the case is what strikes the good man as being so. If that holds — and it is the usual view, that the good man, just because he is good, is the measure of everything — then the real pleasures are the ones that strike him as pleasures, and the pleasant things are the ones he himself takes pleasure in. No wonder what he finds unpleasant strikes someone else as pleasant, for men suffer many corruptions and deformities; those things are not really pleasant, only pleasant to these particular people in their particular state.

So with the openly shameful pleasures it is clear we must deny they are pleasures at all, except to ruined tastes. But of the pleasures thought good, which one, or what sort, should we call the pleasure proper to man? Surely the answer is plain from the activities, since the pleasures follow on them. Whether there is one activity, or several, that belongs to the complete and blessed man, the pleasures that complete those activities are the ones we should call properly and chiefly the pleasures of man; the rest are his only in a secondary sense, and to the degree that the activities are.

Now that we have spoken of the virtues, of friendship in its kinds, and of pleasure, it remains to sketch happiness, since we take it to be the one end of all that men do; and we will save labor by gathering up what was said before. We said happiness is not a mere state. If it were, it could belong to a man asleep his whole life, living like a vegetable, or to a man sunk in the worst disasters. If we will not have that, and rank it instead among activities — and activities come in two sorts, those chosen for the sake of something else and those chosen for themselves — then plainly happiness goes among the activities chosen for their own sake, not those aimed beyond themselves; for happiness lacks nothing, it is self-sufficient. The activities chosen for their own sake are those from which we seek nothing past the doing — and the actions that accord with virtue are of this kind, since doing what is noble and good is a thing chosen for itself alone.

Pleasant amusements seem to be of this kind too, since we do not pursue them for anything further — though in truth they do us more harm than good, making us careless of our bodies and our property. Most of the men reputed happy take refuge in such pastimes, which is why men clever at them stand high in the favor of tyrants: they make themselves agreeable in just the things the tyrant wants, and so the tyrant wants such men. These amusements pass for parts of happiness because men in power spend their leisure in them — but we should not argue from such men, for there is no virtue and no intelligence in holding power, and these are the only sources of good activity. And if these men, having never tasted a pure and free pleasure, fall back on the pleasures of the body, that is no reason to count those pleasures higher; children too think their own treasures the finest things there are. It stands to reason that as children and grown men prize different things, so do the bad and the good. As we have said many times, the things truly worth prizing and truly pleasant are those that seem so to the good man; and for each person the activity most worth choosing is the one that accords with his own state — so for the good man, the activity that accords with virtue.

Happiness, then, does not consist in amusement. It would be absurd for the end to be amusement — for a man to toil and suffer all his life in order to play. We choose almost everything for the sake of something else, except happiness, which is itself the end; and to take pains and labor for the sake of play is plainly silly and childish. To play in order to work afterward, as Anacharsis says, is the right way round, for amusement is a kind of rest, and we rest because we cannot work without a break. Rest is not an end; it is taken for the sake of the work to come. The happy life is held to be a life lived in virtue, and such a life is lived in earnest, not at play. What we do in earnest we count better than the merely comic and playful; and the activity of the better part, or the better man, is the more earnest — and the activity of the better is at once better and more capable of happiness. As for bodily pleasures, any ordinary man, even a slave, can enjoy them as fully as the best man alive; but no one grants a slave a share in happiness, except in so far as he shares in a human life — because happiness lies not in such pastimes but in activity in accordance with virtue, as we have said before.

If happiness is activity in accordance with virtue, it stands to reason that it accords with the highest virtue — the virtue of the best thing in us. Call that best thing intellect, or whatever else it is that naturally rules and leads and grasps what is noble and divine, whether it is itself divine or only the most divine thing we have: its activity, in accordance with its own proper virtue, will be complete happiness. That this activity is contemplative we have already said, and it agrees both with what we argued before and with the truth. For one, it is the highest activity, since intellect is the highest thing in us, and the objects it works upon are the highest things there are to know. For another, it is the most continuous: we can contemplate truth more steadily, without a break, than we can do anything else. And we hold that happiness must have pleasure mixed in, and of all the activities that accord with virtue the one that accords with wisdom is admittedly the most pleasant — the pursuit of wisdom holds pleasures of remarkable purity and permanence, and it stands to reason that those who already have knowledge pass their time more pleasantly than those still hunting for it.

The self-sufficiency people speak of will belong most of all to contemplation. The bare necessities of life the wise man needs as much as the just man and all the rest; but grant them all an equal supply of these, and the just man still needs people toward whom and alongside whom to act justly, and so does the temperate man, and the brave man, and the others — while the wise man can contemplate even on his own, and the wiser he is the more he can. He may do it better with fellow-workers, but for all that he is the most self-sufficient. Contemplation, too, seems to be the one thing loved for itself alone, since nothing comes of it beyond the contemplating, whereas from action we always mean to get something more than the act, be it much or little. And happiness is thought to lie in leisure: we work in order to have leisure, and make war in order to be at peace. But the practical virtues find their exercise in politics or in war, and these actions seem to allow no leisure — war least of all, for no one chooses war or prepares for war just for war's sake; a man would be thought a bloodthirsty monster who made enemies of his friends to keep the fighting and the killing going. The statesman's work is no more leisured: beyond governing itself, it reaches for power and honors, or at least for the happiness of the statesman and his fellow-citizens — a happiness plainly distinct from the public business, and which we pursue as something separate from it.

So if, among the actions that accord with virtue, those of politics and war stand foremost in nobility and scale, yet are unleisured, aim at an end beyond themselves, and are not chosen for their own sake — while the activity of intellect, being contemplative, is held to exceed them in earnestness, to aim at no end beyond itself, and to carry a pleasure all its own that makes the activity grow — and if self-sufficiency, leisure, and tirelessness (as far as a human being can be tireless), with every other mark of the highest happiness, plainly belong to this activity, then this must be a man's complete happiness, given a full span of life, for nothing about happiness can be left unfinished. But such a life will be higher than the merely human. A man will live it not in so far as he is a man but in so far as something divine is present in him; and as far as that thing surpasses the composite creature, so far does its activity surpass activity in accordance with the rest of virtue. If intellect is divine compared with a human being, then the life of intellect is divine compared with an ordinary human life.

We should not listen to those who tell us that, being men, we should think only mortal thoughts and mind only human affairs. As far as we can, we should make ourselves immortal, and strain every nerve to live by the highest thing in us; small as it is in bulk, in power and worth it far surpasses everything else. And this thing would seem to be each man's very self, since it is the sovereign and better part of him; so it would be strange for a man to choose not his own life but the life of something other than himself. What we said before applies here: what is proper to each thing is by nature best and most pleasant for it. For a man, then, that is the life of intellect — since this, more than anything, is the man. So this life is also the happiest. Second comes the life of the other kind of virtue, for the activities that accord with it are properly human: we do just, brave, and other virtuous acts toward one another, in contracts and services and every sort of action and feeling, holding to what befits each case — and all this is plainly human. Some of moral virtue even seems to arise from the body, and in much of it virtue is closely tied up with the passions. Practical wisdom, too, is bound up with virtue of character, since the starting-points of practical wisdom accord with the moral virtues, and the moral virtues are made right by practical wisdom. Bound up with the passions as they are, these belong to the composite creature; and the virtues of the composite creature are human virtues, and so the life and the happiness that accord with them are human too. The happiness of intellect stands apart by itself. Let this much suffice — to go into it exactly is more than our purpose needs.

It would seem, too, to need external goods only slightly, or at least less than moral happiness needs them. Grant that both lives need the necessities equally — though in fact the man who lives in society takes more trouble over his body and the like, so the difference there is small — when we come to their activities the difference is wide. The generous man needs money to do generous acts with, the just man needs it to meet his obligations (since mere wishes are invisible, and even the unjust pretend they wish to act justly), and the brave man needs strength if he is to do anything brave, and the temperate man needs the chance of temptation — else how shall any of them show what he is? But the man at his contemplation needs none of these things for his work; if anything they get in the way. As a man, and one living among others, he chooses to do what virtue requires, so he will need such goods for living a human life — but not for contemplating.

That complete happiness is a kind of contemplation can be seen another way as well. We take the gods to be above all others blessed and happy — but what sort of actions shall we credit them with? Just ones? It is laughable to picture the gods making contracts and returning deposits. Brave ones — standing their ground against terrors, facing danger because it is noble? Generous ones — and to whom would they give, and is it not absurd to suppose they have money or the like? As for temperate acts, what would theirs be? Surely it is a sorry compliment to say the gods have no base desires. Run the thing through and every circumstance of moral action looks trivial and unworthy of gods. Yet everyone believes the gods are alive, and therefore active — we do not suppose they sleep their lives away like Endymion. And if you take action from a living being, and still more if you take making, what is left but contemplation? So the activity of the gods, supreme in blessedness, must be contemplative; and of human activities the one nearest akin to this has the greatest share in happiness. A sign of it is that the other animals have no part in happiness, being wholly shut out from any such activity. The gods' whole life is blessed; man's is blessed so far as it holds some likeness of that activity; and no other animal is happy, since none shares in contemplation at all. Happiness reaches just as far as contemplation does: the more a being contemplates, the happier it is, not as a by-product but in virtue of the contemplation itself, for that is precious in its own right. And so happiness will be a form of contemplation.

Still, the man we are speaking of is a man, so he will need outward prosperity too — his nature is not by itself enough for contemplation; the body must be healthy, and fed, and otherwise cared for. But we must not conclude that, because a man cannot be greatly happy without external goods, he therefore needs many great ones to be happy at all. Self-sufficiency does not lie in excess, nor does action, and it is quite possible to act nobly without being master of land and sea; with moderate means a man can act in accordance with virtue. You can see it plainly — private men are thought to act justly no less than men in power, and even more. It is enough that just so much be ours as is necessary, for the life of the man who acts in accordance with virtue will be a happy one. Solon, perhaps, drew the happy man well: moderately furnished with external goods, having done (as he thought) the noblest deeds, and having lived temperately — for it is quite possible for men of moderate means to do as they ought. Anaxagoras, too, seems to have thought the happy man neither rich nor powerful, saying he would not be surprised if such a man struck the crowd as strange, since the crowd judges by outward things, the only things it perceives. So the opinions of the wise agree with our account. Such opinions carry some weight, but in matters of action the truth is judged from the facts and from how life is actually lived, for there the decision rests. The thing to do, then, is to test what we have said against the facts and against real life: where it agrees with the facts, accept it; where it clashes, treat it as mere theory.

The man who works with his intellect and tends it, keeping it in its best condition, seems also to be the one the gods love most. For if the gods care at all for human affairs, as men believe, it stands to reason that they delight in what is best and most akin to themselves — which must be intellect — and that they reward those who love and honor it above all, as men who cherish what the gods cherish and who act rightly and nobly. That the wise man combines all this is plain; so he is dearest to the gods, and likely the happiest of men. On this count too, then, the wise man will be happiest.

Now that we have said enough, in our outline way, about the virtues, and about friendship and pleasure, are we to suppose our task is done? Surely not. In matters of action, as everyone says, the end is not to study and know but to do; it is not enough to know about virtue — we must try to have it and use it, and take whatever other path there is to becoming good. If arguments and talk were by themselves enough to make men good, they would have earned, as Theognis says, great and many rewards, and providing such talk would be all we needed. But in fact, while arguments have the power to spur and steer the generous among the young, and to make a noble, well-bred character take hold of true virtue, they have no power to turn the mass of men toward goodness. For the many are not by nature obedient to a sense of shame but only to fear, and they hold back from base things not because those things are disgraceful but because of the punishment. Living by their feelings, they chase their own pleasures and the means to them and run from the opposite pains, while of the noble and the truly pleasant they have no notion, never having tasted it.

What argument could reshape men like that? It is impossible, or nearly so, to shift by talk what has long since settled into a man's character. Perhaps we should be content if, with every aid to goodness in hand, we manage to get some share of virtue. Some say we become good by nature, some by habit, some by teaching. Nature's part, whatever it is, plainly does not rest with us; it belongs, by some divine cause, to the truly fortunate. And argument and teaching, I fear, have no power over everyone. The soul of the learner must first have been worked, by habits, into the right loves and hatreds — like soil that must be prepared before it can nourish the seed. A man who lives by his feelings will not listen to an argument meant to turn him, and could not grasp it if he did; and how do you reform a man in that state? In general, feeling seems to yield not to argument but to force. So there must be, to begin with, a character with some kinship to virtue, one that loves the noble and loathes the base.

But to get the right upbringing toward virtue from earliest youth is hard unless one is raised under laws of the right kind, for a life of temperance and endurance is not pleasant to most people, least of all to the young. This is why upbringing and the whole way of life ought to be ordered by law: once a thing has become habitual, it stops being unpleasant. And it is not enough to get the right rearing and care while young; men must keep up these practices, and grow used to them, even after they are grown, so we need laws for this too — laws, in short, covering the whole of life. For the many obey compulsion sooner than argument, and penalties sooner than the noble. Hence some hold that lawgivers should urge men toward virtue for the sake of the noble, expecting those well trained in good habits to listen, while imposing punishments and penalties on the disobedient and the poorly endowed, and banishing the incurable altogether — since the good man, who lives with his eye on the noble, will heed reason, while the base, who chase pleasure, must be checked by pain, like beasts of burden. This is also why they say the pains should be those most opposed to the pleasures the offender loves.

To say it again: the man who is to be good must have been well brought up and habituated, must then go on living under good institutions, and must never do anything base, whether against his will or with it. And this can only happen where men live under some governing intelligence and a right order with the power to enforce it. A father's authority has neither the strength nor the compulsion for it, and neither does the authority of any one man, unless he is a king or the like; but the law has power to compel, being reasoned speech that issues from practical wisdom and intelligence. We resent the men who oppose our impulses, however rightly they do it, but no one hates the law for laying down what is right. Yet Sparta is just about the only city where the founder seems to have given thought to the citizens' upbringing and way of life. In most cities these things are neglected, and each man lives as he pleases, laying down the law to wife and children Cyclops-fashion.

Best of all, then, would be a right public system, well carried out. But since these matters are publicly neglected, it seems to fall to each man to advance virtue in his own children and friends — or at least to set himself to do it. And from what we have said, he will be best able to do this by becoming a kind of lawgiver himself. Public systems are everywhere shaped by laws, and good ones by good laws; whether written or unwritten, whether they train one person or many, makes no real difference, just as it makes none in music or athletics or any pursuit gained by practice. As public laws and customs hold sway in a community, so a father's words and the family's customs hold sway in a household — and hold it even more firmly, through blood and the benefits given, for there you start with people who love you and are naturally disposed to obey. Private training has its advantages over public, as it does in medicine: as a rule a feverish man should rest and fast, but in a given case this may not hold; the boxer does not use the same guard against every opponent. The individual, it seems, is most exactly served by private care, since each is more likely to get what suits him. Even so, anyone — physician, trainer, or other — will handle the single case better for knowing the general rule, that such-and-such is good for all, or for all of a certain kind; for the sciences are said to deal, and really do deal, with what is general. A man may, from sheer experience, take excellent care of one particular case without any science of it — as some are thought to be their own best doctors, though they could do nothing for anyone else. Still, for one who means to be a true expert, master of the theory of his art, there is no avoiding the general principles; he must come to know them and all they cover.

If, then, we become good through laws, the man who means to make others better by care and training must try to make a lawgiver of himself, for to handle skillfully whoever is set before you is not work for just anyone, but for one who knows — as in medicine and every art that calls for care and skill. Then is our next task to ask from what sources, and how, a man may acquire this power of legislation? As in other cases, we might say, from the statesmen, since this seemed to be a branch of the science of the community. But the political art plainly does not stand like the other sciences and skills. Everywhere else the same men both teach a power and exercise it — physicians, painters — but with statecraft the sophists profess to teach it while not one of them practices it; that is left to those actually in politics, who seem to act by some knack and by experience rather than by thought. They neither write nor speak on these matters, though that might do them more credit than composing courtroom and assembly speeches; and they have not made statesmen of their own sons or their friends — which they surely would have done if they could, since they could leave their cities no finer legacy, and would have wished no power above this one for themselves or their dearest. Practice, all the same, counts for no little, for merely breathing the air of politics would never have made statesmen of them; so anyone who wants to understand statecraft must have practice as well.

As for the sophists who profess to teach it, they are plainly far from doing so — they have no notion of what it even is, or they would not class it with rhetoric, or rank it lower, and would not think legislating an easy matter of "just collecting the famous laws and picking the best," as if selection were not itself a skill, and judging rightly the hardest thing of all, as it is in music. Only those with practical knowledge of a thing can judge its products rightly, or see by what means and in what way they are achieved, and what fits with what; the inexpert can only tell, at best, whether the result is good or bad, as with a painting. Laws are the products, so to speak, of the political art — so how could a man learn from them the power to legislate, or to pick the best? We do not see men made physicians by reading handbooks, though these try to give not only the cases but how to cure them and how to treat each kind of constitution — useful to the professional, useless to the layman. So too collections of laws and constitutions may be very useful to those able to study them and judge what is good and bad and what suits what; but those who go through them without that capacity cannot judge rightly, except by luck, though they may grow sharper in such matters.

Since, then, our predecessors have left the subject of legislation unexamined, it is better, perhaps, that we look into it ourselves, and into the whole question of the constitution, so that the philosophy of human affairs may be completed as far as we are able. First, let us try to glean whatever is sound in what our predecessors said. Then, from the constitutions we have collected, let us see what kinds of things preserve and what kinds destroy communities, and what does the same for particular constitutions, and why some are well governed and others badly — for after such an inquiry we shall be better placed to take a whole view of which constitution is best, how each should be ordered, and what laws and customs each should use. Let us begin, then.
