# The Aeneid, Book VI

The Descent to the Underworld

So he spoke, and wept; then he let out the sails and at last reached the shores of Cumae. The Trojans swung their prows to face the sea and dropped anchor; the curved sterns lined the beach. The men sprang out, hungry for Italy. Some struck fire from the veins of flint; some raided the woods that house the wild beasts, hunting for kindling, and pointed the way to the streams they found. But dutiful Aeneas made for the heights where Apollo reigns, and for the secret cave set apart, the vast retreat of the dread Sibyl — the prophetess into whom the seer of Delos breathes his great mind and his will, laying the future open before her.

Now they came to the grove of Diana and the temple roofed with gold. The story goes that Daedalus, fleeing the kingdom of Crete, dared to trust himself to the sky on beating wings, and floated up by that strange road toward the cold north, until at last he came to rest, gently, on this Cumaean height. Here, on the ground he first touched again, he dedicated his oars of feathers to you, Apollo, and built a great temple. On its doors he carved the death of Androgeos; then the Athenians, ordered to pay for it each year with seven of their sons; there stood the urn, the lots already drawn. Facing it across the door rose the land of Crete out of the sea. There was the brutal love of the bull, and Pasiphae coupling with it by stealth; the mongrel breed, the Minotaur, half man and half beast — the record of a monstrous lust. There too was the famous house of toil, the Labyrinth and its hopeless winding; but Daedalus himself, pitying the great love of the princess, untangled the maze's tricks and its windings, guiding blind footsteps with a thread. And you, Icarus — you would have had a great part in this carved work, if grief had let him. Twice he tried to shape your fall in the gold, and twice the father's hands fell.

The Trojans would have read through it all, every figure, but Achates, sent ahead, came back, and with him the priestess of Apollo and Diana, Deiphobe, daughter of Glaucus. She spoke to the king: "This is no time to stand and feed your eyes on pictures. Better now to sacrifice seven bullocks from an untouched herd, and seven chosen ewes, as the rite requires." The Trojans were quick to obey her sacred command, and the priestess called them into the high temple.

The vast flank of the Cumaean cliff is carved into a cave, and a hundred wide mouths, a hundred doors, lead into it; out of them rush as many voices, the Sibyl's answers. They had come to the threshold when the maiden cried: "Now is the time to ask your fate. The god — look, the god is here!" And as she spoke, there at the doors, all at once her face changed and its color drained; her hair fell loose; her breath came in gasps and her wild heart swelled with frenzy. She seemed taller, and her voice was not the voice of a mortal, for the god was on her now, breathing close. "Are you slow with your prayers and your vows, Aeneas of Troy?" she cried. "Are you slow? Not until you pray will the great mouths of this awestruck house gape open." With that she fell silent. A cold shudder ran through the hard bones of the Trojans, and the king poured out his prayers from the bottom of his heart:

"Apollo, you who have always pitied the heavy sufferings of Troy, who guided the Trojan arrow and the hand of Paris into the body of Achilles — under your lead I have entered so many seas that wash so many lands, the far-off Massylian tribes and the fields the Syrtes fringe. Now at last we have the shores of Italy in our grasp, always fleeing before us. Let the bad luck of Troy follow us no farther than here. And you, prophetess, most holy, you who know what is coming: grant — for I ask no kingdom my fate does not owe me — that the Trojans may settle in Latium, with their wandering gods and the storm-tossed powers of Troy. Then I will raise a temple of solid marble to Apollo and Diana, and ordain festival days in Apollo's name. And you too, prophetess, a great shrine waits for you in my kingdom; for there I will set your oracles and the secret words of fate spoken to my people, and consecrate chosen priests to keep them. Only do not trust your prophecies to leaves, or they will fly scattered, the playthings of every wind. Speak them aloud yourself, I beg you." And he said no more.

But the prophetess, not yet broken to Apollo, raged through the cave like something wild, trying to shake the great god from her breast; and all the harder he wore down her raving mouth, mastering her wild heart, breaking her to his will. And now the hundred huge mouths of the house swung open of themselves and carried the seer's answer through the air: "You who are at last done with the great dangers of the sea — but worse ones wait by land. The Trojans will come into the kingdom of Latium; put that fear aside; but they will wish they had never come. Wars, terrible wars I see, and the Tiber foaming with much blood. You will not lack a Simois, a Xanthus, a Greek camp; another Achilles is already born in Latium, he too the son of a goddess. And Juno, who fastens on the Trojans, will never let go; while you, brought low, will go begging through all the towns and tribes of Italy. The cause of all that ruin will be, once more, a foreign bride taken in, a foreign marriage. Do not give way to your troubles; go to meet them, bolder than your luck will let you. The first road to safety — and you will not believe it — will open from a Greek city."

With words like these the Sibyl of Cumae chanted out her dreadful riddles from deep in her cave, the truth wrapped in darkness — Apollo shaking the reins as she raged, twisting the goad under her breast. When the frenzy faded and the raving mouth fell quiet, Aeneas the hero began: "No new face of hardship can rise up before me now, prophetess; I have already lived through it all in my mind, gone over every part of it. One thing I ask. Since they say the gate of the underworld is here, and the dark marsh where Acheron floods back — let me go down to the sight of my dear father, into his presence. Teach me the way; open the sacred doors. On these shoulders I carried him out through the flames and a thousand spears at our backs, and saved him from the heart of the enemy. He shared my journey, bore the seas with me, all the threats of ocean and sky, frail as he was, past the strength of an old man, past his allotted span. He himself charged me, pleading, to come to your door and beg you. Pity the son and the father, kind one — for you have the power; not for nothing did the queen of the dead set you over the groves of Avernus. If Orpheus could call back the shade of his wife, trusting his Thracian lyre and its singing strings; if Pollux could buy back his brother by dying for him in turn, going down and coming back, again and again — why name Theseus, why name great Hercules? I too am sprung from highest Jupiter." So he prayed, with his hand on the altar.

Then the prophetess began: "Son of Anchises, born of the blood of gods, the way down to the underworld is easy: the door of black Dis stands open night and day. But to retrace your step, to climb back out to the air above — that is the labor, that is the task. A few have done it, sons of the gods whom Jupiter favored, or men whose blazing courage carried them up to heaven. Forests choke all the middle country, and Cocytus glides around it, looping it in its black coils. But if such longing is in you, such a hunger to swim the Stygian lake twice and to see twice the black depths of Tartarus — if you must do this mad thing — then hear first what you have to do. There is a tree, hidden in the shadows, with a bough whose leaves and pliant stem are gold, sacred to Juno of the underworld; the whole grove screens it, and the dark of the deep valleys shuts it in. But no one is let down into the hidden places of the earth until he has plucked that golden-leaved growth from the tree. Beautiful Proserpina has decreed this be brought to her as her gift. When the first bough is torn away a second comes, golden, and the same metal leafs out on the twig. So search with your eyes lifted, and when you find it, reach up and take it as the rite commands. If the fates are calling you, it will come away willingly and with ease; if not, no force will master it, no hard blade hack it free. And one thing more — you do not know it, but the body of a friend lies dead and unburied, and his corpse is fouling your whole fleet while you linger here at my door asking your questions. Carry him first to his resting place, and lay him in the tomb. Lead black cattle to the altar; let those be your first offerings of atonement. Only then will you look on the Stygian groves, the kingdom no living foot may tread." She finished, and pressed her lips shut.

Aeneas went, his eyes fixed on the ground, his face heavy, and left the cave, turning the dark riddle over and over in his mind. Loyal Achates walked at his side, weighed down by the same cares. The two of them traded guess after guess between them — which dead friend was the Sibyl speaking of, whose body was there to bury? And then, as they came down, they saw on the dry shore Misenus, fallen by a death he did not deserve. Misenus, the son of Aeolus — no man better at firing the line with his war-trumpet, at kindling the rage of Mars with its brazen call. He had been the companion of great Hector, fighting at Hector's side with trumpet and spear alike; and when Achilles cut Hector down, the brave man had joined Aeneas, following no lesser lord. But that day, blowing a hollow conch over the sea, the fool, he had dared to call the gods to a contest; and Triton, jealous — if the story can be believed — had caught him and drowned him among the rocks in the foaming surf. So they all stood around him, crying out, and dutiful Aeneas most of all. Then, weeping, without delay, they set about the Sibyl's orders, and worked to raise the funeral pyre, a tree-built altar reaching for the sky. They went into the old forest, the deep lair of the beasts; down came the pitch-pines, the holm-oak rang under the axes, ash-logs and oak were split with wedges, and they rolled great rowan-trunks down from the hills. Aeneas was first among them at the work, urging his men on, taking up the same tools.

And as he labored he looked out over the great forest, and turned a prayer over in his own heart: "If only that golden bough would show itself to me now, somewhere in this huge wood — since everything the prophetess foretold of you, Misenus, was true, too cruelly true." He had barely spoken when, by chance, a pair of doves came flying down out of the sky and settled on the green grass right before his eyes. The great hero knew his mother's birds, and prayed in joy: "Be my guides, if there is any road; steer your flight through the air to the grove where the rich bough shadows the fertile ground. And you, goddess my mother, do not fail me now." So he spoke, and held still, watching what signs they gave, which way they went. They fed, then flew on, only as far ahead as the eyes of a follower could keep them in sight. Then, when they reached the foul-breathing throat of Avernus, they rose quickly, slid down through the clear air, and lit on the longed-for tree, where the strange gold of the bough gleamed out against the green. As mistletoe greens in the cold of winter on a tree that did not breed it, wrapping the smooth trunk with its yellow shoots — so the gold leafed out of the shadowed oak, so the thin metal foil whispered in the light wind. Aeneas seized it at once, eager, broke it off though it held back, and carried it to the cave of the Sibyl.

Meanwhile the Trojans on the shore were weeping for Misenus, paying the last rites to ashes that could not feel them. First they raised a great pyre, rich with resinous pine and oak, and wove the sides with dark branches, set funeral cypress along the front, and crowned it on top with his shining armor. Some heated water, set the cauldrons boiling over the flames, and washed and anointed the cold body. There was a groaning then. They laid the wept-over corpse on the bier, threw the purple robes over it, the clothes he knew. Some shouldered the great bier — a sad office — and held the torch under it the old way, with their faces turned aside. Up it all went in fire: the heaped gifts, the incense, the bowls of oil. When the ash had fallen and the flames died, they washed the thirsty remains in wine, and Corynaeus gathered the bones and shut them in a bronze urn. Three times he carried pure water around the men and sprinkled them lightly with a dewed olive branch, cleansing them; and he spoke the last words of farewell. But dutiful Aeneas heaped a great mound over him for a tomb, and set on it the man's own gear — his oar, his trumpet — under a steep headland that is called, after him, Misenus to this day, and keeps his name alive through all the years.

This done, he hurried to carry out the Sibyl's commands. There was a deep cave, vast, with a wide and jagged mouth, sheltered by a black lake and the gloom of the woods; over it no bird could wing its way unharmed, so deadly was the breath that poured up out of those black jaws into the vault of the sky — which is why the Greeks named the place Avernus, the Birdless. Here first the priestess set up four black-backed bullocks and poured wine on their brows; then, plucking the bristles between their horns, she laid them on the holy fire as the first offering, calling aloud on Hecate, mighty in heaven and in Erebus. Others slid the knife in below and caught the warm blood in bowls. Aeneas himself killed a black-fleeced lamb for the mother of the Furies and her great sister Night, and a barren cow for you, Proserpina. Then he raised altars by night to the king of the dead, and laid whole bulls' bodies on the flames, pouring rich oil over the burning entrails. And look — just before the rim of the rising sun, the ground bellowed under their feet, the wooded ridges began to shake, and dogs seemed to howl through the half-dark as the goddess came. "Away! Stand off, you who are unhallowed," the prophetess cried, "clear out of the whole grove! And you, draw your sword and take the road; now, Aeneas, you need all your courage, all your steady heart." With that she plunged, raging, into the open cave; and he kept pace with his guide, step for step, unafraid.

You gods who hold the kingdom of the dead, you silent shades, Chaos and Phlegethon, you wide hushed places of the night — let me tell what I have heard; let me, with your blessing, lay open things buried deep in the dark earth.

On they went, dim figures under the lonely night, through the dark, through the empty halls of Dis and his kingdom of nothing. It was like walking a forest under a thin moon, a grudging light, when Jupiter has buried the sky in shadow and the black night has drained the world of color.

There, right at the entrance, in the very jaws of the underworld, Grief has made her bed, and gnawing Cares; pale Sicknesses live there, and sad Old Age, and Fear, and Hunger that drives men to evil, and squalid Poverty — shapes terrible to see — and Death, and the toil that comes before Death; and Death's own brother, Sleep; and the mind's guilty Pleasures; and on the threshold facing them, deadly War, and the iron bedchambers of the Furies, and mad Strife, her snake-hair bound with bloody ribbons. In the middle a vast dark elm spreads its ancient boughs and arms, and the empty Dreams are said to roost there in their crowds, clinging under every leaf. And there are many monstrous beasts of every kind stabled at the gates — Centaurs, and Scyllas in their double shape, and Briareus with his hundred hands, and the beast of Lerna hissing horribly, and the Chimera armed with fire, Gorgons and Harpies, and the shape of the three-bodied shade. Here Aeneas, caught by sudden terror, snatched up his sword and turned the bare blade to meet them; and if his wise guide had not reminded him that these were thin bodiless lives flitting under a hollow show of form, he would have rushed in and slashed the empty air with his steel for nothing.

From here runs the road to the waters of Acheron, the river of Tartarus. A whirlpool boils there, thick with mud, vast, and vomits all its sand into Cocytus. The ferryman who guards these waters and this flood is Charon, hideous, filthy: a mass of gray unkempt beard hangs from his chin, his eyes are two standing flames, and a foul cloak hangs knotted from his shoulder. He poles his boat himself, works the sails himself, and carries the dead across in his rust-colored skiff — old now, but a god's old age is green and hard. Down to the bank streamed the whole crowd, pouring toward it: mothers and husbands, the bodies of great-hearted heroes with the life gone out of them, boys, unmarried girls, young men laid on the pyre before their parents' eyes — as many as the leaves that fall and drift in the woods at the first chill of autumn, as many as the birds that flock to land from the deep sea when the cold of the year drives them across to sunlit countries. They stood there, begging to be first across, reaching out their hands in longing for the far shore. But the grim boatman takes now these, now those, and pushes the rest back, holding them off from the sand. Aeneas, moved and amazed at the uproar, said: "Tell me, prophetess, what does this crowding at the river mean? What do the souls want? By what rule are some turned away from the bank while others sweep the gray water with their oars?"

The aged priestess answered him shortly: "Son of Anchises, true child of the gods, what you see is the deep pool of the Styx, the marsh by whose name even the gods are afraid to swear and break their word. All this helpless crowd are the unburied. The ferryman is Charon; the ones he carries across are the buried dead. He is not allowed to take them over the dreadful banks and the roaring water until their bones are at rest in a grave. For a hundred years they drift, fluttering around these shores; only then are they let across and see again the pools they have longed for." The son of Anchises stopped, deep in thought, pitying in his heart their unfair lot. And there he saw, grieving, robbed of the honor of death, Leucaspis and Orontes the captain of the Lycian fleet — men who had sailed with him from Troy, and gone down together when the south wind rolled over both ship and crew in the storm.

And there the helmsman Palinurus came drifting up — Palinurus, who not long before, on the run from Africa, watching the stars, had fallen from the stern into the middle of the sea. Aeneas hardly knew the sad shape in all that gloom, but he spoke first: "Which of the gods tore you from us, Palinurus, and drowned you in the open water? Tell me. For Apollo, whom I never found false before, deceived me in this one answer: he swore you would come through the sea unharmed and reach Italy. Is this how he keeps his word?" But the shade answered: "Apollo's oracle did not lie to you, my captain, and no god drowned me. The truth is that the rudder I was clutching, the one I was set to steer, was wrenched away by a great force as I fell, and I dragged it down with me. By the rough seas I swear it: I was not afraid for myself, but for your ship — that it would lose its gear and its helmsman and founder in those rising waves. For three stormy nights the south wind drove me over the endless water; on the fourth dawn, riding the top of a swell, I sighted Italy. Little by little I swam to shore, and I had all but reached safety — I had my hands on the rough rocks of the cliff, my soaked clothes dragging me down — when a savage people fell on me with the sword, fools who took me for plunder. The waves have me now; the winds roll me along the shore. So by the sweet light and air of heaven I beg you, by your father, by the hope of your growing son Iulus, save me from this misery, unconquered one. Either throw earth over me — you can; go back and find the harbor of Velia — or, if there is some way, if your goddess mother shows you one (for you do not cross these great waters, this Stygian marsh, without a god's help), give your hand to a wretch and take me with you across the water, so that at least in death I may rest in a quiet place." He had hardly finished when the prophetess broke in: "Where does this longing come from, Palinurus? You, unburied, to look on the waters of the Styx, the dread river of the Furies, and come uncalled to the bank? Stop hoping that the will of the gods can be bent by prayer. But hear this, and let it comfort you in your hard fate. The people who wronged you will be driven by signs from heaven, far and wide through their cities, to atone for your bones, to build you a tomb and bring offerings to it; and the place will keep the name of Palinurus forever." These words eased his cares; for a little while the grief went out of his sad heart, glad to give his name to a stretch of land.

So they went on with their journey and drew near the river. The boatman saw them from the Stygian water while they were still in the silent wood, making for the bank, and he called out first, harshly, to challenge them: "You there, whoever you are, coming armed to my river — stop where you are and tell me why you have come. This is the country of shadows, of Sleep and the drowsy Night; living bodies are forbidden the Stygian boat. It brought me no joy to take Hercules across, or Theseus and Pirithous, sons of gods though they were, men of unbeaten strength. One came to drag the watchdog of the underworld up in chains from the very throne of the king, and left him trembling; the others tried to carry off the queen from her lord's own bed." The Sibyl of Apollo answered him in a few words: "There is no such treachery here; calm yourself. These weapons threaten no one. Let the huge watchdog bark on in his cave forever and frighten the bloodless dead; let Proserpina keep chaste in her uncle's house. Aeneas of Troy, famous for his devotion and his arms, is going down to the deepest shades to see his father. If the sight of such great faithfulness moves you not at all — then know this bough at least." And she drew the bough out from under her cloak and showed it. The swelling rage in his heart sank. He said no more, but stared in wonder at the sacred gift, the fated branch he had not seen in so long, swung his blue-black boat around, and brought it in to the bank. He cleared the other souls off the long thwarts, opened the gangway, and took the great Aeneas aboard. The patched boat groaned under his weight and shipped the marsh-water through its seams. But it carried the prophetess and the man safely across, and set them down on the foul mud among the gray reeds.

Cerberus, the huge dog, set these whole kingdoms ringing with the bark of his three throats, sprawled across the cave that faces the door. The prophetess, seeing the snakes bristle now along his necks, threw him a cake of honey and drugged meal to make him drowse. Mad with hunger, he opened all three mouths and snapped it up, then his enormous back went slack and he sank his whole vast bulk across the floor of his den. With the guard buried in sleep, Aeneas seized the entrance and was past, quickly, beyond the bank of the river there is no recrossing. At once a great wailing of voices came to his ears, the souls of babies crying — those whom the dark day stole from the breast at the very threshold of sweet life, snatched off to a bitter death. Beside them were the men condemned to die on a false charge; but the places here are not handed out without a lottery, without a judge. Minos shakes the urn and presides; he calls the silent court of the dead, and hears out their lives and what they were accused of. Next, in the place beyond, are the sad ones who, though guiltless, did away with themselves by their own hand, and threw their lives away because they hated the light. How gladly now they would bear poverty and hard toil up in the open air! But the law forbids it; the sad marsh and its hateful water hold them in, and the Styx, nine times wound around, pens them.

Not far from here lie the Fields of Mourning, spread out on every side — that is the name they are given — where those whom love wasted away with its cruel sickness wander hidden paths, screened by a forest of myrtle; not even in death does the ache leave them. Here Aeneas saw Phaedra and Procris, and Eriphyle showing the wounds her own son gave her; Evadne and Pasiphae; Laodamia walked with them, and Caeneus, once a young man, now a woman again, changed back by fate into the shape he was born with. And among them, her wound still fresh, Dido of Carthage was wandering in the great wood. The Trojan hero, the moment he stood near and made out her dim shape through the shadows — the way a man sees, or thinks he sees, the early-month moon rise through the clouds — wept, and spoke to her tenderly, with love: "Unhappy Dido — so the news that reached me was true, that you were gone, that you had run yourself through with the sword? Was I, then — was I the cause of your death? By the stars I swear it, by the high gods, by whatever faith there is here under the deep earth: against my own will, my queen, I left your shore. The orders of the gods, which drive me now through these shades, through this country of rot and bottomless night, forced me on; and I could never have believed my leaving would bring you a grief so terrible. Stop. Don't pull away from my sight. Who are you running from? This is the last time fate will let me speak to you." With words like these, with tears starting, Aeneas tried to soften that burning, fierce-eyed spirit. She kept her face turned away, her eyes fixed on the ground; his speech moved her no more than if she were set in hard flint or Parian marble. And then she tore herself away and fled, hating him, back into the shadow of the wood, where Sychaeus, her first husband, answers her grief and matches her love. And Aeneas, shaken still by the unfair stroke of it, followed her a long way with his eyes, weeping, and pitied her as she went.

From there he held to his given road. Now they had come to the farthest fields, the country set apart where the great warriors live. Here Tydeus came to meet him, and Parthenopaeus famous in arms, and the pale ghost of Adrastus; here were the Trojans, fallen in war and much wept-for in the upper world — and he groaned to see them all in their long ranks: Glaucus and Medon and Thersilochus, the three sons of Antenor, and Polyboetes the priest of Ceres, and Idaeus still gripping his chariot, still gripping his weapons. The souls crowded around him on the right and the left; it was not enough just to see him once — they wanted to keep him there, to walk at his side and learn why he had come. But the chiefs of the Greeks and the army of Agamemnon, when they saw the man and his armor flashing through the gloom, shook with terror; some turned to run, the way they once ran for their ships; some lifted a thin little cry — but the shout they tried for died, mocked, in their gaping mouths.

And here he saw Deiphobus, son of Priam, his whole body one wound, his face cruelly butchered — the face and both hands, the temples robbed of their ears, the nose hacked off to a shameful stump. Aeneas barely knew him, shrinking as he was and hiding the horror done to him; but he spoke to him in the old familiar voice: "Deiphobus, mighty in arms, son of Teucer's royal line — who wanted to take such savage vengeance on you? Who was allowed to do this? The last night of Troy, I heard, you went down exhausted on a great heap of mingled slaughter, the dead piled around you. So I raised an empty tomb for you on the cape of Rhoeteum, and three times I called your spirit aloud. Your name and your armor mark the place. But you yourself, my friend, I could not find, could not lay in your native ground as I went." To this the son of Priam answered: "You left nothing undone, my friend; you paid all that was owed to Deiphobus and his shade. It was my own fate, and the deadly crime of that Spartan woman, that sank me in this ruin; these are the keepsakes she left me. You know how we passed that last night in false rejoicing — you must remember it too well. When the fatal horse came leaping over our high walls, heavy in its belly with armed men, she led the Trojan women in a dance, pretending a holy frenzy, and held a great torch in the center of them, signaling the Greeks from the citadel's height. Then I, worn out and weighed down with care, was lying in my unlucky bedroom, sunk in a sleep deep and sweet, the very likeness of a peaceful death. And my fine wife, meanwhile, cleared every weapon out of the house and slipped my faithful sword from under my head; she called Menelaus in and threw the doors wide open — hoping, no doubt, it would be a great gift to the man who once loved her, and would burn away the memory of her old sins. Why drag it out? They burst into the room, and with them Ulysses, who urges men on to evil. Gods, pay the Greeks back the same, if it is a just thing I pray for. But you, now — tell me, what turn of fortune brings you here alive? Driven here off course by the sea? Sent by the gods? What luck wears you down, that you should come to these joyless, sunless houses, this country of confusion?"

While they talked, the dawn in her rose chariot had already crossed the high middle of the sky; and they might have spent all the time they were allowed in talk like this, but the Sibyl, his companion, warned him sharply: "Night is coming on, Aeneas; we waste the hours in weeping. Here is the place where the road forks in two. The right-hand way runs under the walls of great Dis; this is our road to Elysium. The left deals out punishment to the wicked and sends them down to godless Tartarus." Deiphobus answered her: "Don't be angry, great priestess; I will go, I will fill out my number in the shadows and go back to the dark. Go on, our glory, go — and may you find a better fate than mine." That was all he said; even as he said it he turned away.

Aeneas looked back suddenly and saw, under a cliff on the left, a broad fortress ringed with a triple wall and circled by a racing river of fire, Phlegethon of Tartarus, that rolls thundering rocks in its current. In front stands a huge gate, with pillars of solid adamant no force of men, not even the gods in war, could ever break; an iron tower rises into the air, and Tisiphone sits there in her bloody cloak, sleepless, guarding the entrance night and day. From inside came groans, and the cruel crack of the lash, the clank of dragged iron, the grinding of chains. Aeneas stopped, frozen, drinking in the noise. "What kinds of crime are these? Tell me, prophetess. What punishments fall on them? What is that great wailing in the air?" The seer began: "Famous leader of the Trojans, no one clean of heart is allowed to set foot on that wicked threshold; but when Hecate set me over the groves of Avernus, she herself taught me the gods' punishments and led me through it all. Rhadamanthus of Crete rules this iron kingdom, hard and pitiless; he hears men's crimes and forces confession, dragging out into the light whatever sin a man took comfort in hiding up above, putting off his atonement till the late hour of death — too late. At once avenging Tisiphone, armed with the whip, leaps on the guilty and lashes them, holding out her fierce snakes in her left hand, and calls up the savage ranks of her sisters. Then at last the dreadful gates grind open on their shrieking hinges. You see the kind of guard that sits in the doorway, the shape that watches the threshold? Inside, a worse thing still — a Hydra with fifty black gaping mouths sits crouched, and the pit drops straight down into the dark, twice as far below as the height of Olympus is above. There at the bottom the ancient brood of Earth, the young Titans, hurled down by the thunderbolt, roll in the deepest dark. There I saw the two giant sons of Aloeus, who put their hands to tearing down the great sky and pulling Jupiter from his throne. And I saw Salmoneus paying a terrible price — the man who aped the fire of Jupiter and the thunder of Olympus. Drawn by four horses, swinging a torch, he rode in triumph through the peoples of Greece and through the heart of his own city, and claimed the worship owed to a god — the fool, who thought he could copy the storm and the unmatchable lightning with the clatter of bronze and the beat of horn-footed horses. But the all-powerful Father drove a bolt through the thick clouds — no smoking torch, no pinewood glare, this — and hurled him headlong in a great whirlwind down to the deep. There too was Tityos, the nursling of Earth that mothers all, his body stretched over nine whole acres of ground; an enormous vulture, with its hooked beak, crops at his deathless liver and the flesh that breeds new pain, digging deep for its feast, living lodged under his ribs; and the strands of his renewed gut are given no rest. Why speak of the Lapiths, of Ixion, of Pirithous, over whom a black crag hangs forever, slipping, always about to fall? High couches gleam with golden bedposts, and a feast laid out before their eyes with royal splendor; but the eldest of the Furies lies beside them and lets no hand reach the table — she springs up with her torch raised and thunders at them. Here are those who hated their brothers while they lived, or struck a parent, or cheated a dependent who trusted them; those who brooded alone on the riches they had won and shared none with their own people — that crowd is the greatest of all; men killed for adultery; men who took up arms in an unholy cause and did not flinch from betraying their masters' trust. Walled in, they wait their punishment. Don't ask what punishment, what shape of doom drowned them. Some roll a great boulder; some hang spread on the spokes of a wheel; unlucky Theseus sits, and will sit forever; and Phlegyas, most wretched of all, warns them all, crying through the dark, 'Learn justice — be warned — and do not despise the gods.' This one sold his country for gold, and set a tyrant over it; this one made and unmade laws for a bribe; this one forced his way into his daughter's bed, a forbidden union. All of them dared some monstrous wrong, and got what they dared. If I had a hundred tongues and a hundred mouths and a voice of iron, I could not run through all the shapes of their crime or name over all their punishments."

When the old priestess of Apollo had said this, she added: "But come now, take up the road, finish the task you set out on. Let us hurry. I can see the walls forged in the furnaces of the Cyclopes, and the arched gates facing us, where we are told to lay down our gift." She finished, and the two of them walked side by side through the dark of the road, crossed the open space between, and came up to the doors. Aeneas took the entrance, sprinkled his body with fresh water, and fixed the bough upright on the threshold facing them.

When this was done at last, the offering made to the goddess, they came out into a country of joy, the green pleasure-gardens of the blessed groves, the homes of the happy. Here a fuller air clothes the fields in shining light, and they know their own sun, their own stars. Some are working their bodies on the grassy wrestling-grounds, playing their games, grappling on the yellow sand; some beat the ground in dance and sing their songs. And the priest of Thrace, Orpheus, in his long robe, makes the seven notes answer in turn, plucking them now with his fingers, now with the ivory quill. Here was the old line of Teucer, the most beautiful of families, great-hearted heroes born in better years — Ilus, Assaracus, and Dardanus who founded Troy. Aeneas marveled at the empty chariots and the armor standing far off; the spears were fixed in the ground, the horses grazed loose all over the plain. The love these men had for chariots and weapons while they lived, the care they took to feed their sleek horses — all of it followed them under the earth, and is the same here. And look — others to the right and left, feasting on the grass, singing a glad hymn together under a fragrant grove of laurel, where the great river Eridanus rolls up in full flood through the wood. Here were the men wounded fighting for their homeland; priests who kept their lives pure; faithful poets, who sang what was worthy of Apollo; those who enriched human life with the arts they discovered; those whose good deeds made others remember them. The brows of all of them were bound with snow-white ribbons.

The Sibyl spoke to them as they crowded around, and to Musaeus first, who stood at the center of the great throng, all of them looking up at him as he towered above their shoulders: "Tell us, happy souls, and you, best of poets — where does Anchises live? Which way do we go? It is for his sake we have come, and crossed the great rivers of the underworld." The hero answered her in a few words: "None of us has a fixed home. We live in the shadowed groves, lie on the cushioned riverbanks, keep to the water-meadows fresh with streams. But if your heart leads you so, climb this ridge; I will set you on an easy path from there." He spoke, and walked ahead, and showed them the bright fields below; and they came down off the hilltop.

But deep in a green valley, father Anchises was studying with loving care the souls penned there, waiting to go up into the light above — counting over, as it happened, the whole roll of his descendants, his dear children's children, their fates and fortunes, their lives and their deeds. And when he saw Aeneas coming toward him across the grass, he stretched out both hands in eager welcome, the tears wetting his cheeks, and the words broke from his mouth: "You've come at last — has the love I counted on beaten the hard road? Is it given to me, my son, to look on your face, to hear and answer the voice I know? This is what I told myself, what I reckoned would come; I counted the days, and my care did not deceive me. Over what lands, across how many seas have you been carried to reach me? What dangers have battered you, my son? How I feared the kingdom of Libya would do you harm!" Aeneas answered: "It was your sad ghost, father — yours, coming to me again and again — that drove me to seek this threshold. My fleet rides on the Tyrrhenian sea. Let me take your hand, father, let me; don't pull back from my arms." As he spoke, his face was streaming. Three times he tried to throw his arms around his father's neck; three times the shade slipped through his hands, caught in vain, light as the wind, very like a winged dream.

Meanwhile Aeneas saw, deep in a side valley, a grove set apart, a rustling thicket of trees, and the river Lethe gliding past those peaceful homes. Around it countless peoples and tribes were hovering, the way bees in a meadow on a clear summer day settle on the bright flowers and stream around the white lilies, and the whole field hums with the sound. Aeneas, not knowing what it meant, shivered at the sight, and asked what river that was over there, and who were the men crowding its banks in such a multitude. Father Anchises answered: "These are the souls owed a second body by fate, and at the water of Lethe they drink the long draught that frees them from care, the deep forgetting. For a long time now I have wanted to tell you of them, to show you face to face this line that is to come from you, so that you may rejoice with me all the more at finding Italy." "But father," said Aeneas, "are we to believe that any souls go up from here into the daylight again, and slip back into slow bodies? What terrible longing for the light is in these poor souls?" "I will tell you, my son," said Anchises, "and not keep you wondering."

"First, know that one spirit feeds the sky and the earth and the moving waters within, the shining globe of the moon, the Titan sun, and a mind, poured through every limb of it, stirs the whole great body and mingles with that vast frame. From it come the races of men and beasts, the lives of winged things, and the strange shapes the ocean carries under its glittering floor. A fiery energy is in those seeds, a heavenly origin, so far as the heavy body does not slow them, the limbs of dying earth, the flesh marked for death. From the body come fear and desire, grief and joy; shut in the dark of its prison, the soul cannot see the open sky. And even on the last day, when the light of life leaves them, the wretched souls are not wholly freed of every evil, every plague of the body; it is fixed deep, and the long-grown stain has worked its strange way in. So they are tried with punishment and pay for their old sins. Some are hung out, stretched, to the empty winds; in others the deep guilt is washed away in a great flood, or burned out in fire. Each of us suffers the punishment that is his own; then we are let through into broad Elysium, and a few of us keep these fields of joy — until the long day, when the circle of time is complete, has taken out the ingrown stain and left the pure feeling of the spirit, the bare fire of the air. All these, when the wheel has turned a thousand years, a god calls in a great line to the river Lethe, so that they lose their memory and come to look again on the vault above, and begin to want to return into bodies."

Anchises finished, and led his son and the Sibyl both into the thick of the murmuring crowd, and took a rise of ground where they could look out over the whole long file as it came, and read the faces of each as they passed. "Now then — I will show you the glory that is coming for the Trojan stock, the children who wait among the people of Italy, bright souls who will rise to bear our name, and I will tell you your own destiny. That young man you see leaning on a headless spear stands nearest to the light by lot; he will be the first to rise into the upper air with Italian blood mixed in him — Silvius, an Alban name, your last-born son, whom your wife Lavinia will raise late, in the woods, a king and the father of kings, from whom our house will rule Alba Longa. Next to him is Procas, the pride of the Trojan people, and Capys, and Numitor, and the one who will bring your name back to life — Silvius Aeneas, as great in goodness as in arms, if ever he comes to the throne of Alba. What young men! Look at the strength in them. But the ones with the oak-leaf crown of honor on their brows — they will build Nomentum and Gabii and the city of Fidenae, set the towers of Collatia on the hills, and Pometii, and the Fort of Inuus, Bola, Cora; these will be famous names one day, though now they are only nameless land.

"And here, look — Romulus, son of Mars, will join his grandfather, born of the blood of Assaracus and brought into the world by his mother Ilia. See the twin crests already standing on his helmet, the mark his own father, lord of the gods, sets on him for the world above. Under his sign, my son, great Rome will set her empire over the whole earth and lift her spirit to the height of heaven, ringing seven hills with a single wall, blessed in the breed of her sons — like the Berecyntian Mother riding crowned with towers in her chariot through the cities of Phrygia, glad in her brood of gods, with a hundred grandchildren about her, every one a dweller in heaven, every one a power on high.

"Now turn your eyes this way and look at this people, your own Romans. Here is Caesar, and the whole line of Iulus that will come up one day under the great arch of the sky. And here is the man, here, the one you keep hearing promised to you — Augustus Caesar, son of a god, who will found again the age of gold in Latium, across the fields that Saturn ruled once long ago, and stretch his empire past the Garamantes and the Indians, to a land that lies beyond the stars, beyond the year's road and the sun's, where Atlas, who holds up the sky, turns the burning axle thick with stars on his shoulder. Even now the kingdoms by the Caspian Sea shudder at the gods' warnings of his coming, and the mouths of the Nile, and the lands of Maeotis, are troubled. Hercules himself never crossed so much ground, though he shot the bronze-footed deer and brought peace to the woods of Erymanthus and made Lerna tremble at his bow; nor did Bacchus in triumph, driving his tigers with reins of vine from the heights of Nysa. And do we still hesitate to spread our courage by our deeds? Does any fear keep us from setting foot in the land of Italy?

"But who is this set apart, marked out by his olive crown, carrying the sacred vessels? I know the gray hair and beard of Numa, the king who will set Rome on a foundation of law, called up from the small town of Cures and its poor land to a great kingdom. After him comes Tullus, who will shatter the quiet of the country, rouse men who have forgotten war, and call his sleeping armies back to the field. Behind him, that boastful Ancus, even now too pleased with the breath of the crowd's favor. Would you see the Tarquin kings, and the proud avenging spirit of Brutus, the man who took back the rods of power? He will be the first to win the consul's authority and the dread axes, and when his own sons stir up war to bring back the kings, the father will call them to a terrible death in the name of liberty — unlucky man, however later ages tell the story. The love of his country wins, and the boundless hunger for honor. And look, off there — the Decii, the Drusi, Torquatus cruel with the axe, and Camillus carrying home the standards. But those two souls you see shining in matching armor, at peace now, as long as they are shut in night — what a war between them, what battle lines, what slaughter they will raise against each other, if they once reach the light of the living! The father-in-law sweeping down from the Alpine wall and the citadel of Monoecus, the son-in-law drawn up against him with the armies of the East. My sons, do not school your hearts to such great wars, do not turn the strength of your country against her own heart; and you be the first to hold back, you who trace your blood from heaven — throw the sword from your hand, child of mine!

"That one, triumphant over Corinth, will drive his chariot up to the high Capitol, famous for the Greeks he killed. That one will tear down Argos and Mycenae of Agamemnon, and the heir of Achilles himself, mighty in arms — avenging his Trojan ancestors and Minerva's defiled temple. Who could leave you unspoken, great Cato, or you, Cossus? Who would pass over the family of the Gracchi, or the two Scipios, twin thunderbolts of war and the ruin of Africa, or Fabricius who was great on so little, or Serranus sowing his own furrow? Where are you rushing me, you Fabii? You are the great one, Fabius Maximus — the one man who saved the state for us by holding back. Others will hammer out the breathing bronze more gently, I have no doubt, and draw living faces out of the marble; others will plead the cases better, and trace with the pointer the wheeling sky and tell the rising of the stars. You, Roman, remember: rule the nations with your power. These will be your arts: to set the custom of peace, to spare the conquered, and to crush the proud."

So father Anchises spoke, and while they marveled he added: "Look how Marcellus comes, glorious in the spoils he stripped from a king, towering over all the men around him, a conqueror! He will steady the Roman state when a great rising shakes it, beat down the Carthaginians and the rebel Gaul, and hang up, for the third time, captured armor before father Quirinus." And here Aeneas — for he saw a young man walking beside Marcellus, beautiful, in shining armor, but with no light in his eyes, his face cast down — said: "Father, who is that, walking at the great man's side? A son? One of the long line of his children? What a crowd around him, what a stir! But a black night hovers around his head, a sad shadow." And father Anchises answered, the tears starting: "My son, do not ask after this great grief of your people. The fates will only show him to the world and not let him stay. The Roman line would have seemed too strong to you, you gods above, if this gift had been allowed to last. What a wailing of men the field by Mars's city will send up past the great walls — and you, Tiber, as you glide by his fresh grave, what a funeral you will see. No boy of the Trojan blood will lift his Latin grandfathers so high with hope; never again will the land of Romulus be so proud of any son she bore. The faithfulness of old, the honor, the right hand unbeaten in war! No enemy would have met him under arms and gone away unhurt, whether he went in on foot or drove his foaming horse against their ranks. Poor boy — if only you could break the bitter grip of fate. You will be Marcellus. Give me lilies by the armful; let me scatter the bright flowers, heap these gifts at least on the shade of my descendant — pay him this empty service." So they wandered the whole region, the broad and misty fields, and looked at everything. And when Anchises had led his son through it all, kindling his heart with longing for the glory to come, he told him then of the wars he would have to fight, of the peoples of Latium and the city of Latinus, and how he should meet each trial or escape it.

There are two gates of Sleep. One is made of horn, and through it the true shades are given easy passage out; the other shines, finished and perfect in gleaming ivory, but through it the dead send false dreams up into the world above. There, talking with his son, Anchises walked him to the gate and let him go — both of them, the man and the Sibyl — out through the gate of ivory. And Aeneas cut straight back to his ships and his companions, and ran up the coast to the harbor of Caieta. The anchors were thrown from the prows; the sterns lined the shore.
