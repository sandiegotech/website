The Aeneid, Book X

The Council of the Gods, the Death of Pallas, and the End of Mezentius

Book X10 of 12

Meanwhile the house of almighty Olympus swung open, and the father of gods and king of men called a council in his starry hall. From his high seat he looked down over the whole earth — the Trojan camp, the Latin lines, the war and all its turns. The gods took their places in order, the great and the lesser, and he began: "Powers of heaven — why has it all turned back on itself? Why these murmurs, this change of heart? I forbade Italy to go to war with the Trojans. What is this quarrel, then, against my command? What fear has driven the one side or the other to take up arms? The right time for war will come — don't rush it on. The day will come when fierce Carthage opens the Alps and pours destruction down on the strongholds of Rome. Then you can hate, then you can take sides, then you can tear each other apart for it. For now, leave it alone. Settle your hearts, and keep the peace I set."

Jupiter said it in few words. But golden Venus answered him at length: "Father — eternal power, where else can we turn for help? Look at the Rutulians, swaggering across the field unpunished. Look at Turnus, riding high through his ranks in his shining armor, sure of himself. The Trojans can't even hold their own walls now; the camp is choked with slaughter, the trenches running red. And Aeneas is far off, knowing none of it, his men left exposed with no one to lead them. Is this to go on? Is Troy to be stormed and burned a second time, a new army at her gates, a new Diomedes risen against her? I, your daughter, am bracing for one more wound. If my people came to Italy against your will, without your leave, then let them pay for breaking the law — take your hand from them, I won't ask you to save them. But if they came following all the oracles heaven and the dead alike gave them, who has the right now to overturn your word and write another fate? Need I remind you of the storm at sea, of Aeolus loosed against Neptune's own waters? Of Iris sent down to drive the Trojan women mad and burn the fleet? And now Juno has gone down to the underworld itself — that was the one outrage left to try — and stirred up the Fury Allecto to madden the quiet hearts of the Latin women. I don't care about empire anymore. I hoped for it once, while heaven was still kind. Let those you favor win. But I beg you one thing, father — if I may still call you that — by Troy in her ashes: let Ascanius be drawn out of this war and live. Let the father be cast where fate will, but spare the boy. Cythera is mine; the towers of Cyprus are mine. Let him lay his weapons down there, out of the light, with no crown and no claim, and let Carthage trample Italy unafraid. What good did it do my son to escape the fire, to carry his gods and his father out through the flames, to cross all those seas, only to raise a second Troy and watch it fall too?"

Then royal Juno, swelling with deep anger, broke in: "Must I say out loud what should have stayed decently silent — what I'd have kept to myself, if you hadn't forced it with this slander? Did any god or any man tell your darling Aeneas to fall on the Latins by surprise and start a war? Fate brought him, you'll say, and the will of the gods. Say the truth, then — it was mad Cassandra he listened to, not heaven. Did I send him off to leave his half-built second Troy to a beardless boy and go wandering the waves again? Did I tell him to go begging the Etruscans for help and arm a country that wanted peace? Did Iris give that advice, or I? You think it monstrous that Latins should defend their own soil with fire and sword. But it's perfectly fair, is it, for foreigners to seize land that isn't theirs, to carry off another man's promised bride, to sue for peace with one hand while they arm with the other? You can snatch Aeneas out of the hands of the Greeks, and set fog and empty wind where the man had stood. You turned the fire from the burning ships and changed the hulls into sea-nymphs. But let me lift one finger to help my own, and that's a crime? You say your son is absent, knowing nothing of what his enemies are planning. Then let him stay absent. Cythera is yours, you said, and the towers of Cyprus and the soft retreats. Why come stirring up a warlike people, then? Did I burn down Troy, or you? Who let loose the disaster — me, or the man whose lust began the whole war? Remember whose word the adulterer trusted; remember who promised him the Spartan queen. When all of Greece banded together to wipe out that treacherous breed — that was the time to fear for Troy. Now your grief and your complaints come too late."

So Juno spoke, and a murmur ran through the gods, some for her, some against — like the first stir of winds still trapped in the woods, trying out their voices in whispers before they break loose onto the open sea and roar a storm at the trembling sailors. Then the almighty Father, who shakes heaven on its hinges with a nod, answered them both. When he speaks, the whole assembly stands still and listens; the clouds break apart, the winds hold their breath, the waves lie down flat. "Hear me, then, and take it to heart. Since the Trojans and the Latins cannot be joined in the alliance I wanted, and since your endless quarreling only ruins the peace of heaven — from this hour I leave the war to fate. Let each man stand or fall by his own fortune. Rutulian or Trojan, I make no difference between them; I look on all of them with the same eye. Whether the camp lies besieged because fate favors the Italians, or through Troy's own blunder and misleading oracles, I'll favor neither. Let each side find the lot fate deals it. Jupiter is king over all alike; the Fates will find their way." And he sealed it by the river of his brother's kingdom, by the banks of pitch and the black torrent of Styx, and bent his head — and all of Olympus shook at the nod. That ended the council. Jupiter rose from his golden throne, and the gods gathered round and walked him back to his halls.

Meanwhile the Rutulians pressed their siege on every gate, cutting men down, setting fires, hemming the Trojan army in behind its own walls. The defenders stood thin on the towers, longing in vain for their absent commander, with no hope of escape and less hope of relief. Still, some of them held the line in the face of it: Asius son of Imbrasus and Thymoetes son of Hicetaon, the two Assaraci, and old Thymbris with Castor, in the front rank; and with them both brothers of Sarpedon, Clarus and Thaemon, from high Lycia. From Acmon's hands came a stone so huge it half deserved to be called a mountain; he was a big-boned, hard-sinewed youth, no less than his father Clytius or his brother Mnestheus. Some hurled firebrands, some loosed flights of arrows, some fought with spears, some with stones. And there in the thick of it stood the beautiful boy — Ascanius, the care of Venus and the hope of Troy. His head was bare, his lovely face uncovered, his hair falling in curls over his shoulders, a band of gold around his brow; he stood out among the rest like a gem set in yellow gold, or ivory gleaming inlaid in boxwood.

So the deadly war was waged on both sides. And all that while Aeneas was cutting through the night on the open sea. For when he left Evander, he had gone to the Etruscan camp and the tent of King Tarchon, told him his name and his country, asked for help, laid out the terms — his own slim strength, the vengeance proud Mezentius was preparing, and Turnus's headlong fury. He reminded Tarchon how uncertain human fortune is, and mixed his warning with a plea. Tarchon signed the treaty without delay and joined his Etruscan forces to the Trojans. So the fleet set sail, fate no longer holding it back, their cause now trusted to a foreign hand. Aeneas led the way. On his stern stood two carved lions of Mount Ida — Ida, forever dear to the wandering Trojans. Under the figurehead's shade he sat, turning the war over in his mind, the changing chances of it. At his left side young Pallas kept close, asking him now about the winds, now about the stars and the dark sea-road, now about all he had borne by land and water.

Open your fountains now, you Muses — sing the Etruscan captains and their host, the men who followed great Aeneas to war. Tell their arms, their numbers, their names. First Massicus, cutting the foaming sea in the bronze-beaked Tiger, leading a thousand men he had brought from Asium and from Cosa — armed with light quivers, bows, and arrows. Next fierce Abas, his men in bright bronze, a gilded statue of Apollo on his stern; Populonia had sent him six hundred soldiers trained for war, and the island of Ilva three hundred more, an island rich in iron from its bottomless mines. Third came Asilas, the man who reads heaven and the wandering stars, who finds omens in the entrails of victims and in the rolling thunder — a thousand spears stood under his command, sent by the people of Pisa. After him fair Astur, proud of his fine horse and his painted shield: Caere and the fens of Gravisca sent him three hundred, with the men of Minio's fields and of Pyrgi, all bred to arms.

And you, Muse — bring back the name of Cinyras, and brave Cupavo with his few followers, whose helmet wore the swan's plumes that told his family's story. For his father Cycnus, grieving for the loss of Phaeton he had loved, sang his sorrow in the poplar groves under the shade of the mourning Sisters, until heaven turned his gray hair to soft white feathers and lifted him into the air, singing as he flew. His son Cupavo now swept the salt water, a Centaur carved on his stern, heaving a great rock as if to hurl it, threatening the sea below. Next came Ocnus, leading his own men, son of the prophetess Manto and the Tuscan river, who gave Mantua its name — an ancient town of mixed blood, three tribes in it, four peoples under each tribe, all of them ruled by Mantua and her Etruscan law. And hatred of Mezentius had armed five hundred more under Mincius — son of Lake Benacus, his brow crowned with gray reeds — led by grave Auletes, his hundred oars sweeping the glassy deep all at once. The sea-green Triton bore him, frowning on his prow, blowing his curved conch shell so the waves danced at the blast: hairy and human above the waist, a great fish below, the surf foaming white where his breast cut the water. So thirty chosen ships drove on across the brine, bearing relief to Troy.

Now the sun had left the world, and gentle Phoebe was halfway through her night-ride across the sky. Aeneas, who never closed his eyes, sat at the helm himself, tending the rudder and the sails. And midway over the sea a chorus of sea-nymphs met him — his own ships, once cut from the wood of Ida, now changed into as many maidens of the deep, swimming where the tall hulls had ridden. They knew him from far off, and ringed his vessel round. Cymodoce, whose voice was clearest of them all, came up behind, laid her right hand on the stern, lifted her white breast above the waves, and rowed with her left through the curling water. She spoke for the whole chorus to the man who knew nothing of it: "Are you asleep, son of the goddess? Wake — and crowd on all your sail, and drive your course! We are your fleet, the pines of Ida, now nymphs of the sea. When Turnus came at us with fire and sword while we lay at anchor, we broke our cables — much against our will, to leave our master — and we have been searching the Etruscan deep for you ever since. The great Mother had pity and gave us these shapes and a deathless life in the water. But your son Ascanius is hard pressed inside his walls, ringed by the ditches and the spears of the Latins. The Arcadian horse and the Etruscans are already in their places. Turnus means to throw his squadrons across their road and cut them off before they reach the Trojan lines. So rise — and when the rose of dawn breaks, call your men to arms first of all. Take up the unbreakable shield the fire-god forged for you. Tomorrow's sun, unless my skill is empty, will look down on great heaps of the enemy slain." She finished, and as she dove she pushed the hull forward with a deathless strength — she knew the sea-road well — and the ship shot ahead faster than the wind. The rest of the fleet caught up. Aeneas, not knowing the reason, marveled at the speed of it, and took heart from the omen.

Then he prayed, lifting his eyes to heaven: "Great Mother of the gods, crowned with towers, queen of Ida where the leashed tigers obey you — confirm your own omens for me. Lead us into the fight, and let your Trojans win under your hand." That was all. And now the new day had chased the shadows off. He gave the order: the men were to follow the standards, ready their weapons, and steel themselves for the war that was coming. Then, from his high stern, he caught sight of his camp below, ringed by the enemy — and he raised his blazing shield in his left hand and held it high. The Trojans on the walls saw the sign and roared back, every voice at once, their hope kindled fresh; from the towers they let their spears fly, and their shout was like the cry of cranes from the Strymon calling under black clouds as they sweep across the sky, flying before the south winds. King Turnus and his captains wondered at the fighting flaring up again — until they looked back, and saw the sea covered with sails and the swift ships running for the shore. The Latins stared with dazzled eyes at the crest on Aeneas's helmet, a flame seeming to rise from his head and pour fire across the field, and the keen glitter of the golden shield — the way a comet burns blood-red in the night and saddens the sky, or the dog-star Sirius flashes its sickly light and brings disease and drought to frighten mankind.

But Turnus's nerve never failed. He set himself to hold the beach and keep the Trojans from landing, and he fired his men's hearts: "What you've prayed for so long, kind fortune hands you now — to meet the enemy with weapons in your hands. The day is yours. Only dare it, and your swords will win the war. Each of you, remember your fathers, your sons, your homes, your fields, your wives — all of it is in your hands today. Live up to the men you came from; match your fathers' fame in arms. Hit them now, while they're still staggering up the strand on unsteady feet. Fortune favors the bold." And he weighed which men to lead down to the shore and which to leave penning the camp.

Meanwhile Aeneas was putting his men ashore — some down gangplanks, some leaping straight off the boats where the tide ran shallow. Tarchon watched the coast with a careful eye, and where he found no surf breaking, no ridge of foam, only the swell sliding smooth up the beach, he turned his ships in and called out: "Now, men — bend to the oars, and drive her aground at any cost. Let the keel cut a furrow in this hateful enemy soil. Once I've landed I don't care if the ship breaks up under me." The men threw themselves on the oars and ran the vessels onto the dry sand, every hull jarring with the shock but holding — all but Tarchon's own, which stuck on a sandbank, hung there pounded by the waves until her back cracked and her timbers split and pitched the Etruscan soldiers into the sea, the broken oars and floating planks tangling around them as they struggled for shore.

Now Turnus led his men down without delay, hard against the water's edge, and the trumpets sounded. Aeneas struck first, into the new-raised farmer-levies, and broke them — and that was the omen of the day. Great Theron fell to him first, a giant of a man who had dared to face the prince head-on: the gilded scales of his armor were no defense, the sword went through his shield and into his bare side. Then Lichas, cut living from his dead mother's womb and dedicated to Apollo because the blade had spared him at birth — but it did not spare him now. Near him Aeneas laid out Cisseus and the huge Gyas, who had been clubbing down whole ranks with the weapons of Hercules; their strength and their famous descent from Alcides' old companion meant nothing. He silenced the bawling Pharos with a thrown spear in mid-shout. He would have killed Cydon too, who chased after Clytius with his soft new beard — except Cydon's seven brothers ringed him round and flung seven spears at once; some glanced off the shield, some rang on the helmet, and the rest his mother Venus turned aside in the air. Then Aeneas called to faithful Achates: "Bring me spears — the ones that drank Greek blood under the walls of Troy. Not one of them will I throw in vain at these Rutulians." He seized a great spear and threw: it tore through Maeon's bronze shield and into his heart, and as his brother Alcanor reached out to hold him up, a second spear pinned that reaching arm and dragged it down dead at his side. Then Numitor wrenched the spear from his dead brother's body and threw it back, but fate turned it aside; it only grazed Achates' thigh.

Now Clausus the Sabine came on in the pride of his youth and took aim at Dryops from a distance: the spear flew hissing and drove through his throat, cutting off voice and breath together, and he pitched forward, the dark blood pouring out with his life. Clausus killed three sons of the north wind's line, and three more whom their country Ismara had sent to war, all of them Thracians. Halesus came up leading the Aurunci, and Messapus, son of Neptune, fought beside him on horseback. On this side and that they struggled — one army to hold the land, the other to take it — and the soil of Italy ran with the blood of both, like two winter winds locked in the sky, equal in their fury, roaring against each other while the clouds hang motionless between them and the sea lies undriven, neither able to give way. So the lines stood, foot against foot, man against man.

But in another part of the field the Arcadian horsemen were faring badly against the Latins. Where a flooding torrent had torn down crags and uprooted trees and left the ground broken, they had left their horses behind — and, unused to fighting on foot, they were scattering in a shameful rout. Pallas saw it: his own men running, the enemy at their backs, and grief and disdain rose in him. He used threats and prayers both, the last thing left to him, to turn their minds and fire their blood. "Where are you running? Where? By your own selves, by the great battles you've won, by my father's name and the early fame I've staked my life to earn — don't shame yourselves with flight. We have to hack our way home through the thick of that black mass of them. That's the only road there is. No power above and no fate below is pressing us; we go with mortal strength to meet a mortal enemy. Look where we stand — a narrow strip of beach, the sea at our backs, the enemy in front, no road left unless we swim. Forward, then, through them, and on to the Trojan camp." And he strode in among the thickest of them.

Lagus was the first he met, dragged there by an unlucky fate — he had stooped to lift a heavy stone, and Pallas drove the spear through the middle of his back, where the spine divides the ribs, and had to wrench the point loose from the bone. Hisbon came at him next, too slow for the revenge he wanted; Pallas met the blow and plunged his sword into the man's wide-open chest. Then he laid lewd Anchemolus in the dust, the man who had defiled his own stepmother's bed. And then the twin sons of Daucus, Larides and Thymber, so alike in face and build that their own parents used to mistake one for the other — a sweet confusion, until the sword settled it cruelly: Thymber's head was lopped off, and Larides' right hand, cut away, went groping after its owner across the ground, the dying fingers still twitching on the hilt. The Arcadians, shamed and stung by the sight of his deeds, rallied and came back into the fight. Then Pallas brought down Rhoeteus as he fled past in his chariot — the spear had been aimed at Ilus, but Rhoeteus crossed its path and took the death meant for another, rolling from his chariot to beat the ground with his heels in his last spasm.

And as a shepherd in summer, when the wind serves, scatters fire through the heart of a wood, and the flames leap and catch from crown to crown until the whole forest goes up in one roaring blaze and the shepherd sits watching the sheet of fire climb the sky in triumph — so all the courage of Pallas's men gathered into one and poured against the enemy. But Halesus came at them, fierce for blood, and cut down Ladon, Demodocus, and Pheres; he swung his bright sword and sheared off Strymonius's hand, lifted to guard his throat, then crushed Thoas's forehead with a thrown stone so the bone burst and brain and blood flew together. Halesus's father, a seer who had read his son's fate, had hidden the boy away in lonely woods; but when the old man's own life ran out, the Fates laid hold of the son and dragged him to the war, to die under the spear of Evander's son. Pallas met him, but first he prayed to the Tiber: "Holy stream, guide my flying spear and grant it a clear way through proud Halesus's heart. Your oak shall hold his arms and his spoils." The god heard. And while Halesus stood shielding a friend in trouble, the spear came driving on and pierced his unguarded breast.

But Lausus, no small part of that war, would not let panic spread among his men because so famous a fighter had fallen; he steadied the line by his own example. First he killed fierce Abas, the brace and mainstay of the Trojan ranks, the one who had held the day. The men of Troy went down, and their mixed allies with them. Then both armies crashed together, the leaders evenly matched, the strength on each side the same, the rear so packed against the front that no man had room to swing his weapon. Here Pallas pressed forward, there Lausus — two boys, alike in youth and beauty, both forbidden by fate ever to see their homelands again. Yet the king of Olympus would not let them meet hand to hand: each was doomed, but to fall under a greater hand soon enough.

Now the divine sister Juturna warned Turnus that Lausus was in danger, and he wheeled his chariot through the crowd to reach his friends, calling out: "Stand off — leave the field clear. This fight is mine alone. Pallas belongs to me, and to me only. I wish his father were here to watch." His men drew back from the ground he had claimed. Pallas, struck with wonder at the man's haughty command, his towering height and his proud face, ran his eyes over him and answered the king: "Either I'll win the great spoils I'm after, or I'll win a good death — and my father can bear either one. Spare me your threats." And he stepped out into the open space. The blood drained from every Arcadian face. Turnus leapt down from his chariot to fight on foot, on equal terms, and came on like a lion that has spotted a bull far off on the plain, lowering its head to charge, and comes roaring down from its hilltop. That was Turnus, rushing his unequal enemy.

When Pallas judged him near enough for the cast, he threw first — staking everything on courage, since his strength fell short — and he prayed: "By the table you shared and the welcome my father Evander gave you, Hercules, help me now. Let Turnus see me strip his bloody armor with his dying eyes." Hercules heard the boy, and choked back a groan deep in his chest, and wept useless tears. Then Jupiter spoke gently to his son: "Each man has his own short day. The span of life is brief and never given back. But to stretch your fame by what you do — that is the work of courage. Under the high walls of Troy so many sons of gods went down. My own son Sarpedon fell there. Turnus, too, has his fate calling him; he stands already at the edge of his allotted years." So he spoke, and turned his eyes away from the Latin fields. Pallas hurled his spear with all his force and drew his shining sword; the point flew home where the armor joins the shoulder, and forcing through the rim of the shield it even grazed great Turnus's body. Then Turnus, taking long aim with his oak-shafted spear, threw, and said: "See now whose weapon bites the deeper." The iron drove clean through the center of the shield, through all its plates of bronze and bull's-hide layered over each other, through the corselet, and into the great breast. In vain the boy tore the hot spear from the wound — blood and life came out the same way together. He fell forward over the wound, his armor clanging upon him, and bit the enemy ground with his bloody mouth as he died.

Turnus stood over him and said: "Arcadians — carry this word back to Evander: I send him the son he deserved. Whatever honor there is in a tomb, whatever comfort in burial, I grant him freely. His welcome to Aeneas has cost him dear." And with that he set his left foot on the dead boy and tore away the heavy sword-belt, with all its weight of crime worked into the gold: the figures of fifty young men butchered on a single wedding night, the bridal chambers fouled with blood. Eurytion's hand had wrought it. Now Turnus carried it off, glorying in the spoil. Blind, the minds of men, blind to fate and to the lot that is coming, and blind to all restraint when good fortune lifts them up. The day will come when Turnus will give anything to have left Pallas untouched, when he will hate this hour and the plunder he took in it. But for now the Arcadians, weeping, laid their boy on his shield and carried him back off the cruel field — pride and grief of war in one, given back to his father the same day he was sent out, the day that saw him cut down whole heaps of the enemy, and the day that saw him dead and borne home on his shield.

The news reached Aeneas, and not from any vague rumor but from men who had seen it: his friends were on the very edge of ruin, and only his own hand could save the broken Trojans now. Whatever stood in front of him he mowed down with his sword, hacking a wide lane through the enemy in his fury to find Turnus, who was so proud of his fresh kill. Pallas, Evander, the table he had been welcomed at, the hand he had clasped in pledge — all of it stood before his eyes. He took four sons of Sulmo alive and four whom Ufens had raised, taking them living to slaughter as offerings to the shade of Pallas, to be burned in sacrifice at the boy's funeral fire. Then he hurled his spear at Magus — who ducked under it and, as it flew past, came creeping to clasp the hero's knees and beg: "By your father's spirit and by the hope of young Iulus, spare my life for the sake of my own son and my own father. I have a great house, and wealth buried in it — bars of silver, ingots of gold, untold sums no one has counted. The fate of Troy doesn't hang on one life. One soul won't tip the scale." He finished. Aeneas answered him hard: "All your silver and gold — keep it for your children. Turnus tore up the rules of war when he killed Pallas. So judges my father's shade; so judges my living son." And with that, all mercy gone, he gripped the man's helmet, bent his neck back, and drove the sword in to the hilt.

Nearby stood Haemonides, a priest of Apollo and Diana, the sacred fillet bound at his brow, gleaming all over in white robes and bright arms. Aeneas chased him across the field, stood over him where he stumbled and fell, and sent him down into the great shadow; Serestus gathered up his armor to carry off as a trophy for Mars. Then Caeculus, son of Vulcan, and Umbro down from the high hills rallied the Latins again. Against them the Trojan raged on. He sheared off Anxur's left arm with one stroke, shield and all, to the ground — Anxur who had boasted of his magic charms and thought his armor proof against any wound, who had counted on long years secured by spells. Then Tarquitus came strutting up in his bright armor, son of the woodland god Faunus and the nymph Dryope, and dared the prince; Aeneas beat his shield back, pinned his pleading hand, dragged him down, and stood over him while he was still mouthing useless prayers, and cut off his head. He rolled the warm trunk over with his foot and spoke to it: "Lie there now, you fearsome thing. No loving mother will lay you in the earth or weigh your limbs down with your fathers' tomb. You'll be left to the wild birds — or the flood will sink you and carry you off, and hungry fish will lick your wounds."

He chased down Antaeus and Lucas, Turnus's front-line men, and brave Numa, and golden-haired Camers, son of that Volcens lately killed, the richest man in all the Latin land, who had ruled in quiet over silent Amyclae. Like Aegaeon, the hundred-handed giant who once stood against Jupiter himself — they say he flashed fire from fifty mouths and fifty breasts and clanged on fifty shields and brandished as many swords against the lightning — so Aeneas raged victorious across the whole plain once his blade had tasted blood. He flew at Niphaeus and his four-horse team: the horses, seeing him come on with his spear lowered at their chests, wheeled in terror, threw their master headlong, and bolted with the empty chariot toward the shore. Then Lucagus and Liger drove up with their two white horses, Liger holding the reins, bold Lucagus on the high seat with his sword. Liger shouted: "These aren't Diomedes's horses, this isn't Achilles's chariot, and Venus's veil won't save you here. Your last hour has come on this field." And as Lucagus bent forward to whip the team on, his left foot braced for the fight, the spear came and tore through the lower rim of his shield and into his groin, and pitched him from the chariot to the ground. Aeneas spoke to him in scorn: "Lucagus, no slow horses lost you the chariot, no shadow on the field turned them back. You jumped off it yourself." And he caught the team by the reins; Liger had already fallen with the same crash, and now he stretched out his hands and begged: "By your own self, by the parents who made you so godlike, spare me — pity a suppliant." Aeneas cut him short: "That isn't the way you talked before. Die — and stay a good brother to your brother." And he sent the sword through his chest, where the soul came out the open wound. So the Trojan stormed across the plain like a flood, like a tearing torrent, scattering death — until at last Ascanius and the Trojan garrison broke out of the camp that had been besieged so long in vain.

Meanwhile Jupiter spoke to Juno of his own accord: "My sister, and my wife — and how rightly so — you were sure Venus's help was holding the Trojans up. But look: see how high their courage runs, how they stand the fight, undaunted. Judge for yourself whether such men need a goddess at their backs." Juno answered him low, her tone all submission: "Why hurt me with this, my lord, when I dread your frown and can't bear your anger lightly? If I still had the power over your will that I once had, you would not deny your wife this much — to lift Turnus out of the battle and give him back safe to his old father's sight. As it is, let him die, then, since it pleases you, and let his pious blood glut the Trojans. And yet he comes of our own line; Pilumnus, four generations back, was his ancestor; and his hand has heaped your altars high with offerings." Jupiter answered briefly: "If all you're asking is a little stay, a short reprieve from death for a man already doomed, and you trust me to grant that much — then take Turnus away in flight, and snatch him from the fate that's bearing down on him. So far I can yield. But if some deeper hope is hiding under your plea, if you think to change the whole course of the war and the fate of it, you are feeding yourself on air." Juno answered, weeping: "What if your heart granted what your tongue refuses, and Turnus were given not a brief reprieve but long, sure life? As it is, a swift death waits for the guiltless boy — or my foreboding fails me. Oh, may I be wrong, frightened of nothing; and you have the power — bend the fates, and lengthen his years."

Having said it, she dropped down out of high heaven wrapped in cloud, driving a storm before her through the sky, and came to earth where the Latins and the Trojans were locked in their doubtful fight. There, out of thin mist, she shaped a phantom — a hollow likeness of Aeneas, an eerie thing — and dressed it in Trojan armor, with the crest and the shield, and gave it the great hero's bearing and an empty voice and a stride that mimicked his. It moved among the lines, daring Turnus, brandishing its sword in the air. Turnus came at it and threw his spear; the phantom wheeled and ran as if in fear. Believing Aeneas had fled, drunk on the false hope of it, Turnus shouted after it: "Where are you going, Aeneas? Don't desert your promised bride! This is the hand that will give you the land you crossed the seas for." And he chased the fleeing shadow, his sword out. A ship happened to lie moored to the shore, the one that had brought King Osinius from old Clusium, the gangplank still down; onto it the trembling phantom fled and hid below the hatches. Turnus, careless in his triumph, ran up the plank and onto the deck — and the moment he reached the prow, Juno cut the cable and shot the ship out to sea on a running wind. Meanwhile the real Aeneas was hunting his absent enemy across the field, sending down to the dead every man he met.

Now the empty phantom no longer sought a hiding place; it rose into the air and melted into a dark cloud, while Turnus was carried far out over the water, still driving away from the land. He looked back, understanding too late how he had been used, and lifted his hands and his wild eyes to heaven, ungrateful for a life saved by such shame: "Almighty Father — did you think me so guilty as to deserve this disgrace? Where am I being taken, and from what? How can I ever go back, and with what reproach? Will I ever see the Latin plain again, or the high towers of Laurentum? What will they say of me — the men I led to slaughter and then abandoned, whose dying groans I can hear even now behind me, while I run? Open, earth, and swallow me down alive! Or you, winds, take pity — drive the ship onto the rocks, onto the shoals, onto some empty shore where no Rutulian can ever find me, where even rumor can't follow." Three times he reached for his sword to fall on it, three times he made to throw himself into the sea — but Juno, in her pity, three times held him back. The ship ran on across the swell on a strong wind, and brought him at last to his native shore and to his father's longing arms.

Meanwhile, by Jupiter's prompting, Mezentius took the field where Turnus had left it, and fell on the victorious Trojans, breathing his own fury into his men. The Etruscan ranks closed on him together — on this one man and no other they spent all their hate, all their longing for revenge, all their spears. He stood like a rock thrust out into the deep, taking the full rage of the winds and the waves head-on, looking down from his height on the menace of the sea, and never moving. He cut down Hebrus, son of Dolichaon, and Latagus, and Palmus fleeing — he flung a great stone at Latagus that smashed his face and helmet, and hamstrung Palmus from behind so he sprawled writhing on the ground, and gave Palmus's crest and armor to his son Lausus to wear. He killed Evanthes the Phrygian, and Mimas, born on the same night Hecuba bore Paris — but Paris died in the fields of Troy, and Mimas, far from home, lay dead on a Latin plain. Like a wild boar bred on the mountains, fattened in the marshes, when the hunters and the baying hounds have ringed it in — it whets its tusks and turns at bay, and they dart their javelins from a safe distance and shout but none dares come close, while the beast froths and bristles and shakes the spears from its flanks — so the men whetted by their hatred of the tyrant kept up the fight only from a distance, clamoring and hurling, and none dared close with him.

From Corythus came Acron to the war, a Greek who had left his betrothed and the wedding night unconsummated; Mezentius saw him riding through the ranks, bright in the crimson favors his bride had given him, and rushed him like a starved lion that has spotted a frisking goat or a tall stag and runs, roaring, mane rising, jaws gaping wide — and the prey lies pinned and panting under the bloody paws, the lion's mouth running over with gore. So Mezentius hurled himself on his enemies, and Acron went down first, stretched full-length on the dark ground, the broken spear lying red in the wound. But Mezentius scorned to strike Orodes in the back as he fled, or to win him by a thrown spear; he ran round, met him face to face, and beat him in fair fight, the better man not by stealth but by strength. Then he set his foot on the fallen body, leaned on his spear, and cried: "Here lies Orodes — no small part of the war." His men took it up with a shout of triumph. But Orodes gasped out with his dying breath: "You won't go long unpunished, whoever you are. The same death is waiting for you on this same field." Mezentius answered with a grim half-smile: "What concerns me, let the Father of gods and king of men see to. Die now." And he drew the weapon from the wound. A heavy mist came swimming over Orodes' eyes and sealed them in everlasting night.

Caedicus killed Alcathous, Sacrator killed Hydaspes, Rapo brought down Parthenius and the mighty Orses; Messapus killed Clonius and the Lycian Ericetes — Clonius thrown from his bolting horse and pinned on the ground, Ericetes met on foot. Agis of Lycia stepped out to challenge the boldest, but Valerus, no shame to his father's prowess, cut him down. Salius killed Thronius; Nealces killed Salius — Nealces, deadly with the spear and with the far-reaching arrow that strikes before it is seen. So death was traded for death, grief for grief, between them; by turns they gave ground and gained it, killers and killed alike, neither side wholly winning, neither wholly broken. And the gods in Jupiter's halls looked down and pitied the useless rage of both, and grieved that mortals had to bear such suffering — Venus on one side watching, Saturnian Juno on the other; and pale Tisiphone raged in the thick of the thousands.

But now Mezentius, brandishing his great spear, stormed into the middle of the plain — towering there in the center like giant Orion wading the deep, his head above the topmost waves where he cuts a path through the water on foot, or like an old mountain ash, its head hidden in the clouds, its roots driven deep. Aeneas saw him from far off and came on to meet him. Mezentius held his ground unmoved, a mass waiting for its enemy; he measured with his eye the throw of the spear and cried: "My right hand, and the spear I'm balancing now — be my gods. I make no other vow. I'll dress you, Lausus, in the armor I strip from this Trojan pirate's body; you yourself will be my trophy over Aeneas." And he threw with all his force; the spear flew hissing, glanced off Aeneas's shield, and drove on at an angle into the body of Antores nearby — Antores, the companion of Hercules, who had come from Argos and stayed in Italy at Evander's side; now he fell by a wound meant for another, and looked up at the sky, and thought of Argos far away as he died.

Then dutiful Aeneas threw his spear in turn. It went through the hollow shield's three layers of bronze, through the linen and the three bull-hides, and lodged in Mezentius's groin, but the force was spent. Glad at the sight of the man's blood, Aeneas drew his sword and pressed in hard on his shaken enemy. And Lausus, seeing it, groaned aloud for love of his father, and the tears ran down his face. Here, brave boy, I will not be silent about your death, nor about your deed — so noble, so hard to believe, that men to come may doubt it. Mezentius, crippled by the wound and dragging the enemy spear that hung in his shield, gave ground and tried to pull back out of the fight. His son sprang forward into the middle of it. Just as Aeneas rose with his sword lifted to strike, Lausus came up under the blade and took it on his own shield and held the blow, while his father slipped away behind him, alive. His comrades roared and rained their spears to drive the Trojan off, and Aeneas, furious, sheltered under the cover of his shield — like a farmer caught in a hailstorm who huddles in any hollow until the sky clears.

But Aeneas kept calling out a warning even then, in anger and in pity both: "Why are you rushing on a death you can't survive? Why dare more than your strength can bear? Your love for your father is making a fool of you." Still the boy would not stop, pressing on, taunting; and the prince's patience gave way at last, and his whole chest filled with rage. The Fates were already gathering up the last threads of Lausus's life. Aeneas drove his strong sword full into the boy, to the hilt — through the light shield, the soft armor, the tunic his mother had woven him with gold thread — and filled his breast with blood. The soul went out of him sorrowing through the air, down to the shades, and left the body behind. But when Aeneas, son of Anchises, looked at the dying boy's face, that face going strangely pale, he groaned in deep pity and stretched out his hand, and the thought of his own love for his own father went through him. "Poor boy — what can dutiful Aeneas give you that's worthy of such courage, such a heart? Keep the armor you were so glad of; and I give your body back to your own people, your own dead, to be buried as you'd want. And let this be a comfort, if comfort there is: you fell by the hand of great Aeneas." And he lifted the body up himself, the hair clotted with blood, and called the boy's hanging-back companions to come and take it.

Meanwhile his father stood by the Tiber's tawny stream, washing his wounds in the water, his weary body propped against a tree-trunk, his bronze helmet hung on a branch overhead, his heavy arms laid out on the grass. The chosen young men stood around him while he leaned his sick, heavy head on his hand, his beard spilling over his chest, asking again and again for word of Lausus, sending messenger after messenger to call the boy back out of the fight — in his father's name, to come away. But they were already carrying Lausus, dead, weeping over him on his shield, a great body brought down by a great wound. The father's mind read the sound of their grief from far off, before they came. He fouled his gray hair with dust, lifted both hands to heaven, and threw himself on the body. "My son — was I so in love with living that I let you stand in my place against the enemy's sword, your wounds for mine? Am I saved by your death? Is this exile of mine only now, at last, truly bitter — the wound now driven home? It was I, my son, who stained your name with my crimes, I who got myself driven from my throne and my fathers' kingdom by the hate I earned. I owed my country that punishment; I should have paid it with my own guilty life a hundred deaths over. And yet I'm still alive — I haven't yet left the light and the company of men. But I will leave it."

And with that he raised himself on his crippled thigh, and though the deep wound slowed him he did not flinch; he called for his horse — Rhaebus, his pride and his comfort, that had carried him out of every battle — and spoke to him with sorrow: "Rhaebus, we've lived a long time, if anything for mortals lasts long. Today you'll either carry back the bloody head and the spoils of Aeneas and avenge Lausus's death with me, or, if no road opens that way, you'll go down with me. For I don't think, brave one, you'd ever take a foreign master's orders, or a Trojan rider." Then he mounted, settling his weight onto the horse's back as he had always done, and loaded both hands with sharp javelins, his bronze helmet on his head, bright with its tossing horsehair crest, and drove headlong into the thick of the fight. In his heart at once a great wave of shame, of madness, of grief, of love mixed with fury, and the knowledge of his own worth.

Three times he shouted Aeneas by name, in a great voice. Aeneas knew it, and rejoiced, and prayed: "So may the Father of the high gods grant it, and Apollo — come, begin." That was all he said, and he advanced with his spear leveled. Mezentius answered: "Why try to frighten me, you cruel man, now that you've taken my son? That was the one way you could destroy me. I don't fear death, and I have no regard for any god. Enough — I come to die, but first take these gifts from me." And he hurled a javelin, then another, and another, wheeling in a wide ring around his enemy; but the golden shield held. Three times he rode his circle round Aeneas, throwing as he went, and three times the Trojan turned with him, carrying a whole forest of spears in his shield. At last, weary of pulling out dart after dart and of fighting always on the defensive, hard-pressed and unequally matched, Aeneas made up his mind: he flung his spear straight between the temples of the war-horse. The animal reared up, lashing the air with its hooves, threw its rider, and came down on top of him in a tangle, pinning his shoulder under its weight, its own forequarters dislocated.

Trojans and Latins both filled the sky with their shouting. Aeneas ran up, drew his sword, and stood over him: "Where is fierce Mezentius now, and that wild spirit of yours?" The Etruscan, staring up at the sky, gathering back his senses, answered: "Why taunt me, enemy, why throw death in my face? There's no shame in my dying; I didn't come to the field expecting to live, and my son struck no bargain with you for my life. I ask one thing only — if a beaten enemy may ask any mercy: let my body be covered with earth. I know how my own people's hatred surrounds me. Keep their fury off me, I beg you, and let me share my son's grave." He said it, and offered his throat to the sword, knowing what was coming, and poured his life out over his armor in a flood of blood.
