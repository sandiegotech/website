The Aeneid, Book V

The Games for Anchises, and the Loss of Palinurus

Book V5 of 12

Meanwhile Aeneas held his course, sure of his voyage now, cutting the dark sea before the wind. But he looked back, and saw the walls of Carthage lit up by Dido's funeral fire. What had kindled such a blaze, no one aboard could say; yet a foreboding rose in Aeneas at the sight of it — he knew what a woman is capable of when her love has been wronged, how deep the wound runs, what dark place grief can drive her to. And a heavy dread settled over the Trojans.

Then the fires fell behind, the shoreline sank away, and there was nothing left but open water — sea below, sky above, and the ships alone between them. But over their heads a black cloud came rolling in, freighted with storm and night, and the swell darkened beneath it. Palinurus, the helmsman, shouted from the high stern: "Why is the whole sky closing over us like this? Father Neptune, what are you bringing down on us now?" Then he called to the crew to make fast the rigging, lean hard on the oars, and bring the sails about into the wind. And to Aeneas he said: "Great captain — no, not even if Jupiter himself stood surety for it, would I hope to make Italy under a sky like this. The wind has swung round and is rising against us out of the west, and the air is thickening into night. We can't fight it, and we can't hold our course through it. Fortune has the upper hand; let's go where she calls. Sicily is not far off, if I've read the stars right — the friendly shores of your brother Eryx, and the harbor we know. Let's turn back there." "I've seen for a while now," Aeneas answered, "that the sea wants otherwise, and you've been fighting it for nothing. Come about. Is there any land I'd rather make than the one that holds my father Anchises in its earth, where Acestes, a man of our own Trojan blood, is king?" So they ran for the shore before the wind and came swiftly into the harbor they knew.

High up on a headland Acestes saw them coming — the friendly ships standing in toward land — and he came down the cliff to meet them at a run, bristling with javelins, a rough bearskin across his shoulders. His mother had been a Trojan woman; his father was the Sicilian river-god Crinisus. He remembered his old kinship with these people, and he welcomed them home with a glad heart and all the plain plenty of his country.

The next morning, when the dawn had chased the stars from the sky and brought back the light, Aeneas called his people together and spoke to them from a rise of ground. "Trojans — sons of a great line, blood of the gods themselves. The sun has come full circle through the year; the months have closed the ring. It is a year to the day since I laid my father's bones and ashes in this earth and raised his tomb. And here is the day again, the day I will keep in grief and keep in love as long as I live. If this day found me a castaway on the African sands, or trapped on the open sea, or held in some enemy's country, I would still keep it — I would still hold the solemn rites, pile the altars high, and make the offerings due. But instead a god has driven us, I am certain of it, to these very shores, to the friendly ground that holds my father's blessed remains. So let us hold his honors gladly, and pray for fair winds to carry us on — and pray that one day, in a city and a temple of our own, we may keep this same day in Anchises' name. Acestes, born of our own Trojan stock, gives two oxen for every ship: feast, and call our household gods, and his, to the table. And nine days from now, if the dawn comes up clear and kind, I will hold the games. There will be a race of ships on the water; a footrace for the swift; a contest for the strong, who fight with rawhide gloves; and a match of the bow. Let every man come ready, and let the winners look for their prizes. Now — silence, and bind your brows with green branches." With that he wreathed his own head with myrtle, his mother's plant, and Helymus did the same, and old Acestes, and young Ascanius, and all the Trojan host after them.

Then Aeneas walked out from the gathering, thousands following him across the flowering plain, to the tomb of Anchises. There he poured two bowls of fresh wine onto the holy ground, and two of milk, and two of the dark blood of sacrifice, and scattered red flowers over the grave, and spoke aloud to his father's spirit: "Hail again, blessed father — hail, ashes I saved from the fire and have come back to in vain, soul and shade I will never hold again. The gods would not let you sail with me to the shores of Italy that fate has promised, nor see the Tiber, whatever river that turns out to be."

He had barely finished when, from deep inside the tomb, a great snake came gliding — seven huge coils of it, seven slow loops dragging across the ground. Its back was a sheen of blue washed over with flecks of gold, the way a rainbow throws a thousand colors when the sun strikes the rain. Aeneas stood frozen at the sight. The serpent wound its long body between the bowls and the altars, tasting the offerings with its flickering tongue, harming nothing, and then slid back down into the depths of the tomb and was gone. It left Aeneas more awed than ever, and more eager to finish the rites — though he could not say whether what he had seen was the guardian spirit of the place itself, or some attendant of his father's ghost. He killed the offerings as custom required: five sheep, five swine, five black bullocks; he poured the wine fresh from the bowls and called up the soul of great Anchises, his shade set free for a while from the world below. And every man who could bring a gift came forward and laid it on the tomb — some leading more oxen, some heaping the altars, some kindling the fires and turning the offered flesh upon the coals.

The awaited day came at last, and the ninth dawn rose clear, the horses of the sun lifting it into a bright sky. The whole coast had heard — the name of Acestes, the rumor of Trojan games — and the people came crowding down to the shore, some to watch, some to try their own skill against the strangers. First the prizes were set out in the open for all to see: green wreaths of palm and laurel for the victors, and in the ring, weapons, and bronze tripods, and ingots of gold and silver, and robes dyed deep with Tyrian purple. Then the trumpet sounded the start of the games. Four well-matched galleys came forward first, the pick of the whole fleet, to race on the water. Mnestheus drove the Sea-Dragon, hard-pulling — Mnestheus, from whom the Roman house of Memmius would one day claim descent. Gyas commanded the huge Chimaera, a ship as big as a town, three banks of Trojan oarsmen straining at her in three tiers. Sergestus, founder of the Sergian line, took the great Centaur; and Cloanthus, from whom your house comes, Cluentius of Rome, rode the sea-green Scylla.

Far out from the foaming shore there stands a rock, drowned under the breakers when the sea runs high but bare and dry when the water is calm, a quiet place where the gulls come to bask in the sun. Aeneas set up a leafy oak on it as the turning-mark, so the captains would know where to swing about and double back for home. They drew lots for their lanes. Up on the high sterns the captains shone in their purple and gold; the crews below had crowned their heads with poplar leaves and stripped to the waist, their shoulders gleaming with oil. They took their benches, hands tight on the oars, every chest tense — hope and fear pulling at them by turns — waiting for the signal. The trumpet blared, and at once they were off, all four in a line. The sailors' shout went up to the stars; the water churned white and smoking under the dragging oars. They cut their furrows together, dead even, and the whole sea seemed to tear open before their bronze prows. No chariot bursting from the gate, no team of horses lashed down the track, ever flew faster. And the crowd roared and split — this side, that side — and the cheering rolled back and forth from the woods to the hills and up into the sky.

Out of the noise and the spray, Gyas shot ahead of them all. Cloanthus came up close behind — a better crew, but a heavier ship. Behind them the Sea-Dragon and the Centaur ran neck and neck, trading the lead. But now Gyas was nearly at the rock, well in front, and he shouted to his helmsman Menoetes to hug the turn — "close in, scrape the rocks if you have to; let the others waste the open water!" But Menoetes was afraid of hidden reefs, and swung the bow wide toward the deep. "Where are you going? The rocks, Menoetes, hold to the rocks!" Gyas was screaming it now — and even as he screamed, he saw Cloanthus slip inside him, between his ship and the turning-mark, cutting the corner clean on the landward side. Cloanthus rounded the mark and got clear ahead into open water. That was more than Gyas could bear. The tears of rage stood in his eyes. He forgot his own dignity and his crew's safety both, seized the old, slow Menoetes, and threw him bodily off the high stern into the sea. Then he grabbed the steering-oar himself, took the helm, called encouragement to his men, and brought her hard about toward the shore.

Menoetes came up heavily at last, weighed down by his soaked clothes and his years, and dragged himself dripping up onto the rock. The whole crowd had watched him fall and surface and flounder, and they laughed — they roared with it — as he climbed out coughing the salt sea back up out of his chest. That gave heart to the two who trailed, the Centaur and the Sea-Dragon: with Gyas faltering, they had a chance. Sergestus pulled into the lead near the rock, though not by a whole length, his stern still lapped by the Sea-Dragon's prow. And Mnestheus paced the deck among his men and called out to them: "Now — pull, you who followed Hector, the men I chose out of the last hour of Troy and led through the flames! Show me now the strength of those arms, the courage that took you through the surf off Cape Malea and the wild water of the Syrtes. I don't ask for first place now — though, oh, if only — but let those have it whom the gods have chosen. Just don't let us come in dead last. Spare us that, my friends; spare yourselves the shame." And they threw everything into it, the whole crew bent double at the oars, the bronze hull shuddering, the sea sliding away beneath them, the sweat running off their straining sides in rivers. Then chance handed them the very thing they could not earn. Sergestus, hungry for the inside line, drove the Centaur's beak in between the Sea-Dragon and the rock — too close — and ran her hard aground on the jutting reef. The rock shook with the blow; the oars splintered against it; the prow stuck fast and hung there. The rowers scrambled up off their benches, shouting, frightened for their own skins now, the prize forgotten, and went at the reef with iron poles and boathooks, fishing their broken oars out of the sea. But Mnestheus, with the wind at his back and his blood up, ran for the open water and the home stretch — like a dove startled out of the rock where she nests, clattering her wings in fright through the cave, then bursting into the clear air and gliding smooth and fast on still wings without a beat. So the Sea-Dragon flew for home under her own gathered speed. She left Sergestus behind, wedged on his reef, thrashing in the shallows, crying uselessly for help and trying to row with shattered oars. Then she ran down Gyas and the great Chimaera, who, with her helmsman gone, gave way; and only Cloanthus was still ahead. Mnestheus went after him with everything he had. Now the shouting doubled, the whole crowd roaring him on, and the sky rang with it. Cloanthus's men heard it and could not bear to lose what was theirs by rights, the glory and the prize both; they would sooner die than yield. And the others were lifted by their own success — they could win, because they believed they could. They might have come in together, dead even, if Cloanthus had not flung out both hands over the water and prayed: "Gods of the sea I'm racing on — if you give me this crown, I'll gladly owe it to you. I'll bring a white bull down to your shore, cast its entrails into the surf, and pour out clear wine to you." And down under the waves the whole chorus of sea-nymphs heard him, and Phorcus, and the maiden Panopea; and Portunus himself reached up with his great hand and shoved the ship forward, and she flew to land faster than an arrow or the wind, and won.

The herald called for silence and proclaimed Cloanthus the winner of the sea-race. Aeneas crowned his head with laurel, gave three fat bullocks to his ship, with wine besides and a weight of silver for the crew to share, and to the captain himself a finer prize: a cloak heavy with gold thread and a double border of Tyrian purple, and worked into it, in living detail, the boy Ganymede running down the wooded slopes of Mount Ida, breathless, hurling his spear at the deer — and the eagle of Jupiter stooping out of the sky to seize him in its talons and carry him off, his old guardians stretching their hands up after him in vain, and the dogs barking up at the empty air.

Mnestheus was named second, and took the second prize: a shirt of mail, link on link of gold — the armor Aeneas himself had stripped from Demoleos in single combat under the walls of Troy, a magnificent thing, so heavy that the servants Phegeus and Sagaris could barely lift it between them, though Demoleos in his day had worn it and run the scattering Trojans down across the plain. For third, Aeneas gave a pair of great silver bowls worked with raised figures, and two cauldrons of polished bronze from Dodona.

When all of them had been rewarded from the hero's own hands, their heads bound in purple, Sergestus at last worked his battered ship free of the reef and brought her limping home — most of her oars gone, one whole bank dead, hooted at by the crowd. She was like a snake caught crossing a road and crushed under a wheel, or half-killed by a traveler's blow, that drags its broken length along, still hissing and rearing up in fury, its front fierce and its eyes blazing, while its ruined body trails useless behind it. That was how the Centaur came in, oars gone, but limping along under sail. And still Aeneas, glad just to have the ship and her men back safe, rewarded Sergestus: he gave him Pholoe, a Cretan slave-woman skilled at the loom, with twin infants at her breast.

That contest done, Aeneas walked over to a grassy clearing ringed by wooded hills, a natural amphitheater with a stage of level ground in the middle. There he took his seat on a high platform, his crowd of followers around him, and he laid out prizes to draw any man who cared to run a footrace. Trojans and Sicilians came forward together and lined up. First of all came Nisus and Euryalus — Euryalus in the first flower of his youth, the handsomest of them, and Nisus, who loved the boy with all his heart. After them came Diores, of Priam's royal line; then Salius and Patron, one of them Arcadian, the other of Greek stock; then two Sicilian boys, Helymus and Panopes, woodsmen both, raised in the forest, in Acestes' own service; and a crowd of others whose names time has not kept.

Aeneas stood among them and said: "Listen, and be glad — no one goes home empty-handed. Every runner gets two Cretan javelins of polished steel and a silver-mounted axe to carry away. The first three win olive crowns besides. The winner takes a horse in fine harness. The second, a quiver of the Amazons, packed with Thracian arrows, slung on a wide gold belt clasped with a jewel. The third can go content with this Greek helmet." That said, they took their marks. The signal dropped, and they broke from the line all at once and were gone like a gale, eyes already fixed on the far goal. Nisus shot out ahead of the whole field, faster than the wind, faster than the lightning. A long way back came Salius next, and a little behind Salius, Euryalus — and behind Euryalus, Helymus; and right on his heels, almost shoulder to shoulder, Diores, pressing up so close that with a few more strides he would have passed him, or at least made it too near to call.

They were spent now, almost at the line, when Nisus — luckless Nisus, out in front — went down. His foot slipped in a slick of blood where the bulls had been slaughtered for sacrifice, blood that had soaked the grass, and his heels flew up and he came down hard, fouled in the holy gore and the filth of it. But even falling he did not forget Euryalus, did not forget the love between them. He got up just enough to throw himself into Salius's path as the man came on, and Salius tripped over him and went sprawling on the hard ground. So Euryalus sprang past into the lead — winning by his friend's doing — and crossed the line first to a roar of applause. Helymus came in behind him, and Diores third.

But now Salius was on his feet, filling the whole arena with his shouting — robbed, he cried, cheated of first place by a foul; let the judges hear his case. The crowd's sympathy was with Euryalus, though — the boy was weeping, and beauty pleads better than argument; and Diores backed him up, loud, since if first place went to Salius, Diores lost his own third prize. Aeneas settled it: "There's no quarrel here. The places stand as they fell. But let me make up for the unlucky turn of things for an honest man." And he reached into the spoils and brought out the hide of a great lion, mane and gilded claws and all, and gave it to Salius. At that Nisus could not hold his tongue: "If men who lost get prizes like that, and a fall is the way to a reward, what does Nisus get? I'd have won first and the glory both, if the same bad luck that took Salius hadn't taken me." And he held up his face and his whole body, smeared with the blood and the muck. The good father of his people smiled, and had a fine shield brought out — the work of the craftsman Didymaon, once a war-prize torn from a Greek temple of Neptune. He gave it to Nisus, and shared out the rest, each man fairly served.

The footrace done and the prizes given, Aeneas spoke to the watching crowd again: "If any man here has the heart and the steady hands for it, let him step out, strip bare, and put up his fists with the leather wrapped on. Two prizes. To the winner, a bull with gilded horns and ribbons. The loser won't go away with nothing — he gets a sword and a fine helmet to ease the sting."

Out came Dares, swaggering, his head held high, a great rumble of applause following him — Dares, the only man who used to stand up to Paris himself, fist to fist; the same Dares who, at Hector's funeral games, took on the giant Butes of the line of Amycus and laid that huge body out flat in the dust with one blow. That was the kind of man who came stalking into the ring now, the crowd gaping at him. He squared his broad back and showed his chest, threw a few jabs at the empty air, and looked around for a match. Not one man in the trembling crowd would face him. So, sure no one would, he went up to Aeneas, took hold of the bull's horns, and said: "Goddess-born — if no one dares fight me, how long am I supposed to stand here? It's pointless to keep me waiting. Just let me lead the prize away." And the crowd roared for him to be given it.

But old Acestes could not stand to see the prize handed over without a fight. He was sitting beside Entellus on the grass, and he turned on him: "Entellus — once the bravest of all of us, and for nothing now? Are you going to sit here and let a prize like that be carried off in front of you without lifting a hand? Where is Eryx now, the god we called our master, who taught your arm the game? Where's the name that filled this whole island? Where are the spoils that used to hang in your house?" And Entellus answered: "The will is still there — fear hasn't taken it, nor love of glory. It's the cold blood, slowed in my veins; the strength gone numb with age. If I had back the young body this braggart is so sure of, I wouldn't have needed a prize to bring me out. I'd be in that ring."

And with that he stood up and threw down two enormous leather gloves into the open ring — the very gloves the fierce Eryx used to fight in, the bull-hide wound seven times thick and sewn through with iron and lead. The whole crowd drew back, stunned. Even Dares balked at the sight of them, and would not take them up. Aeneas himself came and weighed the great things in his hands, turning them over. And the old man said: "And what would you have said if you'd seen the gloves Hercules himself wore — the fight that happened right here, on this unlucky ground? Your own brother Eryx wore these once, look — they're still caked with brains and dried blood. He stood up to Hercules in these. I wore them too, while my blood still ran hot and old age hadn't frosted my head or unstrung my arms. But if Dares won't have them — if good Aeneas approves and Acestes, who urged me into this, agrees — then let's even the odds. I'll give up Eryx's gloves; you can stop being afraid of them. And you take off your Trojan ones."

With that the old man stripped off his cloak and stood bare — the great frame of him, the heavy bones, the muscle still corded on the arms — a tower of a man on the sand. Aeneas brought out matched pairs of gloves and bound them on the wrists of both. They came up at once on their toes, fists high, heads back out of reach, and opened the fight. The young one had his quickness and his light legs; the old one his reach and his sheer mass — but his knees were stiff, his breath came hard, his whole body shook with it. They traded blow after blow, raining them on each other's ribs and chests, hammering at temples and ears, the punches whistling past or landing with a crack along the jaw. Entellus stood his ground, heavy and rooted, slipping the blows with a small twist of his body, his eyes never leaving his man. Dares was the one moving — circling, feinting, probing for an opening like a general working around the walls of a fort, trying here, trying there, all craft, no force. Then Entellus reared up, his right hand high, ready — and Dares read it coming and slid out from under it, light on his feet, and the great blow came down on nothing but air. Entellus, all that weight behind a punch that hit nothing, lost his balance and crashed down full-length, a huge fall — like an old pine torn up by the roots on the heights of Ida. Trojans and Sicilians came to their feet shouting, and the noise went up to the sky. Acestes ran in first to lift his old friend up. But the old man was neither hurt nor cowed by the fall — it only stoked him. Shame and pride and the knowledge of his own worth lit a fire in him, and he drove Dares all over the ring, raining punches with both hands, right and left, never stopping, never letting him breathe, a storm of blows, a hailstorm coming down on his head. Then Aeneas, seeing the fury rising and Entellus past all reason, stepped in and ended it. He pulled the spent Dares out and gave him gentle words: "What madness has hold of you, my friend? Can't you see the strength in him is more than human now, the gods have turned against you? Give way." And so they ended it. Dares' own men half-carried him off, his head lolling, his legs dragging, spitting blood and broken teeth, while behind him the prize, the bull and the palm, stayed with Entellus.

And the old champion, prouder of his arm than of the prize, stood over the bull and said: "Goddess-born, and you, Trojans — see now what I still had in me, and what death you saved Dares from." And he set himself square in front of the animal, drew back, and brought the hard glove straight down between its horns — one blow, and the skull shattered and the brains burst out, and the bull dropped without a twitch and lay there. Then he said, over it: "Eryx, I pay you this better life in place of Dares' death. Here, the victor, I lay down the gloves, and here I leave the ring for good."

Next Aeneas called for the archers. He had the mast from Sergestus's wrecked ship raised on the shore, and tied a dove to the top of it by a cord around the foot — a live target for the arrows. The bowmen drew lots out of a bronze helmet for their order. First out, to a cheer, was Hippocoon. Then young Mnestheus, fresh from his prize in the ship-race. Third was Eurytion, brother of the great Pandarus — that Pandarus whom the gods once drove to break the truce at Troy and put an arrow into the Greeks. Last in the helmet, last drawn, was Acestes himself, who was not too old to try a young man's sport. Hippocoon bent his bow first and let fly, and the arrow tore whistling through the air and stuck in the wood of the mast. The whole pole shook, the dove beat her wings in terror, and the crowd's roar split the sky. Then Mnestheus drew, aiming high, eyes lifted, and just missed the bird — but so near that he cut the cord that held her foot, and she was loose, free, climbing into the open sky on clapping wings. But Eurytion stood already with his bow drawn, his prayer to his brother's god already spoken, and he caught her up there in the open air. She dropped, dead, leaving her life among the stars, and fell back to earth with the arrow still in her. Only Acestes was left, with nothing now to shoot at and no prize to gain. Still, he drew and sent his shaft straight up into the air, to show his skill and the song of his bowstring — and there it happened. The arrow caught fire as it climbed, racing so fast it kindled, and drew a long trail of flame behind it, burning across the sky like a falling star, and burned out into wind and was gone. Trojans and Sicilians alike stood staring, and the wonder turned to fear, and the fear to prayer. Great Aeneas did not refuse the omen; he took it for a good sign. He folded Acestes in a long embrace and heaped greater gifts on him than on the rest: "Take these, father. The king of heaven himself has marked you out for the first prize with this sign, past all the casting of lots. Here — a bowl chased with figures of gold, that Cisseus of Thrace once gave my father Anchises, a token of their old friendship. I give it now to you, my second father." And he sounded the trumpets and crowned Acestes the winner over them all; and good Eurytion did not grudge it, though he was the one who had brought the bird out of the sky.

Before the games were quite over, Aeneas called Periphas, who looked after his son, and said quietly: "Go, find Ascanius. If he has his troop of boys ready and the horses lined up, tell him to ride for his grandfather's day, and to show himself in arms." Then he had the crowd cleared back, opening up the long stretch of level ground, and the boys came riding out in front of their watching fathers, in good order, on glittering horses — a beautiful sight, and a murmur of pride ran through the men of Troy and Sicily both as they came on.

Every boy wore a wreath of cut leaves on his hair, as custom is. Each carried two cornel-wood spears tipped with iron; some had polished quivers slung on their backs; and around every neck hung a flexible torque of twisted gold. They rode in three companies, three captains at the head, twelve boys following each, in three gleaming lines. The first troop was led by little Priam, named for his grandfather, the great Priam — a fine boy, Polites's son, destined to give children to Italy one day; he rode a Thracian horse dappled white, white-footed in front, with a white star on its brow. The second was led by Atys, the boy from whom the Roman house of the Atii would come, little Atys, dear to little Iulus. And last, and finest of all of them, rode Iulus — Ascanius — on a horse that lovely Dido had given him, of Tyrian breed. The rest of the boys were mounted on Sicilian horses that old Acestes provided.

The Trojans welcomed them with applause, glad and a little afraid at once, seeing the fathers' faces in the sons. When they had ridden once around the whole crowd, paraded before their elders, the signal came, and Epytides cracked his whip and gave a shout, and the three troops split apart and wheeled away into three; and then, called back, they swung their lances and charged. Then they fell into new figures and counter-figures, circle answering circle, line meeting line, a mock-battle drawn up and dissolved and drawn up again — now offering their backs in flight, now turning their spears to attack, now riding side by side at peace. It was like the old labyrinth on Crete, they say, with its blind walls and its thousand turnings, a tangle no one could thread, no way back out — that was how the boys of Troy crossed and recrossed their tracks in the weave of their war-game, flight braided into pursuit, like dolphins playing through the water, cutting circles in the sea. Ascanius was the one who first brought this riding-game back, this drill, when he walled in Alba Longa long after, and he taught it to the old Latins; and they passed it to their sons, and from them mighty Rome received it and keeps it still, and calls the boys ‘Troy’ and the game ‘the Trojan ride.’

So far the sacred games for Anchises had kept their holy course. But here Fortune turned, and showed her old hatred again. For while the Trojans were paying their dead his yearly honors at the tomb, Juno, daughter of Saturn, was watching those rites with envy, and she sent Iris down from heaven to the Trojan ships, breathing a wind into her wings to speed her. Down the long arch of the rainbow Iris came, unseen by anyone, and crossed the crowd and saw the harbor and the beach — empty, the fleet riding there with no one to guard it. Off by themselves on the lonely shore, the Trojan women were weeping for the dead Anchises, and as they wept they looked out at the wide sea and wept for themselves too. "After all we've been through," one of them was saying, "all that water still ahead of us, all that road —" and they all took up the word, and groaned together, and begged the gods for a city, a home, an end to the sea.

Iris dropped into the middle of them — no fool at making trouble — and put off her divine shape and her shining limbs and took the form of Beroe, the old wife of Doryclus, a woman of good family who had once had children and a name. In that shape she moved among the weeping women and said: "We poor women! The Greeks didn't kill us under the walls of Troy in our own country, where at least we'd have died at home. Unhappy people — for what ruin is Fortune keeping us alive? Seven years now this miserable voyage has dragged on, hauled from shore to shore over every cruel rock and barren sand, chasing an Italy that runs away from us across the storms. Here is our brother Eryx's land, here is Acestes who would take us in — what's to stop us building our walls right here and giving our people a city at last? O my country, O household gods we carried out of the fire for nothing — are we never to see a wall called Troy again, a river we can name a new Simois? Come — burn the cursed ships with me. In my sleep the ghost of the prophet Cassandra came to me and put burning brands in my hands and said, 'Look for your Troy here. This is your home.' Now is the time; the gods themselves are giving us the fire. Look — Neptune's own altars are right there, handing us the torches. The god is with us." And she snatched a brand from the altar fire, drew her arm back high, and hurled it into the ships.

The women stood frozen, staring. Then Pyrgo spoke up — Pyrgo, oldest and most honored of them, who had nursed Priam's many children: "That is not Beroe, women, whatever her face says. Look at her — the light coming off her, the burning eyes, the way she carries herself, the voice. There's a goddess in her. I left the real Beroe just now, sick in her bed, eating her heart out that she alone had to miss these rites." But the women were torn. They stood there casting dark looks from the ships to the land, pulled both ways — sick with longing for the country waiting under their feet, and dragged on by fate toward the sea. And then the goddess rose up on level wings and cut the bright bow of her flight as she went. And at the sight of that, the women lost themselves to a madness sent from heaven. They screamed; they tore the fire off the altars with their own hands; they piled the ships with brushwood and green branches and smoking torches. The flames took, and ran wild and unchecked through the benches and the oars and the painted sterns. Eumelus carried the news up to the amphitheater where the crowd was still gathered, and then they could see it for themselves: a black storm of sparks and smoke rolling up off the ships. Ascanius — still leading his boy-troops in their ride — was the first to break away; he spurred down toward the shore, far out ahead, his frightened guardians unable to hold him. As soon as he came in sight of the women he shouted to them while he was still riding hard: "What madness is this? What are you doing? It isn't an enemy fleet you're burning — it's your own hope, the last of Troy! Look — it's me, Ascanius!" And he pulled the helmet off his head, the helmet he had worn for the game. By then Aeneas was coming, and the whole crowd behind him. The women scattered in shame and fear, slinking off to the woods and the caves, sick at what they had done, hating the light — themselves again, knowing their friends, shaking the goddess out of their minds.

But the fire would not give up so easily. It had crept down into the seams and the caulking, smoldering under a false calm, eating slowly but surely through the green timber and breathing out a sullen flame. It worked down to the keels and up toward the sails, and nothing could stop it — not buckets of water, not all the strength of the men's hands.

Then dutiful Aeneas tore the cloak from his shoulders, threw up his hands to heaven, and prayed: "Jupiter almighty — if you don't yet hate every last Trojan to the man, if there's any of the old mercy left for human suffering, let the fleet escape the flames now, snatch this little remnant of Troy back from death. Or if I've earned it, strike me down instead — hurl your thunderbolt at this one head, and let me be the offering."

He had barely said it when a black storm broke loose, a downpour like nothing they had seen. Thunder rolled across the hills and the plain, the whole sky came down in sheets of rain, the clouds burst black and full. Water poured into the ships, drowned the smoldering wood, killed the fire — and out of the whole fleet only four ships were lost. The rest were saved.

But the disaster left Aeneas torn. Should he forget what fate had promised and settle here in Sicily after all, or push on for Italy? It was old Nautes who steadied him — Nautes, whom the goddess Pallas had taught beyond all other men, gifted at reading the will of heaven and what the fates required. He spoke to Aeneas gently, and gave his troubled mind something to hold: "Goddess-born — let us go where fate drags us and drags us back. Whatever comes, the way through it is to bear it. You have Acestes here, a Trojan, born of the gods. Take him into your counsel; he'll be glad of it. Hand over to him the ones the ships can't carry anymore — the ones worn out with the voyage and sick of it: the old men, the women tired to the bone by the sea, anyone too weary or too frightened to face the danger ahead. Let them stay, and build their city here, the tired ones, and call it Acesta after Acestes."

Aeneas was stirred by his old friend's words, but it only pulled his mind a dozen ways at once. Then black Night rode up the sky in her chariot, and as Aeneas slept the shade of his father Anchises came down to him out of heaven and spoke: "My son — dearer to me than my own life while I lived, dearer still now; my son, worn down by the long ordeal of fate. I come at the command of Jupiter himself, the god who drove the fire from your ships and at last looked on you with pity from on high. Take the counsel old Nautes gives you: carry only the chosen young men, the bravest hearts, to Italy. There's a hard, fierce people in Latium you'll have to break. But first you must go down to the underworld, to the house of the dead, and through the deep places find me. I am not among the damned, in the dark with the guilty; I dwell among the blessed, in the sweet meadows of Elysium. The holy Sibyl will lead you down, when you have shed the blood of black victims for the passage. There you will learn the whole line of your descendants and the city that is given them. Now — goodbye. The wet night is wheeling past the middle of its course, and I feel the cruel breath of the dawn coming." And he was gone, melted into the dark like smoke. "Where are you going? So fast?" Aeneas cried after him. "Who are you running from? Who is keeping me from your arms?"

He woke, and stirred the banked embers back into flame, and worshiped the household gods of Troy and the white-haired hearth-goddess Vesta with cakes and incense and a full censer of offering. Then at once he sent for his friends, and first of all for Acestes, and told them the will of Jupiter, the command of his dear father, and what he had decided in his own heart. There was no argument; Acestes agreed. They drew up the rolls of the women and the old, and any man whose heart was not in the great work, who would not stake his life for glory — those they marked to stay. The rest, the worn but willing few, they kept, and refitted the ships, replacing the burned thwarts and oars and cables, a small company now, but all of them ready. Meanwhile Aeneas marked out a city with the plow, and parceled the homes by lot, and named one quarter Ilium and another Troy. Acestes, glad to be a king, took up his throne, called a senate together, gave them laws to keep. And up on the height of Eryx they began a temple to Venus, close to the stars; and they gave Anchises' tomb a priest and a wide sacred grove. For nine days the whole people feasted, and the altars smoked. Then a soft wind came up out of the south to smooth the sea, and the rising breeze called the sailors back aboard.

Now a great grief of farewells rose along the shore, the parting friends locked in each other's arms. Even the women, even the worn-out men who had dreaded the sea, now wanted to go, to take their share of the hard road and whatever war waited at the end of it. Aeneas comforted them, and with tears commended his fearful friends to the care of their new king, Acestes, his kinsman. He sacrificed three calves to Eryx and a young lamb to the storm-gods of the sea, then ordered the cables cast off. He stood high on the prow, his head crowned with cut olive, a bowl in his hands, and threw the offering of entrails into the sea and poured out the clear wine after it. A fair wind rose behind them, and the crew bent to the oars and swept out over the waves.

But Venus, sick with worry, went to Neptune and poured out her fears: "Juno's relentless rage, the bottomless malice of her, that no suffering ever softens — it drives me to beg you for this. Not fate, not time, not all these years of pity have worn her hatred down. It wasn't enough for her to eat the very city out of the heart of Troy and drag what survived through every torment; she has to gnaw the last bones of it. You know why she hates them, you've seen what it does. You saw the storm she raised on the Libyan sea not long ago — heaving the waves up to the clouds, bribing Aeolus, stirring a mutiny in your own kingdom. And now she's driven the Trojan women to a frenzy, burned the ships, and forced Aeneas, with his fleet half gone, to leave his own people stranded on a foreign coast. So I beg you — let what's left of them have safe passage across your water. Let them reach the Tiber and Latium, if what I ask is allowed, if the Fates grant those walls."

Then the lord of the deep sea answered: "You have every right to trust my kingdom, Venus — you were born from it. And I've earned your trust before: more than once I've held back the rage of the sea and sky for your Aeneas's sake. I cared for him on land too, not only at sea. The rivers Xanthus and Simois are my witnesses — that day at Troy when Achilles was driving your people in a rout against the walls and choking the rivers with their dead, when Xanthus himself could not find his own channel for the bodies and Aeneas stood and fought a stronger man and a god who had turned against him, I was the one who wrapped Aeneas in a cloud and carried him out of it — even then, when I was longing to tear down the walls of the Troy I'd built and been cheated for. My mind is the same now. Stop being afraid. He'll reach the harbor you ask for, safe. There will be only one — one life lost out of all of them, one life given for the many." And having soothed her heart with that promise, the god yoked his horses, bridled them, and let the reins run free, and skimmed his blue chariot light over the surface. The swell went down before him; the water lay flat and silent under the thundering axle, and the storm-clouds fled from the wide sky. Around him came all the creatures of his train — the great whales, the old sea-god Glaucus, Palaemon, the swift Tritons, Phorcus and all his host; the sea-nymphs rode on his left, the Nereids on his right.

And now a warm hope came back to Aeneas, and lifted his spirits. He called for the masts to be stepped and the sails set, and the glad crew jumped to it; they let the canvas fill, ran before the wind, and stood out over the open sea. At the head of the whole fleet Palinurus held the course, and the rest were ordered to steer by him. Damp Night had nearly reached the middle of the sky; the crew lay sprawled and resting on their hard benches under the oars. And then the soft god of Sleep came gliding down out of the high air, parting the dark, scattering the shadows — and he was coming for you, Palinurus. To you alone he came, bringing grim dreams to a man who had done no wrong. He settled on the high stern in the shape of Phorbas, a shipmate, and spoke gently: "Palinurus, son of Iasus — the sea is carrying us along by itself, the wind is steady, here's an hour to rest. Lay your head down. Close your eyes a while. I'll take the helm for you." Palinurus barely lifted his eyes. "You want me to trust the smooth face of this sea, this lying calm? You want me to hand Aeneas over to the mercy of the wind — I, who've been fooled too many times by a quiet sky?" And he held on, both hands locked on the tiller, never letting go, his eyes fixed on the stars. But the god took a branch dripping with the water of Lethe, drugged with the sleep of the river of the dead, and shook it across his temples; and Palinurus, fighting it, felt his eyes swim shut at last. He had barely gone slack when the god leaned his whole weight on him and shoved him over the side into the clear sea, tearing the tiller and a piece of the stern away with him. Palinurus went down crying out to his friends again and again — and no one heard. And the god rose up into the empty air. The fleet ran on, safe — Neptune kept his word — and came at last to the cliffs of the Sirens, that old graveyard of ships, white now with the bones of the lost, where the rocks roar and roar under the breaking sea. There Aeneas felt the ship begin to drift, wandering without her helmsman, and he took the tiller himself in the dark water, and steered her clear of the reef — grieving, groaning from the depth of him, struck to the heart by the loss of his friend. "Palinurus," he said, "you trusted a calm sky and a quiet sea too far. Now you'll lie naked on some shore that has no name."
