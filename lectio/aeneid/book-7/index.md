# The Aeneid, Book VII

Latium, and the Opening of the War

You too, Caieta, nurse of Aeneas, dying here gave our shores a name that will not fade. Your honor keeps this place even now, and your bones rest, if that is any glory, marked in great Hesperia by your name.

When dutiful Aeneas had paid her the last rites and raised her mound, he waited for the sea to settle, then loosed his sails and left the harbor. The wind held fair into the night, and the moon would not deny them her white light; the sea trembled and shone under her trembling rays. They coasted close by the shores of Circe's land — Circe, the rich daughter of the Sun, whose untouched groves ring with her singing. In her proud halls she burns sweet cedar against the dark and runs the shrill shuttle through the standing web. And from her shore came the angry roar of lions straining against their chains and growling deep in the night; bristled boars and penned-up bears raged there, and the great shapes of wolves howled — men once, whom the cruel goddess with her powerful herbs had stripped of their human faces and clothed in the bodies and the hides of beasts. To spare the good Trojans that horror, to keep them from landing on the witch's coast, Neptune filled their sails with a following wind and carried them past, out beyond the boiling shoals, and saved them.

Now the sea was reddening under the sun's first rays, and Dawn shone golden out of the high sky in her rose chariot, when the wind dropped all at once and every breath of air went still, and the oars labored against the heavy water. And there, out of the sea, Aeneas saw a great wood, and through the middle of it the Tiber breaking through in lovely water, swirling with quick eddies, yellow with the sand it carried, running out to the sea. Around it and above it birds of every kind, the birds that haunt the riverbanks and the channel, were soothing the air with their song and skimming through the trees. Aeneas gave the order to turn their prows toward land, and gladly they swung in under the shadowed banks and entered the river.

Now help me, Erato — let me tell who the kings were, how things stood in old Latium, when first that foreign fleet beached its ships on the shores of Ausonia; let me trace back the start of the quarrel. Goddess, breathe through your poet. I will sing of grim wars, of battle lines, of kings driven by their own hearts into death; of the Etruscan power, and all Hesperia called to arms. A greater order of things opens before me now; I set my hand to a greater work.

Latinus, old and at peace, had long ruled the fields and the quiet towns of Latium. He was the son of Faunus and the nymph Marica of Laurentum; Faunus was son of Picus, and Picus claimed Saturn for his father — so Saturn was the first founder of the line. By the will of heaven Latinus had no son, no male heir: the boys he fathered had been taken in the first bloom of their youth, and one daughter alone was left to keep the house and its great wealth, a girl now ripe for a husband, full grown for marriage. Many sought her, out of all wide Latium, out of all Ausonia. Handsomest of them all was Turnus, strong in the long descent of his fathers, and the queen, her mother, longed with all her heart to make him her son. But the gods stood in the way, and sign after frightening sign held the match back.

Deep in the inner courts of the palace stood a laurel, its leaves held sacred, kept safe through long years of awe. Latinus himself, the story went, had found it when he first walled the place, and dedicated it to Apollo, and from it he called his people the Laurentines. Onto the top of this tree — strange to tell — a dense swarm of bees came borne through the clear air, loud as they came, and clinging foot to foot they hung suddenly in a great cluster from a leafy bough. At once the prophet said: "I see a stranger coming, an army out of the same quarter of the sky making for the same place, to be lord over the height of our city."

And once more, while Lavinia stood beside her father feeding the altar fire with holy torches, the girl seemed — it is terrible to tell — to catch the flames in her long hair, all her finery burning with a crackling roar, her royal hair on fire, on fire the crown thick with jewels, until she was wrapped in smoke and yellow light and scattered fire through the whole house. People called it a fearful, wonderful thing to see; the seers sang that the girl herself would be glorious in name and fate, but that for her people she foretold a great war.

Troubled by the omen, the king went to the oracle of his father Faunus, the prophet, and sought out the grove under high Albunea, where the greatest of the woods rings with a sacred spring and breathes out, in its darkness, a deadly vapor. From all over Italy, from the whole land of Oenotria, men came here in their need for answers. Here the priest brings his offerings, lies down for the night on the fleeces of the slaughtered sheep, and courts sleep; and there in the dark he sees many shapes drifting in strange ways, hears voices of every kind, speaks with the gods, and calls up Acheron from the depths of Avernus. Here too Latinus, looking for an answer, killed a hundred fleecy ewes as the rite required, and lay down stretched on their hides. And out of the deep grove a voice came suddenly: "Do not look, my son, to marry your daughter to a Latin; do not trust the wedding already prepared. Strangers will come to be your sons, whose blood will lift our name to the stars, and whose children's children will see all the world, wherever the wheeling sun looks down on either ocean, turn beneath their feet and obey them." Latinus did not keep to himself these answers his father Faunus gave him, this warning out of the silent night; and Rumor had already carried it far and wide through the towns of Ausonia, by the time the Trojan crews moored their fleet along the grassy bank.

Aeneas and his chief men and his handsome Iulus stretched out under the branches of a tall tree and laid their meal there; they set wheaten cakes flat on the grass for tables, as Jupiter himself had put it in their minds, and heaped wild fruit on the bread. And when the rest was eaten, hunger drove them, with the food gone, to turn their teeth on the thin disks of bread, to break with hand and bold jaw the fated round of the crust, and they did not spare the wide squares of the trenchers. "Look — we're even eating our tables!" said Iulus, no more than that, joking. That word, heard, was the first to mark the end of all their hardship, and his father caught it from the boy's lips before he could say another, and held still, struck by the will of the gods. At once he said: "Welcome to you, land my fate has promised me — and welcome, you faithful gods of Troy. Here is home; here is our country. For my father Anchises, I remember it now, left me this secret of the fates: 'My son, when hunger drives you, beached on an unknown shore, with the food gone, to eat your very tables — then, worn as you are, look for your home; there, with your own hands, raise your first houses and throw up a wall around them.' This was that hunger; this was the last thing waiting for us, the end that puts a stop to our wandering. So tomorrow, at the first light of the sun, let us search out — gladly — what this land is, who holds it, where the men have set their city; and we'll start from the harbor here. But now pour out your cups to Jupiter, call on my father Anchises in your prayers, and set the wine back on the tables."

So he spoke, and wreathed his brow with green leaves, and prayed to the spirit of the place, and to Earth, first of the gods, and to the nymphs, and to the rivers still unknown to him; then he called on Night and the rising stars of Night, and on Jupiter of Ida, and on the Phrygian Mother in her turn, and on both his parents, the one in heaven and the one below. Then the all-powerful Father thundered three times from the clear sky and showed them, with his own hand, a cloud blazing with golden light. At once the word ran through the Trojan ranks that the day had come to build their promised city. Joyfully they spread the feast again, and gladdened by the great omen they set out the bowls and crowned the wine.

When the next day's light first touched the earth, they went out to search the land, the town, the borders, the shore of the people there; this, they found, was the marsh of the spring of Numicus, this the river Tiber, and here lived the brave Latins. Then the son of Anchises chose a hundred envoys from every rank, and sent them up to the king's noble walls, all of them shaded under the boughs of Pallas's olive, to carry gifts to the man and to ask peace for the Trojans. They went without delay, quick at his order. Aeneas himself marked out the city with a shallow trench, broke ground, and ringed his first settlement on the shore with a rampart and a wall, like a camp.

And now the envoys had finished their road; they could see the towers and the high roofs of the Latins, and they drew near the wall. Outside the town boys and young men in the first flower of their age were exercising on horseback, breaking colts in the dust, bending the stiff bow, or hurling tough javelins from the arm and challenging each other to footrace and to fight. A messenger rode ahead to the old king's ears with the news: tall men had come, in strange clothing, no one knew from where. Latinus ordered them brought into the hall and took his place on the throne of his fathers.

There was a building in the heart of the city, vast, high, set up on a hundred columns, the palace of Picus of Laurentum, kept in awe under a grove of trees handed down from of old. Here it was the custom for the kings to take the scepter and lift the first rods of office; this was their temple, their council house, the hall of their sacred feasts; here, a ram slaughtered, the elders would sit down at the long tables. And along the entrance, ranged in order, stood the images of the forefathers carved in old cedar — Italus, and Father Sabinus the planter of the vine, holding still the curved pruning-hook; old Saturn, and the image of two-faced Janus; and other kings from the first days, and the men who had taken wounds in war fighting for their country. On the doorposts hung weapons beyond counting — captured chariots and curved axes, helmet-crests and the great bars of city gates, spears and shields and the beaks torn off the prows of ships. Picus himself sat there, the tamer of horses, in the short striped robe and carrying the augur's staff, a shield in his left hand — Picus, whom his wife Circe, mad with love, had struck with her golden wand and changed by her poisons into a bird, dappling his wings with colors.

Within that great temple Latinus, seated on the throne of his fathers, called the Trojans in to him and spoke first, with quiet kindness: "Tell me, sons of Dardanus — for we know your city and your line, and we heard you were steering this way over the sea — what do you want? What brought your ships across so much blue water to the shore of Ausonia? Whether you have lost your road, or been driven by storms (sailors meet much of that on the deep), and have come up our river and lie now in our harbor — do not turn away from our welcome, and know that we Latins are the people of Saturn, righteous not because we are forced to it or held by any law, but holding of our own free will to the way of our ancient god. And I remember now — though the years have worn the story thin — the old men of the Aurunci used to tell how Dardanus, born in this country, made his way to the cities of Phrygian Ida and to Thracian Samos, the place they now call Samothrace. He set out from here, from his home at Tuscan Corythus; and now the golden palace of the starry sky holds him, a throne among the gods, and his altars swell their number."

He finished, and Ilioneus answered: "King, great son of Faunus, no black storm drove us over the waves to take shelter in your land; no star, no shoreline turned us from our course. We came to this city on purpose, every one of us, by our own choice, driven out of a kingdom that was once the greatest the sun ever looked down on. Our line begins with Jupiter; the sons of Dardanus are glad to call Jupiter their first father, and our own king, Trojan Aeneas, is of that highest blood — he it is who sent us to your door. How great a storm broke out of cruel Mycenae and swept across the fields of Ida, by what fate the two worlds, Europe and Asia, were driven together and crashed — even the man who lives shut off at the far edge of the world, where Ocean curls back, has heard of it, and so has the man cut off in the middle of the four zones by the cruel sun. Out of that flood, across so much desolate sea, we ask only a small thing — a strip of harmless ground for our country's gods, air and water that belong to everyone. We will be no disgrace to your kingdom, and your good name will be no small thing among men, your kindness never forgotten; nor will Ausonia regret having taken Troy to its heart. I swear it by the fate of Aeneas, by his right hand, proved true to friend and dangerous to foe alike: many a people, many a nation — do not look down on us because we come holding the ribbons and the words of suppliants in our hands — has wanted us for ally and friend; but the will of the gods drove us by their command to seek out your land. Here Dardanus was born; here Apollo calls us back, and drives us on with stern orders to the Tuscan Tiber and the holy waters of the spring of Numicus. And on top of this our king sends you these small gifts, the leavings of his old fortune, snatched out of burning Troy. From this golden bowl Anchises poured at the altars; this was the robe and crown of Priam when he gave the people justice, and this his scepter, and his sacred diadem, and these the robes the women of Troy worked with their own hands."

At these words of Ilioneus, Latinus held his face fixed on the ground, motionless, only his eyes moving, restless and intent. The embroidered purple did not move him, nor the scepter of Priam, so much as he was turning over his daughter's marriage and her wedding bed, weighing in his deep heart the old oracle of Faunus: this, surely, was the foreigner the fates had named, called from abroad to share the throne with him; this was the line marked out for power, whose strength would one day fill the whole world. At last, glad in his heart, he spoke: "May the gods bless what we begin, and their own prophecy. You shall have what you ask, son of Troy; and I do not refuse your gifts. While Latinus is king, you will not lack rich land or the wealth of Troy. Only let Aeneas come himself, if he wants us so much, if he is in such haste for friendship and the name of ally; let him not shrink from meeting a friend face to face. For me, part of making peace will be to take your king's hand in mine. Now carry this answer back to him: I have a daughter, and the oracle of my father's shrine, and many signs from heaven, forbid me to give her to a man of our own people. Sons will come from a foreign shore — so they prophesy — and that is the fate of Latium; men whose blood will lift our name to the stars. This is the man the fates are calling, I am certain of it, and if my mind reads the truth at all, this is the man I want."

So he said, and chose horses for them all — three hundred of them stood sleek in his high stables — and ordered one led out at once for each Trojan, swift, covered with a saddlecloth of purple, gold trappings hanging down their breasts, gold bridles, gold champed between their teeth. And for the absent Aeneas he sent a chariot and a team yoked to it, of the breed of heaven, breathing fire from their nostrils, born of the stock that crafty Circe had bred by stealth, slipping a mortal mare under her father's divine stallion. With these gifts and these words the men of Aeneas rode back, high on their horses, carrying peace.

But look — the cruel wife of Jupiter was on her way back from Argos, riding the air, and from far off, from high over Sicilian Pachynus, Juno saw it all: saw Aeneas glad, and the Trojan fleet at rest. She saw them building houses now, trusting the land, the ships left empty on the shore; and she stopped, pierced through with a bitter grief. Then she shook her head and poured these words out of her heart: "Hateful brood, and the fate of Troy, set against my own fate forever! Could they not die on the fields of Troy? Could the captured not stay captured, the burned town not burn them? No — through the middle of armies and through fire they found a road. My power, I suppose, is worn out at last and lies down beaten; I have glutted my hate and given up. When they were driven from their country I dared to chase them across the waves, to stand against the runaways over the whole sea. The strength of sky and sea has been spent on the Trojans, and nothing has come of it. What good were the Syrtes to me, or Scylla, or the wide whirlpool of Charybdis? They have a bed in the Tiber's longed-for channel now, safe from the sea, safe from me. Mars had the power to wipe out the giant brood of the Lapiths; the Father of the gods himself gave up old Calydon to the anger of Diana — and what crime in the Lapiths, what in Calydon, deserved so much? But I, the great wife of Jupiter, I who left nothing undared, who turned every way I could, I am beaten by Aeneas. Well, then: if my own power is not great enough, I will not hold back from begging help wherever it is. If I cannot bend the gods above, I will stir up Hell. I am not allowed to keep him from the throne of Latium — so be it; by the fates Lavinia stays his wife, unmoved. But I can drag it out, I can pile delay on these great events; I can grind down the peoples of both these kings. Let father-in-law and son-in-law come together at the cost of their own ruined nations. Your dowry, girl, will be Trojan and Rutulian blood, and Bellona stands ready to be your bridesmaid. The daughter of Cisseus was not the only one to carry a torch in her womb and bear a son who was a wedding-fire: Venus has a child of her own just the same, a second Paris, another deadly bridal torch for a Troy raised up again."

When she had said it, she dropped to earth, terrible, and called up Allecto, who breeds grief, from the home of the dread Furies, from the dark below — Allecto, whose heart is set on sad wars, on rage, on treachery and crime. Even her father Pluto hates her; her own sisters in Hell hate her; into so many shapes she shifts, so many cruel faces, so black with snakes. Juno spurred her on with these words: "Daughter of Night, do this one labor of your own for me, this one favor, so that my honor and my name are not crushed and broken, so the Trojans cannot win Latinus over with their marriage or settle on the soil of Italy. You can set brothers who love each other at each other's throats, turn a house upside down with hate, drive in the whip and the funeral torch; you have a thousand names, a thousand ways of doing harm. Shake out your teeming breast; break the peace they have made; sow the seeds of war. Let the young men want weapons, demand them, snatch them up all at once."

At once Allecto, soaked in the poison of the Gorgons, made first for Latium and the high roof of the Laurentine king, and settled on the quiet threshold of Amata's room. Amata was already burning, a woman's heart on fire with worry and anger over the coming of the Trojans and the marriage of Turnus. The goddess threw at her one snake out of her dark hair, drove it down into the queen's breast, into the deep heart of her, so that, maddened by the monster, she would set her whole house in an uproar. Sliding between her clothes and her smooth skin, the snake wound its way around her, felt by nothing, seen by nothing, and breathed its viper's breath into her without her knowing. The great serpent turns into the twisted gold of her necklace, turns into the long ribbon binding her hair, glides over her body, slips its smooth coils everywhere. And while the first sickness, the wet poison, was only working into her senses, touching her bones with fire before the flame had caught her whole spirit, she spoke softly at first, the way mothers do, weeping over her daughter's Phrygian marriage: "Lavinia to be given to Trojan exiles — is that it, father? Have you no pity for her, none for yourself? No pity for the mother that pirate will leave behind the first fair wind, putting out to sea and carrying the girl away? Wasn't it just so the Trojan shepherd worked his way into Sparta and led Leda's Helen off to the cities of Troy? What about your sworn word? What about your old love for your own people, and the right hand you gave so many times to Turnus, your kinsman? If you must have a son-in-law from a foreign nation, if that is fixed, if your father Faunus' command lies heavy on you — then I say every land is foreign that is free and not under our rule, and that is what the gods mean. And as for Turnus, if you trace his house back to its source, his fathers were Inachus and Acrisius — he comes from the heart of Mycenae."

When she had tried all this and saw it broke against Latinus, who stood firm, and when the snake's poison had sunk deep into her and run through her whole body, then the poor woman, hounded now by horrible visions, raged out of her mind through the great city. She was like a top under the lash, the toy the boys send spinning in a wide ring around an empty courtyard, bent to their game, driving it on with the cord; round it goes under the blows, and the children stand over it, lost in wonder, watching the boxwood whirl, and the whip gives it life. No slower than that she was carried through the heart of the city, through its fierce peoples. And more — pretending the madness of Bacchus, throwing herself into a worse, a wilder crime, she fled into the woods and hid her daughter in the leafy mountains, to rob the Trojans of the wedding and put off the marriage day. "Hail, Bacchus!" she screamed, "you alone are worthy of the girl! For you she takes up the soft thyrsus, for you she circles in the dance, for you she grows her sacred hair!" The rumor flew, and the same fury, lit by the same goad, drove all the mothers to leave their homes, their hearths and their decent rooms, and run searching for new places to live; they let the wind have their loosened hair, and filled the air with their wild crying, and dressed in skins, and carried vine-wrapped spears. Amata herself in the middle of them held a blazing pine torch high and sang the wedding song of Turnus and her daughter; she rolled her bloodshot eyes and broke out savagely: "Hear me, mothers of Latium, wherever you are! If there is any love left in your faithful hearts for poor Amata, if any care for a mother's rights, then loose the ribbons from your hair and join me in the rites!" So Allecto drove the queen this way and that with the Bacchic goad, through the woods, through the lairs of the wild beasts.

When she judged the first madness sharp enough, and saw Latinus' plan and his whole house thrown into confusion, the dark goddess lifted off at once on her dusky wings and flew to the walls of bold Turnus, the town they say Danaë built for her Acrisian colonists when the south wind carried her there. Our fathers once called the place Ardea; the great name of Ardea stands to this day, though its fortune is gone. There Turnus, in his high halls, was deep in sleep in the black middle of the night. Allecto stripped off her savage face and her Fury's limbs and shaped herself into an old woman: she ploughed her forehead with ugly wrinkles, put on white hair and a holy ribbon, and twined an olive sprig in it; she became old Calybe, the aged priestess of Juno's temple, and showed herself before his eyes, saying: "Turnus — will you stand for all your labor poured out for nothing, your throne handed over to Trojan settlers? The king is taking from you the bride your blood bought; a stranger is being sought as heir to the kingdom. Go on, then — be laughed at; throw yourself into danger no one thanks you for; go cut the Tuscan armies down; keep the peace and guard the Latins. Almighty Juno herself ordered me to tell you this to your face as you lie sleeping. So up — gladly — arm your men, march them out the gates onto the field; burn those painted ships of the Phrygian chiefs lying in the lovely river. The great power of the gods commands it. Let King Latinus himself feel it, unless he agrees to give you the bride and keep his word, and learns at last what an armed Turnus is."

And the young man, mocking the prophetess, answered her so: "You're wrong if you think the news that a fleet has come into the Tiber never reached me. Don't conjure up such terrors for me. Queen Juno has not forgotten me. But you, mother — old age, worn out and past the truth, is wearing you down with empty worry, making a fool of a prophetess with false fears among the wars of kings. Your business is the gods and their temples; leave war and peace to the men, whose work it is to make war."

At these words Allecto blazed into rage. A sudden trembling seized Turnus even as he spoke; his eyes froze; the Fury hissed with all her snakes, and her vast face opened. She rolled her burning eyes and shoved him back as he stumbled, groping for more to say; she reared two serpents up out of her hair, cracked her whip, and spoke through her raving mouth: "Here I am, then — worn out, past the truth, making a fool of myself among the wars of kings with empty terrors! Look at me. I come from the home of the dread Sisters, and I carry war and death in my hand." With that she hurled a torch at the young man and planted it, smoking with black light, deep in his chest. A monstrous terror broke his sleep; the sweat burst out and drenched his whole body to the bone. Out of his mind he shouted for his weapons, hunted for his sword by the bed and through the whole house. The lust for the blade raged in him, the criminal madness of war, and over it all his fury — the way a fire of crackling sticks roars up under the ribs of a boiling cauldron, and the water leaps with the heat, and the whole pot smokes and seethes and throws its foam high, till it can hold no more and the black steam flies up into the air. So, the peace broken, he ordered the chief men to march on King Latinus, ordered his weapons made ready, and swore to defend Italy and drive the enemy off her shores — he was a match, he cried, for Trojans and Latins both at once. When he had said it and called the gods to his vows, the Rutulians spurred each other to arms: this one drawn on by Turnus' splendid beauty and his youth, that one by his royal line, that one by the great deeds of his own right hand.

While Turnus filled the Rutulians with his daring, Allecto sped on her Stygian wings to the Trojans, and there she set new traps. She had spotted the place on the shore where handsome Iulus was hunting with hounds, running down the game; and there the Fury from the underworld threw a sudden frenzy into his dogs, brushing their noses with a scent they knew, to set them hot on the trail of a stag. This was the first cause of all the trouble; this lit the war in the country hearts.

There was a stag, a fine one, with great spreading antlers, that the sons of Tyrrheus had taken from its mother's milk and raised by hand — Tyrrheus, the king's chief herdsman, master of the royal cattle and the broad pastures. Their sister Silvia had trained the beast and made it all her own, weaving soft garlands for its horns, combing it, washing it in the clear spring. It came to her hand, gentle, knew its master's table, and wandered the woods all day only to come home at night, however late, to the door it knew. Now, far off from its home, swimming the river, cooling itself on the green bank, it was the stag Iulus' raging hounds first started. Iulus himself, fired with the hope of a fine prize, bent his bow and let an arrow fly; and the goddess was at his side, and did not let his hand waver — the shaft went whistling and tore through the belly and the flank. But the wounded beast fled back to the roof it knew, dragged itself groaning into the stall, and filled the whole house, bleeding and begging, with a sound like a person pleading. Silvia the sister, beating her arms, was first to cry out for help and call the tough farmers in. And they came faster than anyone thought — for the cruel goddess was lurking in the silent woods — one with a charred stake, one with a heavy knotted club; whatever each man's hand found, his anger turned into a weapon. Tyrrheus called them out, snatching up his ax where he had been splitting an oak in four with wedges, gasping with rage, and shaking it over his head.

And the goddess, watching from her high seat for the moment to do her harm, climbed to the steep roof of the stable and from the ridge she blew the herdsman's call, straining her hellish voice through the curved horn. The whole wood shuddered at it, the deep forest rang; far off the lake of Diana heard it, the white sulphur stream of the Nar heard it, and the springs of Velinus; and frightened mothers caught their children to their breasts. Then from everywhere, fast, at the sound of that dreadful trumpet, the rough farmers came running, snatching up their weapons; and the young Trojans, too, poured out of the open camp to back up Ascanius. The lines were drawn. This was no country brawl of clubs and burnt stakes now: they fought it out with the two-edged steel, and a dark harvest of bare swords bristled wide across the field, and the bronze flashed, struck by the sun, and threw its light up into the clouds — the way the sea begins to whiten under the first breath of wind, then little by little the swell lifts higher, until from its very floor it climbs straight to the sky. There, in the front rank, an arrow brought down young Almo, the eldest of Tyrrheus' sons: the wound stuck in his throat, and the blood choked the wet road of his voice and stopped the thin breath of his life. Many men fell around him, and among them old Galaesus, killed as he stepped between them to plead for peace — once the most upright of men, and the richest in all Ausonia: five flocks of sheep were his, five herds came home to his stalls, and a hundred ploughs turned his ground.

While the fight raged across the plain with neither side winning, Allecto, her promise kept, glutted now on war and the blood she had drawn, left Hesperia and rode up through the air to heaven, and spoke to Juno in the pride of victory: "There — discord made complete for you, ripened into bitter war. Now tell them to be friends, to make a treaty, now that I have splashed the Trojans with Italian blood! And I will do more than this, if you will only say the word: I will pull the neighboring towns into it with rumors, fan their hearts to a mad love of war, so help comes pouring in from every side, and I will sow the whole countryside with weapons." Then Juno: "There is fear enough now, and treachery enough. The reasons for war stand fast; they fight hand to hand, and the weapons chance first put in their hands are wet with fresh blood. Let them keep that marriage, that fine alliance — Venus' famous son and King Latinus. But the Father who rules high Olympus would not have you stray too freely under the open sky. Leave this place. Whatever the war still needs, I will see to myself." So spoke the daughter of Saturn; and Allecto lifted her hissing, snake-loud wings and left the heights, sinking back to her home below.

There is a place in the middle of Italy, deep under high mountains, famous, and named in many lands — the valley of Amsanctus. A dark wood crowds it close on both sides with its thick leaves, and through the center a roaring torrent thunders over the rocks in a whirling flood. Here a fearful cave is shown, the breathing-holes of cruel Dis, and a vast pit where Acheron bursts open and turns up its plague-bringing throat; and into it the Fury, that hated power, plunged and hid herself, and gave the sky and the earth relief.

And all this while the daughter of Saturn was putting her last touches to the war. The whole crowd of shepherds rushed back from the fight into the city, carrying their dead — young Almo, and Galaesus with his ruined face — and they cried out to the gods, and called on Latinus. Turnus was there, and in the middle of the uproar over the killing, the burning, he doubled their terror: Trojans, he said, were being called to share the kingdom, a mongrel Phrygian breed grafted on, and he himself thrown out the door. Then the ones whose mothers were leaping in the Bacchic dance through the trackless woods — for Amata's name carried weight — gathered from all sides and clamored for war, wore themselves out demanding it. And against every omen, against the will of fate, with the gods turned against them, they all cried out for an unholy war. They crowded around the palace of Latinus, fighting to get at the king. But he held firm, a rock in the sea, a sea-rock that stands when the great crash comes, holding its own weight while the waves howl all around it; the cliffs and the foaming reefs roar in vain, and the weed flung against its side washes off again. But when he had no power to beat down their blind resolve, and the cruel will of Juno had its way, the old man called the gods and the empty air to witness again and again: "We are broken by fate," he cried, "swept off by the storm. You poor fools — you will pay for this with your own cursed blood. As for you, Turnus, a bitter punishment is coming, and you will pray to the gods too late. For me, my rest is won, my harbor in sight; I lose only the happiness of dying in peace." He said no more, but shut himself inside his walls and let the reins of state fall from his hands.

There was a custom in Latium of the West, which the Alban cities held sacred ever after, and which mighty Rome holds to this day, when men first rouse the war-god to battle — whether they make ready to carry grief and tears to the Getae, or the Arabs, or the Hyrcanians, or to march against the dawn and the Indians, and win back their lost standards from the Parthians. There are twin gates of War — that is their name — held in awe and dreaded for the savage god behind them; a hundred bars of bronze, the everlasting iron, lock them, and Janus never leaves the threshold, the watchman at the doors. When the elders have firmly decided on war, the consul himself, in the robe and the war-belt of Quirinus, swings the grinding hinges back and calls the men to battle, and all the army roars after him, and the brazen trumpets blow their rough agreement. By this rite Latinus too should have declared war on the men of Aeneas and unbarred the grim gates. But the old father would not lay his hand to them; he turned away from the hateful office and hid himself in the dark. Then the queen of the gods herself came gliding down from heaven, drove the lingering gates open with her own hand, and as she turned the hinge burst the iron bars of War. Italy, peaceful and still a moment before, took fire. Some made ready to march out on foot across the plains; some thundered up on their high horses in clouds of dust. Every man wanted his weapons. They rubbed the bright shields and the smooth spearheads slick with fat, ground the axes on the stone; they loved to lift the standards now and hear the trumpets call. Five great cities set up their anvils and forged new weapons — strong Atina, proud Tibur, Ardea, Crustumerium, and Antemnae crowned with towers. They hammered out helmets to guard the head, bent the willow into wicker shields; some beat out breastplates of bronze, or smooth greaves of soft silver. The honor of the plough-blade and the sickle, all the love of the field, gave way; they melted their fathers' swords down and forged them new. The trumpets sounded. The watchword went out to muster. One man snatched his helmet from the wall in a fury; another harnessed his stamping horses, pulled on his coat of gold-linked mail and his trusty sword, and buckled on his shield.

Now, Muses, open Helicon and start the song — tell which kings were stirred to war, what armies followed each one and filled the plains, what men even then flowered in Italy, that kind mother of harvests, what weapons burned in their hands. For you remember, goddesses, and you have the power to tell; to us only the faint breath of the story comes down. First into the war, fierce, came Mezentius from the Tuscan shore, a man who scorned the gods, and armed his men. Beside him his son Lausus, finest of them all but for the body of Turnus the Laurentine — Lausus, the breaker of horses and the master of game, who led a thousand men out of the city of Agylla, in vain; a son who deserved a happier father than Mezentius, and a father better than Mezentius to obey.

After them Aventinus, the beautiful son of beautiful Hercules, showed his palm-crowned chariot and his horses crowned for victory across the field, and wore his father's emblem on his shield — the hundred serpents and the snake-haired Hydra. The priestess Rhea bore him in secret, out in the woods on the Aventine hill, a woman lying with a god, after Hercules came triumphant from killing Geryon and touched the fields of Laurentum and bathed the Spanish cattle in the Tuscan river. His men carried javelins and cruel pikes into battle, and fought with the tapered sword and the Sabine spit. He himself went on foot, a great lion's skin swinging over his shoulders, its bristling pelt and its white teeth crowning his head — so he strode into the king's hall, savage, wrapped in the gear of Hercules.

Then two brothers left the walls of Tibur — the people take their name from their brother Tiburtus — Catillus and brave Coras, young men of Argos, and they rode into the thick of the spears in the front rank, like two cloud-born Centaurs coming down from a high mountain, leaving Homole and snowy Othrys behind them at a run; the great forest gives way as they go, and the thickets break apart with a crash before them.

Nor was the founder of Praeneste missing — Caeculus, the king whom every age has believed was born of Vulcan among the country herds and found beside the hearth. A long file of farmers follows him, gathered from high Praeneste and the fields of Juno of Gabii, from the cold river Anio and the dewy rocks of the Hernici, from rich Anagnia and from you, Father Amasenus. Not all of them have weapons or shields or rattling chariots; most of them sling bullets of dull lead, some carry a pair of darts apiece, and for caps they wear tawny wolfskin over their heads. The left foot goes bare as they march; the right is shod in raw oxhide. And Messapus, tamer of horses, son of Neptune, whom no man may bring down with fire or steel, called up suddenly to arms a people long unused to war, peoples that had forgotten the sword, and drew his blade again. He led the ranks of Fescennium and the just Faliscans, the men who hold Soracte's heights and the fields of Flavinium and Ciminus' lake and hill and the groves of Feronia. They marched in order, singing their king's praises as they went — the way the snow-white swans, among the streaming clouds, lift their song out of their soft throats coming home from feeding, and the Asian marsh far off rings with the answering cry. No one would have thought those columns an army of bronze; you would have called them a great cloud of harsh-voiced birds driving in off the deep sea toward the shore.

And look — Clausus, of the old Sabine blood, brought a great army with him, and was an army in himself; from him today the Claudian tribe and house is spread through Latium, since Rome was shared with the Sabines. With him came a vast cohort of Amiternum, the old men of Cures, all the men of Eretum and olive-bearing Mutusca; men who live in the town of Nomentum, in the Rosean country by Velinus, on the crags of rough Tetrica and Mount Severus, in Casperia and Foruli and along the river Himella; men who drink the Tiber and the Fabaris, men sent from cold Nursia, the squadrons of Horta and the Latin peoples, and the men the river Allia cut through with its unlucky name. As many as the waves that roll on the Libyan sea when fierce Orion sinks into the winter water, as thick as the ears of grain scorched ripe by the new sun on the Hermus plain or the yellow fields of Lycia — so many were they; their shields rang, and the ground shook, struck and trampled under their feet.

Next Halaesus, born of Agamemnon's line and an enemy to the very name of Troy, yoked his horses to the chariot and swept a thousand fierce peoples to Turnus' aid: the men who turn the Massic soil that is good for wine, and the men the Auruncan fathers sent down from the high hills, and their Sidicine neighbors from the plain, and the men of Cales, and the men who live by the shallow river Volturnus, and beside them the rough Saticulan and the Oscan ranks. Smooth round javelins are their weapons, but they fasten them with a strap to bring them back; their left arm carries a small shield, and they fight close in with the curved sword.

Nor will you go unsung in our verse, Oebalus — son, they say, of Telon and the nymph Sebethis, when Telon, old already, was king of Capreae; but his son, not content with his father's small kingdom, had spread his sway over the people of the Sarrastes and the plains the Sarnus waters, and over those who hold Rufrae and Batulum and the fields of Celemna, and the men under the apple-laden walls of high Abella. They throw the spear in the Teutonic way; for caps they strip the cork-tree's bark; their crescent shields flash bronze, and the sword flashes bronze.

And the mountains of Nursia sent you to the war, Ufens, famous and lucky in arms; your people the Aequi, hardest of all, hunting the woods always with their lances, scratching their stubborn ground in armor — it pleased them to live by plunder and on the spoils of the hunt. The priest Umbro came too, of the Marsi, sent by King Archippus, his helmet bound with sprigs of the blessed olive — a brave man, who with a charm and a touch of the hand could lay the snake-tribe and the foul-breathing water-serpents to sleep, soothe their fury and cure their bites with his art. But he found no charm against the wound of a Trojan spear-point; his sleep-songs and the herbs gathered on the Marsian hills were no use against it. For him the grove of Angitia wept, and the glassy water of Fucinus, and the clear lakes.

Virbius went out too, the beautiful son of Hippolytus — sent to the war by Aricia his mother, raised in the wet groves of Egeria, where the rich altar of Diana stands kind to those who pray. The story goes that Hippolytus, when his stepmother's cunning destroyed him and he paid his father's anger with his blood, torn apart by his terrified horses, came back into the high air, under the stars, brought back to life by the herbs of the Healer and the love of Diana. Then the all-powerful Father, furious that any mortal should rise from the shadows of the dead into the light of life, struck the finder of that medicine, Apollo's son, down with his thunderbolt into the waters of the Styx. But kind Diana hid Hippolytus away in a secret place and gave him to the nymph Egeria and her grove, where he could live out his life alone and unknown in the Italian woods, and where, his name changed, they called him Virbius. That is why hoofed horses are kept far from the temple and the sacred grove of Diana, ever since they bolted in terror at the sea-monster and threw the young man and his chariot along the shore. His son drove fiery horses across the plain all the same, and rode his chariot to war.

And among them, in the front rank, Turnus himself moved, taller by a head than them all, gripping his weapons. His high helmet, with its triple plume, carried a Chimaera breathing Etna's fire from its mouth; the worse the battle raged and ran red, the more it roared with its savage flames. And on his polished shield was Io, lifting her gilded horns, already turned to a heifer, already covered in bristling hide — a great theme — and Argus her guard, and her father Inachus pouring his river from a carved urn. A cloud of foot soldiers came behind him, and shield-bearing columns filled the whole plain: the young men of Argos, the Auruncan bands, the Rutulians, the old Sicanians, the Sacranian ranks, the Labici with their painted shields; men who plough your woods, Tiber, and the holy bank of Numicus; men whose ploughshare works the Rutulian hills and Circe's headland, the fields where Jupiter of Anxur watches over them and Feronia rejoices in her green grove; men from the black marsh of Satura and the cold valley where the Ufens looks for his way to the sea and sinks at last under it.

And last of all came Camilla, of the Volscian people, leading her column of cavalry, her squadrons bright with bronze — a warrior. She had never trained her woman's hands to the distaff or the wool-baskets of Minerva; she was a girl made to bear the hard work of battle and to outrun the wind. She could have flown over the standing grain at the top of the field and never bruised the tender ears as she ran; she could have skimmed the swell of the open sea and held her course, and never wet her flying feet in the water. The whole countryside poured out of the houses and the fields to watch her pass, the young men and the crowd of mothers, and stared in wonder, struck dumb, as she went by — the royal purple draped over her smooth shoulders, a brooch of gold catching up her hair, and on her back the Lycian quiver and the shepherd's myrtle staff tipped with a spear.
