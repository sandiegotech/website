The Aeneid

Virgil

  • 29–19 BCE
  • Western tradition
  • Epic poem
  • Lectio Edition 2026

Virgil's epic of Rome. Aeneas carries his father and his household gods out of burning Troy and goes to found the people who will become Rome. It is a poem about duty and about what duty costs.

Why this editionGoal and purpose

Dryden is a monument. He is not an on-ramp.

The public-domain Aeneid is John Dryden's verse of 1697. It is one of the great English translations, and it is in couplets more than three centuries old. Dryden also paraphrases freely. "I fear the Greeks even when they bring gifts" becomes "Trust not their presents, nor admit the horse".

The Lectio Edition gives the twelve books as prose in current English. It keeps Virgil's names and keeps each famous line at its own length and shape. Dryden stays in the app beside it.

The openingFrom the Lectio Edition

How it begins.

Book I · The Storm, and Carthage

Arms, and the man, I sing — the man who, driven by fate, was first to flee the shores of Troy and come to Italy and its Lavinian coast. He was battered hard over sea and land by the power of the gods, for cruel Juno's unforgetting rage hounded him at every turn; and bitter were the wars he fought before he could found his city and carry his gods into Latium. From him came the Latin people, the Alban fathers, and the high walls of Rome.

Muse, tell me the causes. What wound to her divinity, what burning grievance, drove the queen of heaven to force a man so faithful, so devoted, through such a gauntlet of disaster — to hurl one trouble after another in his path? Can there really be such anger in the hearts of gods?

There was an ancient city, Carthage, a colony out of Tyre, facing Italy far across the water, opposite the mouth of the Tiber — a city rich in resources and fierce in the arts of war. They say Juno loved it above all places on earth, more even than Samos. Here she kept her armor; here she kept her chariot. And it was her fixed ambition, if fate would only allow it, that this city should one day rule the world.

The choicesPolicies and divergences

What this edition decided.

The review set these rules and applied them across the twelve books.

  • Names are the ones Virgil gives, in his Latin forms: Minerva, never Athena. Tartarus, Erebus and Dis, where the draft had put "hell". No name is added that Virgil leaves out, and none is dropped.
  • No oaths to one God in a poem with many gods. A character swears by Jupiter or simply cries out.
  • No modern idiom that calls up something outside the poem. The draft had "gone with the wind" and "for free".
  • The poet's narration does not use contractions. Direct speech may, when the speaker is talking in the moment. Prayers, oaths and prophecy do not.
  • Famous lines keep their own length and shape.
  • The last line of Book XI and the last line of the poem are the same line in Latin, and are now the same in English.

Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 36 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 7. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

pietas

Throughout

Older
"piety", "pious Aeneas"
Lectio
"duty", "dutiful Aeneas"

Pietas is what a man owes to gods, country and family. "Piety" now means religious feeling. "Duty" is what drives Aeneas, and what the poem counts the cost of.

timeo Danaos et dona ferentis

Book II, line 49

Older
"Trust not their presents, nor admit the horse" (Dryden)
Lectio
I fear the Greeks even when they come bearing gifts

The line is a proverb in English already. The edition keeps it close to the shape people know.

agnosco veteris vestigia flammae

Book IV, line 23

Older
"Too like the sparkles of my former flame" (Dryden)
Lectio
I recognize the marks of the old flame

Dido admits she loves again. Vestigia are traces, footprints, a scar. Dryden's "sparkles" is prettier than the Latin.

hoc opus, hic labor est

Book VI, lines 126 to 129

Older
"In this the task and mighty labour lies" (Dryden)
Lectio
that is the labor, that is the task

Anyone can go down. Coming back is the hard part. The descent to the underworld is built on that contrast, and the doubled close is kept.

parcere subiectis et debellare superbos

Book VI, lines 851 to 853

Older
"To tame the proud, the fetter'd slave to free" (Dryden)
Lectio
to spare the conquered, and to crush the proud

Virgil's statement of what Rome is for. Dryden softens the second half. The edition leaves the cost where Virgil put it.

flectere si nequeo superos, Acheronta movebo

Book VII, line 312

Older
"Hell shall the pow'r of Heav'n and Jove supply" (Dryden)
Lectio
If I cannot bend the gods above, I will stir up Hell

Juno's vow in two plain halves. Note that this line still says "Hell", against the edition's own rule on names. It is on the editor's list below.

vitaque cum gemitu fugit indignata sub umbras

Book XII, line 952

Older
"And the disdainful soul came rushing through the wound" (Dryden)
Lectio
and life fled with a groan, resentful, down to the shades

The last line. The poem ends on the kill, with no funeral and no peace. The same line closes Camilla's death in Book XI and is rendered the same way there.

Still open

What the first review left for the editor to decide or restore.

  • The editor's own read of all twelve books.
  • Books II and III are Aeneas telling his own story. They still have contractions. Decide whether his tale follows the rule for speech or for narration.
  • "Hell" is still in Book VII in three places, and one note argues for it. The rule on names says otherwise.
  • Three of the edition's notes quote wording the text no longer has, including the notes on the first line and on sunt lacrimae rerum. They need rewriting.
  • Prayers and speeches of state in Books IX to XII have not been checked one by one for contractions.
