# The Aeneid, Book VIII

Evander, and the Shield of Aeneas

When Turnus ran up the war-flag on the towers of Laurentum, and the harsh trumpets blared, and he lashed his eager horses and clashed his weapons, every heart was stirred at once. All Latium rose in a panic and swore itself to the cause; the young men raged for the fight. Their chief captains — Messapus, Ufens, and Mezentius, who scorned the gods — drew their forces from every side and stripped the wide fields of their farmhands for soldiers. And they sent Venulus to the city of Diomedes to beg for help, to tell him the Trojans had landed in Latium, that Aeneas had come with his beaten fleet and his exiled gods and now claimed Italy as his own, saying fate had given it to him; that many peoples were rallying to the man, and his name was spreading through the land. What he was building toward, what disaster might follow if luck went his way — Diomedes could weigh that better than Turnus or King Latinus could.

So it stood with the Latins. And the Trojan hero saw the whole storm coming and was tossed on a great tide of worry. His mind darted one way, then another, snatched at this plan and that, turned the trouble over and over and could find no rest — the way light off water in a bronze basin, thrown back by the sun or the bright face of the moon, flickers and skips across a whole room, leaps up of a sudden and trembles on the high paneled ceiling. It was night, and across the wide world a tired sleep held the birds of the air and the beasts; and Aeneas, his heart heavy with the thought of war, lay down on the bank of the Tiber under the cold sky and gave his body, late, to rest.

Then the god of the place himself, old Tiberinus of the lovely river, seemed to rise up among the poplar leaves; fine linen, sea-green, draped him, and a crown of shadowy reeds covered his hair. And he spoke, and his words took the grief away: "Seed of the line of gods, you who carry Troy back to us out of the hands of her enemies, who keep her citadel alive forever — this is the soil you were promised, this the home that has waited for you; do not turn back. The anger of the gods has settled; the swelling rage is spent. And so you will not think a dream has made all this up out of nothing: under the oaks along the shore you will find a great white sow lying with her thirty young, white like her, gathered at her teats. This is the place your city will stand. This is the sure end of your labors. From this sign, when thirty years have rolled around, your son Ascanius will found a city of shining name — Alba.

"What I tell you is true. Now hear how to come through what is coming. An Arcadian people, the sons of Pallas, who followed King Evander here when he was driven from his own land, have chosen a spot in these hills and built a town and called it Pallanteum, after their forefather Pallas. They are forever at war with the Latins. Take these men into your camp as allies; bind a treaty with them. I myself will lead you up my banks and straight along my stream, so your oars can drive against the current. Rise up, son of Venus, and as the first stars are setting, make your prayers to Juno, as the rite demands, and beat down her anger and her threats with vows of submission. When you have won your war, pay me what is owed. I am the one you see brushing these banks with my full flood, cutting through the rich farmland — the dark Tiber, the river heaven loves best. Here is my great house; my source rises among lofty cities." So the river-god spoke, and sank down into the deep pool, and the night and the dream both let Aeneas go.

He rose, and looked up, and saw the dawn flushing the sky. He cupped water from the Tiber in his hands, the rite's own way, and poured out his prayers to heaven: "You nymphs of Laurentum, you who feed the rivers, and you, father Tiber, with your sacred stream — take Aeneas in at last, and keep him from harm. Wherever your waters rise, whatever spring or pool holds them hidden, you who pity our long suffering, you king of the horned floods — I will honor you, I will keep your name with offerings forever. Only be with my people; make good the promise you made." So he prayed; then he picked two galleys from his fleet, fitted them with oars, and armed his men. And then — a thing to marvel at — there on the green bank lay the sow, white, with her white young around her teats, just as the god had said. Dutiful Aeneas took her and her whole brood and laid them on Juno's altar; to you, great Juno, to you he made the sacrifice.

All that long night the Tiber smoothed his swelling stream; he flowed back on himself and held still, hushing his water to the calm of a standing pool, a quiet marsh, so that there was no fight against the oar. The Trojans pushed off and made their way, and the ship slid over the shallows almost without effort; the woods and the waters marveled at the strange sight — the shields flashing, the painted hulls afloat on a river. One whole night and a day they rowed on, up through the green shade, and cut the still water. The sun had climbed to the height of noon when they saw, far off, walls and a fort and a scatter of rooftops — what Roman power has since raised level with the sky, but which then was only Evander's small holding, a poor man's estate. They turned their prows for the shore. It happened that the Arcadian king, that very day, was paying the rite he owed to great Hercules, the son of Amphitryon, and to all the gods, in a grove outside the town. His son Pallas was with him, and the chief young men, and his poor senate; they were burning incense, and the warm blood still smoked on the altars.

But when they saw the tall ships gliding up through the shadowed trees, the Arcadians sprang to their feet, alarmed, and broke off the feast. Bold Pallas would not have the rites abandoned; he snatched up a spear and ran alone to meet the strangers, and called from a rise of ground: "Men — what brings you down these unknown waters? Where are you bound? What is your race, what is your home? Do you come in peace, or with war?" High on the stern, father Aeneas stood and held out a branch of peace-bearing olive in his hand: "You see men born of Troy, and weapons that are no friend to the Latins, who drove us to war though we came in peace. We are looking for Evander. Tell him this: the leaders of Troy have come asking for an alliance." Pallas was struck dumb at so great a name. "Whoever you are, come ashore," he said. "Come, speak to my father face to face, and be our guest." He gave the prince his hand and led him up from the water into the grove.

There Aeneas spoke first to the king: "Best of the Greeks, to you fate has sent me holding these branches of peace. I came without fear, though I know your blood is Greek, and your line runs back to Atreus and the two kings of his house. My own worth, your great name, the old kinship of our fathers, and the word of Apollo brought me here — by choice, not only by need. Our founder Dardanus, born of Electra, came from the loins of Atlas, the same Atlas who holds the sky on his shoulder; and your father is Mercury, whom Maia bore on the cold height of Cyllene — Maia, Atlas's daughter. So our two houses spring from one source. Trusting that, I sent no envoy ahead, I asked no leave; I came myself, my own messenger, with my life in my hands. The same Rutulians who hunt us hunt you too. If they drive us out, nothing will stop them from holding all Italy from sea to sea. Take our hand, and give us yours. We have stout hearts and strong arms for the war; we have been tried, and we have held."

Aeneas finished. All the while he spoke, Evander had been watching his face and his eyes, taking in the whole of him. Now he answered, short and warm: "How gladly I take you in, bravest of the Trojans! How your face brings back your father's — I can still see Anchises in your every look and gesture. I remember it well: Priam came once through this country, going to visit his sister's court, and Anchises came with him. I was only a boy, with the first down on my cheeks, and I marveled at all those great men; but Anchises towered over the rest, and my young heart burned to take his hand. I did, in the end; I brought him home with me, and when he had to go he gave me a fine Lycian quiver, a Cretan bow, an embroidered cloak, and two golden bridles my son's horses still wear. So the alliance you ask, I give. Tomorrow, when the sun is up, I will send you off with men. For now, honor our yearly feast with us — these are rites no delay should keep us from — and take your place at a humble table among friends." He had the cups and the food brought back, and seated the Trojans himself on the grass; for Aeneas alone he spread a lion's shaggy hide over a maple chair. They ate roasted meat and bread, and the priest poured the wine.

When the worst of the hunger was eased, Evander said: "These rites of ours, this altar, this feast — they are not some empty superstition, nor blind habit. We keep them because we were saved, and we are grateful, and we pay back what a god did for us. Look at that crag over there, hung against the sky, with the heaps of rubble at its foot and the cave standing bare and ruined. That was once the den of Cacus, a half-man, half-beast, a thing the sun never reached. The ground at his door was always wet with fresh slaughter, and men's faces hung nailed to the posts, pale with rotting gore. His father was Vulcan, and like his father he breathed black smoke and gouts of fire as he dragged his huge bulk around. But the time came at last that brought us a god. Hercules came through, in triumph, having killed the three-bodied giant Geryon in Spain and driven off his great herd of cattle; and he turned them loose to graze here by the Tiber.

"But Cacus, mad for plunder and ready for any crime, made off with four prize bulls and as many heifers — and so there would be no tracks pointing the right way, he dragged them backward by the tail into his cave, the hoofprints all turned around, and shut them in the dark rock. Whoever came searching, the signs led him nowhere. By and by Hercules was moving his well-fed herd on to fresh pasture, and as the cattle bellowed at leaving, one heifer answered from deep in the cave and lowed, and that single penned-up call gave Cacus away. Hercules blazed with a black, savage rage. He caught up his knotted club and ran for the high mountain — and then, for the first time, we saw the monster afraid, fear in his eyes, his face gone white, faster on his feet than the wind. He threw himself down into his hole and slammed the great rock door his father had hung on iron chains, snapped the chains, and dropped it home.

"But there came the Avenger, raging round the cave, turning his eyes this way and that, gnashing his teeth. Three times, hot with anger, he circled the whole of the Aventine; three times he heaved at the cave-mouth and failed and fell back, panting. There was a spike of black flint at the back of the mountain, sheer, a roost for birds of ill omen, leaning out over the river. Hercules braced his feet on the right, took hold of it, and wrenched — and the rock tore loose from its roots, and the sky thundered at the shock, and the banks leapt apart, and the terrified river ran backward. The whole court of Cacus stood open to the day; the dark den glared in the new light, the way the earth might crack and lay the house of the dead bare to heaven, and the ghosts shrink back, hating the sun that has broken in on their night. Caught in the open, with no way out, the monster howled an unmanly howl, and Hercules rained stones and spears down on him. Cacus, with nowhere left, belched a vast cloud of smoke from his throat — a thing to wonder at — and wrapped the cave in a blind black murk, so no eye could find him. But the god leapt down through it, through the fume, where the smoke rolled thickest, half-blind, half-choked, and groped his way, and got his hands on the beast. He twisted him into a knot and clung and choked him till the eyes burst from his head and the throat ran dry of blood. Then he tore the doors open and let in the day, and dragged out the stolen cattle and the foul carcass by the feet, and the whole countryside came to gape at the giant's shaggy chest and his terrible eyes and the mouth that breathed fire no more. From that day we have kept the rite, and Potitius first set up these yearly vows, with the house of the Pinarii to keep them. So crown your heads, young men, fill the cups, and call together on the god who keeps us safe."

When he had said this, the evening star was already climbing the sky. The priests went in procession, Potitius at their head, wrapped in the skins of beasts and carrying torches; they laid the second course on the tables and heaped the platters at the altar. The leaping Salii came to sing around the kindled fires, their temples bound with poplar — one choir of old men, one of the young — and they sang the deeds of Hercules: how as a baby in his cradle he strangled the two serpents Juno sent; how he broke Troy and Oechalia; the thousand trials Eurystheus and Juno's hate piled on him; how his unbeaten hands brought down the cloud-born Centaurs, the Cretan bull, the great lion, the Hydra of Lerna with its hissing heads; how the doorkeeper of Orcus trembled before him in his bloody cave, and the rivers of the dead shook at the sight of him. "Hail, true son of Jupiter, glory added to the gods! Come to us and to your rites with a kind step, an answering grace." So they sang, and over all the rest they crowned the feast with the den and the death of Cacus, and the hollow valleys carried the sound, and the hills threw it back.

Then, the rites done, they all walked back toward the town, and the old king, weighed down with years, walked with Aeneas and his son on either side of him, easing the road with talk. Aeneas turned his eyes everywhere, taken with each new thing, and asked, glad and curious, about all of it — the relics, the works of the men of old. And Evander, founder of what would be the towers of Rome, said: "These woods were the home of native Fauns and Nymphs, and of a race of men sprung from tree-trunks and hard oak, who had no law, no settled ways, who could not yoke an ox or store what they had; the wild hunt fed them, and the running streams. Then Saturn came, fleeing the weapons of Jupiter, an exile from his stolen kingdom. He gathered that scattered, hill-bred people, gave them laws, and chose the name Latium for the land — the land where he had lain hidden, safe. Under his reign were the ages men call golden, and he ruled the peoples in deep peace, until little by little a baser, tarnished age came on, with the madness of war and the hunger to have. Then the Ausonians came, and the Sicanians, and the land changed its name many times over, and harsh kings ruled it, the giant Thybris among them, who gave his name to our river, the Tiber, that men once called Albula. And I came last of all, driven from my own country across the seas, sent here by all-powerful Fortune and the word of heaven, on my mother the nymph's warning."

So talking he went on, and showed him the gate the Romans would one day call Carmental, after old Carmentis the prophetess, who first sang of the great sons of Aeneas to come and of noble Pallanteum. He showed him the wide grove Romulus would make his sanctuary, and the Lupercal under the cold rock, named in the old Arcadian way for Lycaean Pan; he showed him the wood of the Argiletum, and told the tale of his murdered guest Argus. From there he led him up to the Tarpeian height and the Capitol — golden now, but in those days rough and bristling with woodland thickets. Even then a dread holiness about the place frightened the country folk; even then they trembled at the wood and the rock. "Some god lives in this grove, on this leafy hill," Evander said, "though no one knows which. The Arcadians believe they have seen Jupiter himself, shaking the dark storm-cloud of his shield in his right hand and calling up the rain." Then he showed him two towns with their walls thrown down, the wreckage and memorials of the men of old: Janus had founded the one, Saturn the other, and they were called Janiculum and Saturnia. Talking so between them, they came up to the house of poor Evander, and everywhere they went they saw cattle lowing — in the Roman Forum, and in the fine quarter of the Carinae. At the door he said: "Hercules himself, in his triumph, stooped under this lintel; this little house took him in. Dare, my guest, to think nothing of wealth; shape yourself, as he did, into a man worthy of a god, and do not come to our poor table looking down on it." And he led great Aeneas in under the low roof and laid him on a bed of strewn leaves and a Libyan bear's hide.

Night came down and folded the earth in her dark wings. But Venus, a mother frightened for her son, and not without cause, shaken by the Laurentines' threats and the fierce uprising, lay close beside Vulcan in their golden bed, and breathed the charm of love into every word: "While the Greeks and cruel fate were leveling the towers of Troy, I asked you for nothing, no help to save the doomed, no work of your skill — though I owed much to Priam's house, and grieved hard for all Aeneas suffered. But now, by Jupiter's command and the will of fate, his line is set to reign in Italy; and so I come to you, a mother kneeling for her son, to beg the art that is yours. Thetis once moved you to forge arms, and the Dawn for her son; now look how many fierce nations are bound together against what is left of Troy, what walls behind shut gates are sharpening the sword against me, to destroy my people." So she spoke, and folded her snow-white arms around him as he held back, and warmed him into yielding. He took the old flame at once; the familiar heat went into his marrow and ran through his loosened bones, the way a thread of fire darts through the storm-clouds in a flash of thunder. The goddess felt it, and knew her beauty had won, and smiled.

Then Vulcan, helpless under her charm, said: "Why look so far for reasons? Where has your faith in me gone, your faith in your own beauty? Long ago, if you had asked, I could have armed your Trojans — neither Jupiter nor fate forbade Troy ten more years. And now, if you mean to make war, whatever care my craft can promise, whatever can be made of iron or of molten gold, all that fire and the breath of the bellows can do — it is yours. Stop pleading as if you doubted your own power." And with that he took the willing goddess in his arms and gave himself to a deep and easy sleep. Then, when the first rest had passed and the night had run half its course — the hour when a poor housewife wakes to wake the embers and rouse her maids to the wool and the loom, working by lamplight to keep her household fed and her marriage-bed honest — at that same hour the Lord of the Fire rose from his soft couch to his work.

There is an island that rises sheer with smoking rocks between Sicily and Aeolian Lipari, and beneath it the caves of Aetna, hollowed out for the Cyclopes' forges, thunder. This is Vulcan's house, and the land bears his name. Here he came down through the dark air. The Cyclopes were working the iron in the vast cave — Brontes and Steropes and Pyracmon with his bare arms. They had a thunderbolt half-shaped in their hands, polished in part — one of the many Jupiter hurls down on the world from every quarter of the sky. Three spokes of twisted hail they had put into it, three of rain-cloud, three of red fire, three of the winged South Wind; and now they were working in the terrible flashes, and the noise, and the dread, and the anger with its hounding flames. Elsewhere others were mending the war-chariot of Mars, the wheels he drives to rouse men and cities; and others burnished the dreadful shield of Minerva, the scaly snakes of gold, the writhing serpents, and the Gorgon's severed head with its rolling, dying eyes.

"Clear it all away," Vulcan called. "Set your tasks aside, Cyclopes of Aetna, and turn your minds to this: arms for a brave man. Now you need all your strength, all your speed, all the skill of your hands. Lose no time." That was all; and they bent at once to the work, sharing it out evenly. The bronze ran in streams, and gold, and the deadly steel melted in the great furnace. They shaped a vast shield, a single shield to stand against all the weapons of the Latins, and welded its seven rounds together. Some pumped the air in the wheezing bellows; some dipped the hissing bronze in the trough; the cave groaned under the anvils. They lifted their arms in turn, in time, and turned the glowing mass with the gripping tongs.

While the Lord of Lemnos drove the work on, the kindly morning and the song of the small birds woke Evander in his low house. The old man got up, drew on his tunic and tied his sandals, slung his good sword at his side, threw a panther's hide over his shoulder, and went out, two dogs going before him at the door, to find his royal guest. He had not forgotten the help he had promised, and he met Aeneas already coming to meet him, with young Pallas at his side and faithful Achates at the Trojan's. They clasped hands and sat down apart, and Evander spoke first: "Greatest of the Trojans — while you live I will never believe the cause of Troy is lost. But the help I can give from my own hand is small. On this side the Tiber pens us in; on that, the Latins press our walls and waste our fields. But I mean to join you with mighty nations, a camp rich with kingdoms — a chance fortune has dropped in your way. Not far from here stands an old and famous town, Agylla, that the Lydian people built long ago among the hills; the Greeks called it Caere. It flourished a long time, until the cruel Mezentius seized the crown and ruled it with a heavy, brutal hand.

"How can I tell you the unspeakable murders, the savage deeds of that tyrant? May the gods turn them back on his own head and his children's! He bound dead men to the living — fastened them hand to hand and face to face, a new kind of torture — and so, in that foul embrace, dripping with rot and gore, he killed his victims by a long, slow dying. At last the people had enough; they took up arms and surrounded his palace, cut down his men, and fired the house, and he broke through the crowd and fled in the dark to Turnus, who took him in. So all Etruria has risen in a just rage and demands her king back for punishment, and stands ready to sail; and over these thousands I will set you in command. Their ships crowd the shore, the men cry to weigh anchor — but an old soothsayer holds them back, chanting the fates: 'Lydian chiefs, flower of the old Etruscan stock, whom a just anger arms against Mezentius — no man of Italy may lead so great a people. Look for a foreign captain.' So they wait, awed by the word, on the beach, longing for their promised leader. Tarchon, the Etruscan chief, has sent me their crown and their royal robes, asking me to take the throne and lead them. But the cold blood of age, and my tired limbs, and a heart that knows its own decline, forbid me a crown now. I would put my son forward — but his mother is a Sabine, and half his blood is of this land. You are the man: a foreign line and a man's full strength, called by fate and by fortune both. Take him with you, my Pallas, the staff and hope of my last years; let him learn war at your side, his eyes on your courage. I give him two hundred horse, my own, the best I have, and Pallas adds two hundred more in his own name."

He had hardly finished when Aeneas and faithful Achates dropped their eyes, and a heavy grief came over them — short of help, and braced for so hard a war — until Venus sent a sign from a clear sky: a thunderclap rolled three times overhead, and the lightning flashed three times, and the blast of a war-trumpet rang from the air. They looked up; and again and again the peals came; and there, in the still bright heaven, arms began to shine through a cloud and to clash, a metal music. The rest stood trembling, struck with awe; but Aeneas knew the sign for his own. "Do not be afraid," he said to Evander. "This is for me. Heaven is calling me to the war. My goddess mother foretold she would send this sign if war came on, and bring me arms forged by Vulcan through the air to help me. What slaughter waits, now, for the wretched Latins! What a price Turnus will pay! How many bodies and shields and helmets you will roll down to the sea, father Tiber! Let them break the treaty. Let them call for war."

When he had said this he rose, and first he woke the dying fire on Hercules's altar and gladly sought again the household gods he had honored the day before; and Evander and the Trojans together offered their chosen sheep. Then Aeneas went down to his ships and his men, and picked out the best of them to go with him to war, while the rest drifted down the easy current to bring Ascanius news of his father and of how things stood. The Trojans who would ride to the Etruscan land were given horses; for Aeneas they brought the finest of them, draped in a lion's skin that flashed with gilded claws. The word ran through the little town that horsemen were riding fast to the king of the Etruscans, and the mothers, in their fear, doubled their prayers, and the danger loomed larger, and the face of war came nearer.

Then old Evander caught his son in a long embrace, and would not let go, and the tears ran down. "If only Jupiter would give me back the years I have lost — the man I was under the walls of Praeneste, when I burned whole heaps of captured shields and sent King Erulus down to the dead with my own hand, the man his mother Feronia had given three lives and three sets of arms, so I had to kill him three times over — if I were that man again, no danger, no death, would tear me from your arms, my Pallas, and Mezentius would not have boasted so long unpunished, would not have piled the dead so high. You gods, and you, great Jupiter, pity an old man and a king. If fate means to bring my boy home safe, if I am to see him again — then keep me alive, let me bear any pain, in the hope of holding him once more. But if some unspeakable blow is coming, then let me break off this cruel life now, now, while my fears are still in doubt, while the future is uncertain, while I hold you, dear boy, my one late joy, here in my arms — before any word of grief can reach me." So the father poured out his heart at the last parting; then he fainted, and his servants carried him into the house.

Now the horsemen rode out through the open gates, Aeneas at their head with Achates beside him, the other Trojan captains next, and Pallas in the middle of the column, bright in his armor and his embroidered cloak, like the morning star fresh from the sea — the star Venus loves above the rest — lifting his holy face in the sky and melting the dark. On the walls the trembling mothers followed the dust-cloud with their eyes, watching the bronze flash through it. The troop took the shortest road over the brush and the open country; the shouting rose, the column formed, and the hooves of the horses shook the dry ground. There is a cool dark grove by the river near the Etruscan town, hallowed long ago, ringed with gloomy hills — the first Greek settlers gave it to Silvanus, god of the fields and the flocks. Near it, in a safe camp, Tarchon and his Etruscans lay; and from a rise Aeneas saw the whole army spread across the plain. There he led his weary men and rested them, and fed the tired horses.

Meanwhile Venus, bright through the clouds, came bearing the gifts, and found her son alone in a quiet fold of the valley by the cold river. She showed herself plain before him, no disguise, and said: "Here is what I promised, finished by Vulcan's hand. Now go, and do not be slow to challenge the proud Latins, or Turnus himself, to the field." She embraced him, and set the shining arms under an oak before him. He could not have enough of looking at the goddess's gift; he turned it over, lifted it, weighed it in his hands, marveling — the crested helmet that spat fire, the deadly sword, the stiff bronze of the breastplate, blood-red and huge, like a dark cloud lit from within by the sun; the smooth greaves of gold and electrum, the spear, and last of all the shield, beyond any telling.

There the Lord of the Fire had wrought the story of Italy and the triumphs of Rome — for he knew the prophets, and he was not ignorant of the age to come. There were all the sons of Ascanius's line, the generations down the years, and the wars they would fight, each one in order. He had made the she-wolf, just delivered, lying in the green cave of Mars; the twin boys hung playing at her teats and mouthed her without fear, and she, her sleek neck bent back, licked them by turns and shaped their small bodies with her tongue. Near that he set Rome, and the Sabine women carried off against all custom from the crowded seats at the games; and then the war that flared up between Romulus and old Tatius; and then the two kings, the fighting done, standing armed at the altar of Jupiter, cups in their hands, sealing a treaty over a slain sow. Close by, two teams of four horses had torn Mettus apart, dragged opposite ways — but you should have kept your word, man of Alba — and Tullus hauled the liar's flesh through a wood, and the briars dripped with his blood.

And there was Porsenna, ordering Rome to take the banished Tarquin back, hemming the city with a great siege, while the sons of Aeneas threw themselves on the sword for liberty; you could see him drawn to the life, swollen with anger and threats, because Cocles dared to break the bridge, and Cloelia broke her chains and swam the river. At the top of the shield, Manlius, the watchman of the Tarpeian fort, stood before the temple and held the high Capitol; and there, fluttering through the gilded porticoes, the silver goose cried out that the Gauls were at the gate. The Gauls were in the thickets, close, taking the citadel under cover of the dark and the gift of the murky night — gold their hair, gold their dress, their striped cloaks shining, their milk-white necks ringed with gold, two Alpine pikes flashing in each man's hand, the long shields slung at their arms. There too he had hammered out the leaping Salii and the naked Luperci, the wool-tufted caps, and the sacred shields fallen from heaven; and the chaste matrons leading the holy rites through the city in their soft litters. Far off he set the halls of Tartarus too, the high gates of Dis, and the punishments of crime — and you, Catiline, hanging on a beetling crag and shuddering at the faces of the Furies — and, set apart, the righteous, with Cato giving them their laws.

And between these scenes ran a sea of swelling gold, the blue waves whitening with foam, and bright dolphins, wrought in silver, sweeping their tails through the water in a ring. In the center were the bronze-clad fleets, the battle of Actium: you could see all Leucate boiling with the war and the waves ablaze with gold. On this side Augustus Caesar, leading the men of Italy into battle with the Senate and the People, the household gods and the great gods beside him, stood high on the stern; twin flames of joy streamed from his temples, and his father's star rose over his head. Near him Agrippa, towering, with the wind and the gods behind him, drove his line on, the beaked naval crown — proud emblem of war — shining at his brow. On the other side, with all his barbarian wealth and his motley arms, came Antony, victor over the peoples of the East and the Red Sea shore, bringing Egypt with him, and the powers of the Orient, and farthest Bactria — and following him, the horror of it, his Egyptian wife.

They came on, all at once; the whole sea churned to foam under the dragging oars and the three-pronged prows. They made for the deep — you would think the Cyclades had been torn loose and were floating on the water, or that mountain crashed against mountain, so huge were the towering ships the men drove at one another. Burning tow and flying iron showered down, and the fields of Neptune ran red with fresh slaughter. In the midst the queen called her squadrons on with the rattle of her country's sistrum — and did not yet look back at the two snakes behind her. Monstrous gods of every shape, and barking Anubis, leveled their weapons against Neptune and Venus and Minerva. Mars raged in the thick of the fight, graven in iron, and the grim Furies swept down from the sky, and Discord strode by in a torn robe, glad of the ruin, with Bellona at her heels swinging a bloody whip. And Apollo of Actium, watching from above, bent his bow over it all; and at that terror every Egyptian and Indian, every Arab and Sabaean, turned to flee. The queen herself seemed to call up the winds and spread her sails and let the sheets run slack; the Lord of the Fire had shown her amid the slaughter, pale with the death that was coming, carried out to sea on the wind and the wave; and across from her, the Nile, mourning, his great body stretched wide, opening the folds of his robe and spreading his whole robe wide and calling the beaten home into his blue lap and his sheltering streams.

But Caesar, riding into the walls of Rome in a triple triumph, was paying his deathless vow to the gods of Italy — three hundred great shrines through the whole city. The streets roared with joy and games and applause; in every temple a choir of matrons, an altar at every door, slain bullocks strewing the ground. Caesar himself, seated at the snow-white threshold of shining Apollo, looked over the gifts of the nations and hung them on the proud doorposts, while the conquered peoples filed past in a long line, as different in dress and arms as in tongue — the loose-robed Africans and the Nomads, the Leleges and the Carians, the arrow-bearing Geloni; the Euphrates flowing humbler now, and the two-horned Rhine, the far Dahae, and the Araxes chafing under its new bridge.

Such were the scenes across the shield of Vulcan, his mother's gift. Aeneas marveled at them, glad in the pictures though he could not read what they meant — and he lifted onto his shoulder the fame and the fate of his descendants.
