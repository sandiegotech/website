# The Aeneid, Book I

The Storm, and Carthage

Arms, and the man, I sing — the man who, driven by fate, was first to flee the shores of Troy and come to Italy and its Lavinian coast. He was battered hard over sea and land by the power of the gods, for cruel Juno's unforgetting rage hounded him at every turn; and bitter were the wars he fought before he could found his city and carry his gods into Latium. From him came the Latin people, the Alban fathers, and the high walls of Rome.

Muse, tell me the causes. What wound to her divinity, what burning grievance, drove the queen of heaven to force a man so faithful, so devoted, through such a gauntlet of disaster — to hurl one trouble after another in his path? Can there really be such anger in the hearts of gods?

There was an ancient city, Carthage, a colony out of Tyre, facing Italy far across the water, opposite the mouth of the Tiber — a city rich in resources and fierce in the arts of war. They say Juno loved it above all places on earth, more even than Samos. Here she kept her armor; here she kept her chariot. And it was her fixed ambition, if fate would only allow it, that this city should one day rule the world.

But she had heard that a people descended from Trojan blood would someday rise up and tear down those Tyrian towers — that from them would come a nation broad in power and proud in war, born to bring ruin to Libya. So the Fates were spinning it. The daughter of Saturn feared this, and she had not forgotten the old war she had waged at Troy for the sake of her beloved Greeks. And there were older wounds too, griefs that still festered deep in her heart: the judgment of Paris and the insult to her beauty; the race she hated; the honors paid to Ganymede, stolen up to heaven. Inflamed by all of it together, she kept hurling the storm-tossed Trojans — the few the Greeks and merciless Achilles had left alive — across the whole expanse of the sea, far from Latium. Year after year they wandered, driven around every ocean by fate. So vast was the labor of founding the Roman race.

They were barely out of sight of Sicily, sails set, glad and cutting the salt foam with their bronze prows, when Juno — nursing the eternal wound beneath her heart — said to herself:

"So am I to give up, beaten? Am I unable to keep this Trojan king from reaching Italy? The Fates forbid me, do they? And yet Pallas could burn the whole Greek fleet and drown the men in the sea — all because of the madness and crime of one man, Ajax, son of Oileus. She flung Jupiter's own lightning down from the clouds, scattered their ships, churned up the waters with the winds; and Ajax, his chest pierced and breathing flames, she snatched up in a whirlwind and impaled on a jagged rock. But I, who walk as queen of the gods, sister and wife of Jupiter — I have been waging war on this one nation for all these years. And after this, who will still pray to Juno, or lay an offering on my altar?"

Burning with these thoughts, the goddess came to Aeolia, the homeland of storms, a country teeming with raging winds. There, in a vast cavern, King Aeolus holds the struggling gales and the howling tempests under his command and keeps them chained in their prison. They snarl and roar against the bars, furious, and the whole mountain echoes back their rage. Aeolus sits high above them with his scepter, soothing their tempers and bridling their fury — for if he did not, they would tear sea and earth and the deep sky itself away with them through the empty air. Fearing exactly this, the all-powerful Father shut them away in their dark cave, piled mountains on top of them, and set over them a king bound by a fixed treaty: one who knew, on command, when to rein them in and when to give them their head. To this king Juno now came as a suppliant, and she said:

"Aeolus — the Father of gods and king of men gave you the power to calm the waves or rouse them with the wind. There is a people I hate sailing the Tyrrhenian Sea right now, carrying Troy and its beaten gods toward Italy. Throw all your force into the winds. Sink their ships, or break them apart and scatter their bodies across the water. I have fourteen nymphs of rare beauty in my service. The loveliest of them all, Deiopea, I will join to you in marriage and make her yours forever, so that for this service she may spend all her years with you and make you the father of beautiful children."

Aeolus answered: "My queen, it is yours only to decide what you want; my duty is simply to carry it out. Whatever small kingdom I hold, whatever scepter, whatever favor I have with Jupiter — all of it is your gift. You let me take my place at the feasts of the gods; you made me master of the storms."

He finished, and turned his spear and drove it into the hollow flank of the mountain. The winds, like an army on the march, came pouring out through the breach and swept across the land in a hurricane. They fell on the sea, the East Wind and the South and the squall-bringing Southwest all together, heaving it up from its deepest floor and rolling huge waves toward the shore. Then came the shouts of the men, the shriek of the rigging. In an instant the clouds tore the sky and daylight from the eyes of the Trojans; black night sat down on the water. The heavens cracked with thunder, the air flashed with lightning, and everywhere death stared the men in the face. Aeneas felt his limbs go cold and weak. He groaned, and stretching both hands toward the stars he cried out: "Three times, four times blessed were the men who died in front of their fathers' eyes under the high walls of Troy! Diomedes — bravest of the Greeks — why couldn't I have fallen on the plains of Troy and poured out my life on your spear, where fierce Hector lies cut down by Achilles, where giant Sarpedon lies, where the river Simois rolls so many shields and helmets and brave bodies under its current!"

While he flung out these words, a screaming gust from the north slammed straight into his sail and lifted the waves to the sky. The oars snapped; the prow swung around and gave the ship's whole side to the waves, and a mountain of water came down on them in a sheer wall. Some hung on the crest; for others the trough yawned open and showed bare ground at the bottom, the sea boiling among the sand. The South Wind drove three ships onto hidden reefs — reefs the Italians call the Altars, a vast ridge just under the surface. Three more the East Wind hurled from open sea onto the shallows and the sandbars, a wretched sight, and walled them in with banks of sand. One ship, carrying the loyal Lycians and their captain Orontes, was struck before Aeneas's own eyes: a towering wave crashed down on the stern, pitched the helmsman headfirst overboard, and spun the ship three times in place before the whirlpool swallowed it down. Here and there a few men could be seen swimming in the huge swell, with weapons, planks, and Trojan treasure scattered across the waves. Now the storm took the sturdy ships of Ilioneus and brave Achates, of Abas and old Aletes; every hull sprang its seams and let the deadly water pour in through the cracks.

Meanwhile Neptune sensed that the sea had been thrown into uproar — felt the storm let loose and the still waters churned up from the very bottom. Deeply disturbed, he lifted his calm face above the surface and looked out across the deep. He saw the fleet of Aeneas scattered all over the water, the Trojans crushed beneath the waves and the falling sky. And he saw, clearly, the tricks and the malice of his sister Juno. He called the East Wind and the West to him and said: "What is this arrogance, born of what pride of birth? You winds — you dare to throw earth and sky into chaos and heave up these mountains of water without my leave? I'll — but first I had better calm these waves. Next time you won't get off so lightly. Now get out, and tell your king this: the rule of the sea and the cruel trident were never given to him — they fell to me by lot. He holds those monstrous rocks of yours, winds, the house you live in. Let Aeolus strut around in that hall of his and play king over a prison of locked-up winds." He spoke — and before he had finished he had calmed the swollen sea, scattered the gathered clouds, and brought back the sun. Cymothoe and Triton, heaving together, pushed the ships off the sharp rocks; the god himself lifted them with his trident, opened a path through the vast shoals, and smoothed the sea while his chariot skimmed lightly over the waves. It was like what happens when a great crowd breaks into riot — the mob runs wild, torches and stones fly, fury hands them weapons — and then, if they catch sight of a man whose goodness and service have earned him respect, they fall silent and stand still to listen, and his words master their hearts and cool their rage. Just so the whole roar of the sea fell quiet the moment Neptune looked out over it; and the Father of the waters, with the sky now clear above him, wheeled his horses and gave his willing chariot its head. Worn out, the Trojans made for the nearest land and turned toward the shores of Libya.

There is a place tucked into a long inlet, sheltered by an island whose flanks break the open sea and split the swell into a quiet harbor. On either side huge cliffs rise like twin towers against the sky, and beneath them the water lies safe and silent. Above stands a backdrop of shimmering forest, a grove of dark, overhanging trees; and under the brow of the facing cliff is a cave hung with fresh water and seats of natural rock — the home of nymphs. Here a tired ship needs no cable to hold her, no anchor to bite the ground. Aeneas put in here with the seven ships he had managed to gather out of the whole fleet. The Trojans, aching for dry land, climbed out and stretched their sea-soaked bodies on the sand.

First Achates struck a spark from flint, caught the fire in dry leaves, fed it kindling all around, and coaxed the flame to life out of the tinder. Then, sick of the sea as they were, they hauled out the grain they had salvaged and the tools for grinding it, and set about drying what the storm had spoiled and crushing it on the stones. Meanwhile Aeneas climbed a cliff to scan the whole sweep of the open sea, hoping to catch sight of Antheus driven before the wind, or the Phrygian galleys, or Capys, or the high stern of Caicus. There was not a single ship in view — but down on the shore he spotted three stags wandering, and behind them whole herds grazing along the valley in a long line. He stopped, snatched up the bow and the swift arrows that loyal Achates carried, and brought down the leaders first, their antlered heads held high, then scattered the rest with his shots, driving the whole herd in confusion through the green woods. He did not stop until he had stretched seven huge carcasses on the ground, one for each ship. Then he made his way back to the harbor and divided the kill among all his men. After that he shared out the wine that good Acestes had loaded aboard, jar by jar, when they left the Sicilian shore, and with these words he eased their grieving hearts: "Friends — we are no strangers to hardship. You have suffered worse than this, and a god will put an end to this too. You have sailed right up to Scylla's raging cliffs and heard the rocks roar; you have faced down the Cyclops and his cave. Call back your courage, and put away this fear and grief. Someday, perhaps, it will be a joy to remember even this. Through all these trials, through so many dangers, we are headed for Latium, where the Fates promise us a home and a quiet rest — where it is destined that the kingdom of Troy will rise again. Hold on, and keep yourselves alive for the better days to come."

So he spoke. But sick with worry as he was, he forced the hope onto his face and crushed the anguish down deep in his heart. His men set to work on their prey, ready for the feast. They flayed the hides off the carcasses and laid the meat bare; some cut it into pieces, still quivering, and spitted it; others set bronze cauldrons on the beach and tended the fires. Then they brought back their strength with the food, sprawled on the grass, filling themselves with old wine and rich venison. When their hunger was satisfied and the tables cleared, they fell to talking — and the talk turned, with mingled hope and dread, to the friends they had lost, wondering whether the missing men were still alive or had met their end and were past hearing any call. Above all, Aeneas — devoted as he was — mourned in silence the loss of fierce Orontes, and Amycus, and the cruel fate of Lycus, and brave Gyas and brave Cloanthus.

And now it was over — when Jupiter, looking down from the height of heaven over the sail-flecked sea, the spread of land, the coasts and the wide nations, paused at the top of the sky and fixed his eyes on the kingdom of Libya. And as he stood there turning such cares over in his mind, Venus came to him, sadder than anyone had ever seen her, her bright eyes brimming with tears, and said:

"You who rule the affairs of gods and men with your eternal power, and terrify them with your thunderbolt — what could my Aeneas have done to you that was so terrible? What could the Trojans have done? They have suffered so many deaths already, and now, for the sake of Italy, the whole world is being shut against them. You promised me, surely — at some turn of the years the Romans would come from them, leaders restored from the blood of Troy, who would hold the sea and all the lands under their law. What has changed your mind, Father? That promise was the one thing that consoled me for the fall of Troy and all that ruin — I weighed one fate against the other. And now the same bad luck still hounds these men, beaten down as they are. Where will you let it end, great king? Antenor could slip out from the middle of the Greek army, sail safely up into the Adriatic, past the roaring source of the Timavus where it bursts through nine mouths to the sea, and found his city of Padua, give his Trojans a home, hang up his weapons, and settle at last in peace. But we — your own children, the ones you've promised a place in heaven — we lose our ships, every one of them, betrayed — a thing not to be spoken — to the anger of one goddess, and are kept far from the shores of Italy. Is this the reward for being faithful to you? Is this how you restore us to power?"

The Father of gods and men smiled at her — that smile that clears storms from the sky — kissed his daughter lightly, and answered:

"Spare your fear, Venus. The destiny of your people stands fixed; nothing will move it. You will see your city, the promised walls of Lavinium; and you will lift great-souled Aeneas all the way up to the stars. Nothing has changed my mind. And since this worry gnaws at you, I'll tell you more — I'll unroll the secret book of the Fates further. Your son will fight a great war in Italy, crush its proud peoples, and lay down laws and walls for his men, until the third summer has seen him reign in Latium and three winters have passed since he beat the Rutulians. Then the boy Ascanius — Iulus now, his new name — will reign for thirty long years, and move the seat of power from Lavinium to a new fortress, Alba Longa, which he will build with great labor. For three hundred years the kingdom will stand under the line of Hector's people, until a royal priestess, Ilia, pregnant by Mars, will give birth to twin sons. Then Romulus, in the tawny hide of the she-wolf that nursed him, will take up the line, found the walls of Mars, and call the people Romans after his own name. On these I set no limits of space or time — I have given them empire without end. Even harsh Juno, who now wears out sea and land and sky with her dread, will think better of it, and join me in cherishing the Romans, the masters of the world, the people of the toga. So it is decreed. An age will come, as the years roll on, when the house of Aeneas will subdue the very land of Achilles and Agamemnon, and rule conquered Greece. Then a Trojan Caesar will be born of that glorious line, a man whose empire will reach to the ocean and whose fame will reach the stars — Julius, a name handed down from great Iulus. One day you yourself will welcome him into heaven, weighed down with the spoils of the East, and he too will be invoked in prayers. Then the harsh centuries will soften; war will be laid aside. Faith and Vesta, and Romulus with his brother Remus, will give the laws. The terrible iron gates of War will be shut and barred; and inside, godless Rage, sitting on a heap of savage weapons, his hands chained behind him with a hundred bands of bronze, will roar in horror through his bloody mouth."

Then Jupiter sent Mercury, son of Maia, down from heaven, so that the land and the new fortress of Carthage would open their doors to the Trojans, and Dido — not yet knowing the will of fate — would not turn them away from her borders. Mercury flew down through the wide air on beating wings and quickly stood on the shores of Libya. He carried out his orders, and the Carthaginians, just as the god intended, laid their fierce tempers aside. The queen above all put on a mood of calm and goodwill toward the Trojans.

But Aeneas, devoted as ever, had lain awake all night turning many things over in his mind. As soon as the kindly light of dawn arrived, he resolved to go out and explore this new country — to find out what shore the wind had thrown him on, who lived there, men or wild beasts, since the land looked uncultivated — and to bring back a sure report to his men. He hid his ships in a cove under the hollow cliffs, screened by trees and overhanging shade, and set out with only Achates beside him, two broad-bladed spears in his hand. There in the depths of the forest his own mother met him, wearing the face and dress of a young girl, with a girl's weapons — a Spartan huntress, or like Thracian Harpalyce, who rides her horses to exhaustion and outruns the swift river Hebrus. She had a bow slung from her shoulder, ready, her hair loose to the wind, her knees bare, her flowing robe gathered up in a knot. "Tell me, you two," she called first, "have you seen one of my sisters wandering out here, with a quiver slung on, wrapped in a spotted lynx-skin — or chasing a wild boar with the hunt in full cry?"

So Venus spoke, and her son answered: "I've seen no sister of yours, and heard none — though I don't know what to call you. Young woman? Your face is nothing like a mortal's; your voice doesn't sound human. You must be a goddess — Apollo's sister, or one of the nymphs. Whoever you are, be kind to us; lighten our hardship, and tell us at last what sky we are under, what coast of the world we've washed up on. We're wandering here, strangers, driven by the wind and the huge waves. We don't know the people or the place. I'll see that many a sacrifice falls for you at the altar."

"I hardly deserve such honor," said Venus. "It's only the custom of these Tyrian girls to carry a quiver and lace high purple boots up the leg. What you see is a Carthaginian kingdom — Tyrians, the city of Dido — but the land around it belongs to Libya, a people unbeatable in war. Dido rules here. She came fleeing from Tyre, running from her own brother. It's a long story of wrong, with many turns, but I'll tell you the heart of it. Her husband was Sychaeus, the richest landholder in Phoenicia, and she loved him with all her heart — her father had given her to him as a girl, joined to him in her first marriage. But her brother Pygmalion held the throne of Tyre, a monster of cruelty beyond all other men. Madness came between them. Pygmalion, blind with greed for gold and caring nothing for his sister's love, ambushed Sychaeus at the altar and killed him in secret, in cold blood. For a long time he hid the crime and fed the heartsick wife with empty hopes and lies. But the ghost of her unburied husband came to her in her sleep, lifting up a face strangely pale, baring the pierced chest and the bloody altar, and laid the whole hidden crime of that house open before her. He told her to flee, to leave her homeland fast; and to help her on the journey, he showed her where an old treasure of silver and gold lay buried in the earth. Shaken, Dido gathered companions for her escape — everyone who hated the cruel tyrant or feared him bitterly. They seized some ships that happened to be ready, loaded them with the gold (so Pygmalion's hoard went to sea instead), and a woman led the flight. They came at last to the place where you'll see the great walls and the rising fortress of new Carthage now, and bought as much ground as a single bull's hide could enclose — which is why it's called Byrsa. But what about you? Where do you come from? Where are you headed?"

He answered her question with a deep sigh, the words dragged up from the bottom of his chest: "Goddess — if I went back to the very beginning and traced the whole record of our suffering, and if you had the time to hear it, evening would close the gates of heaven on the sea before I finished. We come from ancient Troy — if the name of Troy has ever reached your ears — and after wandering across every sea, a storm threw us here on the Libyan coast by chance. I am Aeneas, faithful Aeneas, who carries my household gods rescued from the enemy in my ships — my fame known above the sky. I am seeking Italy, my homeland, and a line descended from Jupiter himself. I set out onto the Phrygian sea with twenty ships, my goddess mother showing me the way, following the path the Fates laid down. Barely seven are left, battered by the waves and the wind. And I myself wander these Libyan deserts, unknown and in need, driven out of Europe and out of Asia both." Venus could not bear to hear him grieve any further, and broke in:

"Whoever you are, I don't believe heaven hates you — not when you've reached a Carthaginian city still drawing breath. Just go on, and take yourself to the queen's door. I can tell you this: your friends and your fleet are safe, brought back to harbor by a shift of the wind — unless my parents taught me to read the birds for nothing. Look up there — twelve swans in a joyful line. A moment ago Jupiter's eagle, swooping out of the clear sky, was scattering them across the open air. Now you can see them strung out in formation again, either touching down or already looking down at the ground they've chosen, wheeling and clapping their wings, circling the sky in a chorus of song. Exactly so, your ships and your young men are either safe in port or running into harbor under full sail. Go on now — follow the road where it leads you."

She finished, and as she turned away her neck shone rose-bright; her hair breathed out the divine fragrance of ambrosia; her gown flowed all the way to her feet; and in the way she walked, the true goddess stood revealed. Aeneas knew his mother, and called after her as she fled: "You too — why are you so cruel? Why do you keep mocking your son with these disguises? Why can't I take your hand in mine, really hear your voice and answer it?" With that reproach he turned toward the city. But Venus wrapped the two of them in a thick veil of mist as they walked, a dark cloud poured around them, so that no one could see them, lay a hand on them, or stop them to ask why they had come. She herself rose into the air toward Paphos, glad to see her home again — her temple there, and the hundred altars warm with Sabaean incense and breathing the scent of fresh garlands.

Meanwhile the two of them pressed on along the path. Soon they were climbing the hill that looms over the city and looks down on the towers facing it. Aeneas marveled at the sheer scale of it — where huts had stood not long ago — marveled at the gates, the noise, the paved streets. The Tyrians were hard at work: some pushing up the walls and heaving stones up by hand to build the citadel; some marking out a site for a house and trenching it round. They were choosing magistrates and a senate and sacred laws; over here they were dredging out a harbor, over there laying the deep foundations of a theater, and cutting huge columns out of the cliffs to crown the stage that was to come. It was like bees in early summer, busy in the sun across a flowering meadow — leading out the grown young of the hive, or packing the cells tight with liquid honey until they bulged with sweet nectar, or taking in the loads brought home, or massing in a line to drive the lazy drones away from the hive. The work hums; the honey breathes of thyme. "How lucky you are," Aeneas said, gazing up at the rising rooftops, "you whose walls are already going up!" And, wrapped in cloud — astonishing to tell — he walked straight in among them, into the thick of the crowd, and no one saw him at all.

In the heart of the city there was a grove, deeply shaded and welcome, where the Carthaginians, fresh off the waves and the storm, had first dug into the ground and turned up the sign Juno had promised them: the head of a spirited horse, an omen that their people would be brave in war and rich in the years to come. Here Dido was building a vast temple to Juno, splendid with offerings and with the presence of the goddess herself. Its threshold rose on bronze steps; bronze held the beams together; the doors swung on hinges of groaning bronze. And it was here, in this grove, that something Aeneas saw for the first time eased his fear and let him dare to hope for better days. For while he waited for the queen, taking in the whole temple, wondering at the city's good fortune, at the skill of the rival craftsmen and the weight of their labor — he suddenly saw, painted across the walls in order, the battles of Troy, the war whose fame had already gone out over the whole world: the sons of Atreus, and Priam, and Achilles, savage to them both. He stopped, and the tears came. "Achates," he said, "is there any place left on earth that isn't full of what we suffered? Look — there's Priam! Even here, glory has its reward; there are tears for the way of things, and the troubles of mortal men touch the heart. Let go of your fear. This fame of ours will somehow keep us safe." He spoke, and fed his soul on the empty picture, sighing again and again, the tears running in rivers down his face. For he saw the fighting around Troy — here the Greeks in flight as the young Trojans pressed them, there the Trojans running with crested Achilles bearing down on them in his chariot. Not far off he wept to recognize the white-canopied tents of Rhesus, betrayed in their first sleep and ravaged by bloody Diomedes, who slaughtered the men and drove the fiery horses back to the Greek camp before they could taste the grass of Troy or drink from its river. Elsewhere he saw Troilus, just a boy, no match for Achilles, his weapons thrown away, in flight — fallen flat on his back, dragged behind his own empty chariot, still gripping the reins; his neck and his hair sweeping the dust, the dust scrawled over by the trailing point of his spear. Meanwhile the Trojan women were walking to the temple of Pallas, who would not be appeased, their hair undone, carrying her a robe, beating their breasts in grief — and the goddess kept her eyes fixed on the ground, turned away. Three times Achilles had dragged Hector around the walls of Troy and was selling his lifeless body for gold; and Aeneas groaned aloud, deep from the heart, to see the spoils, the chariot, the very body of his friend, and Priam reaching out his old, defenseless hands. He recognized himself, too, in the thick of the Greek chiefs; and the columns out of the East, and the armor of black Memnon. There Penthesilea led her ranks of crescent-shielded Amazons, raging through the heart of the fighting, a golden belt cinched under her bare breast — a warrior queen, a girl who dared to meet men in battle.

And while Aeneas stood there marveling, fixed and lost in the sight of it all, queen Dido swept toward the temple in radiant beauty, a great throng of young men crowding around her. She was like Diana leading her dancing nymphs along the banks of the Eurotas or over the ridges of Cynthus — a thousand mountain spirits gathering behind her on every side, while she carries her quiver and walks a head taller than them all, and a silent joy stirs in the heart of her mother Latona. Just so was Dido; just so she moved, glad and serene, through the midst of her people, pressing on the work and the future of her kingdom. Then, at the door of the goddess, beneath the very center of the vaulted roof, she took her seat on a high throne, ringed by her guards. She was handing down laws and judgments to her people, dividing the labor of building fairly or assigning it by lot — when suddenly Aeneas saw, surging up in the middle of a great crowd, Antheus and Sergestus and brave Cloanthus, and others of the Trojans whom the black storm had scattered across the sea and carried off to other shores. He froze, and so did Achates, struck at once with joy and fear. They were burning to clasp the hands of their friends, but the strangeness of it left them uncertain and held them back. So they stayed hidden, watching from the hollow cloud — what had happened to the men? On what shore had they left the fleet? Why had they come? For these were chosen men from every ship, come to plead for mercy, and they made for the temple with a great clamor.

When they had entered and been given leave to speak before the queen, the eldest, Ilioneus, began calmly: "Your majesty — to whom Jupiter has granted the founding of a new city, and the power to bridle proud nations with justice — we are Trojans, wretched, storm-tossed over every sea, and we beg you: keep your people from burning our ships. Spare a god-fearing race; look more closely at what we are. We have not come to put your homes to the sword or to drive off plunder to the shore. That is not the way of the beaten, and we have not the heart for it. There is a country the Greeks called Hesperia, an ancient land, strong in arms and rich in soil. The Oenotrians settled it; now, they say, a later people call it Italy, after a leader of theirs. We were sailing there when a sudden storm rose with the wild surf and scattered us on hidden reefs, blew us all apart, and flung a few of us here onto your shores. What kind of people are you? What country lets such savagery pass for custom? We are barred from the shelter of the very sand; they take up arms against us and forbid us to set foot on the edge of dry land. If you have no respect for men and the weapons of men, then at least fear the gods, who remember the just and the unjust. We had a king, Aeneas — no man more just, none greater in devotion, none mightier in war. If fate has spared him, if he still breathes the air and isn't lying yet among the cruel shades, then we have nothing to fear, and you would not regret being the first to do us a kindness. We have cities and fields in Sicily too, and a Trojan, King Acestes. Let us only beach our storm-wracked fleet, cut new planks and oars from your forests, so that — if our king and our friends are restored to us — we may set joyful course for Italy. But if the sea has swallowed you, best father of the Trojans, and there is no hope left for Iulus, let us at least sail back to the straits of Sicily, to the home that is ready for us there, and to King Acestes." So Ilioneus spoke, and all the Trojans murmured their assent.

Dido looked down a moment, then answered briefly: "Trojans, let go of your fear; put your worries aside. Hard times and the newness of my kingdom force me to such caution, to set a guard over all my borders. Who hasn't heard of the race of Aeneas, of the city of Troy and its courage and its men, of the great fire of that war? We Carthaginians are not so dull-witted as all that; the sun does not yoke his horses so far from our city. Whether you choose great Hesperia and the fields of Saturn, or the country of Eryx and King Acestes — I'll send you off safely, with help and supplies. Or would you like to settle here with me, as equals, in this kingdom? The city I am building is yours. Beach your ships. Trojan and Tyrian, I will treat you all the same. And I only wish your king himself, Aeneas, had been driven here by the same wind! In fact I'll send trusted men along the whole coast and order them to search every corner of Libya, in case he's been shipwrecked and is wandering in some forest or town."

These words stirred the spirits of brave Aeneas and faithful Achates, and for a while now they had been longing to break out of the cloud. Achates spoke first: "Son of the goddess — what are you thinking now? You can see for yourself that everything is safe: the fleet recovered, the friends restored. Only one is missing, and we saw him go down in the waves with our own eyes. Everything else matches what your mother told you." He had barely finished when the cloud around them suddenly tore open and melted into clear air.

Aeneas stood there in the open daylight, shining, his face and shoulders like a god's — for his mother herself had breathed beauty into his hair, the rose-glow of youth into his face, and a glad light into his eyes, the way a craftsman's hand gives grace to ivory, or sets silver and Parian marble in gold. Then, to the queen, and to everyone's astonishment, he spoke at once: "Here I am — the man you are looking for. Aeneas of Troy, snatched from the Libyan waves. You alone have taken pity on Troy's unspeakable suffering. You offer to share your city and your home with us — the leavings of the Greek war, worn down by every disaster on land and sea, stripped of everything. To thank you as you deserve is beyond us, Dido — beyond any of us, beyond the whole scattered Trojan race. If the gods have any care for the good, if justice means anything anywhere, if a clear conscience counts for something, may the gods reward you as you deserve. What lucky age brought you into the world? What great parents gave the world a daughter like you? As long as rivers run to the sea, as long as shadows move across the slopes of the mountains, as long as the sky feeds the stars, your honor and your name and your praise will live on, wherever I am called." With that he reached out his right hand to his friend Ilioneus, and his left to Serestus, and then to the others in turn — to brave Gyas, to brave Cloanthus.

Dido stood transfixed, first at the sight of the man himself, then at all he had endured. "Son of a goddess," she said, "what fate hunts you through such dangers? What force has thrown you onto these wild shores? Are you really the Aeneas whom kindly Venus bore to Anchises of Troy, by the waters of the Phrygian Simois?

"I remember — I was only a child — when Teucer came to Sidon, an exile from his homeland, looking to my father Belus for a new kingdom; and Belus was sacking rich Cyprus at the time and holding it under his conquering hand. From that day I have known the story of the fall of Troy, and your name, and the kings of Greece. Teucer himself, Troy's enemy, used to praise the Trojans, and liked to trace his own line back to the old kings of Troy. So come in, young heroes, into my house. I too was driven through long hardship and tossed from place to place before fate let me settle at last in this land. Having suffered myself, I have learned to help those in trouble." With that she led Aeneas into the royal palace, and at the same time ordered sacrifices in the temples of the gods. And she did not forget his men down on the shore: she sent them twenty bulls, a hundred bristling boars, a hundred fat lambs with their mothers, and the glad gift of wine. Inside, the palace was being laid out for a feast of royal splendor: tapestries worked with proud purple were spread, the tables loaded with massive silver, and figures of Dido's ancestors chased in gold — the whole long line of a great people, traced back through the years from its first founder.

Aeneas, his father's love for the boy not letting his heart rest, quickly sent Achates ahead to the ships to carry the news to Ascanius and bring him back to the city — Ascanius was all his anxious thought — and to fetch gifts rescued from the ruins of Troy: a mantle stiff with gold-embroidered figures, and a veil bordered with yellow acanthus, that had once belonged to Argive Helen — her mother Leda's gift, that she had carried from Mycenae when she sailed to Troy and her forbidden marriage. There was a scepter, too, that Priam's eldest daughter Ilione had once carried, and a string of pearls, and a double crown of jewels and gold. Achates hurried off toward the ships to do all this.

But Venus was turning new schemes over in her heart, laying new plans. She would have Cupid take on the look and shape of sweet Ascanius, come in his place, and slip his fire into the queen with the gifts, kindling a flame in her very bones. For Venus mistrusted that two-faced house, those treacherous Tyrians; cruel Juno galled her, and her worry came back as night fell. So she spoke to her winged son: "My boy — you are my strength, my whole power; my son, you who laugh at the great Father's thunderbolts — I come to you for help, and beg your power, like a suppliant. You know how your brother Aeneas is driven over every sea, all around the coasts, by Juno's relentless hate, and you have grieved with me over it many times. Now Phoenician Dido has him, and is holding him with soft words; and I'm afraid where Juno's hospitality will lead. She won't sit still at such a turning of fate. So I mean to outrun her — to take the queen by surprise and ring her round with flame, so that no power can change her, and she's mine, bound fast with a great love for Aeneas. Now hear how I think you can do it. The boy Ascanius — my dearest care — is getting ready, at his father's call, to go up to the Carthaginian city with gifts saved from the sea and the fire of Troy. I'll lull him into a deep sleep and hide him away on the heights of Cythera, or in my shrine at Idalium, so that he can't find out the trick or stumble into the middle of it. You take on his shape — it's only for one night, no more — and, a boy yourself, slip into the boy's familiar face, so that when Dido takes you into her lap in the joy of the feast, among the royal tables and the flowing wine, when she hugs you and kisses you, you can breathe a hidden fire into her and pour your poison through her unknowing veins." Cupid obeyed his dear mother, took off his wings, and walked along, delighted, in Iulus's shape.

Venus, meanwhile, poured a gentle sleep over Ascanius and gathered him warm into her arms, carried him up to the high groves of Idalium, where soft marjoram breathed its sweet shade and fresh flowers folded around him. And now Cupid, obedient, was on his way with the royal gifts for the Carthaginians, glad to follow Achates. When he arrived, the queen had already settled on a golden couch beneath splendid hangings and taken her place in the center; and now Aeneas and the Trojan youth came in and reclined on the spread purple. Servants poured water over their hands, brought out bread from baskets, and handed round smooth napkins. Inside, fifty serving girls were lined up to keep the long tables stocked and to feed the household fire; a hundred more, and as many young men their equals in age, loaded the tables with food and set out the cups. The Carthaginians too came crowding in through the festive halls and took their places on the embroidered couches. They marveled at Aeneas's gifts, marveled at Iulus — at the god's glowing face and his counterfeit words — at the mantle and the veil bright with its yellow acanthus border. Above all, the poor queen, already doomed, given over to the ruin to come, could not look her fill: she was moved as much by the boy as by the gifts. He, when he had hung on Aeneas in a long embrace and satisfied the love of the father who thought he was his son, went to the queen. Dido clung to him with her eyes and her whole heart, holding him close, never guessing what a mighty god was settling against her, poor woman. But he, remembering his Cyprian mother, began little by little to blot out Sychaeus, and tried to take a heart long quiet, long out of practice in love, by surprise with a living passion.

When the first lull came in the feast and the tables were cleared, they set out the great mixing-bowls and crowned the wine with garlands. The hall filled with noise; voices rolled through the wide rooms. Lamps hung blazing from the gilded ceiling, and the torches beat back the night. Then the queen called for the heavy golden bowl, thick with jewels, that Belus and all his line after him had used, and filled it with unmixed wine. The hall fell silent, and she spoke: "Jupiter — since they say you give strangers their rights — grant that this be a day of joy for the Tyrians and for these travelers out of Troy, a day our children will remember. Let Bacchus, giver of gladness, be with us, and bountiful Juno; and you, Carthaginians, keep this gathering with goodwill." She tipped the first drops of wine onto the table as an offering, then touched the bowl lightly to her lips and passed it to Bitias with a challenge. He drank deep, draining the brimming gold, and after him the other lords. Then long-haired Iopas, taught by mighty Atlas, struck up on his golden lyre and sang of the wandering moon and the laboring sun; where the human race and the animals came from, and the rain and the fire; of Arcturus, and the rainy Hyades, and the two Bears; why the winter suns hurry so fast to dip into the sea, and what holds back the lingering nights. The Carthaginians roared their applause again and again, and the Trojans took it up. And the doomed queen drew out the night with all kinds of talk, drinking down a long draught of love. She asked endless questions about Priam, about Hector; about the armor the son of the Dawn had worn to Troy; about Diomedes's horses, and how huge Achilles really was. "No — start from the very beginning, my guest," she said at last. "Tell me the whole story: the trap the Greeks laid, the fall of your people, your own long wanderings. For this is the seventh summer now that has carried you, a wanderer, over every land and sea."
