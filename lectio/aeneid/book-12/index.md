# The Aeneid, Book XII

The Single Combat, and the Death of Turnus

When Turnus saw the Latins beaten and worn down, their spirit broken in the failed war, saw that the people's eyes were on him now and that the time had come to make good his promise — then his pride caught fire, past any cooling of it, and he reared up. A lion in the African fields, when the hunters have gored him deep in the chest, only then wakes to the fight: he tosses his great mane in joy, snaps off the planted spear with his teeth, and roars, the blood gone red in his open mouth. That was Turnus, the heat rising in him. And wild now, he turned to the king and spoke: "Turnus will not hold you back. The cowards, the sons of Aeneas, have no excuse left — they can't take back what they swore. I'll meet him. Father, lay out the rites, set the terms. This arm will either send the Trojan deserter down to Tartarus — and the Latins can sit and watch — and clear our shame with my own sword alone; or let him have us beaten, let him have Lavinia for his bride."

Latinus answered him, steady and calm: "Brave young man — the higher your fierce courage runs, the more I'm bound to weigh it carefully, to count the chances of the war you scorn. You have your father Daunus's kingdom; you have all the towns your hand has taken; and I have gold, and good will toward you. There are other unmarried women in Latium, in the Laurentine country, and none of them low-born. Let me say a hard thing plainly — take it in, and don't shut your heart to it. It was not allowed me to give my daughter to any of her old suitors; every god, every man foretold it. Worn down by my love for you, by our shared blood, by my wife's tears, I broke all of it — I tore Lavinia from the man she was promised to, I took up an unholy war. You see what's come of it since, Turnus — what battles, what disasters, and you above all have borne the worst of them. Beaten twice in the open field, we can barely keep the hope of Italy alive behind our walls. The Tiber still runs warm with our blood; the wide plains are white with our bones. Why do I keep drifting? What madness keeps shifting my mind? If I'm ready to take Turnus into my house as an ally once he's dead, why don't I end the fighting now, while he's alive? What will my own Rutulian kin say, all of Italy say, if I hand you over to your death — may fortune turn the word false — when you're courting my daughter? Look at how war goes both ways. Pity your old father, who's far off in Ardea now, grieving for you."

But none of it bent Turnus's rage. The cure only inflamed the wound; it made the sickness worse. As soon as he could speak he said: "The care you take for me, dear father — I beg you, for my sake, let it go. Let me make the trade: my life for honor. I too, Father, can throw a spear and drive the iron home; I too can draw blood. His goddess mother won't be near to hide him in a woman's cloud of mist and slip away into empty shadows."

But the queen, terrified by the new terms of the fight, wept, and clung to her fierce son-to-be as if her own death were coming with his: "Turnus — by these tears, by any honor Amata still holds in your heart — you are the one hope left to me, the rest of my sad old age. The honor of Latinus, his whole house, rests on you; you alone hold up our sinking home. One thing I beg you, only this: don't fight the Trojan. Whatever waits for you in that duel waits for me too, Turnus. I'll leave this hateful light with you. I won't live to see Aeneas a son of mine." Lavinia heard her mother, and the tears wet her burning cheeks, and a deep blush ran up her face and set it on fire — the way Indian ivory looks when someone stains it with crimson, or the way white lilies blush where the red roses crowd them. The colors poured across her face. Turnus stared at her, lost in love, more on fire for the fight than ever, and he answered Amata with few words: "Mother — don't, I beg you, send me off to a hard war with weeping, with omens like these. Turnus isn't free to put off his death. Idmon — go, carry the Trojan this news, and he won't like it: when tomorrow's Dawn climbs the sky red in her chariot, let him not lead the Trojans against the Latins. Trojan and Rutulian arms can rest. With our own blood we'll settle the war — and on that field Lavinia will be won."

He said it, and strode quickly back to his house, and called for his horses, and took joy in them as they neighed at the sight of him. They were the splendid breed that Orithyia herself had given to Pilumnus, whiter than snow, faster than the wind. The eager grooms stood around them, slapping their chests with cupped hands and combing out their manes. Then Turnus armed: he pulled on the corselet stiff with gold and pale mountain-bronze, and fitted his sword and his shield and the helmet with its red plumes — the sword the Fire-God had forged for his father Daunus, and dipped white-hot, hissing, into the river Styx. Then he caught up the great spear that stood leaning against a tall pillar in the middle of the hall, the spoil he had taken from Auruncan Actor, and shook it till it trembled, and shouted: "Now, spear — you have never failed when I called you — now's the hour. Mighty Actor carried you once; now Turnus's hand carries you. Let me bring down that half-man Phrygian, let me strip the corselet from his body and tear it off in my fists, let me foul in the dust that hair of his, curled with hot irons and soaked in myrrh." This was the fury that drove him: his whole face threw off sparks, and fire flashed in his fierce eyes. He was like a bull working himself up before the first charge, throwing his rage into his horns, butting a tree-trunk, goring the wind, pawing the sand and scattering it in a trial of war.

And no less fiercely, in the armor his mother had brought from Vulcan, Aeneas whetted himself for war and roused his anger, glad the duel had come to settle the fighting on these terms. He comforted his men and the frightened Ascanius, telling them what fate had promised; and he sent men to King Latinus with a firm answer, to fix the terms of peace.

The next day was barely scattering its first light along the mountaintops — the hour the Sun's horses first climb dripping from the deep sea and breathe the daylight out of their lifted nostrils — when Trojans and Rutulians together, under the great city's wall, were measuring out the ground for the fight and laying the field, building turf altars in the middle for the gods they shared, with springwater and fire. Others came in linen aprons, their hair bound with sacred herbs. Out came the Italian army, the columns streaming through the gates; and from the other side the Trojans and the Etruscans poured in under their different arms, all of them harnessed for steel as if war itself had called them — and the captains darted up and down among the thousands, splendid in gold and purple: Mnestheus of the line of Assaracus, brave Asilas, Messapus the tamer of horses, son of Neptune. At the signal each man drew back to his own ground; they planted their spears in the earth and leaned their shields against them. And in a fever to watch, the women streamed out too, and the helpless old men, and crowded the towers and the rooftops, and stood thick in the high gateways.

But Juno, from the top of the hill men now call the Alban Mount — it had no name then, no glory, nothing — looked out over the plain, over both armies drawn up, the Laurentine lines and the Trojan, and the city of Latinus. And straight away the goddess spoke to Turnus's sister, goddess to goddess — for Juturna rules the pools and the loud rivers; Jupiter, king of high heaven, gave her that honor in payment for her stolen girlhood. "Nymph," Juno said, "glory of the rivers, dearest of all to me: you know how I have set you above every other Latin girl who ever climbed into thankless Jupiter's bed, and gladly gave you your place in heaven. Now learn your own grief, Juturna, so you won't blame me for it. As far as Fortune let me, and the Fates let Latium stand, I shielded Turnus and your walls. But now I see the young man going up against a fate too strong for him; the Fates' day is at hand, the hostile force is on him. I can't bear to watch this duel, or the terms they've sworn. You — if you dare anything for your brother, it's a sister's part — go. Do it. Maybe better days will come to the broken Latins." She had barely finished when the tears burst from Juturna's eyes, and three times, four times, her hand struck her lovely breast. "This is no time for tears," said Juno. "Hurry — pull your brother from death, if there's any way. Or stir up the war again, and shatter the peace they're swearing. I'll answer for the daring." And with that she left her, torn and uncertain, sick at heart from the cruel wound.

Meanwhile the kings came out. Latinus rode in a great four-horse chariot, twelve gilded rays shining around his temples, the mark of the Sun, his grandfather. Turnus came behind a pair of white horses, gripping two broad-headed spears. And out of the camp came father Aeneas, the founder of the Roman line, his star-bright shield and his god-made armor blazing, and beside him Ascanius, the second hope of mighty Rome. A priest in spotless white led up the young of a bristling boar and an unshorn ewe and set the beasts at the burning altars. The two men turned to face the rising sun, sprinkled the salted meal, marked the foreheads of the victims with the knife, and poured wine from cups onto the flames. Then dutiful Aeneas drew his sword and swore: "Let the Sun be my witness now, and this land of Italy I have suffered so much to reach; and you, all-powerful Father, and you, Juno, daughter of Saturn — kinder now, I pray, goddess, kinder — and you, great Mars, who hold all war in your hand; the springs and the rivers I call on, and every power of the high air and the blue sea: if the day falls to Turnus, the beaten will withdraw to Evander's city. Ascanius will leave this land, and from this hour the men of Aeneas will never raise the sword again or trouble this kingdom with war. But if Victory grants the fight to us — as I believe she will, and may the gods make it so — then I will not make Italians bow to Trojans, and I claim no throne for myself. Let both nations, unbeaten, come together under equal laws, forever. I'll bring the gods and their worship; my father-in-law Latinus will keep his arms, keep his command, keep his kingly power. The Trojans will raise me a city, and Lavinia will give it her name."

So Aeneas spoke first, and then Latinus, with his eyes and his right hand lifted to the sky: "By the same powers I swear it, Aeneas — by earth, by sea, by the stars, by the twin children of Latona and the two-faced god Janus, by the might of the gods below and the shrines of pitiless Death: let the Father hear me, who seals our oaths with his thunder. I touch the altars; I take the fire and the gods between us as my witnesses. No day will break this peace or this treaty for Italy, however things may fall; no force will turn me from it, not if a flood should pour the land back into the sea and melt the sky down into Tartarus. As surely as this scepter" — he was holding a scepter — "will never again put out a leaf or a green shoot, now that it's been cut from the low stump of its tree and lost its mother and its boughs and its hair to the axe: once a living thing, and now the craftsman's hand has cased it in fine bronze for the fathers of Latium to carry." So they bound the peace between them with such words, in full sight of the chiefs. Then over the fire they cut the throats of the hallowed beasts in the old way, and tore the living entrails out, and heaped the altars high with loaded platters.

But the Rutulians had thought the duel uneven for a long time now, and their hearts had been shifting back and forth — and the more they saw the two men close up, ill-matched in strength, the more it worked in them. Turnus fed it, coming forward without a word, eyes lowered, to worship at the altar — pale, his young body wasted. And when his sister Juturna saw the murmur growing and the people's hearts beginning to waver, she dropped down into the middle of the lines in the shape of Camers — a man of great house, his father famous, himself famous in arms — and she knew well what she was doing. "Aren't you ashamed, Rutulians," she said, "to throw one life away for all of us? Aren't we their equal in numbers and in strength? Look — that's all of them, Trojans and Arcadians and the Etruscans who hate Turnus. We hardly have an enemy apiece if half of us go in. Turnus will go up to the gods whose altars he gives himself to, and live on the lips of men forever. We'll lose our country and be forced to bow to arrogant masters — we, who sit here so idle in the field."

Her words fanned the soldiers higher and higher, and the muttering ran down the ranks. The very Laurentines were changed, the very Latins. The men who had just been longing for rest and a way out of danger now wanted their weapons, and prayed the peace would break, and pitied Turnus and the cruelty of his fate. And Juturna threw something stronger on top of it — a sign high in the sky, and nothing ever shook Italian hearts harder or fooled them with a falser omen. For across the red morning sky the golden eagle of Jupiter came flying, scattering a flock of shore-birds, a loud winged column of them — and then suddenly dropped to the water and snatched up a fine swan in its hooked talons. The Italians strained to watch. And then all the birds turned in their flight, screaming, and wheeled back in a cloud that darkened the sky, and mobbed the eagle and bore it down with their massed weight, until it gave way, dropped its prey into the river, and fled deep into the clouds. Then the Rutulians hailed the omen with a shout and reached for their weapons. And first Tolumnius, their augur, cried out: "This — this is what I prayed for so often. I take the sign; I know the gods. Follow me, take up your swords — I lead the way. The shameless stranger frightens you with war as if you were helpless birds, and ravages your shores; but he will take to flight and spread his sails far out to sea, like that eagle. You'll close your ranks as one and snatch your stolen king back by force." He said it, and ran out ahead and flung his spear at the enemy. The cornel-wood shaft hissed away and cut the air true, and at the same instant a great shout went up and the whole crowd was in an uproar, every heart hammering. The spear came on — and where nine brothers happened to be standing, all sons of one true Tuscan wife to the Arcadian Gylippus, it struck one of them, a beautiful young man in shining armor, full in the middle, where the stitched belt chafes the belly and the buckle bites the joined ends, and ran him through and stretched him on the yellow sand. But his brothers, a hot-blooded band wild with grief, snatched up their swords or their spears and charged blind ahead — and the Laurentine ranks ran to meet them. The Trojans and the Etruscans and the Arcadians in their painted armor poured in against them, and one passion took hold of them all: to let the steel decide it.

They stripped the altars bare. A storm of spears came on, a black iron rain, and the bronze bowls and the firebrands flew. Latinus fled, carrying off his beaten gods, the broken peace gone. Others harnessed chariots or vaulted onto their horses and came on with drawn swords. Messapus, hungry to wreck the truce, drove his horse straight at Aulestes the Etruscan king — a king by his crown — and rode him down. Aulestes fell back over an altar and struck it head and shoulders, and Messapus loomed over him with the heavy spear, and stabbed down hard though the man begged hard, and said: "He's got it now — a better victim, this, for the great gods." And the Italians ran in and stripped the warm body. Corynaeus snatched up a charred brand from an altar and met Ebysus coming at him with a blow, and shoved the fire into his face, and his huge beard went up and gave off the stink of burning hair; and following the blow, Corynaeus caught his enemy's hair in his left hand, drove a knee into him to pin him to the ground, and ran his stiff sword into his side. Podalirius chased the shepherd Alsus through the front of the fighting, his sword bare over him — but Alsus swung his axe and split the man's brow and chin clean down the middle, and the brains spattered his armor, and a hard sleep, an iron rest, closed Podalirius's eyes forever in endless night.

But the dutiful Aeneas, his head bare, stretched out an unarmed hand and shouted to his men: "Where are you running? What is this fighting that's broken out so suddenly? Hold back your rage! The terms are already struck; the rules are set. I alone have the right to fight. Leave it to me — and stop being afraid. This hand will make the treaty good. These rites have made Turnus mine." And in the middle of those words, while he was still calling, a winged arrow came whirring and struck him — no one ever knew whose hand sent it, what gust or what god turned that glory to the Rutulians, for the great honor of that wound was hidden, and no man ever boasted that he had cut down Aeneas.

When Turnus saw Aeneas leaving the field and his captains in disorder, a sudden hot hope set him ablaze. He called for his horses and his arms at once, leapt proudly into his chariot, and gathered the reins in his hands. Flying on, he sent many brave men down to death. He rolled others over half-dead, or crushed whole ranks beneath the wheels, or caught up their own fallen spears and rained them on the backs of the fleeing. He was like Mars on the banks of the frozen Hebrus when he wakes to war, clashing his sword on his shield and loosing his maddened horses across the open plain, and they outrun the south wind and the west, and the far ends of Thrace groan under their hooves, and around them race the faces of his court — black Terror, and Wrath, and Treachery. So Turnus, just as eager, lashed his steaming horses through the thick of the enemy, trampling the pitiful dead, the galloping hooves flinging up a bloody spray, kneading gore into the sand. He killed Sthenelus, and Thamyrus, and Pholus; the first two hand to hand, the third from far off. From far off he killed both sons of Imbrasus, Glaucus and Lades, whom their father had raised in Lycia and matched in armor — both able to fight on foot or to outride the wind on horseback.

Elsewhere Eumedes was charging into the middle of the war — a famous son of old Dolon, named for his grandfather, a match for his father in nerve and in hand. Dolon had once gone to spy out the Greek camp and dared to ask for Achilles's own chariot as his pay — and Diomedes paid him a different wage for that boldness, so he no longer hungers for Achilles's horses. Turnus saw the son of Dolon far across the open field and threw a light javelin the whole length of the air at him, then halted his team, sprang down, and stood over the man as he fell dying, and set his foot on his neck, and wrenched the bright sword out of his hand and dyed it deep in his throat, and said: "There — measure out, Trojan, the Italian land, the West you came to take by war. This is the reward for daring my sword. This is how they found their city." Then with a spear-throw he sent Asbytes to keep him company, and Chloreus, and Sybaris, and Dares, and Thersilochus, and Thymoetes thrown from the neck of his rearing horse. As the breath of the north wind off Thrace roars over the deep Aegean and drives the rollers to the shore, and wherever the gusts come down the clouds run scattering across the sky — so wherever Turnus cut a path the ranks gave way and the lines turned and ran, and his own speed carried him on, and the wind caught the plume on his flying helmet and tossed it.

Phegeus could not bear that fury, that wild charge. He threw himself in front of the chariot and twisted the horses' bridled heads aside by main force. But while he hung there, dragged along by the yoke, the broad lance reached his unguarded side, pierced the double mesh of his corselet, and grazed the skin with the edge of the wound. Even so he turned, shield raised, and went for Turnus with his drawn sword, calling for help — and the wheel and the racing axle pitched him headlong to the ground, and Turnus came down on him between the helmet's lower rim and the high edge of the breastplate, and lopped the head off, and left the trunk on the sand.

And while Turnus was dealing death across the plain, Mnestheus and faithful Achates and Ascanius were bringing the bleeding Aeneas back into the camp, leaning on his spear at every other step. He raged, and tried to drag the broken arrow out by snapping the shaft, and begged them for the nearest cure — cut the wound wide open, find where the iron lay hidden, and send him back into the fighting. Now Iapyx came, son of Iasus, the man Apollo had loved above all others and once, glad in his love, offered his own gifts — augury, the lyre, the swift arrows. But Iapyx, to put off the death of his failing father, had chosen instead to know the power of herbs and the silent craft of healing. Aeneas stood there raging, propped on his great spear, with a crowd of soldiers and a weeping Ascanius around him, unmoved by their tears. The old man, his robe hitched back in the healer's way, worked over him with Apollo's strong herbs and his quick hand, but it was no use — no use to pull at the arrow with his fingers, no use to grip the iron with the pincers. No luck would guide his hand, and Apollo his master gave him no help, and meanwhile the dread of battle grew worse and worse across the field, and the danger came nearer. They could see the sky thick with dust now; the horsemen were closing in, and the spears were falling in the middle of the camp; and the grim cry of men fighting and falling under Mars's hard hand went up to the sky.

Then Venus, struck through by her son's undeserved pain, gathered dittany from Cretan Ida — a stalk with downy leaves and a crown of purple flowers, the herb the wild goats know well when a winged arrow has lodged in their flank. Venus brought it down, her face hidden in a dark cloud; she steeped it secretly in the shining water poured into the basin, and dropped in the healing sap of ambrosia and sweet-smelling cure-all. And old Iapyx, knowing nothing of it, bathed the wound with that water — and all at once the pain fled clean out of the body, and the blood stopped deep in the wound, and the arrow came away of itself into his hand, with no pull at all, and Aeneas's old strength came back to him whole. "Quick — his weapons! Why are you standing there? Bring the man his arms!" Iapyx cried, the first to fire their hearts against the enemy. "This was no work of mine, no human skill; my hand didn't heal you, Aeneas. A greater one, a god, is at work here, and sends you back to greater things." And Aeneas, hungry for the fight, had already cased his legs in gold to left and right, and was raging at the delay, brandishing his spear. When the shield sat right on his arm and the corselet on his back, he caught Ascanius up in an armored embrace and kissed him lightly through the helmet, and said: "Learn courage from me, my boy, and real toil; good luck from others. My hand will keep you safe in this war now and lead you on to great rewards. See that you remember, when your years are ripe; and looking back to your own kin for the example, let your father Aeneas and your uncle Hector stir your heart."

He flung the words out and strode through the gates, swinging the huge spear, and behind him in a dense column came Antheus and Mnestheus and the whole crowd, pouring out of the abandoned camp. The plain blurred with the blinding dust, and the ground shook and groaned under their tramping feet. Turnus saw them coming across the field; the Italians saw them, and a cold ran backward up their bones. Juturna heard it first of all the Latins, knew the sound, and fled trembling. Aeneas swept on, hurrying his dark column across the open ground, like a cloudburst rolling off the sea to land, that makes the farmers shudder far off in their hearts, knowing what is coming — the trees torn down, the crops flattened, the wide wreck of it — as the winds run ahead of the storm to the shore. The Trojan leader drove his men against the enemy, every man pressing close in a packed wedge. Thymbraeus killed huge Osiris with his sword; Mnestheus killed Arcetius, Achates Epulo, Gyas Ufens; and Tolumnius the augur fell too — the man who had thrown the first spear and broken the peace. The shout climbed to the sky, and now it was the Rutulians' turn to wheel and run, their backs to the dust across the field.

But Aeneas would not stoop to cut down the men who fled, nor go after the ones who stood and fought, foot or horse. Only Turnus he hunted, peering through the thick haze, only Turnus he called out to fight. Juturna, the warrior-girl, was sick with fear at it. She tumbled Metiscus, Turnus's charioteer, out of the chariot, between the reins, and left him sprawling far behind, and she herself took the reins, and the look and the voice and the armor of Metiscus. Like a black swallow flitting through the great house of a rich man, beating round the high halls and gathering scraps for her chattering nest, now under the empty porticoes, now over the wet ponds — so Juturna drove the team through the thick of the enemy, flying everywhere, showing her brother whirling past in triumph here and there, but never letting him close to fight, banking always away. And Aeneas wound through the same paths to meet him, hunting the man, calling his name through the broken ranks. Every time he caught sight of his enemy and tried to outrun the wind-swift horses on foot, that often Juturna swung the team away. What could he do? He was helpless, tossed on a churning tide, his mind torn this way and that by anger.

Then Messapus, who happened to be carrying two tough spears tipped with steel in his left hand, came at him at a run and aimed one, and let it fly true. Aeneas planted himself and dropped to one knee behind his shield, but even so the speeding spear carried off the top of his helmet and struck the high crest from his head. Now his anger really rose — driven to it by the treachery, seeing the chariot and the horses always wheeling away — and after calling Jupiter and the broken altars of the shattered peace to witness, he finally threw himself into the middle of them and let his terrible war loose, even-handed, every check off it, mowing them down. What god could unfold for me now all that bitterness, all the different deaths, the captains slaughtered up and down the field, this one driven off by Turnus, that one by the Trojan hero, by turns? Was it your will, Jupiter, to send two nations into so vast a wreck, who were going to live forever after in peace?

Aeneas struck Rutulian Sucro first — that fight first checked the Trojans' run — and the blow went in between the ribs, fast, to the bone-cage that guards the swift heart. Turnus, on foot, met Amycus thrown from his horse, and his brother Diores, and killed them both — one with the long spear as he came, the other with the sword — and hung their two severed heads from his chariot and carried them off dripping. Aeneas sent down Talos and Tanais and brave Cethegus, three at one charge, and grim Onites after them, a man of Echionian blood whose mother was Peridia. Turnus killed the two brothers Apollo had sent from Lycia, and young Menoetes, an Arcadian, who hated war and could not get away from it — Menoetes, who had plied his craft as a poor fisherman around the pools of Lerna and never known the doorways of the great, and whose father worked land that was not his own. Like two fires set loose from opposite sides into a dry wood, the bay-thickets crackling; or like two foaming rivers tearing down off the mountains, roaring to the sea, each cutting its own ruin across the fields — so Aeneas and Turnus tore through the fighting, and the storm of anger broke in them now as never before, and their hearts, that never learned to give way, burst open, and they put their whole strength into the wounds.

Murranus was bragging of his fathers and his fathers' fathers, the old Latin kings, when Aeneas brought him down with a great whirling rock and stretched him on the ground; and the wheels rolled him under the yoke-straps and the reins, and the galloping hooves of his own horses trampled him, with no memory of their master. Hyllus charged in roaring with terrible rage, and Turnus met him and drove the spear through his gilded forehead, so that it stuck fast in the brain through the helmet. Nor did your right hand, Cretheus, bravest of the Greeks, save you from Turnus; nor did his gods cover Cupencus when Aeneas came — he gave his chest to the steel, and the bronze of his poor shield could not hold off death for him. And the Laurentine plain saw you too, Aeolus, fall and spread your back wide on the ground — you, whom the Greek lines could not lay low, nor Achilles who overthrew the kingdom of Priam: here your end was set. You had a high house under Ida, a high house at Lyrnesus, and a grave in Laurentine soil.

Now the whole armies turned to face each other, all the Latins, all the Trojans — Mnestheus and fierce Serestus, Messapus the horse-tamer and brave Asilas, the Etruscan ranks and Evander's Arcadian squadrons — every man straining to the last of his strength and spirit, no holding back, no rest, the whole vast struggle locked. And here Aeneas's lovely mother put it in his mind to march on the city walls, to swing his column straight at the town and rock the Latins with the sudden blow. As he ranged through the fighting this way and that, tracking Turnus, his eye fell on the city sitting untouched by the great war, quiet and unpunished. At once the picture of a larger battle fired him. He called his captains — Mnestheus, Sergestus, brave Serestus — and took a rise of ground, and the rest of the Trojans crowded around him under arms, not laying down their shields or their spears. He stood high among them and said: "No delay on my words — Jupiter is with us. Don't let anyone hang back because this is sudden. That city today, the cause of the whole war, Latinus's own kingdom — unless they take the bridle and agree to obey, I'll level it, I'll lay its smoking roofs flat on the ground. Am I supposed to wait, I ask you, until it suits Turnus to be beaten and stand a duel again after he's lost? This is the head of it, citizens; this is the seat of the wicked war. Bring on the torches. Make the peace good with fire."

He finished, and with one will they all formed a wedge and pressed in a dense mass against the walls. Ladders appeared in a moment, and fire. Some ran for the gates and cut down the first defenders; others flung iron, and the sky went dark with the cloud of their spears. Aeneas himself, among the foremost, stretched his hand toward the wall and reproached Latinus to his face, calling the gods to witness that he was forced into the fight again — that twice now the Italians had been his enemies, twice broken faith. The townspeople fell to quarreling: some wanted to throw open the city and swing the gates wide to the Trojans, and even hauled the king himself toward the walls; others ran for their weapons and went to defend the parapets. As when a shepherd has tracked bees to their hive in a cleft of the rock and filled it with bitter smoke, and the bees inside, frightened for their fortress, race up and down the wax cells and whet their anger with a loud buzzing, and the black reek rolls through the chambers, and the rock hums blind inside with the noise, and the smoke pours out into the empty air — so it was in the town.

And now a fresh grief fell on the worn-out Latins, one that shook the whole city to its roots. The queen, from the roof of the palace, saw the enemy coming on, the walls under attack, the fire climbing the houses, and no Rutulian column anywhere, no sign of Turnus's men. She thought her young man had fallen in the fighting, and grief tore her mind apart in that one moment, and she screamed that she was the cause of it all, the source, the guilty one — wild, broken words, raving. And then, set on dying, she ripped her purple robe with her own hands, and knotted the noose of a hideous death to a high beam, and hanged herself. And when the wretched Latin women heard of it, Lavinia first of all clawed her flowering cheeks and tore her golden hair, and the whole crowd around her went mad with grief, and the long halls of the palace rang with the keening. The black news ran out through the whole city. Hearts sank. Latinus went with his clothes torn, stunned by his wife's death and his city's ruin, fouling his white hair with handfuls of dirt, blaming himself over and over that he had not taken the Trojan Aeneas in of his own will, long ago, and made him his son.

Out on the edge of the field Turnus was running down a few stragglers now, slower, less and less glad of his racing team. The wind carried him a cry, mixed and confused — a dim murmur, a sound of dread out of the unseen city. "What is that?" he said, and pulled hard on the reins, and stopped. And his sister, still in the charioteer Metiscus's shape, answered him: "This way, Turnus — let's keep hunting the Trojans, where the road to victory first opened. There are others who can hold the houses. Aeneas is wading into the Italians, slaughtering them. Let us deal death among the Trojans, no less. You'll come away from it no fewer in kills, and no less in honor." Turnus said: "Sister — I knew you a while ago, when you broke the peace by your own craft and threw yourself into this war, and now you don't fool me, goddess that you are. But who willed it that you should come down from heaven to bear all this? To watch your poor brother die so hard? What am I to do? What luck can promise me safety now? I saw Murranus go down before my own eyes, calling my name as he died, and no man was dearer to me. Mighty Ufens fell, so he wouldn't have to see my shame; the Trojans have his body and his arms. The houses are burning — that was the one thing left — and shall I bear it, and not give Drances the lie with this right hand? Shall I turn my back? Is this ground so terrible to die on? Be kind to me, you shades below, since the gods above have turned their faces away. I'll come down to you with my soul clean, never shamed, never unworthy of my great fathers."

He had barely said it when Saces came galloping through the thick of the enemy on a foaming horse, an arrow lodged in his face, and rode straight at Turnus calling his name: "Turnus — our last hope is in you. Pity your own people. Aeneas is a thunderbolt in arms, threatening to throw down the highest towers of Italy and burn them to the ground; the torches are flying onto the rooftops already. The Latins are looking to you, only you. King Latinus himself doesn't know which way to turn — whom to call his son, which terms to accept. And the queen, who trusted you most of all, has died by her own hand, and fled the daylight in terror. Only Messapus and quick Atinas are holding the line at the gates. Around them the enemy stands packed thick, a harvest of iron bristling with naked swords — and you wheel your chariot up and down an empty field." Turnus stood there stunned, struck dumb by the welter of it, the shame and the grief and the madness all churning in one heart, the love driven on by the goad of fury, and the sense of his own worth. As soon as the shadows broke and the light came back to his mind, he turned his blazing eyes toward the walls and looked back from the chariot at the great city. And there a tower of fire was rolling up through the floors to the sky, a tower he himself had built of jointed beams and set on wheels and laid with high gangways. "Now, sister, now — the Fates have won. No more holding me back. Let's go where the god calls, and hard fortune. I'm set on facing Aeneas; I'm set on bearing whatever bitterness there is in death. You won't see me shamed any longer, sister. Let me be mad this one last time, I beg you, before the end." He said it, and leapt down from the chariot onto the field, and burst through the enemy and their weapons, and left his grieving sister behind, and broke through the lines at a run.

Just as a boulder torn loose from a mountaintop comes hurtling down headlong — ripped free by the wind, or washed out by a storm of rain, or loosened by the slow years — and the wicked mass leaps and bounds down the slope with a great rush, rolling over the ground, and sweeps men and herds and forests with it: so Turnus tore through the scattering ranks to the city walls, where the ground was soaked deepest with blood and the air sang with spears, and he signaled with his hand and shouted: "Stop now, Rutulians — and you, Latins, hold your weapons. Whatever luck there is, it's mine. It's fairer that I alone make the broken treaty good and settle it with my sword." They all drew back from the middle and left him a space.

And father Aeneas, hearing Turnus's name, broke off from the walls, broke off the high towers, threw aside every other thing, every delay, in a burst of joy, and clashed his armor — terrible as Athos, terrible as Eryx, or as Father Apennine himself when he roars with his shaking oaks and lifts his snowy head into the sky and rejoices. Now Rutulians and Trojans and all the Italians turned to watch, the men who held the high walls and the men battering the foot of them, and they lifted the armor from their shoulders. Latinus himself stood amazed, that two great men, born at opposite ends of the world, had come together to settle it with the sword. And the two of them, the moment the field was clear, ran out and hurled their spears from far off, and then closed, hand to hand, the bronze ringing. The earth groaned. They doubled the sword-strokes, and chance and courage ran together in one. They were like two bulls on the great mountain Sila or high on Taburnus, charging head to head in war, while the frightened herdsmen back away and the whole herd stands dumb with dread, and the heifers wait to see which one will rule the woods, which one the whole herd will follow; and the bulls trade wound for wound with all their strength, and drive their horns in deep, and wash their necks and shoulders in their own streaming blood, and the whole forest bellows with their groans. So Trojan Aeneas and the hero of Daunus's line crashed their shields together, and the great noise of it filled the sky.

Jupiter himself held up the scales, balanced even, and laid the two men's fates in them — which one the labor would doom, which way the weight of death would fall. Now Turnus sprang forward, sure of himself, rose to his full height and brought the sword down with all his body behind it. The Trojans and the anxious Latins shouted; both armies strained forward. But the treacherous blade snapped at the stroke and left him in the middle of the blow — and only flight could save him now. He ran faster than the east wind when he saw the strange hilt, the unarmed grip, in his own hand. The story is that in his blind haste, when he first yoked his horses for the fight, he had caught up his charioteer Metiscus's sword instead of his father's blade — and for a long while it served, as long as the Trojans turned and ran; but when it met the god-forged armor of Vulcan, the mortal blade flew to pieces at the blow like brittle ice, and the shards glittered on the yellow sand. So Turnus ran wild across the plain, doubling back this way and that in tangled circles — for the Trojans had closed a tight ring around him, and a marsh hemmed him on one side and the high walls on the other.

And Aeneas, though the arrow-wound, so newly knit, slowed him and his knees sometimes failed under him, came after him no less, foot pressing hard on flying foot, the way a hound runs down a stag caught in a river-bend or fenced in by the hunters' line of crimson feathers — the dog driving in, barking, frantic, and the stag, frightened by the snares and the high bank, running a thousand ways and back again, while the keen Umbrian hound hangs at his haunches with open jaws, snapping, again and again gripping as if he had him, and clashing his teeth on nothing but air. Then the shouting rose, and the banks and the pools answered it, and the whole sky thundered with the noise. Turnus, even as he ran, was cursing all the Rutulians, calling each of them by name and begging for his own familiar sword. And Aeneas threatened death and ruin to any man who came near, terrifying the shaking people with the promise to raze their city, and pressed on though he was wounded. Five times they ran the whole circuit one way and five times back, this way and that — for it was no light prize they were running for, no game; this was for the life and the blood of Turnus.

There happened to stand on that ground a wild olive, bitter-leaved, sacred to Faunus — a tree the sailors used to honor, where, saved from the waves, they would nail their offerings to the Laurentine god and hang up their vowed clothes. But the Trojans had cut the holy trunk down, without a thought, to clear the field for the fight. In its stock Aeneas's spear now stood fixed; the throw had carried it there, and the tough root held it. He stooped over it and strained to wrench the iron loose, to bring down with the cast the man his feet could not catch. Then Turnus, wild with terror, cried out: "Faunus, pity me, I beg you — and you, kindest Earth, hold the iron fast, if I always kept your honor, the honor Aeneas's men have fouled in war." He said it, and called on the god's help, and not in vain. Aeneas wrestled and heaved at the stubborn stump a long while, but with all his strength he could not pry the wood's grip open. And while he strained and struggled, Juturna, taking the charioteer Metiscus's shape again, ran in and gave her brother back his sword. Venus, furious that the bold nymph was allowed so much, came and tore the spear free from the deep root. And the two of them, towering again, their weapons and their nerve restored, one trusting his sword, the other rearing up over his spear, stood face to face, breathing hard, in the duel of war.

Meanwhile the king of high Olympus said to Juno, who was watching the fight from a golden cloud: "How will it end, my wife? What is left now? You yourself know, and admit you know, that Aeneas is owed to heaven as a god of this land, and the Fates are lifting him to the stars. What are you hoping for? What keeps you sitting up here in the cold clouds? Was it right that a god should take a wound from mortal hands? Or that Turnus's sword should be handed back to him — for what could Juturna do without you? — and his strength returned to a beaten man? Stop now, I beg you. Let my asking move you. Don't let this grief eat away at you in silence; don't let your sweet mouth keep pouring this bitterness at me. The end has come. You were allowed to harry the Trojans over land and sea, to light an unspeakable war, to wreck a house and drown a wedding in grief. I forbid you to go further." So Jupiter spoke. And Juno, daughter of Saturn, lowered her eyes and answered: "It was because I knew your will, great Jupiter, that I left Turnus and the earth against my will. Otherwise you wouldn't see me here, alone on this seat of air, swallowing what I deserve and what I don't; I'd be down there, ringed in fire, in the front of the lines, dragging the Trojans into the deadly fighting. I told Juturna to help her wretched brother, I admit it, and I allowed her to dare greater things to save his life — but not to bend the bow, not to throw the spear; I swear it by the pitiless wellspring of the Styx, the one oath the gods above must keep. And now I give way; I leave these battles, sick of them. But one thing I beg you, that no law of fate forbids — for Latium, for the greatness of your own people: when at last they make the peace with a happy marriage — so be it — when they join their laws and their treaties, don't make the old native Latins change their name, or become Trojans, or be called Teucrians; don't make the men change their language or their dress. Let there be a Latium; let there be Alban kings down the ages; let there be a Roman stock made strong by Italian courage. Troy has fallen — let it stay fallen, name and all."

And the maker of men and the world answered her, smiling: "You are Jupiter's own sister, and Saturn's other child — that's the surge of anger rolling so deep in your breast. But come, let go this rage that started all of it for nothing; I grant what you want, and I yield, glad to be beaten. The Italians will keep their fathers' tongue and their ways, and the name will stay as it is. The Trojans will only sink into the mass, mingled in blood. I'll add the manner and the rites of their worship, and make them all Latins, of one speech. The race that rises from this, mixed with Italian blood, you'll see climb above all men, above the gods even, in devotion; and no other nation will keep your honors so faithfully." Juno nodded, and her heart turned glad, and she changed her mind, and left the cloud and the sky.

That done, the Father turned over in his mind another thing — to take Juturna away from her brother's side. There are twin scourges men call the Dread Ones, born in one birth with infernal Night, and the same dark womb bore the third, Megaera of Tartarus. Night wound them about with the coils of writhing snakes and gave them wings of wind. They wait at Jupiter's throne, on the threshold of the angry king, and sharpen the fears of sick mortals whenever the lord of the gods deals out hideous death and disease, or terrifies guilty cities with war. One of these Jupiter sent flying down from the height of heaven, and ordered her to meet Juturna as an omen. She flew, and swept to earth in a black whirlwind, like an arrow shot through a cloud from the string, a Parthian arrow dipped in poison-gall — a wound past curing — hissing from the hand of a Parthian or a Cretan through the deep shadow. So flew the daughter of Night and made for the earth. And when she saw the Trojan ranks and Turnus's lines, she shrank all at once into the shape of the small bird that perches by night on tombs and lonely roofs and sings its late, ill-boding song through the dark. And shrunk to that, the fiend flew screeching again and again into Turnus's face, and beat his shield with her wings.

A strange numbness loosened his limbs with dread; his hair stood up in horror, and the voice stuck in his throat. But Juturna, far off, knew the whir and the wings of the fiend, and tore her loosened hair, and clawed her face, and beat her breast with her fists. "Turnus — how can your sister help you now? What is left for me, hard as I've been? What art can keep you in the light? Can I stand against a thing like that? Now, now I leave the field. Don't frighten me, foul birds — I'm afraid enough. I know the beat of your wings, that sound of death; I'm not deaf to great-hearted Jupiter's cruel orders. Is this his payment for my lost girlhood? Why did he give me life forever? Why was the right to die taken from me? Surely I could end such grief now, and go down with my poor brother through the shadows. Immortal — me? Will anything of mine be sweet to me, with you gone, brother? What earth will gape deep enough to send a goddess down to the dead?" That was all she said, and she drew her grey-green veil over her head, and groaning long, the goddess hid herself in the deep river.

And Aeneas came on, brandishing the great tree of his spear, flashing, and said with a hard heart: "What's the delay now, Turnus? Why hang back? This is no footrace; it's a fight at close quarters, with hard weapons. Turn yourself into every shape there is, gather up everything courage or craft can do, wish for the wings to fly to the high stars or to bury yourself in the hollow earth." Turnus shook his head and said: "It isn't your hot words that frighten me, fierce as you are. It's the gods that frighten me, and Jupiter against me." He said no more, but looked around and saw a huge stone, an old huge stone that happened to lie there in the field, set up to settle the boundary of the plowland and stop the quarrels over it. Twelve picked men, the kind earth bears these days, could barely have hoisted it onto their shoulders. He caught it up in a shaking hand and rose to his height and hurled it, running, at his enemy. But running, hurling, lifting the great stone and moving it, he did not know himself: his knees buckled, his blood ran cold and froze. And the stone itself, rolling through the empty air, fell short — it did not cross the whole distance, and it carried no force in the blow. It was like a dream, when sleep weighs the eyes shut at night and we struggle to run on, hungry to, and fail in the middle of the trying and sink down sick; the tongue has no power, the body's old strength will not answer, no voice and no words will come. So it was with Turnus: whatever way his courage looked for a road, the dread goddess shut it off. The thoughts whirled in his breast. He looked at the city and the Rutulians, hung back in fear, began to shake at the death coming for him. He saw nowhere to run, no force to drive at the enemy, no chariot anywhere, no sister at the reins.

As he wavered, Aeneas aimed the deadly spear, watched for his chance, and threw it with his whole weight from far off. No stone hurled by a siege-engine ever roared so loud, no thunderbolt ever cracked the way it cracked. The spear flew like a black whirlwind, carrying grim death, and tore through the rim of the corselet and the outermost ring of the seven-layered shield, and screamed on through the middle of the thigh. Turnus went down, struck, huge, his knee folding under him, to the ground. The Rutulians sprang up with a groan, and the whole hill groaned it back, and the high woods all around threw the sound of it home. And there, low on the ground, a suppliant, lifting his eyes and stretching out his pleading hand, Turnus said: "I've earned it. I won't beg you off. Use your luck. But if any thought of a wretched father can touch you — and you too once had such a father, Anchises — then pity Daunus's old age, and give me back to my people, or my body stripped of life, if you'd rather. You've won. The Italians have seen me beaten, stretching out my hands. Lavinia is your wife. Don't push your hatred further."

Fierce in his armor, Aeneas stood, his eyes moving — and held his hand. And more and more, as Turnus's words began to work on him, he hung back, hesitating. And then he saw it: high on the man's shoulder, the unlucky sword-belt, the studs Aeneas knew flashing on it — the belt of the boy Pallas, whom Turnus had beaten and cut down, and whose belt he wore now on his shoulders, the badge of his enemy. Aeneas drank in the sight of it, the trophy, the wound that would never heal, and then, on fire with fury, terrible in his rage: "You — dressed in the spoils of the men I loved — you think you'll get away from me now? It's Pallas, Pallas who strikes you down with this wound, and takes his payment from your guilty blood." Saying it, blazing, he drove the iron full into the chest before him. And the cold of death loosened Turnus's limbs, and life fled with a groan, resentful, down to the shades.
