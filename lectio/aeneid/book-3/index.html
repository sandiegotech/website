The Aeneid, Book III

The Wanderings, and the Death of Anchises

Book III3 of 12

When the gods had seen fit to bring down the power of Asia and Priam's people, who had not deserved it, when proud Ilium had fallen and all of Neptune's Troy lay smoking on the ground, the omens of heaven drove us out to seek some far-off, empty land for a home. So below Antandros, at the foot of Mount Ida, we built a fleet, with no idea yet where the Fates would let us settle or where we were allowed to stop. We gathered our men. Summer had barely begun when my father Anchises gave the word to trust our sails to fate. And weeping, I left the shores of my country, the harbor, the plains where Troy had been. I went out onto the deep an exile, with my comrades and my son, with my household gods and the great gods of our people.

Far off lay a wide land of war, the country of the Thracians, with broad plowed fields, once ruled by fierce Lycurgus, and long a friend to Troy, our gods joined to theirs, as long as our luck held. I sailed there, and on that curving shore I laid out the walls of a first city, though fate was already against me, and I called it Aeneadae, after my own name. I was making offerings to my mother Venus and to the gods who bless a new beginning, and killing a sleek white bull on the altar to the high king of heaven. Close by there happened to be a mound, crowned with a thicket of cornel and bristling myrtle. I went up to it and tried to tear some green branches from the ground, to roof the altar in leaves — and I saw a thing horrible to tell, beyond belief. From the first bush I pulled up, the roots tore loose and drops of black blood oozed from them and stained the earth. A cold shudder went through me; my blood froze with terror. Still I tried again, pulling at the stubborn stem of a second, to find out what was hidden underneath — and from the second too the blood ran dark.

I turned it over in my mind, full of dread, and prayed to the nymphs of those fields and to Father Mars, who watches over the land of Thrace, asking them to make the sign harmless and lift the curse. But when I set my knees against the ground and strained at a third shoot, harder still — I can hardly say it — a groan came up out of the depths of the mound, and a voice reached my ears: "Aeneas, why do you tear at me? I am buried here — spare me. Don't stain your faithful hands. I am no stranger to you: I was born in Troy, and this blood you see is not running from a tree. Flee this cruel country, get away from this greedy shore. I am Polydorus. An iron harvest of spears pinned me down here, and out of them this thicket of sharp shafts has grown."

Then I stood dumb, my mind crushed under the weight of fear, my hair standing up, my voice locked in my throat. This Polydorus — long ago, when Troy was already failing, old Priam had sent him in secret to the king of Thrace to be raised, loaded with a great weight of gold, because Priam had given up hope in the Trojan cause and was sick at heart watching the city under siege. And the Thracian, when Troy's power broke and her fortune left her, went over to the winning side, to Agamemnon. He shattered every law of decency; he murdered Polydorus and seized the gold. There is nothing the cursed hunger for gold will not drive men to do. When the fear had drained out of me, I brought the omens of the gods before our leaders, my father first, and asked them what they thought. They were of one mind: leave this poisoned land, this place where the bond of a host had been so foully broken, and let the south wind carry the fleet away. So first we held a funeral for Polydorus. We heaped fresh earth high over the mound and raised altars to his ghost, dark with mourning bands and black cypress; the Trojan women stood around with their hair loose in the way of grief. We poured out bowls of warm milk and dishes of holy blood, laid the soul to rest in its tomb, and called out his name one last time.

Then, as soon as we could trust the sea, when the winds had quieted the waves and a soft southerly was calling us to the deep, my men hauled the ships down to the water and crowded the shore. We left the harbor; the lands and cities slid away behind us.

There is an island worshiped in the middle of the sea, dear to the mother of the sea-nymphs and to Neptune of the Aegean. Once it drifted from coast to coast, until the dutiful Archer-god, Apollo, chained it fast between two of its sisters, Myconos and Gyaros, and gave it a stillness it could keep — a place that laughs at the winds. There the sea carried us, and there the calm waters offered our weary ships a safe harbor at last. We came ashore and bowed to Apollo's city.

Anius, who was both king of the people and priest of Apollo, came out to meet us, his brow bound with the sacred fillets and the laurel of the god. He knew my father at once, an old friend, and we joined hands and went into his house. I went to the god's temple, built of ancient stone, and prayed: "God of Thymbra, give us a home of our own. Give a tired people walls, a line of descendants, a city that will last. Keep alive this second Troy, this remnant the Greeks and merciless Achilles left us. Whom should we follow? Where do you send us? Where do you tell us to settle? Father, give us a sign, and come down into our hearts." I had barely finished when everything seemed to shake at once — the doorway and the laurel of the god, the whole mountain heaving, the tripod groaning as the inner shrine swung open. We dropped to the ground in awe, and a voice came: "Hard-enduring sons of Dardanus, the land that first bore your fathers' line will take you back again to her glad breast. Go, search out your ancient mother. There the house of Aeneas will rule over all the world's shores, and his children's children, and the children born to them." So Apollo spoke, and a great wave of joy broke loose with our shouting, and everyone asked: what city is this? Where is the god sending us, telling us to come home?

Then my father, turning over in his mind the old stories of our people, said: "Listen, leaders of Troy, and learn where your hope lies. There is an island in the middle of the sea, Crete, the island of great Jupiter, where another Mount Ida stands — the cradle of our race. Men live in a hundred great cities there, the richest of kingdoms. From there, if I remember the tale right, our first father Teucer sailed to the Trojan shore and chose the site of his kingdom, before Ilium had walls or the towers of Troy stood; from there came our way of life, and the worship of the Mother of the gods of Mount Cybele, and the bronze cymbals of her rites, and the grove sacred to her, and the lions yoked beneath her chariot. So come — let us follow where the gods lead. Let us calm the winds with sacrifice and make for the harbors of Crete. It is no long voyage: if Jupiter is with us, the third dawn will set our fleet on the Cretan shore." With that he laid the proper offerings on the altars — a bull to Neptune, a bull to you, beautiful Apollo, a black sheep to the storm-god and a white one to the kindly west winds.

A rumor flew that King Idomeneus had been driven out of his father's kingdom of Crete and gone into exile, so the coast lay empty, the houses abandoned, a home standing ready for us. We left the harbor of Delos and flew over the open water, past Naxos with its wine-dark ridges where the worshipers of Bacchus dance, past green Donusa, Olearos, snow-white Paros, and the Cyclades scattered across the sea, threading the crowded channels among the islands. The sailors' cries rose as they bent to the work, and they urged each other on — "For Crete! For our fathers' country!" A following wind sprang up astern and drove us, and at last we came in to the ancient shores of Crete. There, eager, I laid out the walls of the city I had longed for and named it Pergamea, after the citadel of Troy, and I told my people, glad to hear it, to love their new home and raise a fortress over it. The ships were already drawn up on the dry sand; the young men were busy with marriages and new-plowed fields, and I was handing down laws and houses — when out of a rotten quarter of the sky a wasting sickness fell on us, a plague on our bodies and on the trees and the crops, a whole season of death. Men gave up their sweet lives, or dragged themselves along sick; the Dog Star scorched the fields barren, the grass withered, the blighted harvest gave us no food. My father told me to go back across the sea to Delos and Apollo's oracle, to beg the god for mercy: where would he set an end to our suffering, where did he tell us to turn for help, which way should we steer?

It was night, and sleep had taken hold of every living thing on earth. The sacred images of the gods, the household gods of Troy, which I had carried out of the burning city through the flames, stood before me as I lay there — clear and bright in the full light of the moon as it poured through the windows. And they spoke to me, and lifted the weight off my heart: "What Apollo would tell you if you sailed all the way to Delos, he tells you here instead, and sends us to your door to say it. We followed you out of Troy when it burned, with you we crossed the swelling sea in your ships, and we will lift your children yet unborn up to the stars and give your city power over the world. Build great walls for great men. Don't give up the long road, the labor of wandering. You must change your home. This is not the coast Apollo of Delos meant for you; he never told you to settle on Crete. There is a country the Greeks call Hesperia, an old land, strong in war and rich in soil; the Oenotrians lived there, but now, men say, a later people have named it Italy, after their leader. That is our true home. There Dardanus was born, and Father Iasius, the first root of our race. Get up. Go gladly to your old father and tell him there is no mistaking it now: he must look for Italy. Jupiter forbids you the fields of Crete."

I was stunned by the vision and the voices of the gods — for this was no dream; I seemed to know their faces there in front of me, the sacred bands in their hair, their living features. A cold sweat broke out all over me. I sprang up from the bed and lifted my hands to heaven with my prayers and poured an offering of unmixed wine on the hearth-fire. Glad to have made the rite, I went to Anchises and laid the whole thing out for him in order. He saw the truth of it at once — that our line came from two stocks, two ancestors, and that he had been deceived by the old confusion of names. He said: "My son, you whom the fate of Troy has driven so hard — Cassandra alone foretold all this to me. I remember now how she kept naming Hesperia for our people, kept naming the kingdoms of Italy. But who then believed a Trojan would ever reach the shores of Hesperia? And who paid any attention to Cassandra's prophecies? Let us give way to Apollo, and take the better road he shows us." So he spoke, and we all obeyed him gladly.

We left that place too, leaving a few of our people behind, and spread our sails and ran before the wind over the wide and empty sea. When the ships had reached deep water, with no land left in sight anywhere — only sky on every side and sea on every side — a black storm-cloud massed over my head, bringing night and foul weather, and the waves shuddered in the dark. At once the winds churned the sea and threw up huge swells; we were scattered far apart across the heaving water. Clouds smothered the day, and the wet night blotted out the sky; lightning split the clouds again and again. We were knocked off our course and wandered blind across the deep. Even Palinurus, our helmsman, swore he could not tell day from night in the sky and had lost the way in the middle of the sea. For three whole days, as far as we could judge in the blind dark, we drifted on the water, and for three nights without a star. On the fourth day at last we saw land begin to rise — distant hills, and threads of smoke going up. We dropped the sails and bent to the oars, and the sailors whipped the blue water into foam.

The shore that took me, saved from the waves, was the Strophades, as the Greeks name them, set in the great Ionian Sea, the dreaded home of Celaeno and the other Harpies, ever since they were shut out of Phineus's house and fled in fear from the tables they used to foul. No fouler monster than these, no crueler plague, no curse of the gods, ever rose from the waters of the underworld. They have the faces of girls and the bellies of filthy birds; their hands are hooked claws; their faces are always pale with a hunger nothing can fill.

When we put in there, we saw fat herds of cattle scattered across the fields and flocks of goats grazing the grass with no one tending them. We fell on them with our swords, and called the gods and Jupiter himself to take a share of the spoils. Then we built up couches on the curving beach and feasted on the rich meat. But all at once, swooping down from the mountains with a horrible beating of wings, the Harpies came, and tore at the food, and fouled everything they touched with their filthy claws, and through the sickening stench came their dreadful screams. So we moved off, deep under a hollow rock walled in by trees and trembling shadows, and laid the tables again and lit the altar fires fresh. And again, out of another quarter of the sky, out of their hidden roosts, the screeching pack came down on the prey with their hooked claws and slimed our meal with their mouths. Then I told my men: arm yourselves, we will make war on this foul brood. They did as I said. They hid their swords in the grass and laid their shields out of sight, and when the Harpies came swooping along the curving shore with their loud wings, Misenus from a high lookout sounded the charge on his bronze horn. My men attacked — a strange kind of battle, to gash these filthy sea-birds with the sword. But no blow could pierce their feathers or wound their backs; they shot up into the sky and left the prey half-eaten and the foul tracks behind them.

Only one stayed, Celaeno, perched high on a crag, a prophetess of doom, and these words burst from her: "Sons of Laomedon — first you slaughter our cattle, now you are ready to make war on us and drive us, who have done nothing wrong, out of our own country? Then take these words of mine and fix them in your hearts. What the all-powerful Father foretold to Apollo, and Apollo to me, I, the eldest of the Furies, now reveal to you. Italy is the land you are steering for; you call on the winds, and to Italy you will go, and you will be allowed into her harbors. But you will not wall in the city you have been promised until a terrible hunger and the wrong you have done us drive you to gnaw and devour your very tables." She finished, and beat her wings and fled back into the forest. The blood froze in my men; their courage broke; they wanted no more war, but begged me to ask the gods' pardon with prayers and offerings — whether these things were goddesses or only foul and monstrous birds. And my father Anchises, on the shore, stretched out his open hands and called on the great gods and named the offerings owed them: "Gods, turn this threat aside! Gods, keep this curse from falling! Be kind, and save a people who keep faith with you." Then he told us to wrench the cables from the shore and let out the sails. The south wind stretched the canvas, and we ran over the foaming water wherever the wind and the helmsman carried us.

Now the wooded island of Zacynthos rose from the waves, and Dulichium, and Same, and Neritos with its sheer cliffs. We steered clear of the reefs of Ithaca, the kingdom of Laertes, and cursed the land that bred cruel Ulysses. Soon the cloud-wrapped peak of Leucata came into view, and Apollo's headland that sailors dread. Worn out, we made for it and put in to the little town. We dropped anchor from the prows and beached the sterns. And so, having reached dry land at last, beyond all hope, we cleansed ourselves before Jupiter and burned our offerings on his altars, and held games on the shore of Actium in the old Trojan way. My men stripped, slick with oil, and wrestled in the contests of home — glad to have slipped past so many Greek cities and held our course through the heart of enemy country. Meanwhile the sun rolled through the full circle of the year, and icy winter roughened the sea with its north winds. I nailed up on the temple doors a shield of hollow bronze that great Abas had once carried, and marked the gift with a line of verse: AENEAS DEDICATES THESE ARMS, TAKEN FROM THE CONQUERING GREEKS. Then I gave the order to leave the harbor and man the oars. My men struck the sea and swept it white, and quickly we lost sight of the heights of Phaeacia, skirted the coast of Epirus, and ran into the harbor of Chaonia, up to the high city of Buthrotum.

There an unbelievable rumor reached us: that Helenus, a son of Priam, was ruling Greek cities, that he had taken the throne and the wife of Pyrrhus, son of Achilles, and that Andromache had passed once more to a husband of her own people. I was astonished, and my heart burned to speak to the man and learn the strange story. So I left the ships in the harbor and walked up from the shore. As it happened, I found Andromache in a grove outside the city, by the waters of a stream they called by the name of Simois, pouring out the yearly offerings, the sad gifts to the dead, at an empty tomb of green turf she had raised to Hector, with two altars, a place where she could weep. When she saw me coming, and saw Trojan arms around me, she went out of her mind with terror at the impossible sight, and stiffened in the middle of staring at me, the warmth going out of her body. She nearly fell, and only after a long silence did she find her voice: "Is it really you, alive — a true face, a true messenger, son of the goddess? Are you living? Or if the kind light has left you, then where is my Hector?" She said it, and broke into tears, and filled the whole grove with her crying. There was little I could say to her grief; shaken, I managed only a few broken words: "I am alive — alive through every kind of misery. Don't doubt it; what you see is real. What has become of you, fallen so far from such a husband? Or has a fortune worthy of you found you again? Hector's Andromache — are you still the wife of Pyrrhus?"

She lowered her eyes and answered in a low voice: "Happiest of all was Priam's virgin daughter, ordered to die on an enemy's tomb, under the high walls of Troy — who never had to draw lots for a master, never had to lie a captive in the bed of a conqueror. We, our city burned, were carried over far seas, and I bore the arrogance of Achilles' son, that boy in his pride, and gave him a child in slavery. Then he chased after Hermione, Helen's daughter, a marriage among Spartans, and handed me, his slave, to Helenus, his slave, to keep. But Orestes, on fire for the bride that had been stolen from him, and driven mad by the Furies for his own crimes, caught Pyrrhus off his guard and cut him down at his father's altar. When Pyrrhus died, part of his kingdom passed to Helenus, who named these plains Chaonia, and this whole country Chaonia, after Chaon of Troy, and built a Pergamum, a Trojan citadel, on the ridge. But you — what winds, what fates set you on this course? What god drove you, not knowing it, onto our shores? And the boy Ascanius — does he live? Does he still breathe the air? Born to you while Troy still stood... Does the boy grieve at all for the mother he lost? Do his father Aeneas and his uncle Hector fire him toward the old Trojan courage, the old manhood?" She poured all this out weeping, raising long sobs that came to nothing, when Helenus, the hero, son of Priam, came down from the city walls with a great company.

He knew his own people, and led us joyfully up to his gates, weeping freely as he spoke at every word. As I walked I recognized a little Troy, a copy of the great citadel, and a dry streambed they called Xanthus, and I threw my arms around the posts of a Scaean Gate. The other Trojans, too, took their pleasure in the friendly city. The king received them in his wide colonnades; in the middle of the hall they poured offerings of wine and held the cups while the feast was set on gold. Day followed day, and the breezes called us to the sails; the south wind bellied out the canvas. So I went to the prophet-king and put my questions to him: "Son of Troy, voice of the gods — you who know the will of Apollo, the tripod, the laurel of the god, the stars and the language of the birds and the omens of their flight — tell me. Every sign has promised me a fortunate voyage; all the gods have urged me by their will to make for Italy and try those distant shores. Only Celaeno the Harpy sings a strange new horror, an unspeakable curse, and threatens a black famine. What dangers should I shun first? What must I do to ride out so much hardship?"

Helenus first sacrificed the bullocks in the proper way and begged the gods' favor; then he loosed the bands from his holy head and led me by the hand to the threshold of Apollo's temple, my mind trembling at the nearness of the god, and from his prophet's mouth he sang: "Son of the goddess — for it is beyond all doubt that you sail the deep under the high blessing of heaven; the king of the gods deals out the fates this way and rolls events through their turns; such is the order of things — I will tell you a few things out of many, so you can cross the strange seas more safely and find a berth at last in an Italian harbor. The rest the Fates forbid Helenus to know, and Juno will not let me speak. First, the Italy you think so near, whose ports you mean to make as if they were close at hand — a long, trackless distance of sea keeps it far off and shuts it away from you. You must bend your oars in the waters off Sicily, and cross the salt plain of the Italian sea, and pass the lakes of the underworld and the island of Circe before you can build your city on safe ground. I will give you the signs; hold them in your heart. When you are anxious, and you find by the waters of a hidden river, under the oaks along its bank, a great white sow stretched on the ground with a litter of thirty white piglets around her teats — that is the place for your city, that is the sure rest from your labors. And don't shrink from the threat of gnawing your tables; the Fates will find a way, and Apollo will be there when you call him.

"But these nearer shores of Italy, the coast washed by our own sea — keep away from them; every town is held by hostile Greeks. The Locrians of Naryx have walled a city there; Idomeneus has filled the Sallentine plains with his soldiers; there stands the little fortress of Petelia, raised by Philoctetes of Meliboea, trusting its walls. And when your fleet has crossed and lies anchored, and you raise the altars to pay your vows on the shore — veil your hair with a purple cloth, so that in the worship of the gods no hostile face can cross your sight and break the omens. Keep this way of covering the head, you and your men; let your children's children hold to it as a sacred rule.

"When you leave that place, and the wind carries you up to the coast of Sicily, and the narrows of Pelorus open out — steer for the land on your left, and the long sea on your left, and keep wide of the shore and the waters on your right. These two, men say, were once one country, until an age long ago tore them apart with a vast convulsion — time can work such change — and the sea rushed in with force between them and cut Italy off from Sicily, and now a narrow channel washes the towns and fields along both sundered coasts. On the right side waits Scylla; on the left, merciless Charybdis, who three times a day sucks the whole sea sheer down into the pit of her whirlpool and three times spews it back up to lash the stars. But Scylla hides in a blind cave, and thrusts out her mouths to drag ships onto the rocks. From the waist up she has the look of a girl, a fair-breasted woman; below, she is a sea-monster of huge size, with dolphins' tails joined to a belly ringed with wolves. Far better to lose time and go the long way around Cape Pachynus, swinging wide, than to catch one glimpse of misshapen Scylla in her vast cave, or hear the rocks ring with her sea-blue hounds.

"And one thing more — if Helenus has any foresight, if a prophet is to be trusted, if Apollo fills his heart with truth — one thing above all I will tell you, son of the goddess, and tell you again and again: pray to great Juno first; pray to her with all your heart. Win the powerful queen over with willing vows and gifts, and bend her to you with prayers. Only so, with Sicily behind you, will you be sent across to the borders of Italy. And once you are landed there, when you come to the town of Cumae, to the haunted lakes and the rustling forest of Avernus, you will find a prophetess in her frenzy, deep in a cave below the rock, who chants the fates and writes their words and names on leaves. Whatever verses she writes on the leaves she sets in order and shuts away in her cave; they lie still and keep their place. But if the door swings open and the smallest breath of wind stirs them, scattering the tender leaves through the hollow rock, she never tries to catch them again as they fly, never puts the verses back in order; her visitors go away with no answer, cursing the Sibyl's cave.

"No matter how your men grudge you the delay, no matter how loudly the voyage calls and the fair wind fills your sails — beg the prophetess to chant the fates aloud, in her own voice, and to unseal her lips and speak. She will tell you the peoples of Italy, the wars to come, and how to flee each hardship or bear it, and if you honor her she will grant you a safe passage. This is all I am allowed to warn you of. Go now, and lift the greatness of Troy to the stars by all you do."

When the prophet had said this in his kindness, he had heavy gifts of gold and carved ivory carried down to my ships, and stowed great weights of silver in the holds — cauldrons of Dodona, a coat of mail triple-woven with hooks of gold, and a splendid helmet with a flowing crest of horsehair, the armor of Pyrrhus himself. He had gifts for my father too. He found us horses and guides, filled out the crews, and fitted my comrades with arms.

Meanwhile Anchises was telling us to rig the sails and not waste the favoring wind. And the prophet of Apollo spoke to him with great respect: "Anchises — you whom Venus thought worthy of her proud love, twice cared for by the gods, twice saved from the wreck of Troy — there before you lies the land of Italy; sail for it. And yet you must coast far past this nearer part of it; the Italy Apollo opens to you is far away. Go on, blessed in the love your son bears you. Why do I say more, and hold you back while the south wind rises?" Andromache, too, sad at this last parting, brought robes worked with figures of gold and a Trojan cloak for Ascanius, and would not be outdone in kindness; she loaded him with gifts of woven cloth and said: "Take these as well, my child — keepsakes made by my own hands, tokens of Hector's Andromache and her lasting love. Take the last gifts of your own people, you who are all that is left to me now of my Astyanax. He had your eyes, your hands, your face; he would be growing up beside you now, a boy your own age."

As I turned to go, the tears welled up, and I said: "Live happily, you whose destiny is already complete. We are called from one fate into another. For you, peace is won; you have no sea to plow, no ever-retreating fields of Italy to chase. You look on an image of the river Xanthus, on a Troy your own hands built — under a better star, I pray, and out of the path of the Greeks. If I ever reach the Tiber and the fields along its banks, if I ever see the walls granted to my people, then someday we will make our two cities, our two kindred nations, one in spirit — Hesperia and Epirus, both sprung from Dardanus, both broken by the same fate — one Troy in two hearts. Let our children keep that bond forever."

We sailed on, past the Ceraunian heights, where the crossing to Italy is shortest by sea. Meanwhile the sun went down and the hills lay dark in shadow. We drew lots for the oars and stretched out on the lap of the welcome land at the water's edge, and slept along the dry shore, our bodies eased with rest. Night, driven by the Hours, had not yet climbed to her height when Palinurus, never one to sleep on watch, got up to read the winds and listen for every breath of air, marking all the stars sliding through the silent sky — Arcturus, the rainy Hyades, the two Bears, and Orion in his golden armor. When he saw the whole sky calm and settled, he gave the clear signal from the stern; we broke camp, set out, and spread the wings of our sails.

And now, as Dawn reddened and put the stars to flight, we saw far off the dim blue hills and the low line of Italy. Achates was the first to call out "Italy!" and my men shouted "Italy!" with a roar of joy. Then my father Anchises wreathed a great bowl with garlands, filled it with unmixed wine, and standing high on the stern called the gods: "You gods who rule the sea and the land and the storms, give us an easy run with the wind, and breathe kindly on us!" The wind freshened as he prayed; the harbor opened nearer, and a temple of Minerva came into view on the heights. My men furled the sails and swung the prows toward shore. The harbor is curved like a bow against the eastern surge, with two jutting reefs that foam with spray and hide the haven within; the temple drew back and seemed to flee inland behind its sheltering rocks. There the first omen I saw was four horses, white as snow, grazing the wide grass. And my father Anchises said: "War — that is what you bring us, land of strangers; horses are armed for war, and these herds threaten war. Yet these same beasts can be broken to the chariot and made to take the bit and pull in harness, side by side; so there is hope of peace as well." Then we prayed to the holy power of Pallas of the clashing arms, who was the first to receive us in our joy, and we veiled our heads before the altar with Trojan cloths, and following Helenus's strict command we burned the proper offerings to Argive Juno. Without delay, the moment our vows were paid, we turned the tips of our sail-clad yards out to sea and left those shores, that ground we could not trust, the homes of Greeks.

From there we sighted the bay of Tarentum, the city of Hercules, if the story is true, and across from it the temple of the Lacinian goddess rose, and the towers of Caulon, and Scylaceum, the wrecker of ships. Then, far off across the waves, Mount Etna came into view, and we heard the great groan of the sea hammering the rocks, the broken voices on the shore, the surge leaping in the shallows and the swell churned up thick with sand. My father Anchises cried: "This must be the Charybdis the prophet warned of — these are the rocks, the terrible reefs Helenus foretold! Pull away, men, all together — lay on the oars!" They did as he said. Palinurus was the first to throw the groaning prow to port and out to sea, and the whole fleet bent that way under oar and wind. The swell heaved us up to the sky on its curling back, then the trough sank away beneath us down to the underworld. Three times the cliffs roared in their hollow caves; three times we saw the spray burst and run down dripping from the stars. Meanwhile the wind dropped and the sun set, and worn out, not knowing the way, we drifted in to the shore of the Cyclopes.

The harbor itself is wide and untroubled by any wind, but close beside it Etna thunders in ruin. Now it heaves a black cloud up to the sky, a tower of pitch-dark smoke shot through with glowing ash, and throws up balls of fire that lick at the stars; now it spews out boulders, the very entrails torn from the mountain, and groaning, hurls molten rock into the air, and boils up from its deepest pit. The story is that the giant Enceladus, his body half-burned by Jupiter's bolt, lies pinned under all that mass, with mighty Etna piled on top of him breathing flame through its burst vents; and as often as he rolls his weary side, the whole of Sicily shudders and groans and a pall of smoke veils the sky. All that night we hid in the woods, enduring the monstrous terrors, with no way of seeing what made the noise. For there was no fire of the stars, no clear vault lit by the constellations; the sky was thick with cloud, and the dead of night held the moon shut behind it.

The next day was just breaking, the first sun chasing the wet mist from the sky, when out of the woods, suddenly, a strange shape of a man came toward us, worn to nothing, in pitiful rags, his hands stretched out to the shore in supplication. We stared. He was filthy past belief, his beard wild, his clothes pinned together with thorns — but a Greek for all that, one of those who had once sailed against Troy in the war. When he saw the Trojan dress and the Trojan arms in the distance, he froze a moment at the sight, then broke and rushed down to the shore in tears and prayers: "By the stars, I beg you, by the gods above, by this clear air we all breathe — take me away, Trojans, carry me off to any land at all, and that will be enough. I admit I am one of the Greeks who sailed against Troy; I admit I made war on your gods. For that, if my crime is so great, scatter me piece by piece across the waves, drown me in the wide sea — I would rather die, if die I must, at the hands of men." He had thrown himself at our knees and clung there, groveling. We pressed him to say who he was, of what blood, and what fortune had hunted him here. My father Anchises, after only a moment, gave the young man his hand and steadied his courage with the plain pledge of it. At last, his fear set aside, he told us his story:

"I am from Ithaca, a comrade of luckless Ulysses, and my name is Achaemenides. My father was Adamastus, a poor man — and I wish I had stayed poor at home. I sailed for Troy, and here my shipmates left me behind, forgotten in the huge cave of the Cyclops as they bolted in terror from that cruel door. It is a house of gore and bloody feasts, vast and dark inside. The monster himself is so tall he strikes the high stars — gods, take such a plague off the earth! — no man can bear to look at him or speak to him. He gorges on the flesh and the black blood of the wretched. I saw it with my own eyes: I saw him snatch up two of our company in his great fist, sprawl back in the middle of his cave, and dash their bodies against the rock until the threshold ran and swam with their blood; I saw him chew the limbs, dripping black gore, the warm joints still quivering under his teeth.

"But Ulysses did not let it pass — he never forgot himself, not even in that. When the Cyclops, gorged on his meal and drowned in wine, let his head loll and lay sprawled and enormous across the cave, snoring, belching up gore and gobbets of flesh soaked in wine in his sleep — we prayed to the great gods, drew lots for our parts, and crowded all together around him and bored out that one huge eye, the single eye sunk under his savage brow, as broad as an Argive shield or the disk of the sun, with a sharpened stake. And so at last we took our joy in revenge for the friends he had killed. But run — run, you poor men; cut your cables and flee! For there are a hundred more Cyclopes, as huge and as horrible as Polyphemus penning his woolly flocks in that cave and milking them, scattered all along this curving coast and roaming the high hills. Three times now the horns of the moon have filled while I have dragged out my life in the woods, among the lairs of wild beasts, watching the monstrous Cyclopes from a rock, shaking at the sound of their feet and their voices, living on the bitter berries and stony cornels the branches give, tearing up roots and grass. I watched the whole horizon, and at last I saw your ships come in. Whatever they are, I gave myself up to them; it is enough to have escaped that unspeakable race. I would rather you take my life by any death you please."

He had hardly finished when we saw the shepherd Polyphemus himself on the height of the mountain, heaving his huge bulk among his flocks and making for the shore he knew — a horror, a shapeless mass, monstrous, his eye put out. A lopped pine guides his hand and steadies his step. His woolly sheep go with him; they are his one comfort, his only solace now. When he reached the water and waded out into the deep, he washed the running blood from the socket of his gouged-out eye, grinding his teeth and groaning, and strode far out into the sea — and even there the waves never wet his towering sides. In terror we ran, taking aboard the stranger, who had earned it, and cut the cables in silence, and leaning into the oars we swept the sea. He heard us, and turned to follow the sound of the strokes — but when his groping hand could not reach us and he could not match the Ionian swells in his stride, he let out a roar so vast the sea shook to its depths, and the land of Italy far off was struck with fear, and Etna bellowed back through her winding caves. The whole tribe of the Cyclopes, roused, came pouring down from the woods and the high hills and crowded the harbor — we saw them, the brotherhood of Etna, standing there helpless with their wild eyes, their heads in the clouds, a fearful gathering, like tall oaks or cone-bearing cypresses standing in the high forest of Jupiter or the grove of Diana. Sharp fear drove us to shake out the sheets any way we could and spread our sails to the friendly wind. But Helenus had warned us not to steer between Scylla and Charybdis, where either way runs a hair's breadth from death; so we resolved to turn back. And then a north wind came down from the narrow headland of Pelorus and carried us, and I passed the living rock at the mouth of the Pantagias, the bay of Megara, and low Thapsus. Achaemenides showed us the landmarks, retracing the coast of his wanderings with luckless Ulysses.

There is an island that lies across the mouth of a Sicilian bay, facing wave-beaten Plemyrium; the men of old called it Ortygia. The story goes that Alpheus, a river of Greece, forced a secret channel under the sea all the way to here, and now, Arethusa, he mingles with your Sicilian waters at your spring. As we were told, we worshiped the great powers of the place, and from there I passed the rich marshland of low-lying Helorus. Then we skirted the high rocks and jutting reefs of Pachynus, and Camerina came into view in the distance — the town the fates forbade to be moved — and the Geloan plains, and Gela, that takes its grim name from its river. Then steep Acragas showed its great walls far off, the breeder of brave horses once; and I left palm-rich Selinus behind with a following wind and threaded the shallows of Lilybaeum with their hidden reefs. After that the harbor of Drepanum took me in — a joyless landing. There, after all the storms of the sea had battered me, I lost my father Anchises, the comfort of all my cares and griefs. There, best of fathers, you left me worn out, saved from so many dangers all for nothing. Not Helenus the prophet, for all the horrors he foretold, ever warned me of this grief; not the Harpy Celaeno. This was my last ordeal, the end of the long road. And as I sailed on from there, a god drove me at last onto your shores.

So Aeneas, alone, with everyone listening, told the story of the fated road the gods had set him on, and all his wanderings. And here at last he fell silent, and ended, and went to his rest.
