The Aeneid, Book XI

The Death of Pallas, the Council, and Camilla

Book XI11 of 12

Dawn rose meanwhile out of the sea and left the ocean behind her. Aeneas, though grief pressed him hard, gave the morning to his vows — there was the burying of his dead, and his mind was a wreck with sorrow, but a victor pays the gods first. So on a rising mound he set a great oak with its boughs lopped clean away, and dressed the bare trunk in the gleaming arms he had stripped from the captain Mezentius: a trophy to you, mighty Lord of War. He fixed the plumed crest on it, still wet with blood, the broken spears, the breastplate punched through and dented in twelve places; he bound the bronze shield to the left and hung the ivory-hilted sword from its neck. Then, ringed by his officers — for they all crowded close — he gave them their triumph in words:

"The hardest of it is done, men. Let go your fear of what's left. These are the spoils — the first-fruits taken from a proud king, and Mezentius, by my own hand, is what you see here. Now the road runs to the king and the walls of the Latins. Get your weapons ready; lift up your hearts; look for the war so that the moment Heaven gives the word no one is caught unready, no one fumbling, no one pleading he didn't know. But first — while the gods above let us — let's lay our unburied comrades in the earth. That is the one honor that still means anything down in Acheron. Go," he said, "give these brave souls their last rites, the men whose blood bought us this country. And before anything else, send Pallas home to the grieving city of Evander — Pallas, who lacked no courage, and whom a black day stole away and drowned in bitter death."

So he spoke, weeping, and turned back to the doorway where the body of Pallas lay. Old Acoetes was keeping watch over it — once squire to Evander in Arcadia, now sent out under far worse stars to attend the son. Around them stood the whole household of the dead boy, the Trojan ranks, the Trojan women with their hair torn loose in mourning. When Aeneas came through the high doors they lifted a great cry to heaven and beat their breasts, and the palace rang with the sound of grief. But when he saw the pale face and the open wound — the spear-gash high in that smooth young chest, made by an Ausonian point — he could not hold back the tears, and he said:

"Poor boy. Did Fortune, when she came at last in all her brightness, begrudge me only this — that you should never see my kingdom, never ride home beside me in triumph? This is not the promise I made your father the day I rode away. He threw his arms around me and sent me off to win a great command, and warned me, frightened, that the men I faced were fierce, that I was going to war with a hard people. And right now, maybe, fooled by an empty hope, he is heaping his altars high with offerings and prayers — while we walk back to him in this useless funeral pomp, grieving, bringing him his lifeless son, who owes nothing now to any god above. The poor father will see the burial of his own boy before his own life is run. These are my triumphs in the Latin war. This is what my given word was worth, and all my care. And yet, Evander — you will not look on a son shamed by some wound in the back. You will not have to wish, too late, that you'd died yourself rather than live to see him. Italy, what a champion you have lost. Ascanius — what a friend."

When he had finished mourning he gave the order to lift the wretched body and chose a thousand men, the flower of the whole army, to walk in the funeral train, to share Evander's tears and carry the boy home — a small comfort for so great a grief, but the most they could give. They wove a soft bier out of oak shoots and arbutus and shaded it over with green leaves, and on this rough country litter they laid the boy on his high bed. He looked like a flower a girl's fingers have cut from the stem — a soft violet, a hyacinth letting go its head — the sheen and the shape of it still there, not yet faded; but the earth that mothered it no longer feeds it, no longer gives it strength.

Then Aeneas brought out two robes stiff with gold and purple, which Sidonian Dido had once made for him with her own hands, glad in the work, threading the gold through the weave herself. One of these he wrapped around the boy, a last honor; in the other he covered the hair that the fire would soon take. He piled up beside the bier the spoils of the men Pallas had killed in the Latin fighting, and ordered his trophies carried out in a long line — the captured horses, the captured arms. He brought out the men too, hands bound behind them, to be sent down as offerings to the dead, their blood meant to sprinkle the flames. And he had the officers carry tree-trunks dressed in enemy armor with the names of the conquered fixed to them. Acoetes was led along, broken with age, bruising his breast with his fists, raking his old face with his nails, and again and again throwing himself full-length on the ground. They led out the war-chariots too, splashed with Rutulian blood. Behind came the war-horse, Aethon, his harness stripped off, weeping — yes, weeping, great drops wetting his face. Others carried Pallas's spear and helmet; the rest of his gear Turnus had taken. After came the whole grieving column — Trojans, Etruscans, the Arcadians with their weapons reversed and trailing in the dust. When the long procession had wound far ahead, Aeneas stopped and gave a last groan: "The same grim war that calls us calls me away from these other tears. Goodbye forever, great Pallas. Goodbye." He said no more, but turned for the walls and went back to camp.

And now men came from the Latin city carrying olive branches, asking for mercy: would he give back the bodies that lay strewn over the field, let them bury their dead? No one makes war on men with no breath left in them, they said; spare those who only yesterday were his hosts, the kin of the bride he had been promised. Aeneas, who could not refuse so fair a thing, granted it, and said more besides: "Latins — what cruel turn of luck has tangled you in a war like this and set you against a man who is your friend? You ask me for peace for your dead, for men cut down by the lot of war. I'd gladly give it to your living too. I never came here except that the fates assigned me this place to settle. I make no war on your people. It was your king who broke off our bond and chose to trust himself to Turnus instead. It would have been fairer for Turnus to face this death himself. If he means to end the war by his own hand, if he means to drive the Trojans out, this is where he should have come and crossed weapons with me — and let the one live to whom the god or his own right arm gives the day. Now go, and light the fires under your poor dead." He finished. They stood stunned, silent, looking at one another.

Then Drances — an older man, who had long carried a grudge against young Turnus and never hid his hate — broke the silence and answered: "Trojan, great in your fame and greater in your arms — how can I raise you to the heaven you deserve? Should I admire your justice first, or your strength in war? We'll carry your words home gratefully, and if luck lets us in any way, we'll join you to Latinus our king. Let Turnus go find his own alliances. We'll even be glad to set the stones of the city the fates have promised you, and carry the Trojan walls on our backs." So Drances said, and all of them murmured the same. They struck a truce of twelve days, and in that peace Latins and Trojans wandered the wooded hills together, harming no one, and felled the timber for the pyres. The axes rang through the groves; they brought down the high ash trees, split the oak and the sweet-smelling cedar with wedges, and hauled the loads away on groaning wagons.

And now Rumor — who only days before had carried news of Pallas's triumphs ahead of him — flew through the little Arcadian town with word of his death, and filled Evander's ears, and his house, and his streets. The people poured out of the gates with funeral torches in their hands, and the long line of fire lit up the fields and threw a sullen glare over the marching column and the dead prince at the heart of it. When the two crowds met, the wail went up, and the women answered it from the walls, and the whole town shook with the cry. Nothing could keep Evander back. He came stumbling out into the middle of it all, threw himself down on the bier where they had set Pallas, and held on, weeping and groaning, until at last grief forced a path for his voice:

"This is not the promise you gave your father, Pallas — that you'd be careful, that you'd not throw yourself into the savage god of war. I knew too well how much a young man's first taste of glory can carry him away, how sweet that honor is in a first battle. Bitter first-fruits of your soldiering, hard lesson in a war fought so close to home, and not one of my vows heard, not one of my prayers answered. And you, my dear wife, lucky in your death — you went before this grief could find you. I have lived past my own time, outlasted my own fate, just to be left standing here, a father who has buried his son. If only I had ridden with the Trojans and gone down myself under the Rutulian spears — then this funeral would be bringing me home, not Pallas. And yet I won't blame the Trojans, won't curse the bond we made; this loss is the lot old age was always owed. But if an early death was waiting for my boy, at least let it comfort me that he fell leading the way into Latium, with Volscians cut down in heaps before he died. Pallas, I could wish you no better funeral than the one dutiful Aeneas has given you, than the Trojans and the great Etruscan chiefs and all their host. They carry the trophies of the men your own hand killed. You too, Turnus, would be standing here now, a great trunk hung with armor, if Pallas had matched you in years and strength. But why do I hold the Trojans here, miserable as I am? Go — take this message to your king. The one reason I drag out a life I hate, now that my son is dead, is your right hand: you owe me Turnus, owe him to the son and the father both, and you know it. This is the one thing left for your courage and your luck to give. I ask no joy in this life — there is none — only to carry the news to my Pallas, down among the shades."

Dawn meanwhile had lifted her kindly light again and brought work and grief back with it. Now Aeneas and Tarchon raised the pyres along the curving shore. Each man carried his own dead there by the old custom, and as the fires were kindled the black smoke smothered the high air and blotted out the sky. Three times in their gleaming armor they rode around the burning piles; three times on foot they circled the flames, crying out the names of the dead; and the tears ran down their cheeks and wet the ground. Trumpets and shields rang into the sound of weeping. Some threw onto the fire the spoils stripped from the slain Latins — helmets, fine swords, bridles, the hot chariot-wheels; some cast in offerings each man knew, the very shields and unlucky spears those friends had carried. All around, oxen were slaughtered to the fire, and bristle-backed boars, and sheep gathered from the fields, killed and laid in the flames. Along the whole shore they watched their comrades burn and would not leave the smoldering pyres until damp night wheeled the sky over, set thick with burning stars.

Elsewhere the wretched Latins built their own countless pyres. Many of their dead they buried where they fell; many they carried off to the neighboring fields or home to the city. The rest — a vast tangled heap, beyond counting and beyond names — they burned together unhonored, and the fields were thick with fires on every side. When the third dawn had driven the cold shadows from the sky, the men still keeping the pyres raked the deep ash, sifted out the white bones, gathered them while they were warm, and heaped a mound of earth over them. But in the city, in the king's own house, the grief ran deepest and longest — mothers, brides, the loving hearts of sisters, orphaned boys all cursing the war and Turnus's marriage. Let the man fight it out himself, they said; let the one who claims the crown of Italy, who wants to be king, settle it hand to hand. Drances fed the fire, swearing Aeneas wanted no one but Turnus, called only for Turnus. But Turnus had his own party too, and plenty of them; the queen's great name covered him like a shield, and the trophies of his old victories held his fame up.

Into all this heat the envoys came back from Diomedes — the great Greek hero who had built a new city in Italy. They came with the worst possible news: nothing had come of all the cost and the long road; their gifts had moved him not at all, their prayers not at all; the Latins would have to look elsewhere for an ally, or buy peace from the Trojan king. Latinus, sunk in grief, saw now too late that the fates plainly pointed to a foreign son-in-law, an outsider Heaven itself was bringing in — and that until Aeneas married Lavinia, divine anger would hang over the land. The anger of the gods and the fresh graves before his eyes told him so. So, full of dread, he called the whole Latin council to the great hall. The chiefs came at his summons and crowded the road to the palace. Latinus, oldest of them and first in power, took the throne in their midst, the weight of the day in his face, and told Venulus to report.

At once the murmuring died and a hush fell, and Venulus began: "We did as you ordered, citizens — crossed a long stretch of dangerous country and reached the place. We saw with our own eyes the Greek camp, the rising towers. Diomedes has walled in the city he calls Argyripa, named for his own Argos. We were brought in; we set our gifts before him; we gave our names and our country and told him plainly why we'd come. He listened to it all, and then answered us, gently and without anger: 'You old race of Ausonia, you children of Saturn's golden peace — what madness has come over you, to give up your ancient calm and stir up a war you don't understand, to lay hands on the sword? Every one of us who put steel into the fields of Ilium — set aside the men killed in the fighting, or swallowed by the Simois — every one of us has paid for it across the whole world, in suffering no king would wish on Priam himself. Minerva's storm knows it, and the rocks of Caphereus, and the avenging coast of Euboea. Menelaus, who led us, was driven all the way to Egypt; Ulysses saw the Cyclops' cave. Why even name Idomeneus, thrown out of his own kingdom — or Agamemnon, the lord of all the Greeks, the man who avenged a stolen wife, cut down on his own threshold by an adulterous wife of his own, the moment he came home? As for me — the gods grudged me ever seeing my own country again, my own dear wife. Even now strange portents follow me: my lost companions turned to birds and gone up into the air, and they haunt the rivers — oh, the cruel punishment of my men! — and fill the cliffs with their crying. I should have known what was coming, the day I was mad enough to lift a mortal hand against the gods, and wounded Venus herself. No. Don't push me into wars like that again. I have no quarrel left with Troy now that her towers are down; I take no joy in the old wrongs. The gifts you've brought me from home — give them to Aeneas instead. I have stood against his spear, fought him hand to hand: believe a man who knows, how he rises behind his shield, the whirlwind he throws a spear with. If the land of Ida had bred two more like him, the Trojans would be the ones besieging Greek cities now, and Greece would be in mourning. Whatever held us up so long under the walls of Troy — what blunted the Greek victory and dragged the war out ten years — it was the hands of those two, Hector and Aeneas; both of them brave past telling, both first in war, but Aeneas first of all in duty to the gods. Whatever terms he offers, take his right hand. Just be careful your weapons never cross with his.' That, my king, is his answer, and his counsel on this great war." So Venulus closed his report.

A jarring murmur ran through the troubled council, the way water roars when a current jams between rocks, and the river, hemmed into a narrow channel, churns and rebounds and the banks throw the sound back. When the noise died down, Latinus called on the gods and spoke from his high throne: "I wish, Latins, we had settled this before — rather than holding a council now, with the enemy camped at the walls. We are fighting a hopeless war, citizens, against men who can't be beaten in the field and won't put down their swords even when they are. Whatever hope you pinned on Diomedes, let it go. You've seen for yourselves how thin our own hope is, how near ruin we are. I don't blame anyone — every man fought with all his strength, and we threw the whole weight of the kingdom into it. So now hear what I think. There's an old tract of land of mine along the Tuscan river, running far to the west, past the old Sicanian borders; Rutulians and Auruncans sow it now and graze the rough hills. Let us give that whole upland, and the pine-covered ridge, to the Trojans — make them partners in our country on fair terms, and invite them to settle there. Or if they'd rather build elsewhere and leave our soil, let us cut them twenty ships of Italian oak, or more if they can man them; the timber's down by the water's edge — let them name the number and the shape, and the bronze and the labor and the shipyards are mine to give. And to carry the terms, let a hundred of our noblest go, olive in hand, with gifts — gold, a robe of state, the ivory chair and the kingly crown. Talk it over among yourselves, and save a kingdom that is sinking."

Then Drances rose — the man who had hated the bright young Turnus for years. He was rich, a power at the council table, and a fine speaker; but in the field his hand went cold. He was a born plotter, brave with his tongue. Highborn through his mother, his father he never named. He stood up and laid it on thick: "You've said it all, great king, and said it so plainly there's no arguing — and there's not a man here who doesn't see what the country needs. They just don't dare to say it. Let him give us leave to speak — that arrogant man whose pride and whose luck started this miserable war. I'll say it though it costs me my life. Because of him the plains of Latium run with blood; because of his ambition our best men lie in the dirt; the whole town is in mourning, the whole land in tears. And he, the cause of all of it, the man who keeps threatening the gods with his arms — when it came to the worst he ran, and looked to his own safety in a shameful flight. Best of kings, since you're sending all these rich gifts to the Trojan, add one more, the one he wants above the rest, and let no man's bullying stop you: give Aeneas your daughter Lavinia for his wife. Seal the bond with that marriage and give this bleeding land a lasting peace. And Turnus — I know you count me no friend, and I won't fight you on that. I'll only beg you: pity your people, lay down your pride, and step aside, beaten as you are. We've buried enough of our own. If it's glory you want, if you're that hungry for the throne and the bride, then have the courage to face the man yourself. So that Turnus can win a king's daughter, are the rest of us — cheap, nameless, unwept — to keep falling in the field? Go, then. If there's any fight in you, any of your fathers' courage left, look the man in the eye who's calling you out."

Turnus's fury blazed at this. He groaned from deep in his chest and threw the words back: "Drances, you always have plenty to say, and it's always when the war needs hands, not voices. First in the council hall, you fill it up with talk — brave words, while the ramparts keep the enemy off and the ditches aren't yet brimming with blood. So thunder away, eloquent as ever; brand me a coward — you, Drances, whose own hand has piled the field so high with Trojan dead, whose trophies stand so tall! There's the open country; the foe isn't far to seek. Shall we go meet them, the two of us? Come on, why wait? Or does the war-god live only in that windy tongue of yours, and your feet, so quick to run? Me, beaten? Me, driven off the field? Who could call that a lie to my face and not be the lowest coward alive — with the Tiber swollen by Trojan blood, with Evander's whole house cut down root and branch, and the giant brothers stripped of their arms? That's not how the Trojans found me the day I was fenced inside their walls, alone, one man against the lot of them. A destructive war? Then go threaten the Phrygian pirate with it, and yourself. Keep on filling the people's ears with your terrors, keep glorifying a race twice beaten, keep running down the arms of Latinus. So the Greek princes tremble before the Trojans now — Diomedes, Achilles, all of them. The river Aufidus runs backward in fear of the Adriatic. And you, master of tricks, pretend to tremble at my anger to make your charge sting more — don't waste your shaking on it, Drances; a soul like yours isn't worth this hand. Keep it. Let it sit safe in that breast of yours.

"Now, royal father, to you and to the real matter of this council. If you've already given up on our arms, if one defeat has us so low we'll never face the field again, if our luck has turned for good and can't turn back — then let's sue for peace, hold out our empty hands and beg. And yet — if there were any of our old courage left in us — to me the man who fell rather than see this, who bit the ground rather than live it, he is the one I'd call blessed beyond the rest. But we still have men in reserve, we still have allies, cities and peoples of Italy ready to bleed for us — and the Trojans paid plenty for what little glory they bought; they have their dead too, and the same storm of war went over them. So why do we throw down our courage at the very door, why does the trembling take us before the trumpet sounds? Time changes many things for the better; Fortune has come and gone and come back to mock men and set them on solid ground again. We'll get no help from Diomedes — but we have Messapus, and lucky Tolumnius, and all the chiefs the Italian peoples have sent; and no small glory will come to the Volscians and to Camilla, their queen. But if it's me alone the Trojans are calling out, if I alone stand in the way of the public good — Victory has not so fled this hand that I'd refuse any hope to chase her down. I'll go to meet him gladly, even if he's a second great Achilles and wears armor forged by Vulcan's own hands to match. To you, my king, and to Latinus my father-in-law, I, Turnus, no coward to any man before me, give my life. Aeneas calls for me alone? I pray that he does. Let Drances keep clear — he won't pay the gods' price for this with his blood, or take the glory if there's glory to win."

While they wrangled like this, neither side giving way, Aeneas was already moving his camp into the field. A scout came tearing through the city with the news, throwing the whole place into uproar: the Trojans were in battle order, marching down from the Tiber, sweeping toward the town. At once they snatched up their arms in a panic; the young men shouted for the fight; the old men wept and groaned. On every side the noise went up to the sky in a clash of confused voices — the way swans cry across the loud pools, or the birds fill a noisy marsh. Turnus seized the moment. "Yes," he cried, "keep at your meetings, sit there praising peace — while they come at us under arms!" And he wheeled and strode out of the hall. "Volusus, get the Volscian companies to horse; lead out the Rutulians yourself. Messapus, Coras and your brother, spread the cavalry across the plain. Some of you hold the passes and man the towers; the rest, follow me where I order." In a moment they were swarming up onto the walls from every corner of the city.

Good old Latinus broke off the council, seeing too late the storm bursting over him, and blamed himself bitterly — that he had not taken Aeneas in as a son the moment the man came, and walled him into the city as kin. They dug trenches before the gates and rolled up stones and stakes. The harsh trumpet screamed the call to blood. A ragged ring of mothers and boys lined the walls; the last crisis had drawn out every hand. And the queen rode up to Minerva's temple on the heights with a crowd of women, gifts in their hands, and beside her, eyes lowered, the girl Lavinia, the cause of all the grief. The women filled the shrine with incense and poured out their grief from the high doorway: "Lady of war, virgin power, break the pirate's spear in your own hand; throw him down face-first inside our gates."

Turnus armed for the fight in a fury. He had the breastplate on, the bronze scales bristling, the greaves of gold clasped to his legs; his head was still bare, but the sword hung ready at his side, and he came down from the high citadel flashing gold, his whole heart leaping, already in his mind closing with the enemy. He was like a stallion that has snapped its tether and bolts free of the stalls, master at last of the open plain — off to the mares and the pastures, or down to some well-known river to plunge in and bathe, and he rears up neighing into the bright air with his mane streaming over his neck. Camilla came riding to meet him with her Volscian column, and at the very gate the queen swung down from her horse, and her whole band followed her down, and she said: "Turnus — if a brave heart may trust itself for anything — I dare it, and I promise you this: I'll meet the Trojan cavalry alone, ride out alone against the Etruscan horse. Let me take the whole danger of the first charge. You hold the foot back at the walls and guard the city." Turnus stood a moment, his eyes fixed on her, in awe of her, and answered: "Glory of Italy — how can I begin to thank you, how repay you? But since your courage rises over everything, share the work with me. The Trojan — by the reports my scouts bring back — has sent his light horse on ahead to feel out the plain, while he himself comes round by the high ridges and the trackless passes to take the city from above. I'm laying an ambush in the gorge where the road bends, blocking it with armed men on both sides. You take the Etruscan cavalry head-on; brave Messapus rides with you, and the Latin squadrons, and Tiburtus's men — and the whole command is yours." He said it, and warned Messapus and the other chiefs to the fight, and went off to spring his trap.

There is a valley ringed with hills, made for an ambush, the kind of place that calls for treachery. A narrow path winds in through thick brush; above it stands a steep ridge with a hidden ledge on top from which a man can drop down on either side, hurl a rockslide, charge straight downhill. Turnus took the trail he knew, seized the high ground, and lay there waiting in the choked woods.

Meanwhile, up in heaven, Diana spoke quickly to her swift girl Opis, one of the band of sacred maidens around her, and her voice was heavy with grief: "Camilla is riding out to a cruel war, child, buckling on my arms — and it will do her no good, for I have loved her above all others, and this love is not new in me. When her father Metabus was driven out of the old city of Privernum — hated for his tyranny — he carried the baby off with him through the heart of the fighting, his one companion in exile, and changed a little of her mother Casmilla's name and called her Camilla. He held her against his chest and ran for the forest, the spears thick behind him, the Volscians close on his heels. He came to the Amasenus, but the river was up over its banks, the rains had swollen it past fording. He almost threw himself in — then drew back, afraid, not for himself but for the precious thing he carried. In a flash he decided. He happened to have a great war-spear of seasoned oak; he bound the baby to the middle of the shaft, wrapped tight in cork and bark, balanced the weight in his hand, and lifted his eyes to heaven: 'Gracious one, dweller of the woods, Latona's daughter — I, her father, give this child to you to be your servant. She comes to you through the air, holding your weapon, a suppliant — take her, I pray, as your own.' And he drew back his arm and threw, and the spear sang across the roaring water and carried poor Camilla safe to the far bank. With his enemies almost on him he plunged into the flood and fought across, and on the other side he tore his spear from the turf and his little girl with it, the gift he'd vowed to Diana. After that no town would take him in; he was too proud and too hunted for walls, and he lived out a shepherd's life in the wild hills, and fed his daughter on the milk of mares and wild grazing beasts, pressing it through her tender lips. The moment she could stand, he put a sharp javelin in her hands, slung a bow and quiver from her small shoulder. No gold band held her hair, no long robe trailed behind her — just a tiger's skin hung down her back from her head. Already her child's hand was throwing the light spear, whirling the smooth sling around her head, bringing down the crane and the white swan out of the sky. The mothers all through Tyrrhenia wanted her for their sons; but she stayed mine alone, faithful to her spear, untouched, in love with her own maidenhood. I only wish she hadn't gone to this war, hadn't tried to take on the Trojans — she'd still be one of my own, still beside me. But since a bitter fate is driving her on, go down out of the sky, child, to where the Latins are fighting under unlucky stars. Take my bow and draw an arrow of vengeance from the quiver: whoever wounds that sacred body, Trojan or Italian, let him pay me for it with his blood, the same price. And I will lift her up myself in a hollow cloud — her body unrobbed, her arms unstripped — and carry her, poor girl, home, and lay her in the earth of her own country." Opis heard, and slid down through the light air to earth, her body wrapped in a black whirling cloud.

By now the Trojan ranks and the Etruscan chiefs and all the cavalry, drawn up in squadrons, were coming up to the walls. The horses pranced and fought the bit, swerving this way and that; spears and shields flashed across the whole field, and the bronze of the lifted war blazed over the plain. Against them came Messapus and Coras and his brother and Camilla's column, lances couched, leveled, leaning forward. They closed in until the gap between them shrank to nothing, and then with a great shout they charged, spurring the screaming horses on, and spears flew thick as a snowstorm and put the daylight out. Tyrrhenus and bold Aconteus came together first at a dead gallop, breast to breast — the horses crashed head to head and burst apart, and Aconteus was thrown like a stone out of a siege-engine and rolled in his own blood and was gone. The Latins broke at once and slung their shields behind them and bolted for the walls — until the Etruscans, Asilas in front, drove them so hard they wheeled at last out of shame and turned to face it, and now it was the Etruscans who fled. The line surged back and forth like the sea on a shore — the long surge driving in, foaming up over the rocks, flinging the spray far inland, then dragging back in a long retreat, sucking the stones and the churned sand back with it. Twice the Etruscans drove the Latins to the city walls; twice they were thrown back and ran with their shields slung behind. But the third time the lines locked for good, man chose man, and now it was real killing. The dying groaned; the ground was a litter of bodies and weapons and dead horses, and the blood ran deep.

Through all of it Camilla rode, a wild thing, her quiver on her shoulder, one side bared for battle. Now she rained her light spears thick; now she snatched up the strong axe and would not stop; and Diana's bow and golden weapons rang on her back. Even when she gave ground she shot backward over her shoulder as she rode. Her chosen women rode at her sides — Larina, Tulla, Tarpeia with her bronze axe — all Italian girls she had picked herself, her glory in peace, her companions in war. They were like the Amazons of Thrace pounding the streams of Thermodon, fighting in their painted armor, the way they once rode around Hippolyte, or around Penthesilea coming home in her chariot from the war, and the women clashing their crescent shields and shrieking their high war-cry. Who did you cut down first, fierce girl, who last? How many dying men did you stretch on the earth? Eunaeus first — she drove her ash spear clean through his open chest, and he went down vomiting blood, biting the gory ground, twisting on his own wound. Then Liris, and Pegasus over him — one as he gathered the reins of his stumbling horse, the other reaching out a hand to catch his falling friend; both pitched down together. To these she added Amastrus, son of Hippotes, and chasing them at the spear's reach she ran down Tereus, Harpalycus, Demophoön, Chromis — and for every dart that left her hand a Trojan dropped. Far off rode Ornytus the hunter in strange Italian gear, a stripped bullock's hide on his broad shoulders, his head sunk inside the gaping jaws of a wolf, a farm-pike in his hand; he towered over the rest. She singled him out of the crowd — it was no trouble — ran him through, and stood over him: "Did you think you were chasing game in the woods, you Etruscan? The day has come that answers your boasting with a woman's weapon. Still, it's no small name you'll carry down to the dead: you fell to Camilla's spear."

Then Butes and Orsilochus, the two biggest men of the Trojan side. Butes she took breast-first, the spear driving in at the gap where the helmet ends and the breastplate begins, the shield slung on his left arm. Orsilochus she fled — or seemed to — drawing him in a tightening circle until she was behind him; then she rose and brought the heavy axe down again and again through helmet and bone while he begged for his life, and his split skull spilled its warm brains over his face. The son of Aunus, an Apennine man, came on him by chance and froze at the sight — and then, a true Ligurian and a born cheat while the fates still let him cheat, called out: "What's so brave about trusting your horse instead of yourself? Give up the speed of it. Get down and fight me on equal ground, on foot — then we'll see whose boasting the wind carries off." It stung her. Burning with rage she handed her horse to one of her women, faced him on foot, fearless, with a bare sword and a plain shield. The young man, sure his trick had worked, swung his own horse around and bolted, raking the spurs into its bleeding flanks. "You fool, you Ligurian, all that slippery pride for nothing — your home-country lies won't carry you off alive." And she flew after him on foot, faster than the galloping horse, caught the reins, ran in front of him, and took her revenge in his blood — easily, the way a hawk drops from a rock onto a dove high in the cloud, pins it, and tears the heart out, and the blood and torn feathers drift down out of the sky.

But the Father of gods and men, watching from his high seat, did not let all this pass. He stirred Tarchon the Etruscan into the thick of it, goaded him with bitter shame. So Tarchon rode through the breaking line, rallying his men by name, calling the runaways back, putting the heart back into the fight: "What is this — no shame in you, Etruscans, never anything but slow? A woman is driving you, scattering you, putting this whole crowd to flight! What are your swords for, what are these useless spears in your hands? You're quick enough at Venus and the night battles, quick enough when the curved flute calls you to the feast — that's the work you wait for, that's your love: the dishes and the full cups. Get to it, when the priest cries the lucky omen and the fat victim calls you into the deep groves!" And with that he spurred into the middle of the enemy, ready to die, and crashed into Venulus and tore him off his horse and pinned him across his own saddle-bow and carried him off by main force, and a great shout went up. The Latins turned to stare. Tarchon flew over the plain with the man and his armor, snapped the head off the man's own spear, and felt along the joints of the armor for a place to drive a killing wound, while the other fought to keep the point from his bare throat and twisted to put off death. It was like a tawny eagle carrying a snake up into the sky, its talons sunk deep, while the snake writhes in coils, stiffens every scale, rears its hissing head — and the eagle keeps tearing at it with its hooked beak even as it beats the air. So Tarchon carried his prize off in triumph out of the heart of the enemy, and the Etruscans, who had been giving way, took heart and charged.

Then Arruns — a man already marked for death — began to circle Camilla with his spear, watching for an opening, working out the cunning way. Wherever she rode in the fury of the fight he was there, silent, tracking her steps; when she came back from a kill he wheeled his horse quietly away; he stalked her from every side, all the while shaking his sure spear, relentless. Now Chloreus, once a priest of Cybele, came shining far off in his Phrygian armor, spurring a foaming horse whose chest was sheathed in scales of bronze and gold; the man himself blazed in foreign purple and saffron, shooting Cretan arrows from a Lycian bow, a gold quiver ringing on his shoulder, a gold helmet on his head, his saffron cloak gathered up in a clasp of yellow gold, his tunic embroidered with strange needlework. Camilla saw him, and out of all the field she hunted only him — whether she wanted to nail his Trojan arms up in some temple, or to ride decked in that captured gold herself, a girl on fire with a woman's love of plunder. She chased him blindly through the press, careless of everything, careless of herself. And Arruns chose his moment. He rose from cover and threw — but first he prayed: "Apollo, highest of the gods, guardian of holy Soracte, whom we serve first of all, for whom the pine-fire blazes, and whose worshippers walk barefoot through the coals trusting in you — grant me this, Father almighty: let me wipe out the shame of this day. I want no spoils from her, no trophy, nothing she wore. My other deeds will make my name. Let me only strike down this terrible plague and go home inglorious to my own town." Apollo heard him, and granted half the prayer in his heart and scattered the other half on the racing winds: he gave him the kill he begged for — but his safe road home he tore to pieces and threw to the storms of the south.

When the spear whizzed singing through the air, every Volscian turned and looked to their queen — but Camilla herself, of all of them, heard nothing and saw nothing, so set was she on the gold, until the weapon came down under her bared breast, lodged, and drank deep of her maiden blood. Her frightened women rushed to catch her as she fell. Arruns ran — more terrified than anyone, half in dread and half in a guilty joy — and he did not dare press the blow home or even look at her dying; he was like a wolf that has gored a shepherd or torn down a great bullock and, knowing what it has dared, slinks off into the high hills before the spears can find it, its tail tucked trembling under its belly. So Arruns fled, glad to be gone, and lost himself in the press of armed men. Camilla, dying, pulled at the spear with her hands, but the iron point was driven fast between her ribs, deep in the bone. She slid down, the blood draining out of her, her eyes glazing and growing cold, the bright color gone out of her face. And as her breath went she turned to Acca, the one of all her women she trusted most, and said: "This far, Acca, sister — this far I could go. The bitter wound has finished me; everything is going dark around me. Run to Turnus, tell him: he must come and take my place in the fight, and keep the Trojans off the city. And now — goodbye." And as she spoke she let go the reins and slipped helpless to the ground, and little by little the warmth left her, the slack neck bowed, death's hand took her head, and she dropped her weapons, and life fled with a groan, resentful, down to the shades.

Then a great shout rose and struck the golden stars, and with Camilla down the fighting turned savage. The whole Trojan force pressed in, the Etruscan chiefs and Evander's Arcadian squadrons. But high up, watching the field, Diana's nymph Opis had been sitting unmoved all this while — until she saw Camilla brought low at last, weltering in her blood and ringed by enemies. Then she drew a deep sigh from the bottom of her heart and said: "Too cruel, poor girl, too cruel the price you've paid for daring to take on the Trojans. It did you no good to have served Diana faithfully in the wild, no good to wear our arrows on your shoulder. And yet your goddess will not leave you unhonored in death. Your killing will be told for ages — and the coward will not slip away unpunished from this field." Below the high field stood a great earthen mound, the tomb of Dercennus, an old Latin king, shaded with dark oaks. There the beautiful Opis lighted, and from the height she picked out Arruns. She saw him, gleaming, swollen with pride, and called: "Why are you slinking away? Turn this way. Come here and die — come and take the reward you've earned for Camilla. You too will die by Diana's bow." And she drew a winged arrow from the gold quiver, bent the bow till the curved horns came together and her two hands met, one on the iron point, one on the string against her breast — and the arrow sang, and Arruns heard the bowstring and the whistling shaft in the same instant the iron went into his heart. He fell, gasping out his last in a far country, in the dust, forgotten — his fleeing friends left him where he lay. And Opis on her wings rose back up to high Olympus.

Camilla's column, their leader lost, broke first; then the Rutulians broke, then brave Atinas; the scattered chiefs and the leaderless squadrons all wheeled and ran for the safety of the walls. No one could turn and stand against the killing Trojans, no one face them — they slung their slack bows over their shoulders and the hooves drummed the crumbling field in a four-beat gallop. A black rolling cloud of dust came in toward the city, and the women up on the walls beat their breasts and sent the high wail of grief to the stars. The runners pouring through the open gates were swallowed in a flood of the enemy mixed among them, and they could not escape even at the threshold of home; they died in the very gates, inside their own walls, in sight of their own houses. The men inside slammed the bars across and could not bring themselves to open them for their own people begging at the foot of the wall — and so they shut their own out to be cut down, and let the killing in among them. Some, locked out, threw themselves down the steep ditch and died in the fall, or galloped blind at the walls and broke against the stone. The mothers, in a frenzy, snatched up whatever they could and flung javelins and stakes hardened in the fire from the towers, true love of country showing them how, racing to die first for the walls, as they had seen Camilla do.

Meanwhile word reached Turnus where he lay in the woods. Acca came running with the dreadful news: the Volscians wiped out, Camilla dead, the enemy sweeping everything before them, the whole field theirs, the panic rolling already up to the walls. Wild with grief — and the will of Jupiter drove him too — Turnus left the ambush, abandoned the rough hills, and came down onto the open plain. He was barely gone when Aeneas, finding the pass at last clear of hidden men, climbed up out of it, came through the dark forest, and led his army down to the open ground. So now both of them were racing for the city, neither far behind the other, their columns full in view across the smoking fields. Aeneas saw the Latin line; Turnus saw the Trojans and heard their horses snorting toward him. They would have joined battle there and then — but the Sun was already dipping his weary horses into the western sea, and the daylight was draining out of the world. So both sides drew up before the city, and dug in, and walled their camps for the night.
