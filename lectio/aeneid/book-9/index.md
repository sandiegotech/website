# The Aeneid, Book IX

Turnus at the Walls, and the Night of Nisus and Euryalus

While all this was happening far away, Juno sent Iris down out of heaven to bold Turnus. He was sitting apart, alone, in a sacred grove that had belonged to his ancestor Pilumnus, deep in his own thoughts. And Iris, daughter of Thaumas, spoke to him with her rose-colored lips: "Turnus — what no god would have dared to promise you if you'd prayed for it, the turning day has brought you unasked. Aeneas has left his city, his men, his fleet, and gone off to the court of King Evander, far up the Tiber, to the Arcadian settlements. And not content with that, he's pushed on into the farthest Etruscan towns, raising an army out of their farmers. So why wait? This is the hour. Call for the horses and the chariots. Break off your delay, and take their camp while it's caught off guard." She finished, and rose into the sky on level wings, cutting the great arc of her rainbow through the clouds as she climbed. Turnus knew her, and lifted both hands to the light, and called after her as she fled: "Iris, glory of the sky — who sent you down to me through the clouds, down to the earth? Why has the weather broken open so suddenly? I can see the heart of the sky tearing apart, and the stars wandering loose at the pole. I'll follow the sign, whoever you are who calls me to arms." And with that he went to the river, scooped up water in his hands, and prayed long to the gods above, loading the air with his vows.

Then the whole army was on the move across the open plain, rich in horses, rich in embroidered cloaks and gold. Messapus led the front; the sons of Tyrrhus brought up the rear; and in the center came Turnus himself, the commander, a head taller than the rest, weapon in hand. So the deep Ganges rises in silence, fed by seven quiet streams, or the Nile, when its rich flood ebbs back from the fields and sinks again into its channel. The Trojans saw it from the walls — a sudden black cloud of dust gathering, a darkness rolling up over the plain. Caicus was the first to shout it from the rampart facing the enemy: "Men — what's that mass of dark coming on toward us? Quick, your swords, your spears, up onto the walls! The enemy's here, the enemy's here!" And with a great roar the Trojans poured in through all the gates and filled the walls. For Aeneas, their wise commander, had given the order before he left: if anything happened while he was away, they were not to draw up a line and risk open battle in the field; they were only to hold the camp, safe behind their walls and their banked-up works. So now, though shame and anger urged them to fight, they did as they were told. They shut the gates, and waited in their towers, under arms, for the enemy to come to them.

Turnus had flown ahead of the slow column, and now he came galloping up to the camp with twenty picked horsemen — sudden, unlooked for, on a dappled Thracian stallion, his helmet gold, its crest blood-red. "Who's with me?" he shouted. "Who'll be first against the enemy?" And he flung a javelin spinning into the sky, the opening shot of the war, and rode forward across the field while his men roared behind him. He was amazed to find the Trojans had no fight in them — that they would not come out and meet him man to man on the level ground, but huddled inside their fort. So he rode up and down, raging, hunting for a way in where there was none. He was like a wolf prowling a full sheepfold in the dead of night, howling at the gaps, soaked by the wind and the rain, while the lambs bleat safe under their mothers; and he grinds his teeth, savage with a hunger he cannot reach, his throat dry with the old craving and no blood in it. That was Turnus, burning as he stared at the walls, the fury rising in his hard bones — how to get at them, how to shake the cooped-up Trojans out into the open. Then he saw the fleet, drawn up close against the camp, hemmed in by the earthworks and the river that washed its flank, and he went for it. He called to his cheering men for fire, and his own fist closed hot around a blazing pine-brand. They threw themselves into it — his presence drove them — and every man armed himself with a torch snatched from the fires. The smoking pitch-pine threw up a black, greasy smoke shot through with sparks, and the cloud of it rolled up toward the stars.

What god, Muses, turned that fire away from the Trojan ships? Who saved the fleet from burning? The story is old, but its fame will never die. Back when Aeneas was first building this fleet on the slopes of Mount Ida, getting ready to put out to sea, they say the great Mother of the Gods herself, Cybele, came to her son Jupiter and said: "Grant your dear mother this one thing, now that you rule Olympus. There was a stand of pine I loved, a grove on the top of Ida — they brought me offerings there for years, dark with firs and maple, shadowed and holy. I gave that wood gladly to the young Trojan when he needed ships. But now I'm sick with fear for it. Take the fear from me. Let no storm wreck those ships, no gale drive them under. It should count for something that they grew on a mountain of mine." And her son, who turns the wheeling stars, answered her: "Mother, what are you asking the fates to do? You want ships built by human hands to last forever? You want Aeneas to cross uncertain seas with a certainty no mortal gets? What god has that kind of power? No. But this much I can grant: when they've done their work and reached their harbor in Italy, every ship that comes through the storms and carries Aeneas safe to the Laurentine fields — I'll strip its mortal shape away and turn it into a goddess of the deep sea, to swim the water like the Nereids do." And to seal it he swore by the river of his brother's kingdom, by the banks of pitch and the black flood of Styx, and bent his head, and all of heaven shook.

Now the appointed day had come. The fates had wound their thread out to the mark, and Cybele's hour was here — the moment to drive Turnus's torches off her holy ships. First a strange light flashed across the sky from the east and ran down it; then out of the clouds came a great noise, drums and the wild music of the Mother's worship rolling from Mount Ida; and a voice fell through the air on both armies at once, terrible to Trojan and Rutulian alike: "Trojans, don't rush to defend my ships. Don't take up arms for them. Turnus will set the sea itself on fire before he burns my sacred pines. And you — go free. Go, goddesses of the sea. The Mother commands it." And on the instant every ship snapped the cable that held it, dipped its prow like a dolphin, and dove under — and rose again, transformed: where the tall hulls had ridden at anchor, now that many maiden faces broke the water and swam out to sea. The Rutulians stood frozen in horror. Even Messapus was shaken, his horses balking under him; and the river itself, old Tiber, checked his current with a hoarse roar and drew his waters back from the sea.

But Turnus's nerve held. He rallied them, even rebuked them: "It's the Trojans those omens threaten, not us. Jupiter himself has taken away the one thing they could have run to. They didn't even wait for our swords and our fire — their own ships have abandoned them. The sea's no road for them now; there's no escape that way. Half the world is closed to them, and the land is ours — thousands of us in arms all around them. Their famous fate, their oracles, mean nothing to me. They got what fate gave them: they reached Italy, they touched the soil. Well, I have a fate of my own — to wipe out this criminal race that stole my bride. And it isn't only the sons of Atreus who feel that wrong; it isn't only Greeks who have the right to take up arms over it. 'Once destroyed is enough,' you'll say. They should have learned, then, the first time, to loathe every woman alive. These are men who put their trust in a wall between them and us — one thin ditch — as if that were any distance at all from death. Didn't they watch Troy go down in flames, and Troy was built by the hands of gods? So which of you, my brave men, will help me tear that rampart down and storm the camp while their courage shakes? I don't need a thousand ships, or the armor Vulcan forged. Let every Etruscan in Italy join them, for all I care. And let them not be afraid of the dark — no one's going to creep in and steal their luck the way they stole the Palladium, or murder their watchmen on the citadel. We won't hide inside a wooden horse. We'll do it in broad daylight, in the open, and we'll ring their walls with fire. I'll make them see they're not facing some pack of Greeks, the boys Hector held off for ten long years. Now — the better part of the day is gone. What's left of it, men, rest. You've done well. Look to your bodies, and be ready: the fight is coming."

Meanwhile the job of setting the watch and ringing the camp with sentries went to Messapus, and of feeding the night-fires. Fourteen Rutulian captains were chosen to keep guard at the walls, each with a hundred men behind him, every one of them helmeted, crested, flashing with gold. They spread out, took their stations, stretched on the grass; they drank, they tipped the wine-bowls, they kept the fires up. And the Trojans watched from above — they manned the walls in arms, and they checked the gates, anxious and awake, throwing up bridges from tower to tower and stockpiling weapons. Mnestheus drove them, and fierce Serestus, the two men Aeneas had left in charge of the camp and its young soldiers if anything went wrong. Up and down the walls the whole company drew lots for the watches and took their turns, each man covering his share of the danger.

Nisus was on guard at one of the gates — the keenest fighter of them all, son of Hyrtacus, sent to join Aeneas by Ida the huntress, quick with the javelin and the light arrow. Beside him was his friend Euryalus, the most beautiful of all who wore Trojan arms, a boy whose cheeks had only just begun to darken with the first beard. The two of them shared one love and went into every fight together, and tonight they shared the same watch at the gate. Nisus said: "Euryalus — is it the gods who put this fire in us, or does each man make a god of his own fierce wanting? Something has been driving me, a long time now, to throw myself into a fight, into some great thing — I can't sit still and rest. You see how sure of themselves the Rutulians are. Their fires are scattered and low; they're sprawled out drunk and asleep; the whole place is quiet. Listen — here's what I've been turning over. Everyone, the soldiers and the council both, wants Aeneas brought back, wants men sent to carry him word. If they'll promise you what I'm about to ask for myself — for me the glory of it is reward enough — then I think, down at the foot of that hill, I can find a way through to the walls of Pallanteum, where Aeneas is."

Euryalus was struck through with a great love of glory, and he answered his eager friend: "And you'd leave me out of this — send you off alone into the worst of it? Is that what you think of me? No. That isn't how my father Opheltes raised me, in the thick of the wars at Troy and the terror of the Greeks. That isn't how I've borne myself at your side, following Aeneas and his fate to the last. There's a heart in me that holds the daylight cheap and would think a life well spent to buy the honor you're after." Nisus said: "I never doubted you — I couldn't. So may great Jupiter, or whatever god looks kindly on this, bring me back to you in triumph. But if something goes wrong — and you can see how much can go wrong in a thing like this — if some chance or some god drags me down, I want you to outlive me. You're younger; you deserve more of life than I do. Let there be someone to carry my body out of the fight, or to ransom it back, and lay it in the ground; or, if luck even denies that, to make the offerings to an empty tomb and keep my name. And I won't be the cause of such grief to your poor mother, the one woman of all of them brave enough to follow you here, who wouldn't stay behind safe in Acestes' city." But Euryalus answered: "You're spinning out reasons that don't hold. My mind's made up and it won't change. Let's go — now." And he shook the sleeping guards awake to take their places. Then the two of them left their post and went to find Ascanius.

Every other living thing on earth had let its cares go and lay easy in sleep. But the chief men of Troy, the pick of them, were holding a council under arms about the desperate state of things — should they take up weapons, who could carry word to Aeneas. They stood there in the middle of the camp leaning on their long spears, shields on their arms. And into the middle of them came Nisus and Euryalus, in a hurry, begging to be let in: it was urgent, they said, worth the interruption. Ascanius welcomed the two flushed boys and told Nisus to speak. So the son of Hyrtacus said: "Men of Aeneas — hear us out, and don't judge what we're offering by our years. The Rutulians have drunk themselves into sleep; their fires are dying. We've spotted a gap for ourselves, where the road forks at the gate down by the sea. Their fires are broken there, the smoke is rolling up black. Give us the chance, and we'll make our way to Aeneas and the walls of Pallanteum, and you'll see him back here soon, loaded with the spoils of a great slaughter. There's no fear of losing the road. We've hunted that valley in the dim light over and over; we've seen the first of the city's roofs from there, and we know the whole river, every ford of it."

Then old Aletes, heavy with years and ripe in judgment, spoke: "Gods of our fathers — Troy is never wholly abandoned while you raise up young men with hearts like these." And as he said it the tears ran down, and he caught both of them by the shoulders and the hands. "What reward could match what you two are giving? The finest the gods will pay, and your own good conscience. The rest, dutiful Aeneas will give you with his own hands, the moment he's back; and so will Ascanius, who'll never forget a service like this as long as he lives." "More than that," said Ascanius — "my whole hope hangs on my father's safe return. I swear to you both, Nisus, by our household gods, by the great Penates of Assaracus, by the hearth of white-haired Vesta: everything I have, every hope I hold, I put in your hands. Bring my father back to me. Let me see his face again, and nothing else matters. I'll give you two cups worked in silver, embossed with figures, that my father took at the sack of Arisba; two tripods; two great talents of gold; and an old wine-bowl that Dido of Sidon gave me. And if we win Italy and take the throne and divide the spoils — you saw the horse Turnus rides, his gold armor, his crest? That horse, Nisus, and the shield, and the blood-red crest — I'll set them apart now: they're already yours, your prize. And my father will give you twelve chosen women and twelve captive men, all with their own armor, and besides that, all the land King Latinus himself holds. As for you, dear boy, whose years are closer to my own — I take you to my heart here and now, for everything that's coming. There's no glory of mine I won't share with you. War or peace, I'll trust my deepest counsel and my whole faith to you."

Euryalus answered him: "No day will ever find me unequal to a thing this brave — only let fortune fall my way and not against me. But there's one gift I'll ask, past all the rest. I have a mother, of Priam's ancient line, and the poor woman couldn't be kept from following me — not by the land of Troy, not by King Acestes' walls. I'm leaving her now without a word, without the blessing of a goodbye — I swear it by the night and by your right hand — because I couldn't bear her tears. So you be a comfort to her, I beg you. Stand in for me when she's left helpless. Let me carry that hope away with me, and I'll go braver into anything." The hearts of the Trojans broke at that, and the tears came — Ascanius most of all, the picture of a son's love going straight through him. And he said: "I promise you everything your great venture deserves. Your mother will be my mother — she'll lack nothing but the name Creusa — and a son like you has earned her no small honor, whatever comes of this. By my own head I swear it, the oath my father swore: whatever I promised you if you come back and it all goes well, the same will go to your mother and your house." And weeping as he spoke, he took the gold-mounted sword from his own shoulder — a wonder of craft, the work of Lycaon of Cnossos, sheathed in fitted ivory — and gave it to Euryalus. Mnestheus gave Nisus a lion's shaggy hide; loyal old Aletes traded helmets with him. So armed, they went, and the whole company of the Trojan leaders, young and old, walked them to the gate with their prayers — Ascanius above all, a man before his time, his heart already a grown man's, loading them with messages for his father. But all of it the winds tore loose and scattered to the empty clouds.

They crossed the ditch, and went out through the shadows toward the enemy camp — bound to be the death of many before their own night ended. They found the Rutulians stretched out drunk and asleep all over the grass, the chariots tipped up empty on the shore, the men sprawled among the reins and the wheels, wine-cups lying where they had dropped. Nisus spoke first: "Euryalus — now's the time to use our hands. The road's open. You keep watch behind me; look out, all around, that no one comes up at our backs. I'll clear the way ahead and cut you a wide path through them." He said it under his breath, and then he went at proud Rhamnes with his sword. Rhamnes lay there propped high on his heaped rugs, breathing deep in his sleep — a king, and Turnus's own favorite seer; but no skill at prophecy could ward off death. Three of his servants lay sprawled nearby among the weapons, and Nisus killed them, and the squire too, and the charioteer caught under the horses' feet, and lopped their lolling heads off. Then their master Remus — he cut the head clean from his shoulders and left the trunk pumping, the warm black blood soaking the bedding and the ground. He killed Lamyrus and Lamus and young Serranus, a beautiful boy who had gambled late into the night and now lay overcome by the god of wine — happy, if he had kept the dice going till dawn. He went through them like a starving lion turned loose in a crowded fold, mad with hunger, mauling and dragging the soft helpless flock that bleats and dies under its jaws, his mouth running blood.

And Euryalus was killing too, no less, blazing with it, mowing down the nameless crowd as he came on — Fadus, Herbesus, Rhoetus, Abaris, all caught unawares. Rhoetus was awake, watching it all; he tried to hide behind a great wine-bowl, but as he rose Euryalus drove the whole length of the blade into his chest and pulled it out red. He spat up his life in a flood of dark blood and wine, dying where he lay. And Euryalus pressed on, hot with the killing. He was nearly at Messapus's men now, where the last fires were guttering and the war-horses stood tethered, cropping the grass — when Nisus saw how far the lust for slaughter was carrying them both, and pulled him up short: "Enough. The light's coming, and the light is no friend to us. We've cut our lane through them; we've had our blood. Let's stop here." They left behind a fortune in arms, in solid silver, in worked bronze and rich rugs. But Euryalus took Rhamnes's medallions and his belt studded with gold — a gift rich Caedicus once sent to Remulus of Tibur to seal their friendship from afar; Remulus left it to his grandson when he died, and after his death the Rutulians took it in war; and he caught up Messapus's helmet too, light and crested, and slung the belt over his shoulder. Then they slipped out of the camp and made for safe ground.

But meanwhile a column of cavalry had been sent ahead from the Latin city while the main army waited on the plain — three hundred horsemen, all carrying shields, with Volcens at their head, riding to bring Turnus an answer. They were close to the camp now, nearing the walls, when they caught sight of the two friends slipping off down a fork in the road to the left — and there, in the dim shadow of the night, Euryalus's stolen helmet betrayed him, the moon catching it and throwing it back, the one thing he had forgotten. It did not pass unnoticed. "Halt! You there — who are you?" Volcens shouted from the column. "Why under arms? Where are you going? Whose men?" The two said nothing; they bolted for the woods and put their trust in the dark. But the horsemen scattered to block the known paths and threw a ring of guards around every way out. The forest was a wide tangle of black underbrush and bristling holm-oak, choked everywhere with dense thornbushes; here and there a faint track glimmered through the broken ground. Euryalus, slowed by the dark of the branches and the weight of his plunder, lost his bearings and missed the path in his fear. Nisus got clear; he had already run past the enemy and the place that later took its name from Alba, where King Latinus then kept his high cattle-pens — when he stopped, and looked back for his friend, and his friend was not there. "Euryalus," he cried, "poor boy — where did I lose you? Which way? Where?" And he turned, and ran back the whole tangled length of the wood, retracing his own steps, hunting through the silent thickets.

He heard horses, then the noise of pursuit, then the signal-calls. And a moment later a shout reached him — and there was Euryalus. The whole troop had him; the dark and the strange ground had betrayed him, and they had swept down and dragged him off, struggling, helpless. What could Nisus do? What weapon, what strength could tear the boy loose? Should he just throw himself into their swords and die, and make his death glorious with wounds? He drew his arm back fast, lifted his eyes to the high moon, and prayed: "You, goddess — you, glory of the stars, watcher over the woods, Latona's daughter — help me now. If my father Hyrtacus ever brought offerings to your altars for my sake, if I ever added to them from my own hunting, hung your dome with spoils or nailed them to your sacred eaves — let me throw this crowd into chaos. Steady my hand." He finished, and threw, putting his whole body behind the spear. The shaft flew hissing through the shadows of the night and drove into Sulmo's back, snapped off, and the splintered wood went on through his heart. He rolled over, vomiting a hot stream of blood from his chest, his sides heaving in long spasms, and died. The riders looked wildly around. And Nisus, fiercer now, was already balancing a second spear by his ear — and while they were still in confusion it went whistling clean through both of Tagus's temples and stuck there, warm, in the pierced brain.

Volcens was wild with rage, and could find no one to take it out on — no sign of who had thrown — nowhere to aim his fury. "You'll pay for both of them, then," he said, "with your own warm blood," and went for Euryalus with his sword drawn. Then Nisus lost his mind with terror — he could not stay hidden, could not hold himself back, could not bear that much grief. He burst out into the open shouting: "Me — me — I'm here, I did it, turn your swords on me, Rutulians! It was all mine, the whole thing. He didn't dare, he couldn't have — I swear it by the sky and the stars that know — all he was guilty of was loving his luckless friend too much." But the words came too late: the sword was already driven home, hard, and it tore through the boy's white ribs. Euryalus rolled in death, the blood streaming down his lovely body, his neck sinking limp on his shoulder — the way a bright flower cut by the plow droops and dies, or poppies bow their heads on tired stalks when the rain has caught them and weighed them down. But Nisus went straight into the thick of them, going for Volcens alone, only Volcens. They closed around him, beat him back, hacked at him from every side; he kept driving on, whirling his sword like lightning, until he buried it full in the Rutulian's open, shouting mouth, and killed the man as his own life went out of him. Then, riddled through, he threw himself down on his dead friend's body, and there at last found his rest in death.

Fortunate pair! If my verse has any power, no day will ever erase you from the memory of time — not while the house of Aeneas keeps its place by the Capitol's unshaken rock, not while a Roman father rules the empire of the world.

The Rutulians, the winners of the field, took their spoils and their losses both — and carried their dead commander Volcens, weeping, back to the camp. And there the grief grew, for they found Rhamnes dead, and so many of their chiefs cut down in one slaughter — Serranus, Numa, all of them. Crowds gathered around the bodies and the dying, around the ground still warm and steaming with fresh blood and the runnels of it. And they knew Messapus's gleaming helmet among the spoils, and the war-gear, all of it won back at a bitter cost.

By now Dawn was rising from Tithonus's saffron bed, sowing the first light over the earth. The sun came up and stripped the world of shadow, and Turnus, fully armed, called his men to arms, and each captain led his bristling lines out for the fight, and whetted their anger with the news. And — a sight to make the eyes shrink from it — they fixed the heads of Nisus and Euryalus on the points of two spears and carried them out high, with a great shout behind them. The hardened Trojans manned the left wall to face it — the right was covered by the river — and held the wide ditches and stood in their towers, grieving, because they knew the faces lifted up there, all too well, even smeared and dripping with dark gore. And rumor flew on its quick wings through the frightened camp and slid into the ears of Euryalus's mother. In a heartbeat the warmth went out of the poor woman's body. The shuttle dropped from her hand and the thread unspooled. She ran out wailing, tearing her hair the way women do in grief, and rushed straight for the walls and the front ranks, careless of the men, careless of the danger, the spears, and filled the sky with her keening:

"Is this you, Euryalus? Is this how I see you — you, the late comfort of my old age? Cruel boy, how could you leave me alone? They sent you into such danger, and they didn't even let your poor mother say goodbye. Now you lie in a strange land, food for the dogs and the carrion-birds of Latium, and I — your mother — I never walked behind your bier, never closed your eyes, never washed your wounds, never wrapped you in the cloak I sat weaving day and night, all those nights, easing my old age with the work. Where do I even go? What ground holds your torn body now, your hacked limbs, your remains? Is this all you've brought back to me, my son — this? Is this what I followed you over land and sea for? Run me through, Rutulians, if you have any pity. Throw all your spears at me. Kill me first. Or you, great father of the gods, take pity, and hurl my hated life down to the underworld with your bolt, since there's no other way I can break off this cruel living." Her wailing went through them and broke their spirit; a groan of sorrow ran the length of the ranks; their fighting edge went numb and dull. And while she was still feeding the fire of grief, Idaeus and Actor, at Ilioneus's word and the word of Ascanius, who was weeping hard, took her up between them and carried her back inside.

Then, from far off, the brazen trumpet rang its terrible song. A shout went up behind it, and the sky echoed it back. The Volscians came on under a roof of locked shields, racing in to fill the ditches and tear down the palisade. Some looked for a way over the wall, where the defenders stood thin and the ring of men showed gaps. The Trojans rained everything down on them — they had learned in ten years of war how to defend a wall — pikes and missiles of every kind, and rolled heavy stakes down to break the shield-roof, though the men under it took the punishment grimly, packed shoulder to shoulder. But it could not hold. Where the enemy massed thickest, the Trojans heaved over a great boulder that crushed a wide lane through them and cracked the shield-roof open, and the brave Rutulians lost their appetite for fighting blind under cover, and fell back to clearing the ramparts with their spears from a distance instead.

Elsewhere, the grim Mezentius shook a smoking Etruscan pine-brand and flung fire. And Messapus, the tamer of horses, son of Neptune, ripped through the stockade and shouted for ladders to scale the wall. I call on you now, Calliope, and all the Muses, to help me sing the slaughter Turnus dealt that day, the men he sent down to death, each warrior felled — unroll with me the great scroll of the war. You remember it, goddesses; you have the power to tell it.

There was a tower, high and commanding, with bridges running up to it — set in a strong spot, and every Italian strained to storm it, every Trojan strained with all his strength to hold it, hurling stones and pouring spears out through the loopholes packed inside. Turnus opened it: he flung a torch and the flame caught the side of it. Driven by the wind, it ran up the planks and gnawed at the gateways where it bit in. Inside, the men panicked, crowded together, tried to fall back from the fire — and as they bunched to the one side that had not caught, the weight tipped the whole tower, and it came down with a crash that shook the sky, the men falling with it to the earth, the great mass on top of them, run through by their own weapons, their chests staked on the splintered beams. Only two broke free, Helenor and Lycus. Helenor was the elder, a son the Lydian king had secretly fathered on the slave Licymnia and sent to Troy in arms forbidden to one of his birth, lightly armed — a plain sword, a white shield with no device on it. When he found himself ringed by Turnus's thousands, lines of the enemy thick on every side, he did what a wild beast does when the hunters have it boxed in — he threw himself, savage, straight onto their spears, choosing the death he knew was coming, and leaping toward the place the steel ran thickest. But Lycus, faster on his feet, dodged through the enemy and the weapons and made the wall, straining to grab the top of it and the hands of his friends reaching down. Turnus had chased him the whole way, spear and voice both, and now he taunted him: "Did you really think you'd slip away from me, you fool?" — and caught him as he dangled and tore him down, ripping away a chunk of the wall with him. He was like the eagle of Jupiter snatching a hare or a white swan up into the sky in its hooked talons, or the wolf of Mars pulling a lamb from the fold while its mother bleats and bleats for it. The shout went up on every side. The Rutulians rushed the ditches, filling them, and flung firebrands up onto the high roofs.

Ilioneus brought down Lucetius with a mountain of broken rock as he came at the gate with fire. Liger killed Emathion, Asilas killed Corynaeus — one the better archer, the other deadly with a hidden spear thrown from far off. Caeneus killed Ortygius; Turnus killed the victor Caeneus, and then Itys, and Clonius, and Dioxippus, and Promolus, and Sagaris, and Idas standing high on the towers. Capys killed Privernus — Themillas's spear had grazed him first, and the fool dropped his shield to clap a hand over the wound, and the arrow came in and pinned the hand to his left side and burst the breath out of his lungs deep in his chest. The son of Arcens stood out among them, in beautiful armor, his cloak embroidered and dyed bright Spanish purple, a striking boy — his father had raised him in the grove of Mars by the streams of the Symaethus, where the rich, gentle altar of Palicus stands. Mezentius set down his spear, took up the sling, whirled the thong three times around his head, and let fly: the lead split the boy's skull straight down the middle and stretched him out full-length in the sand.

That was the day Ascanius — who till then had only ever bent his bow at fleeing game in the woods — first let an arrow fly in war, and brought down a fighting man. He killed bold Numanus, called Remulus, who had lately married Turnus's younger sister. Numanus had been strutting up and down in front of the lines, swollen with his new royal kinship, bellowing things fit and unfit to say, full of himself: "Aren't you ashamed, you twice-captured Phrygians, penned up behind a wall a second time, holding ditches between yourselves and death? And these are the men who came to take our women by war! What god, what madness drove you to Italy? You'll find no sons of Atreus here, no smooth-tongued Ulysses spinning lies. We're a tough race from a hard root. We carry our newborn sons straight to the river and break them in on the cold of it. Our boys go without sleep to hunt the forests; their sport is to ride down game and bend the bow. Our young men take hard work without flinching, lean on little; they tame the soil with the harrow or shake towns loose in battle. Iron wears at us our whole lives long — we turn the goad around and prod the oxen with the butt of the spear. Slow old age doesn't weaken the spirit in us or sap our strength. We press the helmet down over our gray hair, and we still love to haul home fresh plunder and live off what we take. But you — you wear saffron and gleaming purple, embroidered; you live for lying around; you love to dance. Your tunics have sleeves; your bonnets have ribbons. Phrygian women, that's what you are — not Phrygian men! Go play on the heights of Dindyma, where the pipe sings its double notes for ears like yours. The drums are calling you, the boxwood flute of the Mother of Ida. Leave the weapons to men."

Ascanius could not stand to hear such filth, such an evil omen of a boast. He drew the gut taut across an arrow, faced the man, spread his arms wide to bend the bow — and first stopped to pray, with a vow: "Almighty Jupiter, bless what I'm about to dare. I'll bring my own gifts to your temple every year. I'll lead a snow-white bullock to your altar, its horns gilded, head already as high as its mother's, butting and pawing the sand." The Father heard, and thundered on the left out of a clear sky, and in the same instant the deadly bow twanged. The arrow went whistling out, drove straight through Numanus's head, and split both temples open. "Go on," said Ascanius — "mock real courage with your big words. This is the answer the twice-captured Phrygians send back to the Rutulians." That was all he said; but the Trojans took it up with a roar of joy, lifting their hearts to the stars.

And from a cloud high overhead Apollo happened to be watching the Italian lines and the city, and he spoke to the victorious boy: "Blessings on your new courage, boy — that's the road to the stars. Born of gods, you will father gods. All the wars that are fated to come will rightly end under the house of Assaracus; Troy is too small to hold you." And with that the god came down out of the sky, parting the air, and went to find Ascanius. He put on the look of old Butes, who had once been Anchises's armor-bearer and faithful gatekeeper, and whom Aeneas had since set over his son — the wrinkled face, the voice, the gray hair, the grim old soldier's bearing and weapons. He spoke to the boy, whose blood was up: "Son of Aeneas — let it be enough that Numanus fell to your arrow and you took no hurt. Great Apollo grants you this first glory and doesn't grudge a marksman his equal. But now, boy, hold back from the war." And in the middle of his words Apollo left them and vanished out of their sight into the thin air. The Trojan captains knew the god by his weapons, and heard the rattle of his quiver as he went, and so by Apollo's will and word they held Ascanius back from the fighting, eager as he was for glory; and they themselves went back into the danger, where life hung on every blow.

The shout ran the whole length of the walls and the towers. They bent their savage bows; they whirled the sling-thongs. The ground was carpeted with spent shafts, and helmets and hollow shields rang under the blows — the battle swelling like a rainstorm driven up out of the west when the rainy stars of the Kids are rising, like hail rattling onto the sea when Jupiter wheels his black squalls in off the south and the storm-clouds split open in the sky.

Now Pandarus and Bitias, sons of Alcanor of Ida, raised in Jupiter's grove by the woodland nymph Iaera — two giants, tall as the firs and the mountains that bore them — threw open the gate their commander had ordered them to keep shut, and dared the enemy to come in. Trusting their own strength, they stood inside on the left and right like two armed towers, the steel flashing on them, the crests tossing high over their heads — the way two oaks lift their leafy crowns to the sky on the banks of the Po or beside lovely Athesis, swaying their unshorn tops and nodding to each other in the wind. In poured the Rutulians the moment they saw the gate standing open. At once Quercens, and Aquiculus in his fine armor, and headlong Tmarus, and Haemon, son of Mars, were either thrown back with their whole troop, or died right there in the throat of the gate. The fury rose hotter on both sides; and now the Trojans came pouring out where the Rutulians were thickest and dared to fight them hand to hand in the open in front of the gate.

Turnus was raging in another part of the field, scattering the Trojans, when word reached him that the enemy, hot with fresh slaughter, had thrown the gates wide open. He broke off, and ran in white heat for the gate and the two arrogant brothers. The first to fall was Antiphates — Sarpedon's bastard son by a Theban woman — Turnus's spear caught him first; the Italian cornel-wood flew through the soft air, drove into his belly and high into his chest, and the dark wound brought up a foaming gush of blood, the iron warm in the pierced lung. Then he killed Meropes, and Erymas, and Aphidnus; and then Bitias, his eyes blazing, his blood up — not with a javelin — no javelin would have taken that man's life — but with a great siege-spear, hurled whirling, that came roaring in like a thunderbolt. Two bull-hides could not turn it, and the trusty corselet with its double scales of gold could not turn it; the huge body buckled and went down. The earth groaned under it, and the great shield clashed down on top of him. So a stone pier collapses on the shore at Baiae — the kind they build of massive blocks and sink in the sea — dragged down all at once, smashing into the shallows, the water boiling up black and the sand churned to the surface, so that the island of Prochyta shakes with the noise of it, and Ischia, the hard bed laid on Typhoeus by Jupiter's command.

Now Mars, lord of war, breathed strength and fight into the Latins and stung them deep, and loosed Flight and dark Panic among the Trojans. The Latins came on from every side, the war-god in their hearts at last. When Pandarus saw his brother dead, and how the day was turning, how far the luck had run against them, he put his shoulder to the gate and swung it shut on its hinges with all his weight — and so he locked many of his own men out, abandoned to the enemy in the brutal fight; but others, fool that he was, he shut inside with him, and he never saw Turnus among them, never saw he had penned the king of the Rutulians up inside the walls, loose among them like a monstrous tiger turned in among a defenseless flock. A strange new light flared in Turnus's eyes; his armor clashed and rang; the blood-red crest shook on his helmet; and his shield threw off a blinding glare. The Trojans knew that hated face and the huge frame, and panic took them. But Pandarus came charging out, on fire with rage at his brother's death, and shouted: "This is no dowry-palace of Queen Amata's; this is no walled Ardea that takes Turnus in to his home country. You're looking at an enemy camp. There's no way out of here." Turnus said, calmly, smiling: "Then start it, if you've got the heart. Come on. You can tell Priam, when you meet him down there, that you found Achilles here too." Pandarus put his whole strength behind a rough spear, knots and bark and all — but the air took it; Juno turned the flying point aside, and it stuck fast in the gate. "This one you won't dodge," said Turnus. "My hand is stronger, and my blade." And he rose to his full height, his sword high overhead, and brought it straight down through the middle of the brow, splitting the smooth young face clean in two between the cheeks, a terrible wound. The earth shook with the crash of the great body falling; the dying limbs sprawled out, the armor splashed with brains and blood, and the head hung in two even halves, one on each shoulder.

The Trojans scattered in terror. And if Turnus had only thought, right then, to break the bars and let his comrades in through the gate, that day would have been the last of the war and the last of the Trojans. But the blind lust of slaughter drove him on, raging after the men in front of him. He hamstrung Phaleris; he cut Gyges down; he snatched up their spears and threw them into the backs of the fleeing Trojans, Juno feeding him strength and courage. He sent Halys after them, and Phegeus with his shield run through, and then — caught off guard up on the walls, still shouting their war-cries — Alcander and Halius and Noëmon and Prytanis. Lynceus came at him, calling his men on, and Turnus, swinging from the right, met him with one stroke of the sword that sent the head flying far, helmet and all. Then he killed Amycus, the slaughterer of wild game, the best hand alive at smearing iron with poison; and Clytius, son of Aeolus; and Cretheus, the Muses' friend, Cretheus their companion, who had always loved a song and a lyre and the run of his fingers over the strings, and was forever singing of horses and weapons and men and wars.

At last the Trojan captains, Mnestheus and fierce Serestus, heard of the carnage and came running, and saw their men scattered and the enemy inside the walls. Mnestheus rounded on them: "Where do you think you're running to? Where? What other walls do you have left, beyond these? Men — one man, fenced in on every side by your own ramparts, has poured all this blood through the camp, sent all your best young men down to death — and not one of you ashamed of it, not one of you pitying his poor country, the old gods, the great Aeneas?" That fired them. They steadied, drew together, and stood. And Turnus began to give ground, little by little, edging toward the part of the camp the river ringed. The Trojans came after him all the harder, roaring, massing — the way a crowd closes in on a fierce lion, hedging it with spears, and the beast, frightened now but still savage, glaring, backs off slowly, too proud and too furious to turn and run, but unable to force a way through the men and the weapons, much as it longs to. Just so Turnus drew back, unhurried, doubtful, the anger boiling in him. Even then he charged the thick of them twice, twice sent them flying in a panic the length of the walls. But the whole camp was massing against him now, and not even Juno, daughter of Saturn, dared keep feeding him strength against them all, because Jupiter sent Iris down out of the sky with hard orders for his sister: Turnus must leave the Trojan walls.

So now the young man could no longer hold his own — not with his shield, not with his sword-arm — buried under the storm of weapons coming at him from every side. The helmet rang and rang around his hollow temples; the solid bronze split open under the stones; the crest was beaten flat off his head; the shield-boss could not take the blows. The Trojans, Mnestheus at their head like a thunderbolt, doubled their spears. The sweat ran down Turnus's whole body in a black river; he had no breath left; a sick exhaustion shook his shattered frame. Then at last, armor and all, he threw himself headlong into the river. The Tiber took him in its tawny flood as he fell, lifted him gently on its waves, washed the blood off him, and gave him back, glad of it, to his own.
