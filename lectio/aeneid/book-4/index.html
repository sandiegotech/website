The Aeneid, Book IV

The Tragedy of Dido

Book IV4 of 12

But the queen had been wounded for some time now by a heavy care. She fed the wound in her veins, and a hidden fire ate away at her. The man's courage came back to her again and again, and the high blood of his line; his face and his words stayed fixed in her heart, and her love would not let her body rest. The morning after his story, when the new day's torch had driven the dewy shadows from the sky, she went to her sister, sick at heart, and spoke to her like someone half out of her mind:

"Anna, my sister — these dreams keep me awake and frighten me. What am I to make of this stranger who has come to stay with us? How he carries himself; how brave he is, and how much he has endured! I truly believe he is descended from the gods — and it is no idle belief, for fear shows low birth, and there is no fear in him. And the things fate has put him through! The wars he saw to the bitter end! If my mind were not made up, fixed and unshakable, never again to bind myself to any man — not since my first love cheated me and betrayed me with death — if I were not so sick of the very thought of the marriage bed and its torch, this is perhaps the one weakness I could give in to. Anna — I will admit it to you — ever since my husband Sychaeus was murdered, his blood spilled over our own hearth by my brother, this is the only man who has stirred me, who has shaken my heart loose from where it was set. I recognize the marks of the old flame.

"But I would rather the earth opened under my feet and swallowed me down, or the almighty Father struck me with his lightning and drove me down into the pale shadows, the deep night of the underworld, before I broke my faith, before I broke the vows I made. The man who first joined himself to me took my love with him; let him keep it, and guard it in the grave." She said it, and the tears burst from her and ran down, and she could not go on.

Anna answered: "You are dearer to me than the light I breathe — will you really waste your whole young life grieving alone, never know the sweetness of children, never know what love can give? Do you think the ashes care? Do you think the ghost in the grave is keeping count? Granted, in your first sorrow no suitor could move you — not here in Libya, not before, in Tyre. You turned away Iarbas, and every other lord this rich country can boast. But will you fight against a love that is finally welcome? Have you forgotten whose land you have settled on? On one side the Gaetulians hem you in, a people no one beats in war; the Numidians ride unchecked around you; the Syrtes are a wasteland of moving sand. On the other side a barren, thirsty country; and the Barcaeans, raiders, ranging everywhere. And why even mention the wars threatening from Tyre, and your brother?

"I think it was the gods themselves, and Juno on their side, that drove these Trojan ships onto our shore with the wind behind them. Think what a city you could raise, sister, what a kingdom, with a marriage like this — with Trojan arms standing beside ours, how high Carthage would rise! Only ask the gods for their blessing, lay the offerings, give your guest one excuse after another to stay: the winter, the storms still on the water, Orion's rains, his ships still battered, the sky still no friend to sailing." With these words she set Dido's heart on fire — fanned the hope in a soul already burning, loosened the last hold of shame.

First they went to the shrines and begged the gods for favor at every altar. They picked out yearling sheep, as the rite demands, and sacrificed to Ceres the lawgiver, to Apollo, to Bacchus — and above all to Juno, who has the marriage bond in her keeping. Dido herself, lovely as she was, held the cup in her right hand and poured the wine between the horns of a white heifer, or paced before the gods at their rich altars, renewing the offering all day long. And when the breasts of the animals were opened, she leaned over the still-breathing entrails and read them for a sign. But what do the seers know? What good are vows, what good are shrines, to a woman out of her mind with love? All the while the soft flame ate at the marrow of her bones, and the wound lived on, silent, under her breast.

Dido is burning. Half-mad, she wanders the whole city — like a deer struck by an arrow, when a shepherd hunting in the Cretan woods has shot her without knowing it, from far off, and left the flying iron in her, and gone; and she runs in flight through the forests and the high glades of Dicte, but the deadly shaft stays lodged in her side. She leads Aeneas through the heart of the city, showing off the wealth of Tyre, the town already standing for him; she starts to speak, and stops in the middle of the word. As the day fades she wants the feast all over again, begs, out of her mind, to hear the fall of Troy once more, and hangs again on his lips as he tells it. Then, when they have parted, when the moon in her turn puts out her light and the setting stars urge sleep, she grieves alone in the empty hall and throws herself down on the couch he had left. He is gone, but she sees him, hears him, though he is not there; or she holds Ascanius in her lap for the sake of his father's face in him — anything to cheat a love she cannot speak.

The half-built towers stop rising. The young men quit their drills; the harbor works, the ramparts that were going to fight off any attack — all of it stands abandoned, the cranes idle, the walls broken off short against the sky.

But the moment Juno — Jupiter's dear wife — saw that Dido was gripped by this sickness, that her good name no longer stood between her and her passion, she went to Venus and said: "What a splendid victory you have won — you and that boy of yours! What a name you'll both go down by, what trophies — two gods bringing down one mortal woman with a trick! Oh, I'm not blind: I know you fear my walls, that you've never trusted this rising Carthage. But where does it end? Why are we still at each other's throats? Why not make a lasting peace instead, and seal it with a wedding? You have what your whole heart wanted: Dido is on fire with love, the madness is in her bones. So let us rule this people together, as one. Let her serve a Trojan husband; let her hand her Tyrians over to you for a dowry."

Venus saw straight through her — saw that this was meant to swing the future seat of Italy's empire over to the shores of Africa — and answered carefully: "Who would be fool enough to turn that down and go to war with you instead? If only the outcome you describe could really be made to hold! But I'm uneasy about the Fates, and whether Jupiter wants one city for Tyrians and Trojans both, whether he'll bless the mixing of these two peoples into one. You are his wife; it is your right to test his mind with asking. Lead, and I'll follow."

"That part is mine," Juno said. "Now listen, and I'll tell you in a few words how to bring off what's pressing. When tomorrow's sun first lifts his rays and uncovers the world, Aeneas and the lovesick Dido are planning to ride out into the woods to hunt. While the riders are still spreading their nets around the thickets, I will pour down a black storm of rain and hail and roll the whole sky with thunder. Their company will scatter and run, lost in the dark, and the two of them will reach the same cave — Dido and the Trojan leader. I will be there. If I can count on you to want this, I will join them in lasting marriage and make her his. That cave will be their wedding." The goddess of love did not refuse — but she smiled to herself at the trick.

Dawn rose from the sea, and at first light the chosen riders streamed out through the gates: the wide-meshed nets and the snares, the broad hunting-spears, the Massylian horsemen pouring out with their packs of keen-nosed hounds. The lords of Carthage waited at the queen's threshold while she lingered in her chamber. Her horse stood ready in the courtyard, splendid in purple and gold, champing the foaming bit, pawing the ground. At last she came, with a great crowd around her, wrapped in a Sidonian cloak with an embroidered hem; her quiver was of gold, her hair was caught up in gold, and a golden brooch pinned the purple at her shoulder. The Trojan column came on too, with happy Ascanius in the middle of it. But the most beautiful of all of them was Aeneas himself, who came up and joined his men to hers. He looked like Apollo when the god leaves wintry Lycia and the river Xanthus behind and comes home to Delos to set the dances going again — Cretans and Dryopes and the painted Agathyrsi crowding and shouting around the altars; the god himself walks the ridges of Cynthus, his streaming hair bound with soft leaves and a band of gold, his arrows ringing on his shoulder. Aeneas moved no less lightly than that; the same grace and beauty shone in his face.

When they reached the high hills, the trackless lairs, the wild goats came bounding down the rocks from the ridge, and on the open slopes the deer broke cover and ran in a herd across the plain, raising the dust, leaving the high ground behind them. In the middle of the valley young Ascanius gloried in his fast horse, galloping past one group and then another, praying in his heart that out of all this tame quarry a foaming wild boar might come his way, or a tawny lion come down from the hills.

Then a low rumble began to roll across the sky, and the clouds came up, and a storm of rain and hail broke. The Tyrian riders and the young Trojans and Venus's grandson — Dardanus's heir — scattered in fear across the fields, looking for any shelter. Streams came tearing down from the hills. Dido and the Trojan leader came to the same cave. And then the primal Earth and Juno, who makes marriages, gave the sign: the lightning flashed, the sky stood witness to the union, and on the high places the nymphs cried out. That day was the first cause of death, the first cause of every grief that came after. For from then on Dido stopped caring how it looked or what was said. She no longer hid her love. She called it marriage, and used that word to cover what she had done.

Word of it ran at once through the great cities of Libya — Rumor, the swiftest of all evils. Rumor thrives on speed and gathers strength as she goes; small and timid at first, she quickly grows enormous, walking the ground with her head in the clouds. The story goes that Earth, in her rage at the gods, bore her last, as a sister to the giants Coeus and Enceladus — a monster, fast on her feet, fast on the wing, terrible, vast. For every feather on her body there is an eye underneath that never sleeps — strange to say — and as many tongues, as many loud mouths, as many ears pricked up to listen. By night she flies between earth and sky through the dark, hissing, and never once shuts her eyes in sleep. By day she sits on the rooftops or the high towers as a lookout, and throws whole cities into terror — and she clings to lies and slander as tightly as she reports the truth.

Now she went through the towns filling the people's ears with all of it, gloating, mixing fact and fiction without distinction: that a man had come, Aeneas, sprung from Trojan blood, and the lovely Dido had thought him worth marrying; that the two of them were warming the long winter in luxury and pleasure, lost to each other, careless of their kingdoms, slaves to a shameful passion. This is what the foul goddess poured into the mouths of men everywhere. Then she swung her course to King Iarbas, and with her words she set his heart ablaze and piled the fuel of his anger high.

Iarbas was a son of Jupiter Ammon, born of a Garamantian nymph the god had carried off, and across his wide kingdom he had raised a hundred huge temples to his father, a hundred altars, and consecrated the undying fire on them, the gods' perpetual watch; the ground was rich with the blood of sacrifice, the doorways hung with garlands of every flower. They say that out of his mind, stung to the quick by the bitter news, he stood before the altars, among the powers of the gods, and prayed to Jupiter with his hands turned up, begging:

"Almighty Jupiter — to whom the Moorish people pour out wine now as they feast on their embroidered couches — do you see this? Father, when you hurl your thunderbolt, are we right to fear you, or are those fires in the clouds blind and meaningless, the thunder just empty noise? A woman, a wanderer, who came into my country and bought the right to build a little town, who held her strip of coast at my sufferance and on my terms — that woman has turned down marriage with me and taken Aeneas into her kingdom as her lord. And now this second Paris, with that crew of half-men, his chin and his oiled hair bound up in a Lydian bonnet, walks off with what he has stolen — while I keep bringing offerings to your temples and clinging to a name that means nothing."

As he prayed with his hand on the altar, the Almighty heard him and turned his eyes to the royal city and the lovers, who had forgotten their better name. He called Mercury and gave him his orders: "Go, my son. Call the West Winds and glide down on your wings to the Trojan leader, who is loitering in Carthage and giving no thought to the city the Fates owe him. Carry my words to him down through the swift air. This is not the man his lovely mother promised me when twice she saved him from the swords of Greece. She promised a man who would rule Italy — a land pregnant with empire, roaring for war — who would carry on the high line of Teucer and bring the whole world under one law. If the glory of such a future cannot move him, if he will lift no finger for his own honor, will he begrudge his son Ascanius the towers of Rome? What does he think he is doing? What does he hope for, sitting among a people who hate him, with no thought for his own descendants, for the land of Italy? Let him sail. That is the whole of it. Take him that message from me."

Mercury made ready to obey his father's command. First he bound on the golden sandals that carry him on their wings high over sea and land, fast as the wind. Then he took up the wand with which he calls the pale souls up out of the underworld, or sends others down into its gloom, gives sleep or takes it away, and unseals the eyes of the dead. With that in his hand he drove the winds before him and swam down through the storm-wrack, and as he flew he saw the peak and the steep sides of Atlas, who holds the sky up on his head — Atlas, whose pine-dark crown is forever wrapped in black cloud and beaten by wind and rain, whose shoulders are buried in snow, with rivers pouring down his old chin and his beard stiff with ice. Here Mercury checked his flight and hung balanced on his wings, then dropped headlong toward the sea like a seabird that skims low over the water along the shore, hunting fish. So Mercury cut between earth and sky and flew in along the sandy coast of Libya, down the wind, from his mother's father Atlas.

The moment his winged feet touched the huts of Carthage he caught sight of Aeneas — laying out foundations, raising new buildings. His sword was starred with yellow jasper, and a cloak of glowing Tyrian purple hung from his shoulders, a gift Dido had made him, shot through with fine gold thread. Mercury went straight at him: "So this is what you've come to — laying the foundations of high Carthage, building a beautiful city, a slave to your wife. Have you forgotten your own kingdom, your own affairs? The king of the gods himself sends me down to you from bright Olympus — he whose will turns the sky and the earth — and orders me to carry these words to you through the swift air. What are you planning? What do you hope to gain idling here in Libya? If the glory of your own great destiny means nothing to you, if you will lift no finger for your own honor, then think of Ascanius growing up, of all that is owed to your heir Iulus — the kingdom of Italy, the soil of Rome." That said, while the words were still on his lips Mercury vanished out of human sight and dissolved into thin air, far off.

Aeneas was struck dumb at the sight; the words choked in his throat and his hair stood on end with fear. He burned to be gone, to flee this land he loved — terrified, awed, by the sharp warning, by the command of the gods. But what could he do? With what words could he dare to approach the queen, with her passion at full pitch? How could he even begin? His mind raced one way, then another, turning the problem over from every side. As he weighed it, this seemed the best course: he called Mnestheus and Sergestus and brave Serestus and told them quietly to fit out the fleet, gather the men on the shore, get the gear ready, and keep the reason for it hidden. He himself, meanwhile, since the kindhearted Dido knew nothing yet and could not imagine so great a love could ever be broken, would look for the right moment, the gentlest way to speak to her, the right time to approach. They were glad to hear it, and quick to obey.

But the queen — who can deceive a woman in love? — guessed the trick at once. She caught the first stir of what was coming even while it was safe; she was the first to sense the plan. The same wicked Rumor told her, frantic now, that the fleet was being fitted out for sailing. Out of her mind, helpless, she raged through the city, on fire — like a Maenad whipped into the dance when the cry goes up and the sacred emblems are shaken out, and the festival every three years stings her to madness, and Cithaeron calls to her through the night. At last she found Aeneas and went at him before he could speak: "You traitor — did you really think you could keep a thing this huge a secret, and slip out of my country without a word? Does nothing hold you — not our love, not the hand you once gave me, not Dido, who is going to die a cruel death? Even now, under a winter sky, you'd drive your fleet out and rush headlong across the sea in the teeth of the north wind? You hard, cruel man. If you weren't chasing strangers' fields and homes you've never seen — if old Troy still stood — would you be sailing for Troy across a sea like this?

"Is it me you're running from? By these tears, by the hand you gave me — they are all I have left now, poor as I am — by our union, by the marriage we began: if I ever did you any kindness, if anything about me ever gave you joy, take pity on a falling house. I beg you, if there is still any room for begging, change your mind. The peoples of Libya hate me because of you, the Numidian chiefs hate me; my own Tyrians have turned against me. For you I lost my honor, the one thing that was carrying me toward the stars — my good name. To whom are you leaving me to die, my guest? — for that is the only word I have left for you, where I used to say husband. What am I waiting for? For my brother Pygmalion to come and tear down my walls, or for Iarbas the Moor to drag me off as his prize? At least, if I had a child of yours before you ran — if there were some little Aeneas to play in my hall, who would bring your face back to me — I wouldn't feel so utterly deceived and deserted."

She finished. He, obeying Jupiter, kept his eyes still and fought down the pain in his heart by main force. At last he spoke, and not much: "My queen — I will never deny that you have earned more from me than I could ever list. I will never tire of remembering Dido while I have breath and memory, while I am still myself. I'll say a few things for my defense. I never hoped to steal away in secret — don't imagine that — and I never held out the marriage torch to you or came into any such contract. If the Fates would let me live the life I chose and settle my own troubles my own way, I would be tending the city of Troy first of all, and the dear remains of my people; Priam's high halls would still be standing, and I would have raised the citadel of Troy again for the defeated with my own hands. But as it is, Apollo of Grynium, the Lycian oracle, has ordered me to make for Italy — Italy is my love, Italy is my country.

"If the towers of Carthage and the sight of a Libyan city can hold you, a Phoenician, here, why begrudge the Trojans a home in Italy? It is no crime for us, too, to look for a kingdom in a foreign land. Every night, when the dark wet shadows cover the earth and the fiery stars come up, my father Anchises comes to me in my sleep, his face troubled, and warns me, and frightens me. I think of Ascanius too, and the wrong I am doing to that dear head — I am cheating him out of Italy, out of the kingdom the Fates have promised him. And now the messenger of the gods, sent down by Jupiter himself — I swear it on both our lives — came to me through the rushing air, in broad daylight, and I saw him with my own eyes, here, within these walls, and heard his voice with my own ears. Stop wounding both of us with your grief. I do not sail for Italy of my own free will."

All the while he was speaking she had been glaring at him sidelong, turning her eyes over him from head to foot without a word, and now she blazed up: "No goddess was your mother, and there is no Dardanus in your line, you liar. The Caucasus bore you on its hard rocks, and Hyrcanian tigers gave you their teat. Why should I hold back? What worse is there to keep myself for? Did he so much as sigh while I was weeping? Did he turn his eyes to me? Did he break down and shed one tear, did he pity the woman who loves him? What can I even put first? Now neither great Juno nor the Father, Saturn's son, can look on this with even-handed eyes. There is no faith left anywhere. I took him in, shipwrecked, a beggar, and like a fool I gave him half my throne. I saved his lost fleet, I saved his crews from death. Oh, the Furies have me, I am on fire! — Now it's the prophet Apollo, now the Lycian oracle, now the very messenger of the gods sent down by Jupiter to carry this dreadful order through the air. As if that were work for the powers above! As if a worry like this ever broke the calm of a god!

"I won't keep you. I won't argue. Go, chase Italy on the wind, look for your kingdom across the sea. But I hope — if the just gods have any power at all — that you drink your punishment down to the dregs on the rocks, and call on Dido's name again and again. I will be far off, but I will hound you with black fire, and when cold death has parted my soul from my body my ghost will be with you everywhere. You will pay, you wretch. I will hear of it. The news will reach me even among the dead." She broke off in the middle and rushed away, sick at the sight of the sky, and turned from him and left him there afraid and faltering, with much still to say. Her maids caught her as she fainted, carried her to her marble chamber, and laid her on the bed.

But dutiful Aeneas, much as he longed to comfort her grief and to talk her out of her pain, groaning, his heart shaken by the strength of his love, still obeyed the command of the gods and went back to his fleet. Then the Trojans threw themselves into the work and ran their tall ships down the whole length of the shore. The pitched hulls floated; the men brought oars still green with leaves and unworked timber out of the woods in their hurry to be gone. You could see them streaming down out of the city, the way ants do when they plunder a great heap of grain against the winter and carry it home: a black column moves through the fields, hauling the loot along a narrow track through the grass; some heave the big kernels along on their shoulders, some keep the line in order and whip on the stragglers; the whole path boils with the work.

What did you feel, Dido, seeing all this? What groans were torn out of you when you looked down from the top of your tower and saw the whole shore alive with them, and the open sea churned up with all that shouting? Cruel Love — what do you not drive the human heart to do? Once again she was forced to weep, forced to beg, to bow her pride down under her love, so as to leave nothing untried before she died. "Anna, you see them rushing all over the shore. They've gathered from everywhere; the sails are already calling for the wind, and the happy sailors have wreathed the sterns. Sister, if I could have braced myself for a grief this size, I can bear it now. But do this one thing for me in my misery, Anna. That traitor used to confide in you — only you; you alone know the soft way to his secret heart, the right moment. Go to him, sister, and humble yourself before this proud enemy of mine. I never swore at Aulis with the Greeks to wipe out the Trojan race; I sent no fleet against Troy; I never tore up his father Anchises's ashes or his ghost. Why does he shut his hard ears against me? Where is he rushing? Let him grant his poor lover this one last gift: let him wait for an easy crossing and a fair wind. I am not asking for the old marriage now, the one he has betrayed; I am not asking him to give up Latium or his kingdom. I am asking for time — a little empty time, a breathing space for my madness, until my luck teaches a beaten woman how to grieve. This last favor I beg — pity your sister — and when he grants it I will pay it back with interest, dying."

This was the prayer she sent, and Anna carried it, weeping, back and forth. But no weeping moved him; he would not bend, would not listen to any words. The Fates stood in the way, and a god stopped the man's gentle ears. As a mountain oak, hardened by the years, stands when the north winds blow first from one quarter and then another, fighting to uproot it — the gusts shriek through it, the leaves come down and cover the ground, but the tree holds on to the rock, and as high as it lifts its crown toward heaven, so deep its roots go down toward Tartarus — just so the hero was battered, this way and that, by their unending pleas, and felt the grief deep in his great heart; but his purpose held, unmoved, and the tears rolled down for nothing.

Then poor Dido, terrified of what fate had in store, prayed for death; she was sick of looking at the arch of the sky. And to drive her on to leave the light, she saw — horrible to tell — the holy water blacken on the altars and the wine she poured turn to filthy blood. She told no one what she saw, not even her sister. There was more: she had a marble chapel in the palace, sacred to her dead husband, that she tended with extraordinary love, wreathed with white fleeces and festival leaves; and from it, when the dark held the earth, she thought she heard her husband's voice calling her, and the lonely owl on the rooftops kept up its long death-cry into the night. And the dark words of the old seers came back to haunt her. In her dreams Aeneas himself drove her on, savage; and always she seemed to be left alone, going on and on by herself down some endless road, looking for her Tyrians in an empty land — the way Pentheus, out of his mind, sees the columns of the Furies and a double sun and two cities of Thebes; or the way Orestes is hunted across the stage, running from his own mother armed with torches and black snakes, while the avenging spirits wait crouched in the doorway.

So when the madness broke her at last, and she had settled on death, she worked out for herself the time and the way, and went to her grieving sister with a calm face that hid her plan and a false hope shining in her eyes. "Anna — be glad for me. I've found the thing that will bring him back to me, or set me free of loving him. Out where the Ocean ends and the sun goes down, at the very edge of Ethiopia, where giant Atlas turns the wheel of the burning stars on his shoulder — out there I have learned of a priestess, a Massylian, who kept the temple of the Hesperides and fed the dragon and guarded the sacred boughs of the golden tree, sprinkling out honey and sleepy poppy. She claims that with her spells she can set any heart free, or load another with cruel cares; that she can stop rivers and turn the stars back in their courses; that she calls up the ghosts in the night — you'll feel the ground bellow under your feet and see the mountain ashes come walking down the slopes. I call the gods to witness, sister, and your own dear life, that I take up these dark arts against my will. Go, quietly, and raise a pyre in the inner court, open to the sky, and on it lay the arms that godless man left hanging on my wall, and everything he wore, and the marriage bed that was my ruin. I want every trace of him wiped out — the priestess orders it." Then she fell silent, and the color drained from her face.

But Anna never guessed that her sister was masking her own funeral behind these strange rites. She could not imagine such a madness, and feared nothing worse than when Sychaeus had died. So she did as she was told. When the pyre had been built high in the inmost court, out of pine logs and split oak, the queen hung the place with garlands and crowned it with the leaves of death. On top she laid his sword, the clothes he had worn, and an image of him on the bed — knowing perfectly well what was coming. The altars stood around it, and the priestess, her hair loose, called in a voice of thunder on three hundred gods — on Erebus and Chaos and Hecate the threefold, the three faces of the maiden Diana. She had sprinkled water that she said came from Avernus's spring; she gathered herbs cut by moonlight with bronze sickles, swollen with their black milk of poison, and the love-charm torn from the brow of a newborn foal before its mother could snatch it. Dido herself, with the sacred meal in her clean hands, stood close to the altars with one foot bare and her dress unbound, and called on the gods, and on the stars that know what is fated, as she made ready to die; and she prayed to whatever power, if any, is just and remembers and looks after lovers who have been wronged.

It was night, and tired bodies all over the earth were taking their quiet sleep. The woods were still, the wild seas still; it was the hour when the stars are halfway through their slow wheel, when every field falls silent — the cattle and the bright-colored birds, all the creatures of the wide lakes and the rough thorny country, lay sleeping under the silent night, soothing their cares, their hearts forgetting the day's hard work. But not the queen. Her wretched heart could not loosen into sleep; the night gave her no rest for her eyes or her mind. Her pain doubled; her love rose up and broke over her again and again, and she churned in a great tide of anger. And so she fixed on this, turning it over in her heart:

"Look at me. What do I do now? Go back to my old suitors and be laughed at — go begging for a Numidian marriage to men I scorned so many times before? Or follow the Trojan fleet, do anything the lowest Trojan tells me? Because of course they're so grateful for the help I gave them once; of course the memory of what I did sits warm in their hearts. And even if I wanted to, who would let me? Who would take a woman they hate aboard those proud ships? Don't you know yet, poor fool — don't you understand yet how the house of Laomedon breaks its word? And what then? Do I run off alone with the sailors as they cheer, or call out my Tyrians, my whole army, and drive them back to sea — men I could barely tear away from Tyre — and tell them to spread the sails again? No. Die, the way you have earned, and let the sword end the pain. You, sister — you started this; you gave in to my tears, you were the first, and you've loaded me down with these troubles and thrown me to the enemy. I wasn't allowed to live my life apart from marriage, blameless, the way a wild thing does, and keep clear of grief like this. I broke the faith I promised to the ashes of Sychaeus." That was the lament that kept breaking out of her. Meanwhile Aeneas, his mind made up to go, was already asleep on the high stern, everything ready.

And in his sleep the god came back to him in the same shape as before, and seemed to warn him again — Mercury, the same in every way, the voice, the color, the golden hair, the young limbs: "Son of the goddess, how can you sleep through a crisis like this? Don't you see the dangers closing around you, you madman? Can't you hear the west wind blowing fair? She is turning over treachery and terrible crimes in her heart, set on dying, and a whole sea of rage is rising in her. Why aren't you gone already, while you still can be? Any minute now you'll see the water churning with ships and the cruel torches blazing, you'll see the whole shore alive with fire, if dawn finds you still lingering on this coast. Up — no more delay. A woman is always a changing, shifting thing." That said, he melted into the black night.

Then Aeneas, terrified by the sudden vision, tore himself out of sleep and shook his men awake: "Up, men, fast — to the benches, run out the oars, get the sails up, now! For the second time a god has come down from the high sky and ordered us to hurry our flight and cut the twisted cables. We follow you, holy one, whoever you are; gladly we obey your command again. Be with us, help us, set the kind stars in the sky for us." He drew his sword, flashing it from the sheath, and slashed the mooring rope. The same hunger gripped them all; they snatched and ran and cleared the beach. The water vanished under the hulls; they bent to it, churning the foam, sweeping the blue sea.

By now Dawn was leaving Tithonus's saffron bed and beginning to scatter new light over the world. From her tower the queen saw the first whiteness, saw the fleet standing out under even sails, saw the shore and the harbor empty, not an oarsman left. Three times, four times she struck her lovely breast with her fist and tore her golden hair, and cried out: "Jupiter — is he to go? Is a stranger to make a mockery of my kingdom? Won't they take up arms, won't the whole city pour after him? Won't they drag the ships down from the docks? Go — bring fire, hand out weapons, lean on the oars! What am I saying? Where am I? What madness is turning my mind? Poor Dido, only now does your own wickedness come home to you. It should have, when you handed him your crown. So that's his word, his honor — the man they say carries his father's gods with him, who hoisted his worn-out father on his shoulders and saved him! Couldn't I have torn his body apart and thrown the pieces in the sea? Couldn't I have put his men to the sword, and Ascanius too, and served the boy up at his father's own table?

"Sun, whose fire looks down on everything men do; Juno, who knows this agony and presides over it; Hecate, whose name is screamed at the crossroads in the dark; you avenging Furies, and you gods of dying Dido — hear me. Turn your power, as it deserves, on this evil, and listen to my prayer. If it is fixed by fate that that abomination must reach harbor and come to land — if Jupiter's own decree demands it, if that is the end set and unchangeable — then at least let him be ground down by war and the arms of a bold people. Let him be driven out of his own borders, torn from the embrace of Iulus, begging for help, watching his people die undeserved deaths. And when he has bowed to a hard, unjust peace, let him not enjoy his kingdom or the life he wanted, but fall before his time and lie unburied on the open sand. This is my prayer; this is my last cry, poured out with my blood. And you, my Tyrians — hound his whole line, every generation to come, with your hatred. Send that as a gift to my ashes. Let there be no love between our peoples ever, no treaty. And rise, some avenger, out of my bones, to hunt down these Trojan settlers with fire and the sword, now or in the fullness of time, whenever the strength is there. Coast against coast, sea against sea, sword against sword — I call it down: let them fight, them and their children's children forever."

Then she turned her mind every way, looking for the quickest road out of the daylight she hated. And she said a few words to Barce, the nurse of Sychaeus — her own old nurse was a black heap of ashes back in the old country — "Dear nurse, bring my sister Anna here. Tell her to hurry, to sprinkle herself with river water and bring the sheep and the offerings the rite calls for. Let her come like that. And you, bind your own brow with the holy band. I mean to finish the rites of Stygian Jupiter that I have begun and set in order, and put an end to my sorrows by giving the Trojan's image on the pyre to the flames." The old woman went off eagerly, hurrying as fast as her age would let her.

But Dido, frantic, wild with the enormous thing she had set herself to do, her bloodshot eyes rolling, her trembling cheeks blotched and burning, white already with the death that was upon her, burst through into the inner court and climbed the high pyre in a fury and drew the Trojan's sword — a gift she had not asked for any such use. There she looked at the Trojan clothes and the bed she knew so well, and paused a moment to weep and remember; then she lay down on the bed and spoke her last words: "Sweet relics, dear while god and fate allowed it — take my spirit, and free me from this pain. I have lived; I have run the course that Fortune set me, and now my ghost goes down beneath the earth, no small one. I built a famous city; I saw my own walls rise; I avenged my husband and made my brother pay for his crime. Happy, ah, too happy I would have been, if only the Trojan ships had never touched my shore." She pressed her face to the bed. "I shall die unavenged — but let me die. This, this is how I want to go down into the dark. Let that cruel Trojan drink in the sight of this fire from out at sea, and carry the omen of my death with him."

Even as she spoke, her women saw her fall on the blade, the sword foaming with blood, her hands red with it. A scream went up to the high ceilings, and Rumor went rioting through the shaken city. The whole house rang with grief and wailing and the cries of women; the air shook with the great lament — as if all Carthage, or old Tyre, were falling to an invading enemy, and the flames were rolling wild over the rooftops of men and gods alike. Her sister heard, and ran, out of her mind with terror, scratching her face, beating her breast, calling Dido's name through the crowd: "So this is what it was, sister! You were tricking me all along! The pyre, the altars, the fires were meant for this? You left me — what should I grieve for first? You couldn't bear your sister beside you, even dying. You should have called me to share your fate. One stroke, one hour, one sword, should have taken us both. Did I build this with my own hands, did I call on our family's gods, only to be away — cruel — when you lay down here? Sister, you have destroyed yourself, and me, and your people, and the city you built. Quick — water, let me wash her wounds, and if any last breath is still hovering, let me catch it with my lips." She had climbed to the top of the pyre as she spoke, and she gathered her dying sister to her breast, sobbing, and tried to staunch the dark blood with her own dress. Three times Dido struggled to lift herself on her elbow; three times she fell back on the bed. Her wandering eyes searched the high sky for the light, and when she found it she moaned.

Then Juno, all-powerful, took pity on the long agony, the slow and difficult death, and sent Iris down from heaven to release the struggling soul from the body that held it. For since Dido was dying neither by fate nor by any death she had deserved, but before her time, in a sudden fit of madness, Proserpina had not yet cut the golden lock from her head or marked her down for the underworld. So Iris flew down on her dewy saffron wings, trailing a thousand shifting colors against the sun, and stood over Dido's head. "This lock I take, as I am bidden, and give to Dis; and I free you from your body." So she spoke, and cut the lock with her hand. And at once all the warmth went out of Dido, and her life passed away into the winds.
