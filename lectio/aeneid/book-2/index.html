The Aeneid, Book II

The Fall of Troy

Book II2 of 12

They all fell silent and turned their faces toward him, intent. And from his high couch Aeneas, the father of his people, began:

The grief you ask me to bring back to life, my queen, is beyond words — how the Greeks tore down the wealth of Troy, that kingdom worth weeping over. I saw the worst of it myself; I was a part of much of it. Who could tell such a story — a soldier of the Myrmidons, a man of Dolopian blood, even one of hard Ulysses's own — and keep from crying? And now the dewy night is sliding down the sky, and the setting stars are calling us to sleep. But if you long so much to learn what happened to us, to hear the last agony of Troy in a few words — though my mind shudders at the memory and recoils from the grief — I'll begin.

Beaten in the war, turned back year after year by fate, the Greek commanders built a horse as huge as a mountain, with the craft of Pallas guiding the work, and ribbed its flanks with planks of fir. They gave it out as an offering for their safe return home — that was the story that went around. But deep in its dark sides they sealed a chosen body of men, picked by lot, and packed the great hollow belly of the beast tight with soldiers and weapons.

Off our coast lies Tenedos, an island famous and rich while Priam's kingdom stood — now just a bay where ships can find poor anchorage. The Greeks sailed there and hid themselves along its empty shore. We thought they had gone, sailing with the wind for Mycenae. So all of Troy threw off the long years of grief. The gates swung open. What joy it was to walk out and see the Greek camp deserted, the shore abandoned, the empty ground where their army had stood. Here the company of Achilles had pitched; here Ulysses; here the fleet had been drawn up; here the lines had clashed. Some of us stood gaping at the deadly gift to the virgin Minerva, marveling at the sheer size of the horse.

Thymoetes was the first to urge that it be hauled inside the walls and set in the citadel — whether he was a traitor, or whether the fate of Troy was already bending that way, I cannot say. But Capys, and those with sounder heads, told us to throw this trap of the Greeks, this gift we should never trust, into the sea — or burn it — or bore into the belly and search out whatever was hidden there. The crowd split, pulled both ways, and could not decide.

Then Laocoön came running down from the heights of the citadel, a great crowd streaming after him, and while he was still far off he shouted: "You poor fools! What madness is this? Do you really believe the enemy has sailed away? Do you think any gift of the Greeks comes without a trap? Is that all you know of Ulysses? Either there are men shut up inside this wood, or it's an engine built against our walls, made to look down into our homes and come down on the city from above — there's some trick in it, some lie. Trojans, do not trust this horse. Whatever it is, I fear the Greeks even when they come bearing gifts."

With that he put all his strength into a great spear and hurled it at the beast's curved flank, where the belly joins the frame. It struck and stood there quivering, and the whole hollow chamber groaned and gave back a deep boom from the wound. If the gods had not been against us, if our own minds had not been blind, he would have driven us to hack open that Greek hiding place with our blades — and Troy would be standing now, and you, high citadel of Priam, would remain.

But then — look — some Trojan herdsmen came dragging a young man toward the king, hands bound behind him, all of them shouting at once. He was a stranger who had thrown himself in their way on purpose, to bring this very thing about, to open Troy to the Greeks. He was sure of his nerve, and ready for it either way: to pull off the trick, or to die. From every side the young men of Troy came pouring up to see the prisoner, crowding around, jeering at him. Now hear how the Greeks set their trap, and learn from this one man's treachery what the whole nation was capable of.

He stood there in the middle of all those eyes, helpless, unarmed, looking around at the ranks of Trojans, and said: "Where on earth, where on any sea, is there a place left for me now? What's left for me, in the end, with nowhere to turn — no home among the Greeks anymore, and the Trojans furious, screaming for my blood?" That cry turned our feeling. The anger died down. We pressed him to tell us where he came from, what he had to say, what we might trust in a captured enemy. At last he set his fear aside and spoke:

"Whatever happens to me, my king, I'll tell you the whole truth. I won't deny that I'm a Greek — that first of all. Fortune has made Sinon wretched, but she will never make him a liar. Maybe some rumor of the name Palamedes has reached your ears — a famous man, of glorious renown, whom the Greeks put to death on a lying charge of treason, on perjured evidence, just because he spoke against the war. Now that he's dead they mourn him. My father, who was poor, sent me as a boy to fight in this war as Palamedes's companion, for we were kin. As long as he stood firm in power, with weight in the councils of the kings, I too had a name, I too had some honor. But when he fell — through the spite of that schemer Ulysses (you all know the story) — I dragged out my life in the shadows, in grief and rage at the ruin of an innocent man. And I did not keep quiet. I swore that if I ever made it home to Argos, I would have my revenge — and with those words I made a deadly enemy.

"That was the first crack in my ruin. From then on Ulysses kept terrifying me with new accusations, kept dropping dark hints among the men, kept looking for a way to destroy me. He never rested until, with Calchas as his tool — but why am I telling you this bitter, useless story? Why hold you up? If all Greeks are the same to you, and it's enough that I'm one of them, then punish me now. That's what the man of Ithaca wants; the sons of Atreus would pay well for it." Now of course we were burning to know more, to hear the rest — with no idea what we were dealing with, no notion of Greek cunning on this scale. Trembling, he went on, and every word was a lie:

"Many times the Greeks longed to give up, to abandon Troy and sail home, worn out by the endless war — and how I wish they had! But again and again the wild winter sea shut them in, or a gale rose and frightened them out of going. And once the horse was finished, framed from maple beams, the storms thundered across the whole sky. Baffled and afraid, we sent Eurypylus to ask Apollo's oracle what to do, and he came back from the shrine with a grim answer: 'With blood you bought your first crossing to Troy, Greeks — with a girl's life sacrificed. With blood you must buy your return; the price is a Greek soul.' When that ran through the ranks, every man went cold; a shudder went down to the bone. Whose death were the Fates demanding? Whom did Apollo want?

"Now the man of Ithaca made his move. He dragged Calchas the prophet out into the middle of the crowd and demanded to know what the gods were asking. Plenty of men already saw where this cruel trick was aimed, and watched in silence as it came for me. For ten days Calchas held his tongue, refusing to name anyone, to send a man to his death by a word. But at last, hounded by Ulysses, he broke — as the two of them had arranged all along — and named me as the sacrifice. Everyone agreed. Each man, terrified for himself, was glad to see the doom fall on one poor head instead. The dreadful day came. The salted meal was made ready, the ribbons were tied around my brow. But I broke free, I admit it. I snapped my bonds and ran, and hid all night in the mud of a reedy marsh, waiting for them to set sail — if they ever would. And now I have no hope of ever seeing my old home again, my sweet children, the father I long for. The Greeks will make them pay for my escape, poor souls, with their own deaths. So I beg you — by the gods above who know the truth, by whatever honest faith is left anywhere among men — pity a suffering past all deserving. Pity a man who has done you no wrong."

We spared his life for those tears — and more than that, we pitied him. Priam himself was the first to order the chains and tight cords struck off the man, and he spoke to him gently: "Whoever you are, forget the Greeks you've lost from now on. You're one of us. But answer me straight: why did they build this monstrous horse? Whose idea was it? What's it for — worship, or war?" He answered, schooled in all the tricks and treachery of the Greeks, lifting his freed hands to the stars: "You everlasting fires of heaven, you powers no man may violate, I call you to witness — and you, the altars and the cruel blades I fled, the ribbons I wore as a victim: it is right for me now to break the sacred bond I owed the Greeks, right to hate those men and drag every secret of theirs into the light. No law of my own country holds me now. Only you, Troy — keep your promise, and keep faith with me, if what I tell you saves you and is the truth.

"Every hope the Greeks had, all their confidence in this war, rested from the start on the help of Pallas. But from the moment that godless pair — Diomedes and Ulysses, the great inventor of crimes — dared to tear her fateful image, the Palladium, from her holy temple, cut down the guards on the citadel heights, and lay their bloody hands on the sacred statue, defiling the virgin's sacred bands — from that moment the tide of Greek fortune slipped backward and began to ebb. Their strength was broken; the goddess turned her face away. And she gave no doubtful sign of it. The image was barely set down in the camp when flame flashed from its staring eyes, salt sweat ran down its limbs, and three times — a marvel to tell — it leaped from the ground of its own accord, shield and spear quivering. At once Calchas declared they must take to the sea and flee — that Troy could never be taken by Greek weapons unless they went back to Argos for fresh omens, and brought the goddess's blessing home again across the water, the way they had carried it out in their curved ships. And now they have sailed for home, for Mycenae, to gather their strength and the gods on their side, and they'll come back across the sea before you expect them. That is what Calchas reads in the signs.

"Warned by him, they built this image to make up for the wounded Palladium, to atone for the outrage to the goddess. And Calchas told them to raise it this huge, this mountain of timber, on purpose — so it could never be brought through your gates or inside your walls, never shelter your people under the old protection. For if your hands defiled Minerva's gift, then utter ruin would fall on Priam's kingdom and the Trojans — may the gods turn that omen back on Calchas instead! But if your own hands brought it up into your city, then Asia would march all the way to the walls of Mycenae in a great war, and that doom would fall on our children."

So, by such treachery and the cunning of one lying Sinon, the thing was believed. We were trapped by tricks and forced tears — we, whom neither Diomedes nor Achilles of Larissa, nor ten years of war, nor a thousand ships had ever broken.

Then something more terrible, far more frightening, struck our blind hearts. Laocoön, the priest chosen by lot to serve Neptune that year, was sacrificing a great bull at the altar — when two serpents came gliding over the calm sea from Tenedos in enormous coils (I shudder to tell it) and made for the shore side by side. Their breasts reared up among the waves; their blood-red crests rose above the water; the rest of them skimmed the sea behind, their huge backs winding in coil after coil. The water roared and foamed. Now they were on the land, their burning eyes streaked with blood and fire, their tongues flickering and licking their hissing mouths. We scattered, white with fear. But the serpents drove straight at Laocoön. First they seized his two small sons, one in each set of coils, and wrapped around them, and tore at their little limbs with their fangs. Then they took the father himself, who came running to help with his spear in hand, and bound him in their huge loops — twice around his waist, twice around his neck, their scaly backs towering over him. And all the while he was straining at the knots with his hands, his priestly ribbons soaked with gore and black venom, sending up screams to the sky like the bellowing of a wounded bull that has shaken the axe from its neck and fled the altar. Then the two serpents slid away to the heights of the citadel, to cruel Minerva's shrine, and coiled at the goddess's feet, sheltered under the curve of her shield.

At that, a fresh terror crept into every shaking heart. People said Laocoön had paid for his crime, as he deserved — he had wounded the sacred wood with his spear, hurled his cursed weapon into its back. "Bring the image to her temple!" they cried. "Pray to the goddess to forgive us!" So we broke open our own walls and laid the city's defenses bare. Everyone set to work. They fitted rollers under the horse's feet and looped ropes of hemp around its neck, and the deadly thing climbed the wall, pregnant with armed men. Around it boys and unmarried girls sang hymns and reached up, thrilled, to lay a hand on the rope. On it came, gliding into the heart of the city, looming over us. O my country! O Troy, home of the gods, walls of the sons of Dardanus famous in war! Four times it stopped on the very threshold of the gate, and four times the weapons inside it clashed. Yet we pushed on, blind, out of our minds, and set the cursed thing in our holy citadel. And even then Cassandra opened her mouth and foretold the doom to come — but by the will of a god the Trojans never believed her. Poor fools, on what would prove our last day we hung the shrines of the gods with festive boughs all through the city.

Meanwhile the sky wheeled around and night came rushing up out of the sea, wrapping the earth and the heavens and the tricks of the Greeks in its great shadow. Through the town the Trojans lay still, their tired bodies held by sleep. And now the Greek fleet was sailing back in formation from Tenedos through the friendly quiet of the moon, making for the shore it knew so well — when the royal galley raised the signal fire. And Sinon, kept safe by the cruel will of the gods, slipped open the wooden bolts and let the Greeks out of the belly of the horse. It swung wide and gave them back to the air, and out they came, glad of it: the captains Thessandrus and Sthenelus, and deadly Ulysses, sliding down a rope they'd lowered; Acamas and Thoas, Neoptolemus of the line of Peleus, the prince Machaon first of all, and Menelaus, and Epeos himself, the man who had built the trap. They poured into a city drowned in sleep and wine. They cut down the few sentries, threw the gates wide open to welcome in their comrades, and joined the two halves of the army together.

It was the hour when sleep first comes to weary men, the kindest gift of the gods, stealing over them — when in my dream, there before my eyes, Hector appeared, deep in grief, weeping rivers of tears. He was as he had been when the chariot dragged him, black with bloody dust, his swollen feet pierced through with the thongs. Ah, the sight of him — how changed he was from the Hector who once came home wearing the armor stripped off Achilles, or who flung Trojan fire onto the Greek ships. His beard was matted, his hair caked with blood, and across his body were all the wounds he took fighting for the walls of his home. Weeping myself, I seemed to speak to him first, and I forced out these words of grief: "Light of Troy, surest hope of the Trojans — what kept you so long? What shore do you come from, Hector, the one we longed for? After so many of your own men have died, after so much suffering by your people and your city, how worn we are to see you at last. But what has done this shameful thing to your clear face? Why do I see these wounds?"

He said nothing, wasted no time on my empty questions, but groaned heavily from the depths of his heart and said: "Run, son of the goddess — get yourself out of these flames. The enemy holds the walls. Troy is falling from her height. You've given enough to your country and to Priam. If any hand could have saved Troy, mine would have saved it. Troy entrusts her holy things to you now, and her household gods. Take them with you to share your fate; find them the great city you will build at last, when you have wandered the whole sea." With that he carried out in his hands the sacred ribbons, the power of Vesta, and the everlasting fire from the inner shrine.

Meanwhile the city was sinking into chaos and grief, and though my father Anchises's house stood apart, set back among trees, the noise grew clearer and clearer and the horror of war closed in. I shook off sleep and climbed to the roof, to the highest point, and stood there straining to listen. It was like a fire driven by a furious wind into a field of grain, or a mountain torrent in flood, flattening the crops and the labor of the oxen, dragging whole forests down headlong — while some shepherd, knowing nothing, hears the roar from a cliff and stands there stunned. Then the truth was plain; the Greeks' treachery lay open. Already the great house of Deiphobus had crashed down in flame, with Vulcan towering over it; already his neighbor Ucalegon was burning. The wide straits of Sigeum threw back the glare. The shouting of men rose up, and the blare of trumpets. Out of my mind, I grabbed my weapons — though there was no plan in fighting, only the wild urge to gather a band of men and rush the citadel. Rage and fury drove me headlong, and I thought how fine it is to die fighting.

Then Panthus, Apollo's priest at the citadel, escaped the Greek blades and came stumbling to my door, dragging his little grandson by the hand and carrying the holy things, the conquered gods of Troy. "Where is the worst of it, Panthus?" I cried. "What stronghold can we hold?" He barely finished groaning before he answered: "It's come — the last day, the hour Troy cannot escape. We Trojans are no more; Ilium is no more; the great glory of our people is gone. Jupiter has handed it all to Argos. The Greeks are masters of a burning city. The horse stands high in the middle of the town, pouring out armed men, and Sinon, the winner now, mocks us as he spreads the fire. Thousands are streaming through the wide-open gates, as many as ever came from great Mycenae. Others block the narrow streets with leveled spears. A line of naked steel stands ready, flashing, for the kill. The watchmen at the gates barely put up a fight, blindly, in the dark."

His words, and the will of the gods, drove me into the fire and the fighting, wherever the grim Fury called, wherever the roar and the cries that rose to heaven called me. Rhipeus joined me, and Epytus, a great fighter, in the moonlight; Hypanis and Dymas fell in at my side, and young Coroebus, son of Mygdon — he had come to Troy in those last days, on fire with mad love for Cassandra, bringing his men to help Priam, his father-in-law to be. Poor man — he never listened to the warnings his prophetess bride gave him. When I saw them packed together and willing to fight, I said: "Men — bravest of hearts, but it's no use — if you're set on following a man who'll go to the end, you see how it stands with us. Every god who held this empire up has left his altar and his shrine. The city you're defending is already in flames. So let's die — let's throw ourselves into the thick of it. The one safety for the beaten is to hope for none." That put a wild fury into their hearts.

Then, like wolves running blind in a black fog, driven by the cruel hunger in their bellies while their cubs wait dry-mouthed back in the den, we went out through enemy spears toward certain death, straight down the middle of the city, with the dark night around us in its hollow shadow. Who could put into words the ruin of that night, the slaughter? Who has tears enough to match the suffering? An ancient city was falling, after all those years of power. Bodies lay everywhere through the streets, in the houses, on the sacred steps of the gods. And it was not only Trojans who paid in blood — sometimes the courage came back into the beaten, and the winning Greeks went down. Everywhere there was grief, everywhere terror, and death in a thousand shapes.

Androgeos was the first of the Greeks to cross our path, a big troop of his men with him. He took us for friends, fellow soldiers, and called out carelessly: "Hurry up, men! What's kept you so slow? The others are sacking and burning Troy already — are you only now coming up from the ships?" Then — because no answer of ours rang true — he knew at once he'd fallen among enemies. He froze, choked on his words, and shrank back. Like a man who steps hard on a snake hidden in rough ground and recoils in horror as it rears up in fury, swelling its blue-black neck — that's how Androgeos shuddered and tried to run. We charged, ringed them with our blades, and cut them down where they stood, panicked, lost, with no idea of the ground. Fortune smiled on our first stroke.

Coroebus, flushed with the win and his own daring, called out: "Friends — Fortune has shown us the safe road, the road that's on our side. Let's take it. Let's change shields and put on Greek emblems. Trick or courage — who asks which against an enemy? They'll arm us themselves." With that he put on Androgeos's plumed helmet and his fine emblazoned shield, and buckled the Greek sword at his side. Rhipeus did the same, and Dymas, and all the men, gladly, arming themselves from the fresh kill. We moved out mingled with the Greeks, fighting under gods not our own, and in the blind dark we clashed with them again and again and sent many of them down to the underworld. Some scattered to the ships and ran for the safety of the shore; some, in their shameful fear, scrambled back up into the horse and hid in its familiar belly.

But you can put no trust in anything when the gods are against you. Just then Cassandra, the daughter of Priam, was being dragged by her loose hair out of the temple, out of the very sanctuary of Minerva, lifting her burning eyes to heaven in vain — her eyes, for her tender hands were bound. Coroebus could not bear the sight. Mad with grief, he threw himself into the middle of them, ready to die, and we all charged after him into the thick of the fight. And here the first blow fell on us — from the high roof of the temple our own people buried us under a hail of weapons, taking us for Greeks by the false crests we wore. Then the Greeks, roaring with rage at the rescue of the girl, gathered from every side and fell on us — Ajax most fierce of all, the two sons of Atreus, the whole army of the Greeks. It was like the moment when storm-winds break loose and clash, the West, the South, and the East riding high on his horses of dawn; the forests scream, and Nereus rages with his trident and churns the sea up from its floor. And those Greeks we'd scattered in the dark and chased through the city came back too — they were the first to spot our shields and lying weapons, and to mark how strange our speech sounded. In a moment we were crushed by sheer numbers. Coroebus fell first, killed by Peneleus at the altar of the warrior goddess. Then Rhipeus went down — the most honest man, the most just, in all of Troy. The gods saw it otherwise. Hypanis and Dymas died run through by their own friends. And you, Panthus, when you fell, neither your great goodness nor Apollo's holy band could shield you. Ashes of Troy, last flames that took my people — be my witnesses that on that night I never flinched from any Greek spear, from any risk, and that if the Fates had wanted me dead, I earned it by my own hand.

We were torn away from there — myself, and Iphitus, slowed by age, and Pelias, hobbling from a wound Ulysses had given him. New shouting called us toward Priam's palace. There the fighting was so wild it was as if no one were dying anywhere else in the whole city, as if this were the only war on earth. We saw the Greeks storming the place, locking shields over their heads in a tortoise-shell to push up to the very threshold. Ladders clung to the walls; men climbed the doorposts toward the door itself, holding their shields up with the left hand against the spears, gripping the eaves with the right. The Trojans on top tore down the towers and the whole roof of the palace and hurled the pieces, using these for weapons now that the end was on them — gilded beams, the splendor of the old kings. Below, men with drawn swords packed the doorway and held it. We threw ourselves back into it, to help the palace, to put fresh fight into the beaten men.

There was a hidden door at the back, a secret passage running through the rooms of Priam's house, a postern that linked the buildings. While the kingdom stood, poor Andromache used to come and go that way alone, bringing little Astyanax to his grandfather. I climbed through it up to the highest ridge of the roof, where the helpless Trojans were flinging weapons that came to nothing. A tower rose there at the very edge, lifting its top into the sky — all of Troy could be seen from it, the Greek ships, the whole camp. We attacked it with iron where the high floors had loosened the joints, wrenched it out of its bed, and heaved it over. It crashed down with a roar and broke apart across a wide stretch of the Greek ranks, crushing them. But more kept coming, and the stones and every kind of missile never let up.

Out in front of the entrance hall, on the very threshold, Pyrrhus was raging, glittering in the bright bronze of his armor — like a snake fed on poisonous plants that has lain swollen underground all winter and now, sloughed and shining, fresh and new, rears its breast to the sun and flickers its forked tongue. With him came huge Periphas, and Automedon, Achilles's old charioteer, and all the young men of Scyros, hurling fire up onto the roof. Pyrrhus himself, out front, took an axe and split the hard doorposts and tore the bronze-plated doors from their hinges. He cut a beam clean through, gouged a great window in the solid oak, and threw the inner house open to view: the long halls came into sight, the private rooms of Priam and the old kings, and the armed enemy standing on the threshold.

Inside, the house was one long cry of grief and confusion. The vaulted rooms rang with the wailing of women; the sound struck the golden stars. Terrified mothers wandered the great halls, clinging to the doorposts, pressing their lips to them. Pyrrhus came on with all his father's force; no bolt, no guard could hold him. The doors gave way under the battering ram and crashed off their hinges. Force broke a road through. The Greeks poured in, butchered the first men they met, and filled the whole place with soldiers. No flood is so wild when it bursts its banks and overwhelms the dikes and pours raging across the fields, sweeping cattle and barns away over the whole plain. I saw it with my own eyes — Pyrrhus drunk on slaughter in the doorway, and the two sons of Atreus; I saw Hecuba and her hundred daughters and daughters-in-law, and Priam at the altars, fouling with his blood the fires he had himself made holy.

Maybe you'll ask how Priam died. When he saw his captured city fallen, the doors of his palace torn open, the enemy in the heart of his home, the old man pulled his long-unused armor over shoulders shaking with age — useless. He buckled on his useless sword and went to meet the enemy, looking for his death. In the middle of the courtyard, under the open sky, stood a great altar, and beside it an ancient laurel that leaned over it and held the household gods in its shade. There Hecuba and her daughters had crowded together at the altar for nothing — like doves driven down by a black storm — clinging to the images of the gods. But when she saw Priam in the armor of his young manhood, she cried out: "What dreadful thought, my poor husband, made you put on these weapons? Where are you running? This is no time for that kind of help, not even if my Hector himself were here. Come here to me. This altar will protect us all, or we'll die together." And she drew the old man to her and made him sit by the holy place.

Just then Polites, one of Priam's sons, came running, having slipped through the swords of the Greeks, down the long colonnades, across the empty courtyards, wounded. Pyrrhus was after him, hot for the kill, almost on him, reaching for him with the spear — until Polites came out into the open before his parents' eyes and fell, and poured out his life in a flood of blood. Then Priam, though death already had him by the throat, could not hold back. He cried out in rage: "For this crime, for what you have dared, may the gods — if there is any justice in heaven that cares about such things — pay you what you deserve. You have made a father watch his own son die; you have fouled a father's eyes with murder. The man you lie about being your father, the great Achilles, was never like this to his enemy Priam. He respected a suppliant's claim, an enemy's trust. He gave me back the bloodless body of Hector for burial and sent me home to my own kingdom." With that the old man threw a spear, but feebly, with no force behind it; it rang harmlessly off the bronze and hung dangling from the boss of the shield.

Pyrrhus answered: "Then you can take this news to my father, the son of Peleus, in person. Remember to tell him of my grim deeds, and how his son Neoptolemus has fallen short of his father. Now — die." And as he said it he dragged the old man, trembling and slipping in his own son's blood, right up to the altar; he wound his left hand in Priam's hair, and with his right lifted the flashing sword and buried it to the hilt in his side. So ended Priam. This was the death the Fates gave him — to see Troy in flames and her towers fallen, the man who had once ruled so many lands and peoples, the great lord of Asia. Now he lies a huge trunk on the shore, the head torn from the shoulders, a corpse without a name.

Then, for the first time, raw horror closed around me. I stood frozen. The image of my own dear father rose up before me as I watched a king of the same great age gasp out his life from that brutal wound. I thought of Creusa, left alone, of my plundered house, of the fate of little Iulus. I looked back to see what men I still had, but every one of them had given out, exhausted, and thrown himself down to the ground or into the fire.

So I was alone — and then I saw, sitting silent in the sanctuary of Vesta, hiding herself in a corner, the daughter of Tyndareus, Helen. The bright fires lit her up as I wandered, my eyes everywhere. She was afraid of the Trojans, who hated her for the fallen city, afraid of the Greeks' revenge and the rage of the husband she'd abandoned — Helen, the curse of Troy and her own homeland alike. There she crouched, a thing accursed, clinging to the altar. A fire blazed up in my heart: a fury to avenge my dying country, to make her pay for her crimes. "So she'll sail home to Sparta and Mycenae unharmed? She'll walk in like a queen, in triumph? She'll see her husband again, her home, her parents and her children, with a train of Trojan women and slaves to wait on her — while Priam lies butchered, while Troy burns, while our shore has soaked up our blood again and again? Never. There's no glory in punishing a woman — that fight wins no honor — but people will praise me for stamping out something evil, for making the guilty pay what they owe. And it will be sweet to fill my soul with the flame of revenge and satisfy the ashes of my people." These were the wild thoughts pouring through me —

when my gracious mother appeared, never so clear to my eyes before, shining pure light through the dark, showing herself as a goddess, as tall and lovely as the gods above her know her. She caught me by the hand to hold me back, and these were the words from her rose-bright lips: "My son — what bitterness has stirred up this ungovernable rage? Why this fury? Where has your care for me gone? Shouldn't you look first to where you left your father, worn out with age — to whether your wife Creusa is still alive, and the boy Ascanius? Greek lines are roving all around them, and if my care for them weren't standing guard, the flames would have taken them by now, and the enemy's sword would have drunk their blood. It is not the hated face of the Spartan woman, not Paris, that you should blame — it is the gods, the merciless gods, who are tearing down all this wealth and toppling Troy from her height. Look — I'll strip away the cloud that hangs over your mortal sight and blunts it, the damp veil around you. Don't be afraid of any order your mother gives you; don't refuse to obey. There — where you see the great blocks ripped apart, stone torn from stone, the smoke and dust boiling up — Neptune is shaking the walls and the foundations loose with his huge trident, prying the whole city up from its bed. There Juno, the cruelest of all, holds the Scaean gates, sword at her side, raging, calling up the allied army from the ships. Look back — Tritonian Pallas has taken the high citadel, blazing out of the storm-cloud with her Gorgon shield. The Father himself feeds the Greeks their courage and rouses the gods against the weapons of Troy. Run, my son. Make an end of this struggle. I will never leave your side; I'll set you safe at your father's door."

She finished and vanished into the thick of the night. And now the dread shapes came clear to me, the great powers that hate Troy. Then I saw the whole of Ilium sinking into the flames, all of Neptune's Troy uprooted from the ground — like an ancient ash tree high in the mountains that farmers race to bring down, hacking at it turn after turn with iron and the axe. It threatens and threatens, its head shaking, its high branches trembling, until little by little the wounds win and it gives one last groan and tears away from the ridge and comes down in ruin. I climbed down, and with the goddess leading me I made my way through the flames and the enemy. The weapons drew back; the fires gave way.

But when I reached the door of my home, my father's old house, the one man I wanted most to carry first to safety up in the high hills — my father Anchises — refused to live on once Troy was gone, refused to go into exile. "You who still have the warm blood of youth in you," he said, "and the strength to stand on your own — you run. As for me, if the gods above had wanted me to live, they would have spared my home. It's enough, more than enough, that I've already seen one sack of this city and lived through it. Say your goodbye to my body and leave it lying here. I'll find my own death. The enemy will pity me, or want my armor. Losing a grave is nothing. For too long I've dragged out a life the gods hate, useless, ever since the father of gods and men breathed his lightning over me and touched me with his fire."

He kept saying it, fixed in his resolve. We were all in tears — myself, my wife Creusa, Ascanius, the whole household — begging the old man not to drag everything down with him, not to lean his weight on the doom that was already crushing us. He refused, clinging to his place and his decision. Again I felt the pull of the weapons, longing for death in my misery — for what plan, what hope was left? "Did you really think I could take a step and leave you here, Father? Could a thing so monstrous fall from a father's lips? If it's the will of heaven that nothing of this great city be left, if you're set on it — set on throwing yourself and all of us into Troy's grave — the door to that death stands wide open. Pyrrhus will be here any moment, fresh from Priam's blood, the man who butchers the son before the father's eyes and then the father at the altar. Was it for this, dear mother, that you carried me through the fire and the spears — to see the enemy inside my house, to see Ascanius, and my father, and Creusa beside them, slaughtered in each other's blood? Weapons, men, bring me my weapons — the last light is calling the beaten. Send me back to the Greeks. Let me see the fight again. We will not all die today unavenged."

I was buckling on my sword again, working my left arm into the shield, on my way out of the house — when there in the doorway my wife Creusa threw herself down, clinging to my feet, holding little Iulus up to his father. "If you're going out to die, take us with you, whatever comes. But if you've learned anything from fighting, and you put any hope in these weapons, then guard this house first. Who are you leaving little Iulus to, and your father, and me, who used to be called your wife?" Her cries filled the whole house — when out of nowhere a sudden wonder appeared. There between our hands, between the faces of his grieving parents, a soft tongue of flame seemed to pour light from the top of Iulus's head, a fire that did him no harm, licking at his soft hair and feeding around his brow. Frightened, we shook the burning hair and tried to put out the holy fire with water. But my father Anchises lifted his eyes to the stars in joy and raised his hands and his voice to heaven: "Almighty Jupiter — if any prayer can move you, look down on us; that's all I ask. And if our faithfulness has earned it, give us a sign, Father, and confirm this one."

The old man had barely spoken when a sudden crack of thunder broke on the left, and a star shot down through the dark sky trailing a torch of light. We watched it glide high over the roof and bury its brightness in the forest of Mount Ida, marking out our road. It left a long furrow of light behind, and all around the place reeked of sulfur. At that, my father rose, won over at last. He spoke to the gods and worshiped the holy star: "Now, now there's no more delay. I'll follow, gods of my fathers — wherever you lead, I am with you. Save my house, save my grandson. This sign is yours; Troy is in your keeping still. I give in, my son. I won't refuse to go with you."

He finished — and by now the fire was louder across the city, rolling its tide of flame closer. "Then come, dear father, climb onto my back. I'll carry you on my own shoulders; the weight won't trouble me. However it turns out, the danger and the safety will be the same for both of us. Little Iulus will come along beside me, and my wife will follow our steps at a distance. And you, my servants, listen. Just outside the city there's a mound with an old temple of Ceres on it, abandoned now, and an ancient cypress beside it that our fathers kept holy for many years. We'll all meet there, coming by different ways. You, Father, carry the sacred things, our country's gods, in your hands; I've just come from the fighting and the slaughter, and it would be a sin for me to touch them until I've washed in running water."

With that I bent down and spread a lion's skin over my neck and broad shoulders and took up my burden. Little Iulus laced his fingers in mine and trotted to keep up with his father's longer stride, and behind us came my wife. We went on through the shadows. And I — who a moment before had faced flying spears and the whole massed army of the Greeks without flinching — now took fright at every breath of wind, startled at every sound, terrified for the boy at my side and the father on my back. I had nearly reached the gates, and thought I'd come safely through it all, when suddenly there seemed to be a great thudding of feet close by, and my father, peering into the dark, cried out: "Run, my son, run — they're coming. I can see the glow of their shields, the flash of the bronze." Here some cruel power — I don't know what — robbed me of my wits in my fear. For while I hurried down side streets, off the road I knew, my wife Creusa — was she stopped by some grim fate, did she lose her way, did she sink down exhausted? I will never know. She was gone, and I never saw her again. I didn't look back, didn't even think of her, until we reached the mound and the old shrine of Ceres. There, when we all gathered at last, she alone was missing — lost to her husband, her son, her friends.

Out of my mind, I blamed every man and god there is. In all the wreck of the city I saw nothing crueler than this. I left Ascanius, my father, and the gods of Troy in my friends' care, hidden in a winding valley, and went back into the city, strapping on my shining armor again. I was set on hunting through all of Troy once more, on throwing my life back into the danger. First I made for the walls and the dark gate I'd come out by, and traced my own steps backward through the night, searching the way with my eyes. Everywhere was horror; the very silence struck terror into me. Then I went to my house, in case — just in case — she'd found her way back there. The Greeks had broken in and filled the whole place. The hungry fire was rolling up to the rooftop on the wind; the flames towered over it, raging into the air. I pushed on to Priam's palace and the citadel. In the empty colonnades of Juno's sanctuary, the chosen guards Phoenix and grim Ulysses were keeping watch over the loot — the treasure of Troy hauled out of the burning shrines, the altars of the gods, the golden bowls and the stolen robes, all heaped together. Children and frightened mothers stood around it in a long line. I even dared to throw my cries into the dark. I filled the streets with my grief, calling Creusa over and over, hopelessly, again and again.

As I went searching, raging endlessly through the houses of the city, there rose before my eyes the sorrowful ghost of Creusa herself — her very shape, but larger than the woman I had known. I froze; my hair stood on end and my voice stuck in my throat. Then she spoke, to ease the grief out of me: "Why give yourself up to this wild sorrow, my sweet husband? None of this happens without the will of the gods. It is not allowed for you to take Creusa with you from here — the great ruler of high Olympus does not allow it. Long exile is yours, a vast plain of sea to plow, and then you'll come to the land of Hesperia, where the Lydian Tiber flows gently through the rich farmland of brave men. There happiness is waiting for you, and a kingdom, and a royal bride. Dry your tears for the Creusa you loved. I will never see the proud homes of the Greeks, or go to be a slave to some Greek wife — I, a daughter of the line of Dardanus, the daughter-in-law of the goddess Venus. No: the great mother of the gods is keeping me here, on this soil. Now goodbye, and keep your love for the son we share." When she had said this, she left me weeping, with so much I wanted to say, and faded into thin air. Three times I tried to throw my arms around her neck; three times the ghost I grasped at slipped through my hands like a light wind, like a dream that takes wing.

And so, the night spent at last, I came back to my friends. There I found, to my astonishment, that a great crowd of new arrivals had gathered — mothers and men, young people come together for exile, a sad host. They had streamed in from every side, ready in heart and goods to follow me across the sea to whatever land I chose. And now the morning star was rising over the high ridges of Mount Ida, bringing the day. The Greeks held the gates, blocked tight; there was no hope of help. I yielded, lifted my father onto my back, and made for the mountains.
