The Odyssey, Book VI

Nausicaa

Book VI6 of 24

So long-suffering, brilliant Odysseus slept there, worn down by sleep and toil. But Athena went off to the country and the city of the Phaeacians. These people had once lived in the broad land of Hypereia, hard by the Cyclopes, a lawless and overbearing race who were stronger than they were and forever raiding them. So their king, godlike Nausithous, moved them away and settled them in Scheria, far from all other men who live by their labor. He ringed the city with a wall, built houses and temples for the gods, and parceled out the fields. But by now he had gone down, beaten by his fate, to the house of Hades, and Alcinous ruled in his place, a man whose counsels the gods themselves had taught him. It was to his house that gray-eyed Athena came, turning over in her mind the homecoming of great-hearted Odysseus.

She went straight to the richly worked bedchamber where a girl was sleeping who had the form and face of a goddess — Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous. Two handmaids, lovely as the Graces, lay one on either side of the door, and the gleaming doors were shut. Athena drifted to the girl’s bedside like a breath of wind. She had taken the shape of the daughter of Dymas, a famous seafaring man — a girl Nausicaa’s own age, and dear to her heart. In that likeness gray-eyed Athena spoke:

“Nausicaa, how did your mother come to have so careless a daughter? Your bright clothes are lying about, neglected — and your wedding is near, when you’ll need fine things to wear yourself and fine things to give the bridal party who walk beside you. This is how a girl earns a good name among people, and how she makes her father and her lady mother proud. So come, let’s go and wash at the first light of dawn. I’ll go along to help, so the work goes quickly, for you won’t be a maiden much longer. Already the best young men of all the Phaeacians, your own people, are courting you. Go at first light and ask your noble father to make ready the mules and the cart, to carry the sashes and the robes and the bright bedding. It’s better for you to ride than to go on foot — the washing-pools are a long way from the town.”

With that, gray-eyed Athena went away to Olympus, where they say the gods have their seat forever, unshaken. No wind shakes it, no rain ever wets it, no snow comes near; the air spreads cloudless and clear, and a white radiance plays over it. There the blessed gods take their pleasure, all their days. To that place the gray-eyed one went, when she had finished with the girl.

Soon Dawn came to her lovely throne and woke Nausicaa of the fine robes. She marveled at her dream, and went off through the house to tell her father and her mother, and she found the two of them inside. Her mother sat at the hearth with her serving women, spinning yarn dyed with sea-purple. She caught her father at the door, on his way out to join the lords of the Phaeacians at their council, where the noblest men had summoned him. She came up close and said:

“Papa, dear — won’t you have them harness me a high cart with good wheels, so I can take our fine clothes down to the river to wash? They’re lying soiled. And it’s right that you yourself should wear clean things when you sit in council among the leading men. And there are five sons of yours under this roof — two of them married, three still in the flower of youth and unwed, and they always want fresh-washed clothes for the dance. All of this is on my mind to see to.”

So she spoke, for she was shy of naming her own ripe marriage to her dear father. But he understood it all, and answered her: “I’ll not grudge you the mules, child, nor anything else. Off you go — the men will harness you a high cart with good wheels, fitted with a body to hold it all.”

With that he called to the servants, and they obeyed. They wheeled the cart out, smooth-running, and harnessed the mules to it and yoked them in, while the girl carried the bright clothes down from her room and stacked them in the polished cart. Her mother packed a hamper with all kinds of good food to eat, and put in savory things, and filled a goatskin with wine; and as the girl climbed up, she gave her soft olive oil in a golden flask, so she and her maids could rub themselves down after the bath. Then Nausicaa took the whip and the bright reins, and cracked the whip to start the mules. They strained against the harness with a clatter of hooves, carrying the clothes and the girl — not alone, for her handmaids walked along beside her.

When they reached the beautiful stream of the river, where the washing-pools never failed — clear water welling up in plenty to wash the dirtiest clothes — they unyoked the mules from the cart and turned them loose along the eddying river to graze on the honey-sweet grass. They lifted the clothes down from the cart by armfuls, carried them into the dark water, and trod them down in the pits, each girl racing the others at the work. When they had washed them and rinsed every stain away, they spread them out in a row along the seashore, where the surf had washed the shingle cleanest. Then they bathed, and rubbed themselves with the glistening oil, and ate their meal beside the banks of the river, and waited for the clothes to dry in the heat of the sun. And when she and her maids had eaten their fill, they threw off their headscarves and played at ball, and Nausicaa of the white arms led their singing. She moved among them like Artemis the archer ranging the mountains — down the long ridge of Taygetus or Erymanthus, delighting in the wild boar and the swift deer, while the wild nymphs, daughters of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, play at the hunt beside her, and Leto’s heart lifts to see her own girl standing a head taller than them all, easy to pick out, lovely as they all are. So this unwed girl outshone her women.

But when she was about to fold the fine clothes, yoke the mules, and turn for home, gray-eyed Athena thought of something else — that Odysseus should wake and see the lovely girl, and she should lead him to the city of the Phaeacians. The princess threw the ball to one of her maids; it missed her and dropped into a deep eddy of the river, and they all cried out, loud and clear. And brilliant Odysseus woke. He sat up, and a question turned in his heart and mind:

“What sorrow — whose country have I come to this time? Are they cruel men, savage, with no sense of right? Or do they welcome strangers, and fear the gods in their hearts? That sounded to me like the high cry of girls — like the nymphs who haunt the steep peaks of the mountains, and the springs of rivers, and the grassy water-meadows. Or am I somewhere near people who speak as I do? Let me go and see for myself.”

With that, brilliant Odysseus crept out from under the bushes, and with his strong hand he broke off a leafy branch from the thick wood, to hold across his body and cover his nakedness. And out he came, like a lion bred in the hills, sure of its strength, that goes out through wind and rain with its eyes ablaze, and falls on the cattle and the sheep, or hunts the wild deer — its belly drives it to break even into a close-fenced fold after the flock. So Odysseus, in his hard need, made ready to wade in among the girls with their lovely hair, naked as he was. He came toward them, caked with brine, a terrible sight, and they scattered in fear, this way and that, along the jutting spits of the shore. Only the daughter of Alcinous held her ground, for Athena put courage in her heart and took the fear from her limbs. She stood and faced him. And Odysseus turned it over — whether to clasp the lovely girl’s knees and beg, or stand off at a distance and plead in gentle words that she give him clothes and show him the way to the town. In the end this seemed the better course: to plead from a distance, in gentle words, for fear that if he took hold of her knees the girl would be angry. So at once he spoke, in words that were honeyed and shrewd:

“I am your suppliant, my lady — but are you a goddess, or a mortal woman? If you are a goddess, one of those who hold the wide heaven, then to my eyes you are most like Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus, in your beauty and your bearing and your height. But if you are mortal, one of those who live on the earth, then three times blessed are your father and your honored mother, and three times blessed your brothers — their hearts must always glow with warmth and joy on your account, when they watch a flower like you step into the dance. But blessed beyond them all, beyond all others, is the man who will load the wedding-gifts and lead you home. For never yet have my eyes seen such a one among mortals, neither man nor woman. Looking at you, I am struck with awe. Once, at Delos, by the altar of Apollo, I saw the like — a young palm tree shooting up; for there I went, too, and a great host of people followed me, on that road that was to be the start of all my troubles. And just as, seeing that, I stood and marveled long in my heart, for no such tree had ever risen from the earth — so I marvel now at you, my lady, and stand in awe, and dare not touch your knees, though my grief is deep. Yesterday, on the twentieth day, I escaped the wine-dark sea; until then the waves and the rushing winds carried me on and on from the island of Ogygia. And now some power has cast me up here, to suffer still more, no doubt — for I don’t think my troubles are over yet; the gods have much still in store for me before they end.

“So pity me, my lady. After all I have suffered, you are the first I have come to; I know no one else among the people who hold this city and this land. Show me the way to the town, and give me a rag to throw around me — anything you brought to wrap the clothes in. And may the gods grant you all your heart desires: a husband, and a home, and the gift of one mind between you. For there is nothing finer, nothing stronger than this — when two people keep house together, of one heart and one mind, a grief to those who hate them and a joy to those who wish them well; and they themselves know it best of all.”

Then Nausicaa of the white arms answered him: “Stranger, you seem to be no fool, and no bad man. It is Olympian Zeus himself who deals out fortune to people, to the good and the bad alike, each man as he pleases — and these troubles are his gift to you, and you must simply bear them. But now that you have reached our city and our land, you shall not go short of clothes, nor of anything else a hard-pressed suppliant has the right to ask of those he meets. I’ll show you the way to the town, and tell you the name of our people. We are the Phaeacians who hold this city and this land, and I am the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, in whom all the strength and power of the Phaeacians rests.”

Then she called to her maids with their lovely hair: “Stop where you are, girls. Where are you running, at the sight of a man? You don’t think he’s an enemy, surely? There is no man living, and there never will be, who could come to the Phaeacians’ land bent on harm, for we are very dear to the immortal gods. We live far off, at the edge of the world, in the surging sea, with no other people near us. No — this is some luckless wanderer who has come here, and we must care for him now; for all strangers and beggars come from Zeus, and a small gift, given freely, is dear to them. So give the stranger food and drink, girls, and bathe him in the river where there’s shelter from the wind.”

At that they stopped, and called out to one another to come back, and they sat Odysseus down in a sheltered spot, as Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, had told them. They laid out clothes for him, a tunic and a cloak, and gave him the soft oil in its golden flask, and told him to bathe in the river’s stream. But then brilliant Odysseus spoke to the maids: “Stand off a little way, girls, so I can wash the brine from my shoulders myself and rub myself with oil — it’s a long time since my skin has felt it. I won’t bathe in front of you. I am ashamed to stand naked among girls with such lovely hair.”

So he spoke, and they went off and told the girl. And brilliant Odysseus washed the brine from his back and his broad shoulders with the river water, and scrubbed the dried salt from his hair. When he had washed all over and rubbed himself with oil, he put on the clothes the unwed girl had given him. And Athena, the daughter of Zeus, made him look taller and broader to the eye, and made the hair fall thick from his head in curls like the hyacinth flower. As when a master craftsman, schooled by Hephaestus and Athena in every art, washes gold over silver and makes a thing of beauty — so the goddess poured grace over his head and his shoulders. Then he went and sat apart, down along the shore, shining with grace and beauty, and the girl gazed at him. And she said to her maids with their lovely hair:

“Listen to me, my white-armed girls, while I say something. It is not against the will of all the gods who hold Olympus that this man has come among the godlike Phaeacians. A moment ago he seemed to me a wretched thing, and now he looks like the gods who hold the wide heaven. If only a man like that might be called my husband, settled here, and content to stay. But give the stranger food and drink, girls.”

So she spoke, and they listened and obeyed, and set food and drink before Odysseus. And long-suffering, brilliant Odysseus ate and drank, ravenous — for it had been a long time since he had tasted food. Then Nausicaa of the white arms thought of something else. She folded the clothes and stowed them in the fine cart, and yoked the strong-hooved mules, and climbed up herself. Then she urged Odysseus on, and spoke to him plainly:

“Up now, stranger, and into the town — I’ll bring you as far as my wise father’s house, where I promise you’ll meet all the noblest of the Phaeacians. Only do as I say; you seem to be a man of sense. As long as we’re crossing the fields and the farmlands, walk quickly behind the mules and the cart with the maids, and I’ll lead the way. But when we come near the town — there’s a high wall around it, and a fine harbor on each side of the city, with a narrow channel in between, and the curved ships are drawn up along the road, for every man has a slip of his own. There’s the gathering-place, around the beautiful temple of Poseidon, fitted with great stones bedded deep in the ground. There the people see to the gear of the black ships, the cables and the sails, and there they plane down the oars. For the Phaeacians care nothing for the bow and the quiver, only for masts and oars and the trim ships they take such joy in, crossing the gray sea.

“It’s the talk of these people I want to avoid — their cold gossip later on, for there are insolent men among them, and some low fellow might meet us and say: ‘Who’s this — this tall, handsome stranger trailing after Nausicaa? Where did she pick him up? She’ll be making him her husband, no doubt. Some castaway she’s taken in off his ship, from far away — for we have no neighbors here — or some god she prayed and prayed for has come down at last from heaven, and she’ll have him all her days. Better so, if she’s gone and found herself a husband somewhere else; for she looks down on her own people here, the Phaeacians, fine and many as they are, who court her.’ That’s what they’ll say, and it would be a reproach to me. And I’d think the same myself of another girl who did the like — who, with her father and her mother living, went about with men before the day of her open wedding.

“So do as I say, stranger, and you’ll soon win your passage home and an escort from my father. You’ll find a fine grove of poplars near the road, sacred to Athena, with a spring running through it and a meadow all around. There my father has his estate and his flowering orchard, no farther from the town than a man’s shout will carry. Sit down there and wait a while, until we’ve had time to reach the town and come to my father’s house. Then, when you judge we’ve reached it, go into the city of the Phaeacians yourself, and ask the way to the house of great-hearted Alcinous, my father. It’s easy to know — any little child could lead you to it, for the other Phaeacians have no houses built like the hall of the hero Alcinous. But once the house and the courtyard take you in, go quickly across the great hall to my mother. She sits at the hearth in the firelight, leaning against a pillar, spinning sea-purple yarn, a wonder to behold, with her serving women ranged behind her. My father’s chair stands close to hers, and there he sits and drinks his wine like an immortal. Pass him by, and throw your arms around my mother’s knees, if you want to see the glad day of your homecoming soon, however far away your home may be. If she takes you to her heart, then you may hope to see your own people again, and come back to your high-roofed house and the land of your fathers.”

With that she cracked the bright whip over the mules, and they left the streams of the river behind. The mules ran well, working their legs in good rhythm, and she handled the reins with care, so that Odysseus and the maids could keep pace on foot — using the whip with judgment. The sun was going down when they came to the famous grove sacred to Athena, and there brilliant Odysseus sat down. At once he prayed to the daughter of mighty Zeus:

“Hear me, child of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, you who never tire. Hear me now — though you would not hear me before, when the great earth-shaker was breaking me on the sea. Grant that I come to the Phaeacians welcomed, and pitied.”

So he prayed, and Pallas Athena heard him. But she would not yet show herself to him face to face, out of respect for her father’s brother Poseidon, who still kept up his rage against godlike Odysseus until the day he reached his own land.
