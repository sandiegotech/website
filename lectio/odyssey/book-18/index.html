The Odyssey, Book XVIII

The Beggars’ Fight

Book XVIII18 of 24

Now there came a public beggar who used to beg his way all over the town of Ithaca, a man with a name for bottomless eating and drinking. There was no strength in him and no staying power, but he was a great hulk to look at. His real name, the one his mother gave him, was Arnaeus; but the young men all called him Irus, because he would run and carry a message for anyone who sent him. The moment he arrived he set about insulting Odysseus, trying to drive him from his own door.

“Off with you, old man,” he cried, “away from the doorway, before you are dragged out by the foot. Can you not see them all winking at me, wanting me to throw you out? I am ashamed to do it, that is all. Get up and go on your own, or it will come to blows between us.”

Odysseus frowned at him and said, “My friend, I am doing you no harm. People give you plenty; I do not grudge it. There is room in this doorway for the two of us, and you have no cause to begrudge me what is not yours to give. You look to be a tramp like myself — and perhaps the gods will grant us both better luck one day. But do not talk so freely of fighting, or you will rouse me; and old as I am, I will paint your chest and your mouth with blood. I would have more peace tomorrow if I did, for you would never come back to the house of Odysseus again.”

Irus was furious. “Listen to the glutton run on,” he said, “chattering away like an old kitchen-hag. I have a mind to set both fists on you and knock the teeth out of your head like a boar’s tusks. Gird yourself, then, and let all these men stand by and watch. How will you fight a man so much younger than you?”

So the two of them traded insults on the smooth pavement before the great door. And when Antinous saw what was brewing, he gave a great laugh and said to the others, “Friends, here is the finest sport heaven ever sent into this house. The stranger and Irus have fallen out, and they mean to fight. Let us match them now, this minute.”

Up they all sprang, laughing, and crowded round the two ragged beggars. “Hear me,” said Antinous. “There are goat stomachs down at the fire, stuffed with fat and blood, that we set by for our supper. Whichever of these two wins and proves the better man, let him come and take his pick — and from now on he eats at our table, and we will let no other beggar near the house.”

They all liked the sound of it. But Odysseus, throwing them off the scent, said, “Sirs, an old man worn down with hardship is no match for a young one; yet my wretched belly drives me to it, though I know it will earn me a beating. Only swear me this first: that not one of you will strike me a foul blow to favor Irus and hand him the win.”

They swore it as he asked. And when the oath was done, Telemachus put in a word. “Stranger,” he said, “if your heart bids you take this man on, do not fear any of the rest. Whoever strikes you will have more than one man to answer to. I am your host, and Antinous and Eurymachus, both men of sense, are of the same mind as I am.”

Every man there agreed. Then Odysseus girded his old rags tight about his loins, and out came his great thighs, broad and bare, and his wide chest and shoulders and the mighty arms; and gray-eyed Athena came close and made his limbs stronger still. The suitors were struck with wonder, and a man would lean to his neighbor and say, “Irus will soon be un-Irused, and asking for it too. Look at the thigh the old fellow has hauled out of his rags.”

Irus felt his courage drain away as he heard them, but the servants girded him up and hauled him out, his flesh quaking on his bones with fear. And Antinous rounded on him: “You swaggering bully — better you had never been born than to stand there trembling at this broken old tramp. But I tell you, and it shall surely come to pass: if he beats you and proves the better man, I will pack you onto a black ship and send you to the mainland, to King Echetus, who maims every man who comes near him. He will cut off your nose and your ears with the pitiless bronze, and tear out your privates to throw raw to the dogs.”

That made Irus shake the harder. But they led him into the open of the court, and the two men put up their hands. Then Odysseus weighed it in his mind: whether to lay him out with one blow and finish him on the spot, or to strike lighter and only drop him in the dust. In the end he judged the lighter blow the wiser, for fear the Greeks should begin to wonder who he was. So they squared off, and Irus struck Odysseus on the right shoulder; but Odysseus caught him on the neck, below the ear, and crushed the bone within, and the red blood came welling up through his mouth. Down he went groaning into the dust, grinding his teeth and drumming his heels on the ground — and the suitors threw up their hands and nearly died of laughter. Then Odysseus took him by the foot and dragged him out through the doorway and across the yard to the gate of the court, and propped him against the wall, and put his staff in his hand. “Sit there now,” he said, “and keep off the dogs and the pigs. Wretch that you are, do not set up as king of the beggars again, or you will fare even worse.”

Then he slung his shabby, torn old beggar’s bag over his shoulder by its cord, and went back to sit on the threshold. And the suitors filed in laughing and saluting him: “May Zeus and all the other gods, stranger, give you whatever you most desire, for stopping the mouth of that bottomless tramp. We will soon ship him off to the mainland, to King Echetus, who maims every man alive.”

Odysseus took their words for a good omen. Antinous set before him a great goat’s stomach, stuffed with fat and blood; and Amphinomus took two loaves from the basket and brought them to him, and lifted a golden cup and drank his health. “Good luck to you, father stranger,” he said. “You are hard pressed enough now; I hope better days are coming.”

And Odysseus answered, “Amphinomus, you strike me as a man of good sense, as well you might, being the son you are — for I have heard your father, Nisus of Dulichium, well spoken of, a man both brave and rich. They tell me you are his son, and you have the look of one who knows how to listen. So listen now, and take to heart what I say. Of all things that breathe and crawl across the earth, the earth breeds nothing feebler than a man. While the gods grant him strength and spring in his knees, he never thinks any evil will reach him later; and when the blessed gods send him sorrow instead, he bears that too, and against his will, with an enduring heart. For the mind of a man down here is only what the father of gods and men makes it, day after day. I too looked to be a fortunate man once, and did much wrong out of my own headstrong pride, trusting in my father and my brothers to back me. So let no man ever be lawless; let him take whatever the gods give him and hold his peace. Look at the suitors here, and the harm they do — wasting the goods and dishonoring the wife of a man who, I tell you, will not long be gone from his own people. He is close. May some god lead you home and away before then, so you are not in this hall the day he comes back to his own country. For once he is under this roof, I do not think the suitors and he will part without blood.”

With that he poured a libation and drank, and put the cup back in Amphinomus’s hands; and Amphinomus walked off through the hall with his head bowed and his heart heavy, for some shadow of evil had fallen on him. Yet for all that, he did not escape his doom; even then Athena had bound him to go down under the spear of Telemachus. So he went and sat down again in the place he had risen from.

Now gray-eyed Athena put it into the mind of Penelope, daughter of Icarius, to show herself to the suitors — to set their hearts beating all the harder for her, and to stand higher than before in the eyes of her husband and her son. So she gave a hollow laugh and said, “Eurynome, my heart has turned, for all that I loathe them: I have a mind to show myself to the suitors. And I would say a word to my son, too — that he do better than keep such company. They have fair words on their lips and nothing but mischief behind them.”

“All you say is true, my child,” Eurynome answered. “Go, then, and speak plainly to your son. But first wash, and put oil on your face. Do not go down with your cheeks all furrowed with crying. Endless grieving does no good; and your son is grown — a bearded man now, the very thing you always prayed to the gods you might live to see.”

“I know you mean well, Eurynome,” Penelope said, “but do not press me to wash and anoint myself. Whatever beauty I had, the gods took from me the day my husband sailed away in the hollow ships. No — only send Autonoe and Hippodamia to me, that they may stand beside me in the hall. I will not go down among the men alone; that would not be seemly.”

So the old woman went off through the house to call the maids and hurry them to their mistress. And meanwhile gray-eyed Athena had another thought, and poured a sweet sleep over the daughter of Icarius, so that she sank back on her couch and her limbs went slack, while the goddess gave her gifts that would leave the Greeks marveling. First she washed her lovely face with the divine balm that fair-crowned Aphrodite wears when she goes to the lovely dancing of the Graces; she made her taller to the eye, and fuller, and her skin whiter than fresh-sawn ivory. When this was done the goddess departed, and the white-armed maids came in from their room and woke her with their chatter, and sleep let her go.

She passed her hands across her cheeks. “What a soft sleep,” she said, “folded itself around me in all my misery. I wish pure Artemis would grant me so soft a death this very moment, that I might stop wearing my life away in grief and longing for my husband — a man who was best in every way among the Greeks.”

Then she came down from her bright upper room — not alone, but with two of her women attending her — and when she reached the suitors she stood by the pillar that bore the roof of the hall, holding a shining veil before her face, and a faithful maid on either side. And the knees of the suitors went weak; love so bewitched them, every one, that each man prayed to lie beside her in her bed. But she turned to her son.

“Telemachus,” she said, “your mind and your judgment are not what they were. When you were a child you had a better sense of what was fitting. But now you are grown — and any stranger who looked at your height and your good looks would call you the son of a fortunate house — your judgment and your sense of what is right are no longer what they should be. How could you let such a thing happen here in our hall, this stranger so shamefully abused? What if a guest, sitting under our roof, had come to real harm in a brawl like that? It would have been your disgrace before the world.”

“Mother,” Telemachus answered, “I am not surprised you are angry. But I see and understand everything now, and I know in my own heart when things go right and when they go wrong; when I was younger I could not. Even so, I cannot keep everything in good order. These men hem me in on every side with their malice, and I have no one at my back. Still, the fight between the stranger and Irus did not go the way the suitors wanted, for the stranger had the better of it. By Father Zeus, by Athena and Apollo — I wish the suitors in this house lay beaten the same way, some out in the court and some inside, every one of them slack-limbed as Irus over there by the courtyard gate, nodding his head like a drunk man, unable to stand on his feet or find his way home, with all the strength knocked out of him.”

So mother and son spoke together. Then Eurymachus turned to Penelope. “Daughter of Icarius, wise Penelope,” he said, “if every Greek in Iasian Argos could see you now, you would have still more suitors feasting in your halls by tomorrow’s dawn, for there is no woman in the world to match you — in beauty, in stature, and in the good sense within.”

“Eurymachus,” Penelope answered, “whatever excellence I had, of face or form, the gods destroyed the day the Greeks took ship for Troy and my husband Odysseus went with them. If he came home and took up the care of my life again, then my name might stand higher and fairer than it does. As it is, I am eaten up with grief, so many sorrows has heaven let fall on me. And he foresaw it all. When he was leaving, he took me by the right wrist and said, ‘Wife, I do not think all of us well-greaved Greeks will come home safe from Troy. The Trojans are fighters, with spear and bow, and quick in the chariot — and that, more than anything, decides the day in a hard, even battle. So I do not know whether the gods will bring me back, or whether I will fall there at Troy. Look after everything here. Care for my father and mother, as now, and even more while I am gone. But when you see our son with a beard on his cheek, then marry whom you will, and leave this house behind.’ That is what he said, and now it is all coming true. The night is near when this hateful marriage will fall on me, cursed as I am, whose happiness Zeus has stripped away. And here is what cuts deepest of all: your way of courting is not the custom. When men woo a woman of good family, a worthy bride, and rival one another for her, they bring oxen and fat sheep of their own to feast her friends, and lay rich gifts before her — they do not eat up another man’s living and pay nothing for it.”

And Odysseus, hearing her, was glad — glad that she was charming gifts out of them with honeyed words, while her real heart was set elsewhere.

Then Antinous spoke. “Daughter of Icarius, wise Penelope,” he said, “take whatever gifts any of us cares to bring you, for it is not well to refuse a gift. But we will not go back to our own lands, or stir from here at all, until you have married the best man among us.”

The others all said yes to that, and every man sent a herald off to fetch his gift. For Antinous the man brought a wide, lovely robe, beautifully worked, with twelve clasps of pure gold, fitted with curving sheaths. For Eurymachus, a chain of cunning craft, of gold strung with amber beads, glowing like the sun. Two servants of Eurydamas brought earrings, each a triple cluster of mulberry drops, and the grace of them shone out. From the house of Lord Pisander, son of Polyctor, came a servant with a neckband, a thing of rare beauty. And so each of the Greeks brought his own fine gift.

Then the queen went back up to her room, and her women carried the lovely gifts after her. And the suitors gave themselves over to dancing and to the sweetness of song, and so passed the time until evening came down on them. The dark of evening overtook them still at their pleasure, and they set up three braziers about the hall to give light, and heaped them with firewood long dry and well seasoned, splitting fresh kindling among it; and they kept torches burning, the maids of long-suffering Odysseus holding them up by turns. Then Odysseus himself said to the women,

“You maids of Odysseus, your master so long gone — go inside to the queen’s chamber. Sit with her and lighten her heart; turn the spindle, or card the wool in your hands. I will keep the light burning for all these men. Let them stay till the bright Dawn herself; they will not wear me down, for I can bear a great deal.”

At that the maids glanced at one another and laughed; and one of them, the pretty Melantho, began to taunt him to his face. She was a daughter of Dolius, but Penelope had raised her like her own child and given her playthings to her heart’s content; yet for all that she felt nothing for her mistress’s grief, but had taken up with Eurymachus and shared his bed.

“You poor fool of a stranger,” she said, “have you gone clean out of your mind? Why not go and sleep in some smithy, or a tramps’ shelter, instead of running your mouth here, bold as you please, in front of all these men? The wine has gone to your head — or perhaps your wits are always this loose, and that is why you babble so. Or has beating the tramp Irus turned your head? Take care a better man than Irus does not stand up against you and pound your skull with his fists and send you out of the house dripping blood.”

Odysseus scowled at her. “You bitch,” he said, “I will go straight to Telemachus and tell him what you said, and he will have you cut to pieces on the spot.”

His words scattered the women in fear, and they fled away into the body of the house, the knees shaking under them, for they believed he meant it. But Odysseus took his stand by the burning braziers, holding up the light and watching them all — and his mind ran far off, on things that would surely come to pass.

And Athena would not let the proud suitors hold off their cruelty for a moment, so that the bitterness might sink the deeper into the heart of Odysseus. She set Eurymachus, son of Polybus, to mock him, and raise a laugh among the rest. “Hear me, you suitors of the great queen,” he said, “while I say what is on my mind. This man has not come into the house of Odysseus for nothing — the gods themselves have led him. Look, I do believe the gleam of the torches is coming off the man himself — off the top of his head, for there is not a hair left on it, not so much as a wisp.”

Then he turned to Odysseus. “Stranger,” he said, “would you go to work for me, if I took you on out at the far end of the country — building up dry-stone walls, planting tall trees? I would keep you in bread the year round, and find you clothes and sandals for your feet. But no — you have learned only bad habits, and you will not put your hand to honest work; you would rather go cringing round the town to feed that belly of yours.”

“Eurymachus,” Odysseus answered, “I only wish there could be a contest of work between us, you and I, in the long days of spring, when the days run on and on. Give me a good curved scythe, and take one yourself, and let us go fasting into the deep meadow grass from first light till dark, and see who mows the cleaner and the longer. Or give us a yoke of oxen each, big tawny beasts, well matched and full of strength and never tiring, in a four-acre field with the sod ready to turn, and you would see whether I could not cut a straight furrow before me from end to end. Or let Zeus stir up war this very day, somewhere — give me a shield and two spears and a helmet of bronze set close on my temples, and you would find me up among the foremost in the fighting, and you would not be jeering at this belly of mine. But you are insolent, and your heart is hard; and you think yourself some great man because you keep company with a few small men, and poor ones. If Odysseus came home to his own country — those wide doors would seem narrow enough to you, for all their width, as you scrambled to get out through the porch and away.”

Eurymachus burned with rage at that. He glared at him and let fly: “You wretch — I will make you pay for talking like that in front of everyone, and not a shred of fear in you. The wine has gone to your head, surely, or your wits are always loose, that you babble so. Or has beating the tramp Irus turned your head?” And as he spoke he snatched up a footstool. But Odysseus dropped down at the knees of Amphinomus of Dulichium in fear of Eurymachus; and the stool caught the wine-steward on the right hand, so that the jug rang as it hit the floor, and the man fell back in the dust with a cry. The suitors broke into an uproar in the shadowy hall, and a man would lean to his neighbor and say, “I wish this stranger had died somewhere else in his wanderings before he came to us — then he would not have brought all this turmoil among us. Here we are, brawling over a beggar, and there will be no joy left in the feast at all, now that worse has the upper hand.”

Then Telemachus stood up among them. “Sirs,” he said, “you are out of your minds. You can no longer hide that you have eaten and drunk too much; some god is goading you. You have feasted well — go home now and lie down, whenever your hearts move you. I drive no man out.”

They bit their lips and marveled that Telemachus dared speak so boldly. But Amphinomus rose — Amphinomus, son of Nisus, who was son of King Aretias — and said, “Friends, no one should take offense at a fair word, or strike back at it in anger. Lay no hand on the stranger, nor on any servant in the house of Odysseus. Come — let the steward pour for our cups, that we may make our libations and go home to our rest. As for the stranger, let us leave him to Telemachus to deal with, for it is to his house that the man has come.”

His words pleased them all. So the lord Mulius, a herald of Dulichium and servant to Amphinomus, mixed the bowl and carried it round and served every man in turn; and they poured out to the blessed gods and drank the honey-sweet wine. And when they had made their offerings and drunk their fill, they went off, each man to his own house, to lie down for the night.
