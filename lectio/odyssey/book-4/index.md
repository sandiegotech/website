# The Odyssey, Book IV

Menelaus and Helen at Sparta

They came down into Lacedaemon, the city in the hollow of the hills, and drove straight to the house of Menelaus. They found him in his halls, hosting a feast for a great gathering of his kinsmen, celebrating two weddings at once — his daughter’s and his son’s. The daughter he was sending to the son of the warrior Achilles; he had promised her to him long before, while they were still at Troy, and now the gods were bringing the marriage about. So he was sending her off with chariots and horses to the famous city of the Myrmidons, where Achilles’ son was king. For his own son he had found a bride out of Sparta, the daughter of Alector. That son, strong Megapenthes, was born to him late, of a slave woman, for the gods gave Helen no more children after she had borne her first, Hermione, a girl as lovely as golden Aphrodite.

So the neighbors and kinsmen of glorious Menelaus were feasting and enjoying themselves under his high roof. A singer sang among them to his lyre, and two acrobats whirled and tumbled through the middle of the hall as he struck up his song.

Telemachus and the son of Nestor reined in their horses at the gate. Eteoneus, a quick servant of Menelaus, came out, saw them, and ran back through the house to bring his lord the news. He went up close and spoke to him with winged words: “Menelaus, cherished by the gods — there are strangers at the door, two men who look like the sons of Zeus himself. Tell me, shall we unyoke their fast horses, or send them on to find welcome somewhere else?”

Fair-haired Menelaus flared with anger. “Eteoneus, son of Boethous — you were never a fool before, but now you’re babbling like a child. The two of us ate plenty of other men’s bread on the long road home before we got back here, where I pray Zeus may let us rest from sorrow at last. Unyoke the strangers’ horses, of course, and bring the men inside to share the feast.”

Eteoneus hurried back through the hall and called the other servants to come with him. They led the sweating horses out from under the yoke, tied them at the mangers, and threw down a feed of barley mixed with white grain; they tilted the chariot against the bright wall of the courtyard and brought the men into the splendid house. Telemachus and Pisistratus stared in wonder when they saw it, for a kind of brightness ran through the high halls of glorious Menelaus, like the light of the sun or the moon. When they had filled their eyes with it, they went to the polished baths and washed.

When the maids had bathed them and rubbed them with oil and dressed them in soft cloaks and tunics, they sat down on chairs beside Menelaus. A servant came with water in a beautiful golden pitcher and poured it over their hands into a silver basin, and drew up a polished table beside them. A grave housekeeper brought bread and laid it out, and many good things besides, giving freely from her stores. A carver lifted platters of every kind of meat and set them down, and placed golden cups beside them.

Then fair-haired Menelaus greeted them, his hand reaching out to them: “Help yourselves, and welcome. When you’ve had your supper, we’ll ask who you are — for a line like yours can’t have been lost. You’re surely sprung from a family of kings who carry the scepter; common men don’t father sons like you.”

With that he passed them a fat slice of roast loin from his own portion, the cut of honor set near him, and they reached out for the good things spread before them. When they had taken their fill of food and drink, Telemachus leaned in close to Nestor’s son, his head near his so the others wouldn’t hear, and said: “Look around, Pisistratus, man after my own heart — the flash of bronze through these echoing halls, of gold and amber, of ivory and silver. The court of Olympian Zeus must look like this. There’s wealth past counting here. I’m lost in wonder at it.”

But fair-haired Menelaus overheard him, and said: “Dear boys, no mortal man can rival Zeus. His house and everything in it are immortal. Among men, though — well, maybe one man somewhere is as rich as I am, maybe not. I brought it all home over eight long years of wandering and grief, sailing through Cyprus and Phoenicia and Egypt, reaching the Ethiopians, the Sidonians, the Erembians, and Libya, where the lambs grow horns from birth and the ewes bear young three times a year, so that no master or hired man there ever lacks cheese or meat or sweet milk — the flocks give all year round. But while I was gathering this wealth in those far places, my own brother was murdered behind my back, by the treachery of his cursed wife — and so I take no joy in being lord of all of it. Whoever your fathers are, you’ve surely heard this from them: how much I suffered, and how I lost a whole house, a fine one, full of good things. I’d gladly live with only a third of this wealth, if the men were still alive who died on the wide plain of Troy, far from the grazing land of Argos. I grieve for all of them, sitting here in my halls. Sometimes I ease my heart with weeping, and sometimes I stop — a man tires soon enough of cold tears. But of them all there’s one I mourn for most, one whose memory turns me from food and sleep, since no Greek labored as hard or risked as much as Odysseus. For him it was all suffering, and for me a grief that won’t heal, he’s been gone so long, and we don’t even know if he’s alive or dead. By now they must be mourning him — old Laertes, and steadfast Penelope, and Telemachus, the boy he left behind, newly born.”

So he spoke, and stirred in Telemachus a longing to weep for his father. The tears spilled from his eyes to the ground at the sound of that name, and he lifted his purple cloak in both hands to cover his face. Menelaus saw it, and wavered in his heart — whether to let the young man speak of his father in his own time, or to question him first and draw it out of him.

While he was still turning it over, Helen came down from her high, fragrant, vaulted room, lovely as Artemis of the golden arrows. Adraste set a well-made chair for her, Alcippe spread soft wool across it, and Phylo carried in the silver work-basket given to Helen by Alcandra, the wife of Polybus, who lived in Egyptian Thebes, the richest city on earth. Polybus had given Menelaus two silver bathtubs, two tripods, and ten talents of gold; and his wife gave Helen her own fine gifts — a golden distaff, and this silver basket that ran on little wheels, its rim finished in gold. Phylo set it down beside her now, packed with spun yarn, the distaff laid across it charged with violet wool. Helen sank into the chair, set her feet on the stool, and at once began questioning her husband.

“Do we know, Menelaus, cherished by the gods, who these men say they are, these strangers come to our house? I may be wrong, but my heart won’t let me keep silent — I have to say it. I’ve never seen anyone, man or woman, so like another as this young man is like the son of brave Odysseus. He must be Telemachus, the child Odysseus left as a newborn at home when you Greeks came to Troy with war in your hearts, all for the sake of my own shameless self.”

And fair-haired Menelaus answered her: “I see it now too, wife, the likeness you’ve caught. These are Odysseus’ hands and feet, the same set of his head, the same hair, the very eyes. And just now, when I was speaking of Odysseus, of all he endured and suffered for my sake, the bitter tears welled up in this young man’s eyes and he hid his face behind his cloak.”

Then Pisistratus, Nestor’s son, spoke up: “Menelaus, son of Atreus, cherished by the gods — you’re right, this is the son of that man. But he’s modest, and ashamed, coming here for the first time, to throw himself into talk before you, whose voice we delight in like a god’s. My father, the old knight Nestor, sent me along to bring him here, for Telemachus wanted to see you, hoping you might give him some word or counsel. A son has hard troubles at home when his father is gone and no one else stands beside him — and that is just how it is for Telemachus now. His father is far away, and there’s no one in the land to keep the wolves from his door.”

And fair-haired Menelaus answered: “What a thing — that the son of a man I loved so dearly should come to my house, a man who bore so much hardship for my sake. I always swore that if he came home, I would honor him beyond all the other Greeks — if only Zeus had let the two of us sail back safe across the sea. I’d have given him a city to live in here in Argos, built him a house, brought him from Ithaca with his son and all his goods and people, emptying out one of the towns nearby that look to me as their lord. We’d have lived side by side, dear to each other always, and nothing would have parted us but the black cloud of death. But the god himself must have begrudged us that, for he alone of all men is kept from coming home.”

So he spoke, and his words set them all weeping. Helen, child of Zeus, wept; Telemachus wept; and so did Menelaus, son of Atreus. Nor could Nestor’s son keep his eyes from filling, for his heart turned to his noble brother Antilochus, cut down by the splendid son of the Dawn. Thinking of him, he spoke to Menelaus with winged words: “Son of Atreus — my father Nestor always called you the wisest of men, whenever we spoke of you in his halls. So, if you can, do as I ask. I take no pleasure in weeping over supper, and besides, the Dawn will rise soon enough — and then there is no harm in mourning whoever has died and met his fate. This is the only honor we can pay the wretched dead: to cut our hair for them and let the tears run down our cheeks. I lost a brother too, a far from worthless man — you surely knew him. His name was Antilochus, and they say no one was faster on his feet or braver in a fight. I never saw him myself.”

And fair-haired Menelaus answered him: “My friend, you’ve spoken with more sense than your years should allow — clearly the son of a wise father. You can always tell the breeding of a man whose line Zeus has blessed in his marriage and his children, the way he has blessed Nestor, all his days, with a smooth old age in his own halls and sons about him both wise and good with a spear. So let’s leave off the weeping that broke over us, and turn back to our supper — and have them pour water over our hands. There’ll be time enough in the morning for Telemachus and me to talk it all through.”

So Asphalion, one of the quick servants of glorious Menelaus, poured water over their hands, and they reached out again for the good things spread before them.

Then Helen, child of Zeus, thought of something else. Into the wine they were drinking she slipped a drug that quiets all pain and anger and brings forgetfulness of every sorrow. Whoever drank it down, once it was mixed in the bowl, could not let a single tear fall the whole day long, not even if his own mother and father lay dead, not even if armed men cut down his brother or his beloved son right before his eyes, and he watched it happen. Helen had been given this drug, so cunning and so potent, by Polydamna, the wife of Thon, a woman of Egypt — where the rich earth grows herbs past counting, some healing, some deadly when mixed, and every man is a healer skilled beyond all others, for they are the kin of Paeon, the gods’ own physician. When she had stirred the drug in and ordered the wine poured round, Helen spoke again:

“Menelaus, son of Atreus, cherished by the gods — and you young men, sons of good fathers — the god gives us now this thing and now that, for Zeus can do anything, and to one man he sends good and to another evil. So feast at your ease in the hall and take pleasure in the talk; I’ll tell you a story that suits the hour. I couldn’t name every labor of long-suffering Odysseus, but I’ll tell you one — what that bold man dared and brought off in the land of the Trojans, where you Greeks were grinding through such hardship. He laid degrading welts across his own body, threw filthy rags over his shoulders like a slave, and slipped into the enemy’s wide streets disguised as a beggar, nothing like the man he was among the Greek ships. In that disguise he went into Troy, and not one of them suspected him — I alone knew him for who he was, and questioned him, but he was too sly and kept slipping away. Only after I had bathed him and rubbed him with oil and dressed him and sworn a great oath never to give him up to the Trojans until he was safely back at the ships did he tell me the whole plan of the Greeks. Then he cut down a good many Trojans with his long sword and got away to the Greek camp, carrying everything he had learned. The Trojan women wailed aloud — but my own heart sang, for by then it had turned toward home, and I grieved over the madness Aphrodite laid on me when she carried me there, far from my own dear land, my child, my marriage, and a husband who lacked nothing, in looks or in wit.”

And fair-haired Menelaus answered her: “Yes, wife, everything you’ve said is true. I have known the minds and the counsels of many heroes, and traveled over much of the world, but never with my own eyes have I seen a heart like the heart of long-suffering Odysseus. Think what that strong man dared and endured inside the wooden horse, where the best of the Greeks sat waiting in the dark to bring slaughter and doom on the Trojans. You came to us there — some god who wanted the Trojans to win must have sent you, and godlike Deiphobus came at your side. Three times you circled the hollow trap, running your hands along it, calling out the names of the Greek captains one by one, mimicking the voices of all their wives. I was sitting in the middle with Diomedes and brilliant Odysseus, and we heard you crying out. The two of us half rose, burning either to rush out or to answer you from inside — but Odysseus held us back and stopped us, for all our longing. Every other Greek there sat dead still, except Anticlus, who started to call back to you. But Odysseus clamped his two strong hands hard over the man’s mouth and held them there, and so he saved us all, keeping his grip until Athena drew you away again.”

Then thoughtful Telemachus said to him: “Menelaus, son of Atreus, cherished by the gods — it only makes the grief worse, that none of this saved him from a wretched end, for all his iron heart. But now send us to bed, I beg you, so we may lie down and take the sweet gift of sleep.”

So he spoke, and Helen of Argos told her maids to set up beds under the colonnade, to pile them with fine purple rugs, to spread coverlets on top and lay out thick woolen cloaks. The maids went out from the hall with torches in their hands and made up the beds, and a herald led the guests away. So Telemachus and the splendid son of Nestor slept there in the forecourt of the house, while the son of Atreus lay deep within the high hall, and beside him lay Helen in her long robe, loveliest of women.

When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, Menelaus, lord of the great war-cry, rose from his bed and dressed. He slung his sharp sword over his shoulder, bound fine sandals beneath his shining feet, and came from his room like a god to look on. He took a seat beside Telemachus, and asked: “Tell me, Telemachus — what need has brought you all this way over the sea’s broad back to Lacedaemon? Is it some matter of your people, or your own? Tell me the truth.”

And thoughtful Telemachus answered him: “Menelaus, son of Atreus, cherished by the gods — I came in the hope you might tell me something of my father. My house is being eaten alive, my rich fields laid waste; the place is crammed with hostile men who keep slaughtering my flocks and my shambling, twist-horned cattle, all while courting my mother in their arrogance. So I come and clasp your knees, in the hope you’ll tell me how he met his bitter end — whether you saw it with your own eyes, or heard it from some other wanderer. His mother bore him for grief. Don’t soften it out of pity for me. Tell me plainly, just as you saw it. If my brave father Odysseus ever served you well, by word or deed, in the years you Greeks suffered at Troy, remember it now, and tell me everything, exactly true.”

Then fair-haired Menelaus answered him, deeply stirred: “Shameless cowards — to think they’d lie down in the bed of a man like that! It’s as if a deer had laid her newborn fawns to sleep in a lion’s den and gone off to graze the wooded glens, and the lion came home to his lair and tore them apart, both of them, in a moment — that’s how Odysseus will deal with those men, and bring their ugly fate on them. Father Zeus, and Athena, and Apollo — if only the Odysseus who once stood up in lovely Lesbos and wrestled the son of Philomeleides and threw him down so hard that all the Greeks cheered — if only that Odysseus could meet these suitors now. Every one of them would find a quick death and a bitter wedding. But as for what you ask me and beg me to tell — I won’t twist it aside or deceive you. Of all the old sea-god told me, I’ll hide nothing.

“The gods kept me penned in Egypt, hungry to come home, because I hadn’t given them their full offering of sacred hecatombs, and the gods always want their dues remembered. There’s an island out in the surging sea, off the mouth of Egypt — Pharos they call it, about as far offshore as a hollow ship can run in a day with a stiff wind astern. It has a good harbor where men can draw fresh water and launch their trim ships back to sea. There the gods becalmed me twenty days; not once did the steady winds rise off the water that carry a ship across the sea’s wide back. All our food would have given out, and the men’s strength with it, if a goddess hadn’t pitied me and saved me — Eidothea, daughter of mighty Proteus, the old man of the sea. I had touched her heart most of all.

“She came to me one day when I was off alone, away from my men, who were forever roaming the island with their barbed hooks, fishing — for hunger was wearing at their bellies. She stood close and said: ‘Stranger, are you such a fool, so slack of heart, or do you let things slide on purpose, and even enjoy your own suffering? You’ve been trapped on this island so long now, and can’t find any way off it, while the spirit of your men drains out of them.’

“And I answered her: ‘I’ll tell you, whichever of the goddesses you are — I’m not stuck here by any choice of mine. I must have sinned against the deathless gods who hold the wide heaven. So tell me — you gods know everything — which of the immortals is binding me here and blocking my way, and how I’m to sail home over the teeming sea.’

“And the bright goddess answered: ‘Then I’ll tell you, stranger, and tell you straight. An old immortal of the sea comes haunting these waters — Proteus of Egypt, who never lies, who knows the depths of every sea, the servant of Poseidon. They say he is my father, that he sired me. If you could somehow lay an ambush and catch him, he would tell you your course, the stages of your voyage, and how to sail home over the teeming sea. And if you wished it, son of Zeus, he would tell you all that has happened in your halls, good and bad, while you have wandered so long and so hard a road.’

“And I said: ‘Then show me yourself the way to ambush this old god, before he sees me coming and slips away. A god is hard for a mortal man to master.’

“And the bright goddess answered at once: ‘I’ll tell you, stranger, and tell you straight. When the sun has climbed to the middle of the sky, the truthful old man of the sea comes up out of the water, under the breath of the West Wind, hidden by the dark ripple it raises over him. Once he’s out, he lies down to sleep in the hollow of the caves, and around him the seals, the brood of the lovely salt-sea goddess, haul up out of the gray water and sleep in herds, breathing up the sharp stink of the sea’s deep places. Tomorrow at first light I’ll lead you there and lay you down in a row. Choose your three best men, the bravest in your ships. And I’ll tell you all the old man’s tricks.

“‘First he’ll go down the line of his seals and count them. When he has them all tallied off on his fingers, he’ll lie down among them like a shepherd among his flock. The moment you see him stretched out asleep, throw everything you have into holding him fast, hard as he fights to break free. He’ll try to turn into every creature that crawls the earth, and into water too, and into blazing fire. But hold him without flinching, and grip him harder still. Then, when at last he speaks to you and asks his questions in the same shape he wore when you saw him sleeping, let go your hold, hero, and free the old man — and ask him which of the gods is set against you, and how you’re to sail home over the teeming sea.’

“With that she dove under the swell of the sea, and I went back to my ships drawn up on the sand, my heart dark with care as I walked. When I reached the ships and the water, we made our supper, and the holy night came down, and we lay to sleep on the breaking shore. When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, I went out along the shore of the wide sea, praying hard to the gods, with the three men I trusted most for any work. Meanwhile the goddess had dived deep into the sea’s broad lap and brought up four sealskins, all freshly flayed — she was laying a trap for her own father. She scooped out four beds in the sand and sat waiting for us. When we came up she had us lie down side by side, and threw a skin over each of us. That ambush would have been unbearable — the deadly rank stench of the sea-bred seals choked us; who would lie down beside a monster of the deep? But the goddess saved us; she thought of a great relief and dabbed ambrosia under each man’s nose, sweet-smelling, and it killed the seals’ reek.

“All morning we waited, holding ourselves steady, and the seals came up from the sea in herds and stretched out along the breaking shore. At noon the old man came up out of the sea, found his fat seals, and went down the whole line counting them. We were the first he counted off, among his beasts, never dreaming there was any trick — and then he lay down himself. With a great shout we rushed him, and threw our arms around him, and the old man never forgot his cunning craft: first he turned into a great bearded lion, then a serpent, a leopard, a huge boar; he turned into running water, and into a tall leafy tree. But we held on, gripping him, our hearts unshaken, until at last the old wizard, worn out, broke and spoke to me: ‘Which of the gods, son of Atreus, plotted with you to trap me against my will and seize me? What is it you want?’

“And I answered him: ‘You know, old man — why feint and ask? You know I’ve been penned on this island so long, and I can find no way off, and the heart in me is failing. So tell me — you gods know everything — which of the immortals is binding me here and blocking my way, and how I’m to sail home over the teeming sea.’

“And he answered me at once: ‘Why, you should have made fine offerings to Zeus and the other gods before you sailed, if you wanted the quickest crossing home over the wine-dark sea. For it is not your fate to see your own people and reach your fine house and your native land until you have gone back once more to the waters of Egypt, the rain-fed river, and offered the holy gods who hold the wide heaven their sacred hecatombs. Only then will the gods grant you the road you long for.’

“So he spoke, and the heart broke in me, that he should send me back over the misty sea to Egypt, that long and weary way. Still, I answered him: ‘I’ll do it all, old man, just as you command. But tell me this, and tell me true: did all the Greeks come home safe in their ships, all the ones Nestor and I left behind when we sailed from Troy? Or did any die a hard death aboard his own ship, or in the arms of his friends, once the fighting was done?’

“And he answered me at once: ‘Son of Atreus, why ask me this? You’re better off not knowing what I know, for once you’ve heard, I don’t think your eyes will stay dry. Many of them are dead, and many came through. Of the captains, only two of the bronze-clad Greeks died on the way home — you were there yourself for the fighting — and a third is still alive, held somewhere on the broad sea. Ajax went down among his long-oared ships. Poseidon first drove him onto the great rocks of Gyrae, but pulled him clear of the water, and he’d have escaped his doom, hated as he was by Athena, if he hadn’t let one wild, reckless boast slip out: that he’d come through the sea’s depths in spite of the gods. Poseidon heard that great talk, and took his trident in his strong hands and struck the rock of Gyrae and split it. One half held; but the broken piece, the one Ajax had been sitting on when he made his mad boast, plunged into the sea and carried him down with it, into the surging deep, and he drank the salt water and died.

“‘Your brother slipped past the death-spirits in his hollow ships — Hera saved him. But just as he was about to round the steep height of Malea, a storm caught him and carried him groaning out across the teeming sea to the borderland where Thyestes had once lived, and where Aegisthus, his son, was living now. Even from there a safe homecoming opened before him; the gods turned the wind around, and they reached home, and Agamemnon stepped out onto his own soil with joy, and bent and kissed the earth of his fatherland, and the warm tears streamed down at the sight of the land he loved.

“‘But a watchman saw him from his lookout — a man Aegisthus had set there, promising him two talents of gold. He’d been watching a whole year so that Agamemnon couldn’t slip past unseen and call up his fighting strength. He ran to the house with the news, and Aegisthus at once set his treacherous trap. He picked twenty of his best men and hid them in ambush, and on the far side of the hall he had a feast laid out. Then he went with chariots and horses to fetch Agamemnon, his mind full of murder, and led him home all unsuspecting, and cut him down at the feast the way a man kills an ox at the manger. Not one of the men who came with the son of Atreus was left alive, and not one of Aegisthus’ men — they all died there in the hall.’

“So Proteus spoke, and the heart broke in me. I sank down on the sand and wept; I had no wish to go on living or to see the light of the sun. But when I had wept and writhed in the sand till I had my fill, the old man of the sea said: ‘Son of Atreus, don’t keep on weeping like this, so long and so hard — it does no good. Get yourself back to your own land as fast as you can. You may yet find the man alive — or Orestes may have killed him first, and you’ll come in time for the funeral feast.’

“So he spoke, and for all my grief, the heart in my breast warmed again. I said to him with winged words: ‘These two I know of now. Tell me the third — the man you said was still alive, held somewhere on the broad sea. Or is he dead? Tell me, even if it brings me grief.’

“And he answered me at once: ‘The third is Odysseus, son of Laertes, whose home is in Ithaca. I saw him on an island, weeping hard, in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who holds him there against his will, so that he can’t reach his own land; he has no oared ships and no crew to carry him over the sea’s broad back. As for you, Menelaus, cherished by the gods — it is not your fate to die in horse-pasturing Argos and meet your end there. The immortals will send you to the Elysian Fields, the world’s end, where fair-haired Rhadamanthus lives and life is easiest for men: no snow there, no heavy storm, no rain ever, but the Ocean sends up a singing West Wind, always, to bring its coolness to men. This will be yours because you married Helen, and so are the son-in-law of Zeus.’

“With that he dove back under the swell of the sea, and I went to my ships with my godlike men, my heart dark with care as I walked. When we reached the ships and the water, we made our supper, and the holy night came down, and we lay to sleep on the breaking shore. When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, we hauled the ships down into the bright sea and set the masts and sails into the trim hulls, and the men climbed aboard and took their places at the oars and beat the gray sea white. So I brought the ships back once more to the rain-fed waters of Egypt, and there I offered full and faultless hecatombs. And when I had soothed the anger of the everlasting gods, I raised a barrow to Agamemnon, so his name would never die. Then I set sail, and the gods gave me a fair wind and brought me swiftly home, back to my own dear land.

“But now stay here in my halls eleven or twelve more days, and then I’ll send you off in style. I’ll give you splendid gifts — three horses and a polished chariot — and a beautiful cup as well, so that all your days, whenever you pour an offering to the immortal gods, you’ll remember me.”

And thoughtful Telemachus answered him: “Son of Atreus, don’t keep me here too long. I’d gladly sit beside you a whole year and never once feel the pull of home or my parents — your stories and your talk are such a delight to me. But by now my crew, waiting in sacred Pylos, must be growing restless, and you’re holding me here too long. As for the gift you’d press on me — let it be something I can store away. I won’t take horses back to Ithaca; better to leave them here, an ornament for your own stables. You rule a wide flat plain, thick with clover and galingale, with wheat and broad-eared white barley. But Ithaca has no broad running-ground, no meadow at all. It’s a land for goats, and dearer to me for that than any land for grazing horses. None of the islands that lean down to the sea is good for driving horses or rich in meadow, and Ithaca least of all.”

So he spoke, and Menelaus, lord of the great war-cry, smiled, and stroked the young man’s hand, and said: “Good blood shows in your talk, dear boy. Yes — I’ll change the gift; that’s easy enough. Of all the treasures stored in my house I’ll give you the loveliest and most precious of them all: a mixing bowl, finely wrought, of solid silver, its rim finished in gold, the work of Hephaestus himself. Phaedimus, king of the Sidonians, gave it to me when his house took me in on my way home. That is what I want to give you.”

So the two of them talked on, while guests kept arriving at the great king’s house. They drove up sheep, and brought wine to lift the heart, and their veiled wives sent along the bread. So they busied themselves over the feast in the halls of Menelaus.

But back in Ithaca, the suitors were amusing themselves with throwing the discus and the hunting spear on the leveled ground before Odysseus’ house, swaggering as always. Antinous and godlike Eurymachus were sitting apart, the ringleaders, the best of them by far. Noemon, son of Phronius, came up to them and asked Antinous: “Antinous, do we have any idea when Telemachus is coming back from sandy Pylos? He’s gone off with a ship of mine, and I need it now — to cross to the wide pastures of Elis, where I have twelve brood mares with sturdy mule foals at the teat, still unbroken. I want to drive one home and break him in.”

He spoke, and they were stunned; they’d never imagined Telemachus had gone to Pylos, the city of Neleus. They’d thought he was somewhere on the land nearby, out with the flocks, or with the swineherd. So Antinous, son of Eupeithes, said to him: “Tell me the truth — when did he go, and what young men went with him? Picked men of Ithaca, or his own hired hands and slaves? He could have managed that too. And tell me this straight: did he take your black ship by force, against your will, or did you give it freely when he asked?”

And Noemon, son of Phronius, answered him: “I gave it freely myself. What else could anyone do, when a man like that, with such a weight on him, asks a favor? It would have been hard to refuse. And the young men who sailed with him are the best in the land, after ourselves. I saw Mentor go aboard as their captain — or a god who looked exactly like him. But that’s the strange thing: I saw godlike Mentor here, in town, only yesterday at dawn — and yet by then he had already boarded the ship for Pylos.”

With that Noemon went off to his father’s house, and the proud hearts of the other two were stung. They called the suitors in from their games and sat them down, and Antinous, son of Eupeithes, rose to speak, sick with rage, his dark heart swollen and his eyes like blazing fire: “This is a bad business — Telemachus has carried off this voyage in style, and we swore it would come to nothing. The boy has slipped away in spite of us all, with a launched ship and a picked crew. He’ll be more trouble yet. But may Zeus break his strength before he reaches his full growth. Give me a fast ship and twenty men, and I’ll lie in wait for him in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Same, and ambush him as he comes — and bitter will be this voyage he made, hunting for word of his father.”

So he spoke, and they all cheered him on and urged him to it; then they rose and went into the house of Odysseus.

But Penelope did not stay long in the dark about what the suitors were plotting in their hearts. The herald Medon told her — he had overheard their schemes from the courtyard while they laid their plans inside. He went through the house to bring the word to Penelope, and as he crossed her threshold she said to him: “Herald, why have the proud suitors sent you here? To tell the serving women of godlike Odysseus to leave their work and cook a feast for them? I wish they would never woo, never gather again — that this very meal might be their last. You crowd in here day after day, eating up the living of wise Telemachus. Did your own fathers never tell you, when you were children, what kind of man Odysseus was among them? He never wronged a soul, by word or deed, in all the land — though that is the way of kings, to love one man and hate another. Never once did he do a brutal thing to any man. But your black hearts and your ugly deeds are plain to see — and there’s no gratitude left for kindness done.”

Then Medon, the wise herald, answered her: “My queen, I only wish that were the worst of it. But they’re plotting something far more terrible now — may the son of Cronus never let it happen. They mean to kill Telemachus with the sharp bronze as he sails home. He has gone for news of his father, to sacred Pylos and to splendid Lacedaemon.”

So he spoke, and her knees gave way and the heart melted in her breast. For a long while she could not say a word; her eyes filled with tears, and her full voice was stopped. At last she found speech and said: “Herald — why has my son gone? There was no need for him to board the swift ships that men ride like horses across the great water’s salt expanse. Does he want even his name wiped out among men?”

Then Medon, the wise herald, answered her: “I don’t know whether some god set him on, or whether his own heart drove him to go to Pylos, to learn whether his father is coming home, or what fate he has met.” With that he went back through the house of Odysseus.

And a cloud of heart-wasting grief came over Penelope. She could not bring herself to sit on any of the many chairs in the room; she sank down on the threshold of her richly made chamber and wept, a thin, piteous sound, while all the serving women in the house, young and old, mourned around her. Through her sobbing Penelope said to them: “Listen, my dears — the gods have given me more sorrow than any woman born and raised beside me. First I lost my brave husband, the lion-hearted man, finest in every excellence among the Greeks, whose name was great through Hellas and the heart of Argos. And now the storm-winds have snatched away my own dear son, with no glory and no warning, out of these halls — and I never even heard him go. Hard-hearted, all of you — not one of you thought to rouse me from my bed, though you knew it in your hearts, the very hour he went down to board the hollow black ship. If I had known he meant this journey, he’d have stayed, however set he was on going — or left me dead behind him in these halls. But go, someone, and call old Dolius quickly, the servant my father gave me when I first came here, who keeps my orchard of trees. Have him run to Laertes, sit beside him, and tell him everything, in case Laertes can weave some plan in his mind and come out to plead with the people — these men so bent on wiping out his line and the line of godlike Odysseus.”

Then the dear old nurse Eurycleia answered her: “Dear child — kill me with the pitiless bronze if you like, or let me live in the house, but I won’t keep the truth from you. I knew all of it. I gave him everything he asked, bread and sweet wine, and he made me swear a great oath not to tell you for ten or twelve days, unless you missed him yourself or heard that he’d gone, so the tears wouldn’t spoil your lovely face. But wash now, and put on clean clothes, and go up to your room with your women and pray to Athena, daughter of Zeus who holds the storm-shield — she can save him even from death. And don’t trouble the old man; he has burdens enough. I can’t believe the blessed gods so hate the line of Arceisius. Someone of it will live on yet, to hold the high-roofed halls and the rich fields stretching far around.”

So she spoke, and stilled Penelope’s grief and dried the tears from her eyes. Penelope washed, and put on clean clothes, and climbed to the upper room with her women. She set barley grains in a basket and prayed to Athena: “Hear me, child of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, you who never tire. If ever wise Odysseus burned the rich thigh-pieces of ox or sheep to you in these halls, remember it now, and save my dear son, and keep these arrogant suitors far from him.”

She cried out as she prayed, and the goddess heard her. But down in the shadowy halls the suitors were in an uproar, and one of those insolent young men was saying: “There’s no doubt the queen, courted by us all, is getting ready for her wedding — and never a thought that her son’s death has been decided.” So he spoke, in his ignorance of how things stood. Then Antinous spoke up among them: “You fools — give up this big, reckless talk, every one of you, or someone may carry it inside. Come, let’s get up and quietly carry out the plan we’ve all agreed on.”

With that he chose twenty of the best men, and they went down to the swift ship and the shore of the sea. First they dragged the black ship into deep water, then set up the mast and sails in her, lashed the oars in their leather loops in good order, and ran the white sails up. Their proud servants brought down their armor. They anchored her well out in the water, then went ashore and took their supper, waiting for evening to fall.

But upstairs in her room steadfast Penelope lay, tasting no food, touching neither meat nor drink, wondering only whether her blameless son would slip the death they planned or be brought down by the arrogant suitors. Like a lion ringed by a closing crowd of hunters, sick with dread, she turned it over and over in her mind, until sweet sleep came over her and she sank back, every joint loosened, and slept.

Then gray-eyed Athena thought of something else. She made a phantom, shaping it like a woman — like Iphthime, the daughter of great-hearted Icarius, Penelope’s own sister, who had married Eumelus and lived in Pherae. Athena sent the phantom to the house of godlike Odysseus, to ease Penelope of her sobbing and her tears. It slipped into her room past the strap that drew the bolt, and stood above her head and spoke: “You’re asleep, Penelope, your heart worn out with grieving. But the gods who live at ease will not let you weep and suffer so. Your son will come home yet, for he has done the gods no wrong.”

And steadfast Penelope answered her, drowsing sweetly at the gates of dreams: “Why have you come, sister? You’ve never come before — you live so very far away. And you tell me to leave off grieving, to quiet all the pains that crowd my heart and mind — I, who have lost my brave, lion-hearted husband, finest in every excellence among the Greeks, whose name was great through Hellas and the heart of Argos? And now my own dear son has gone off in a hollow ship, a boy who knows nothing of hard work or the councils of men. I grieve for him even more than for his father. I tremble for him, afraid of what may happen — whether harm will come to him among the people he’s gone to, or out on the sea. He has many enemies, and they plot against him, hungry to kill him before he reaches his own land.”

Then the dim phantom answered her: “Take heart, and don’t let your mind be so torn with fear. There is one going with him such as other men would pray to have at their side, for she has the power — Pallas Athena. She pities you in your grief, and it is she who has sent me to tell you this.”

And steadfast Penelope said to her: “If you are a god, and have heard the voice of a god, then tell me of that other unhappy man as well — is he still alive somewhere, and sees the light of the sun? Or is he dead by now, and down in the house of Hades?”

Then the dim phantom answered her: “I will not tell you the whole of it, whether he’s alive or dead. It’s no good speaking words as empty as the wind.” And with that it slipped out past the bolt of the door and melted into the moving air. But Penelope, daughter of Icarius, started up out of her sleep, and the heart in her was warmed — so clear had the dream come to her in the dead of night.

Meanwhile the suitors had boarded and were sailing the sea-roads, their hearts set on Telemachus’ sudden death. Out in the open water, midway between Ithaca and rocky Same, there’s a small rocky island, Asteris, with twin harbors where a ship can shelter. There the suitors lay in wait for him.
