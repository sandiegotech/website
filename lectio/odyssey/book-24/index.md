# The Odyssey, Book XXIV

Peace in Ithaca

Meanwhile Hermes of Cyllene called up the ghosts of the suitors. In his hand he held the golden wand with which he closes the eyes of the living in sleep, or opens them again, whichever he pleases; with this he stirred the ghosts and led them off, and they followed him, squeaking. As bats squeak and flutter in the depths of some great cave when one of them drops from the cluster where they cling to the rock and to each other, so the ghosts went squeaking after him, while Hermes the deliverer led them down the dank ways of the dark. They passed the streams of Ocean and the White Rock, they passed the gates of the Sun and the country of dreams, and came at last to the meadow of asphodel, where the souls live on as phantoms of the dead.

There they found the ghost of Achilles son of Peleus, and with him Patroclus, and Antilochus, and Ajax, who was the best and the handsomest of all the Greeks after the matchless son of Peleus himself. These were gathered about Achilles when the ghost of Agamemnon came up to them, heavy with grief, and around him crowded the ghosts of all the men who had died with him in the house of Aegisthus. The ghost of Achilles spoke to him first.

“Son of Atreus,” he said, “we always thought that Zeus loved you above all other heroes, first to last — you who were lord over so many brave men in the land of Troy, where we Greeks suffered together. And yet the hand of death, that no man living escapes, came down on you too soon. Better for you if you had fallen at Troy, at the height of your glory. The Greeks would have raised a great mound over your ashes, and your son would have inherited your name. Instead it was your fate to die the most pitiable death of all.”

“Fortunate son of Peleus,” answered the ghost of Agamemnon, “to have died at Troy, far from Argos, with the bravest of the Trojans and the bravest of the Greeks falling around you, fighting over your body. There you lay in the swirling dust, great and greatly fallen, your horsemanship forgotten. We fought for you the whole day long, and we would never have stopped if Zeus had not broken it off with a storm. Then we carried you out of the battle to the ships and laid you on a bier, and washed your fair body with warm water and anointed it with oil, and the Greeks wept around you and cut their hair for grief. Your mother heard, and came up out of the sea with her immortal nymphs, and a strange wailing rose across the water, so that the whole army shook with fear. They would have bolted for the ships if wise old Nestor had not held them — he whose counsel was always best. ‘Hold, men of Argos,’ he cried. ‘Do not run, sons of the Greeks. This is his mother, come up from the sea with her immortal nymphs to look on the face of her dead son.’ At his word their fear let go of them.

“The daughters of the old man of the sea stood about you, weeping, and wrapped you in clothing fit for the gods. The nine Muses came too, and lifted their lovely voices in the dirge, answering one to another, and there was not a man among the Greeks but wept, so deeply that singing pierced the heart. Seventeen days and seventeen nights we mourned you, mortals and immortals together, and on the eighteenth we gave you to the fire. We killed many a fat sheep and many a shambling ox around your pyre. You burned in the clothing of the gods, in deep myrrh and sweet honey, while the Greek heroes paraded in their armor around the blaze, both on foot and in their chariots, and a great noise of them went up. When the god’s fire had done its work, we gathered your white bones at dawn, Achilles, and laid them in unmixed wine and oil. Your mother gave us a golden jar to hold them — the gift of Dionysus, she said, and the work of Hephaestus’ own hands — and in it we mingled your white bones with those of Patroclus who had died before you, and set apart, close beside them, the bones of Antilochus, whom you had loved above all your living companions once Patroclus was gone.

“Over you the army of the Greeks heaped up a great and shining mound, on a headland thrust out over the wide Hellespont, so that it could be seen far out at sea by men now living and by those still to be born. And your mother begged splendid prizes from the gods and set them up in the middle of the games, for the best of the Greeks to contend over. You have stood, in your time, at the funeral of many a hero, when the young men gird themselves and ready their hands for the contests held over a dead king. But you would have marveled most of all at the prizes silver-footed Thetis set out in your honor, for the gods loved you well. So not even in death has your name been lost: your glory will live forever among all mankind, Achilles. But what joy was there in it for me, that I brought the war to its end? On my own homecoming Zeus had planned a wretched death for me, at the hands of Aegisthus and of my accursed wife.”

So the two of them spoke together. Then Hermes the guide came up, leading the ghosts of the suitors Odysseus had killed, and the two were amazed and went straight toward them. The ghost of Agamemnon knew the face of Amphimedon, son of Melaneus, who had once been his host in Ithaca, and he spoke to him first.

“Amphimedon,” he said, “what disaster has brought you down into the dark earth — all of you, picked men, of an age, the finest a city could choose? Did Poseidon raise the winds and the great waves against you at sea? Did enemies cut you down on dry land while you were lifting their cattle and their flocks, or fighting over their town and their women? Tell me, for I was your guest once. Don’t you remember when I came to your house, with Menelaus, to press Odysseus to sail with us to Troy? It took us a whole month to cross the wide sea — we could barely talk Odysseus into coming.”

And the ghost of Amphimedon answered him. “Agamemnon, son of Atreus, lord of men, I remember all of it, just as you say, and I will tell you the whole truth of how our end came. Odysseus had been gone a long while, and we were courting his wife. She would not refuse the marriage outright — she could not bring herself to say no — but she would not settle it either, for she was plotting our deaths the whole time. And here is the trick she devised. She set up a great loom in her hall and began weaving a wide, fine cloth, and she said to us, ‘Young men, my suitors, since noble Odysseus is dead, you are eager for the wedding, I know; but wait, be patient, until I finish this cloth — I don’t want all my spinning to go to waste — a shroud for the hero Laertes, against the day that death’s long grief comes and takes him. The women of Ithaca would never forgive me if a man who won so much should lie in his grave without a shroud.’ That is what she said, and our proud hearts believed her. So by day she would weave at the great web, and by night she would set torches beside it and unravel what she had done. For three years she fooled us, and we never knew. But in the fourth year, as the seasons came round, one of her women who knew the secret told us, and we caught her in the act of pulling out the bright cloth. Then she had to finish it, against her will. And when she showed us the great web washed clean at last, it shone like the sun or the moon.

“It was then some cruel god brought Odysseus from somewhere to the edge of his lands, where the swineherd lives. His son had landed there too, home from sandy Pylos, and the two of them, once they had plotted the suitors’ ruin, came down to the famous town — Telemachus first, and Odysseus a little after, led by the swineherd. Odysseus came dressed in filthy rags, leaning on a staff like some miserable old beggar, and he came on us so suddenly that not one of us knew him, not even the older men. We jeered at him, we threw things at him. For a while he bore the blows and the insults in his own halls without a word. But when the will of Zeus who holds the storm-shield at last moved him, he and Telemachus carried the fine armor off into the storeroom and shut it away behind the bolts. Then in his cunning he had his wife set out his bow and the gray iron axes for us doomed men to compete over — and that was the beginning of the slaughter. Not one of us could string that bow; we came nowhere near it. When it was about to reach the hands of Odysseus, we all shouted out that it must not be given to him, whatever he said. Only Telemachus pressed for it, and gave it to him. Then long-suffering Odysseus took the bow in his hands, strung it as if it were nothing, and shot his arrow clean through the iron. He sprang to the great threshold, glaring terribly, and poured the arrows out at his feet. First he shot Antinous, and after that he loosed his deadly shafts at the rest of us, aiming straight, and the men fell thick on one another. It was plain that some god was helping them, for they swept through the hall in their fury, cutting us down, and a hideous groaning rose as heads were broken, and the whole floor ran with blood. That is how we died, Agamemnon, and our bodies are lying still uncared-for in the house of Odysseus. Our families at home do not yet know — those who would wash the black gore from our wounds and lay us out and mourn us, as is the right of the dead.”

“Fortunate son of Laertes, resourceful Odysseus,” said the ghost of Agamemnon, “truly you won yourself a wife of rare worth. How sound the mind of faultless Penelope, daughter of Icarius — how faithfully she kept Odysseus in her heart, the husband of her youth. The fame of her virtue will never die. The immortals will make of it a song, a song to delight the whole earth, in honor of steadfast Penelope. She is nothing like Clytemnestra, the daughter of Tyndareus, who plotted evil and killed the husband of her youth. Her song will be a hateful one among men, and she has fixed an ugly name on women everywhere, even the good ones.”

So the two of them spoke together in the house of Hades, deep beneath the earth. Meanwhile Odysseus and the others had passed out of the town and soon reached the fine, well-worked farm of Laertes, which the old man had won for himself long ago with hard labor. There stood his house, with the lean-to running all around it where the laborers who worked his land would eat and sit and sleep, and inside lived an old Sicilian woman who looked after him out there in the country, far from the town. Odysseus said to his son and the herdsmen, “Go on into the house and kill the best of the pigs for our meal. I am going to test my father — to see whether he will know me after all this time, or whether the long years have put me past knowing.”

With that he handed his armor to the two herdsmen, and they went straight on to the house while Odysseus turned aside into the rich vineyard to try his father. He did not find Dolius there as he went down through the great orchard, nor any of his sons or the other workers; they had all gone off to gather stones for the vineyard wall, with old Dolius leading them. So he found his father alone, hoeing about a vine. Laertes had on a filthy, patched old shirt; oxhide leggings were bound around his shins to keep off the thorns, and there were leather guards on his hands against the brambles, and a goatskin cap on his head, and the whole look of him was sunk in grief. When long-suffering Odysseus saw him so worn down by age and so heavy with sorrow, he stopped under a tall pear tree and wept. He could not decide whether to throw his arms around his father and kiss him and tell him everything — how he had come home at last — or whether to question him first and test him. In the end it seemed best to try him with sharp words, so he went straight up to him, where the old man was bent over the plant, digging.

“Old man,” he said, “you are no clumsy hand at a garden. Everything here is well tended — there is not a plant, not a fig or a vine, not an olive or a pear, not a bed of greens in the whole place, that lacks your care. But I will say this, and don’t take it amiss: you do not take such good care of yourself. Here you are, old and grimy and dressed in rags. It can’t be for any idleness of yours that your master neglects you. There is nothing of the slave in your face or your build — you have the look of a king. You are the kind of man who, once he had washed and eaten, ought to sleep soft, as is the right of the old. But tell me this, and tell me the truth. Whose servant are you, and whose orchard is this you tend? And tell me one more thing, for I need to be sure of it — is this place I have come to really Ithaca? A man I met on the road just now said it was, though he was a dull sort and had no patience to hear me out or answer when I asked after a friend of mine — whether he was still alive, or already dead and gone down to the house of Hades. For I tell you, a man once came to my house, and of all the strangers who ever crossed my threshold no guest was dearer to me. He said he was from Ithaca, and that his father was Laertes, son of Arceisius. I took him in, and treated him well out of all the plenty in my house, and gave him fitting gifts when he went on his way — seven talents of wrought gold, a mixing-bowl of solid silver worked with flowers, twelve single cloaks, twelve rugs, twelve fine mantles, and as many shirts, and besides all this four handsome women skilled in good work, the ones he chose himself.”

His father answered him through tears. “Stranger, you have come to the country you ask for, but cruel and reckless men hold it now. All those gifts you gave, you gave for nothing. If you had found that man still alive here in Ithaca, he would have sent you off well repaid, with welcome and good gifts of his own — that is the right of it, when a man has given first. But tell me truly: how many years is it since you took in that guest, my poor son — if there ever truly was such a man — who now, far from his own people and his home, has been eaten by the fish of the sea, or torn on dry land by the birds and beasts? His mother did not lay him out and mourn him; neither did I, his father, who gave him life; nor did his richly dowered wife, faithful Penelope, weep over her husband on his bier or close his eyes, as is the right of the dead. But tell me this too, for I want to know it plainly. Who are you, and where from? Where is your city, your parents? Where is the ship lying that brought you and your crew here? Or did you come as a passenger on another man’s ship, and they put you ashore and sailed on?”

“I will tell you the whole truth,” said the resourceful Odysseus. “I come from Alybas, where I have a fine house. I am the son of King Apheidas, son of Polypemon, and my own name is Eperitus. Some god drove me off my course from Sicania against my will, and brought me here, and my ship lies out beyond the town, off the open fields. As for Odysseus — it is five years now since he left my country and sailed for home, poor man. And yet the birds flew on the right as he went, a good omen, and I was glad to see him off, and he was glad to go, for we both had high hopes we would meet again as friends and trade fine gifts.”

A black cloud of grief came over Laertes. He scooped up the gray dust in both hands and poured it over his white head, groaning, and the heart of Odysseus was wrung at the sight of his own father; a sharp pang shot up through his nostrils. He could bear it no longer. He sprang to him, threw his arms around him, kissed him, and cried, “Father — I am the man, the one you are asking after. I have come home in the twentieth year, to my own country. Hold back your weeping now, stop the tears, for I tell you — and we have no time to lose — I have killed the suitors in our house and made them pay for their insults and their crimes.”

And Laertes answered him, “If you truly are my son Odysseus, come home at last, then give me some clear sign of it, something I can be sure of.”

“First, then, look at this scar,” said the resourceful Odysseus, “the one the boar’s white tusk gave me on Parnassus, when you and my mother sent me to Autolycus, her father, to fetch the gifts he had promised me when he came here. And here is another thing — let me name you the trees in this fine orchard that you gave me long ago. I was only a boy, following you through the garden, and I begged for them one by one, and you walked me past each tree and named it and told me what it was. You gave me thirteen pear trees, and ten apple trees, and forty fig trees, and you marked out fifty rows of vines besides, each ripening in its own season, all of them heavy with grapes of every kind when the seasons of Zeus weigh down upon them.”

At that the old man’s knees went loose, and his heart with them, for he knew the sure signs his son had given him. He threw his arms around his dear son, and long-suffering Odysseus caught him as he fainted toward the ground. When he came to himself and his spirit gathered again, he cried out, “Father Zeus — so you gods are still on high Olympus after all, if the suitors have truly paid for their pride. And yet I am afraid in my heart that all the men of Ithaca will be up here soon, and will send word out to every city of the Cephallenians.”

“Take heart,” the resourceful Odysseus answered, “and do not let that trouble you. Let us go up to the house here by the orchard. I have already sent Telemachus and the two herdsmen ahead to make the meal ready as quick as they can.”

So the two of them set off toward the fine house, talking as they went. When they came to the great hall they found Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd carving the meat and mixing the bright wine. Meanwhile the old Sicilian woman bathed brave-hearted Laertes inside the house, and rubbed him with oil, and dressed him in a fine cloak. And Athena came close and filled out the limbs of the shepherd of his people, and made him taller to the eye and stronger than before. When he came out of the bath his son was astonished to see him looking like one of the deathless gods, and he said, “Father — surely one of the everlasting gods has made you taller and finer to look on.”

And wise Laertes answered, “Father Zeus, Athena, Apollo — if only I were the man I was when I ruled the Cephallenians and took the strong fortress of Nericus on the headland of the mainland. If I had stood in our halls yesterday with my armor on my shoulders, I would have fought beside you against the suitors and brought down a great many of them — and your heart would have warmed to see it.”

So the two of them spoke together. And when the others had finished their work and the feast was laid, they took their places along the benches and chairs and set their hands to the food. Just then old Dolius came in with his sons, tired from the fields, for their mother, the old Sicilian woman who fed them and cared for the aging Laertes, had gone out to call them home. When they saw Odysseus and knew him in their hearts, they stood still in the hall, struck dumb. But Odysseus spoke to them gently. “Old man, sit down to the food, and let go your wonder. We have been waiting here a long time, eager to begin, holding off for you.”

Then Dolius came straight at him with both arms open, caught Odysseus by the wrist, and kissed his hand. “Dear master,” he said, “so you are back — back to us who longed for you and never thought to see you again. The gods themselves have brought you home. Welcome, and welcome again, and may they bless you. But tell me one thing for certain — does wise Penelope already know that you are home, or should we send someone to tell her?”

“Old man,” said the resourceful Odysseus, “she knows already. There is no need to trouble about it.” So Dolius sat down again on his polished chair, and his sons in their turn gathered around Odysseus, greeting him and clasping his hands, and then took their seats in order beside their father.

So they busied themselves with the meal in the house. But Rumor had gone running through the town as a swift messenger, crying everywhere the grim death of the suitors. The people heard it, and from every side they came streaming to the house of Odysseus, groaning and crying out. They carried off their dead, and each man buried his own; the bodies of those from other towns they laid aboard the fishing boats for the boatmen to take home. Then they gathered in the assembly, all of them sick at heart, and when they were come together Eupeithes stood up to speak. He was crushed by an unending grief for his son Antinous, the first man long-suffering Odysseus had killed, and he wept for him as he spoke. “Friends,” he said, “this man has done the Greeks a monstrous wrong. He led away in his ships a great company of brave men, and he lost the ships, and lost every man with them. And now, come home, he has killed the best of the Cephallenians who were left. Up, then, before he can slip away to Pylos or to fair Elis where the Epeans rule. Up, or we will be shamed forever. It would be a disgrace for men to come — a thing our children’s children would hear of — if we do not avenge our murdered sons and brothers. For my own part, I would have no more joy in living. Better to die at once and go down among the dead. Up, then, after them, before they cross to the mainland.”

So he spoke, weeping, and pity took hold of all the Greeks. Then Medon and the divine singer came up to them, fresh from sleep in the house of Odysseus, and stood in the middle of the assembly, and amazement seized every man. And Medon, wise in his heart, spoke to them. “Hear me now, men of Ithaca. Odysseus did not do these things against the will of the gods. With my own eyes I saw one of the deathless gods standing close beside him, in the very likeness of Mentor. An immortal — now appearing before Odysseus to put heart in him, now sweeping through the hall in fury and driving the suitors down, so that they fell thick on one another.”

At that a pale fear took them all. Then old Halitherses spoke up among them, the son of Mastor, the one man who could see both forward and back. With good will toward them he stood and said, “Hear me now, men of Ithaca. This came to pass through your own cowardice, friends. You would not listen to me, or to Mentor the shepherd of the people, when we told you to put a stop to your sons’ folly. They did a great wrong in the recklessness of their hearts, wasting the wealth and dishonoring the wife of the best of men, who they were sure would never come home. So let it be as I say now. Do not march out, or you will draw the evil down on your own heads.”

So he spoke, and more than half of them sprang up with a great shout and left the assembly. But the rest stayed where they were, for the words of Halitherses did not please them; they were with Eupeithes. They rushed for their armor, and when they had buckled the bright bronze about them they gathered in a body in front of the wide town, and Eupeithes led them on in his foolishness. He thought he would avenge his son’s death; but he was never to come home again — there, in that place, he would meet his own.

Then Athena spoke to Zeus, the son of Cronus. “Our father, son of Cronus, highest of the lords — answer what I ask you. What is the purpose hidden in your heart? Will you stir up still more cruel war and the dreadful clash of battle, or make peace between the two sides?”

And Zeus who gathers the clouds answered her, “My child, why do you question me about this? Was it not your own plan that Odysseus should come home and take his revenge on these men? Do as you please. But I will tell you what is fitting. Now that Odysseus has paid the suitors back, let them swear a binding oath: let him be king for all his days, and let us wipe from their minds the death of their sons and brothers. Let them love one another again as they did before, and let peace and plenty be among them.”

So he spoke, and stirred Athena, who was already eager for it, and down she came swooping from the peaks of Olympus.

Back at the farm, when the men had taken the edge off their hunger, the resourceful Odysseus spoke first. “Let one of you go out and look — they may be close on us by now.” So one of the sons of Dolius went, as he was told. He stood on the threshold and saw them all already near, and he called back to Odysseus with winged words, “Here they are, right on us. Quick — to arms.” They sprang up and armed: Odysseus and his three men, and the six sons of Dolius, and Laertes and Dolius themselves took up their weapons too, gray-headed though they were, soldiers now because they had to be. When they had clad themselves in the gleaming bronze they threw open the gates and went out, Odysseus at their head.

Then Athena, daughter of Zeus, came up to them in the likeness and the voice of Mentor, and long-suffering Odysseus was glad to see her, and turned at once to his son. “Telemachus, now you will learn this for yourself, going into the fight where men prove which of them is best — do not shame the line of your fathers, who have been first in courage and in strength across the whole earth.”

And wise Telemachus answered, “You will see, dear father, if you care to, that I am in no mind to shame your line, as you say.” And Laertes was overjoyed to hear it, and cried out, “What a day this is for me, dear gods — what joy! My son and my son’s son are matching each other in courage.”

Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena came close to him and said, “Son of Arceisius, dearest of all my friends, say a prayer to the gray-eyed maiden and to Father Zeus, then lift your long spear at once and let it fly.” With that Pallas Athena breathed great strength into him. He prayed to the daughter of mighty Zeus, then lifted the long spear and hurled it, and struck Eupeithes through the bronze cheek-piece of his helmet. The helmet did not stop it; the spear drove clean through, and he crashed to the ground, his armor ringing about him. Then Odysseus and his glorious son fell on the front rank and cut them down with swords and double-pointed spears. They would have killed them all and left not one to come home alive — but Athena, daughter of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, cried out in a great voice that stopped the whole field. “Hold back, men of Ithaca, from this grinding war. Break it off now, with no more blood spilled.”

So Athena cried, and pale fear caught them. In their terror the weapons flew from their hands and fell to the ground at the voice of the goddess, and they turned toward the town, desperate to save their lives. But long-suffering Odysseus gave a terrible cry and gathered himself and swooped after them, like a high-soaring eagle. And in that moment the son of Cronus hurled down a smoking thunderbolt, and it fell at the feet of the gray-eyed daughter of the mighty god. Then Athena said to Odysseus, “Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes, hold back. Stop this strife of leveling war, or Zeus who sees far, the son of Cronus, will be angry with you.”

So Athena spoke, and Odysseus obeyed, and his heart was glad. And then Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, in the likeness and the voice of Mentor, set a sworn peace between the two sides, to hold for all the days to come.
