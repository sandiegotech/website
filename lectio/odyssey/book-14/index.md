# The Odyssey, Book XIV

In the Hut of Eumaeus

From the harbor Odysseus climbed the rough track up through the woods and over the shoulder of the hill, to the place where Athena had told him he would find the swineherd — the man who, of all the servants Odysseus owned, had cared the most faithfully for what was his. He found him sitting in front of his hut, in a high yard built where the eye could see far on every side. Eumaeus had raised it himself, spacious and trim, while his master was away — built it of stones he had hauled from the ground and topped with a hedge of thorn, with no word of it to Penelope or to old Laertes; and around the outside he had driven a stockade of split oak posts, set close together. Within he had made twelve sties, side by side, for the sows to lie in, fifty breeding sows penned in each. The boars slept outside, and there were far fewer of them now, for the suitors kept eating them down, and the swineherd had to send up the best he had, day after day, until only three hundred and sixty were left. Four dogs, savage as wolves, slept always among them, for the swineherd had reared them himself. Just then he was cutting himself a pair of sandals from a good stout oxhide. Three of his men were off herding the pigs at one station or another, and he had sent the fourth into town, driving a boar the suitors had forced out of him — for them to sacrifice and gorge their fill of meat.

When the dogs caught sight of Odysseus they came at him all at once, baying furiously. Odysseus had the wit to sit straight down and let the stick drop from his hand — and even so they would have torn him there, on his own land, had not the swineherd come pounding through the gate, the oxhide flung from his fingers, shouting and pelting them with stones until he had driven them off in every direction. Then he said to his master, not knowing him: “Old man, the dogs nearly made an end of you in a heartbeat, and the shame of it would have fallen on me. The gods have given me grief enough already, without that. Here I sit, mourning a master worth all the world, and fattening his pigs for other men to eat, while he — if he is alive at all and still sees the light — goes hungry in some far-off country. But come inside. When you have had your fill of bread and wine, then tell me where you are from and what you have suffered.”

So the loyal swineherd led the way into the hut and had him sit down. He spread a thick bed of brushwood and threw over it the shaggy hide of a wild goat, the great rug he slept on himself, and Odysseus, glad of such a welcome, said: “May Zeus, my friend, and all the other gods grant you your heart’s desire, for the warmth with which you have taken me in.”

And you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd: “Stranger, it would be wrong of me to turn away a guest, even one poorer than you — for every stranger, every beggar, comes to us from Zeus. What I can give is little, but it is given gladly; that is how it is with servants, who live in fear when young lords are over them. The gods have held back the man who would have been good to me — who would have given me a holding of my own, a house, a piece of land, a wife men would court — all that a generous master grants a servant who has worked hard for him, and whose work the gods have prospered, as they have prospered mine in this place. If my master had grown old here, he would have done great things for me. But he is gone. I wish the whole breed of Helen had been wiped from the earth — she has cut the knees from under so many good men. It was that quarrel that took my master off to Ilium, the land of fine horses, to fight the Trojans for Agamemnon.”

With that he hitched up his tunic and went out to the sties where the young sucking-pigs were penned. He picked two, brought them back, and killed them; he singed them, cut them up, ran the pieces onto spits, and when the meat was roasted he carried it in and set it before Odysseus, hot, still on the spit, and scattered white barley meal over it. Then he mixed wine in a bowl of ivy-wood, sat down facing him, and told him to fall to.

“Eat, stranger,” he said, “such as a servant has to offer — sucking-pig. The fat hogs go to the suitors, who eat without shame and without a thought for the gods’ anger. But the blessed gods love no such deeds; they honor fair dealing and the right. Even the cruelest raiders, who fall on another man’s land and carry off plunder that Zeus lets them keep — even they, once their ships are full and they are home again, feel the cold dread of judgment settle on them. But these men here must have heard some god whisper that Odysseus is dead and will not come back; that is why they will not court his wife honestly, in their own homes, but sit on here, devouring his house at their leisure, sparing nothing. Every single day and night that Zeus sends down, they slaughter — not one beast, not two, but more — and they drain his wine like water, for he was a rich man, richer than any other, on the mainland or here in Ithaca. No twenty men together had wealth to match his. Let me tell you what he had: twelve herds of cattle on the mainland, as many flocks of sheep, as many droves of pigs, and as many sprawling herds of goats, kept by his own men and by hired hands. And here on Ithaca eleven wide-ranging herds of goats graze the far end of the island, in the charge of good herdsmen — and every one of those men drives the best goat of his flock to the suitors, day after day. And I, who watch over the pigs you see, I keep choosing out the best of them and sending it up to them too.”

So he spoke, while Odysseus ate and drank, ravenous and silent, brooding all the while on the suitors and their ruin. When he had eaten his fill, the swineherd filled with wine the very cup he drank from and handed it to him, and Odysseus took it gladly and spoke to him with winged words: “My friend, who was this master of yours, who bought you and owned you — so rich, you say, so powerful? You tell me he died for Agamemnon’s sake. Tell me his name; I may have met such a man somewhere. Zeus knows, and the other gods, but I have wandered far, and I may have word of him.”

The swineherd, that leader of men, answered: “Old man, no wanderer who comes here with news of him will ever make his wife or his son believe it. Tramps in need of food and lodging come full of lies, and not one of them cares for the truth. Every drifter who lands on Ithaca goes straight to my mistress with his stories, and she takes him in and makes much of him and questions him on every point, while the tears run down her cheeks, as a woman’s will when her husband is lost far away. You too, old man, could soon stitch together a fine tale, if someone would give you a cloak and a shirt for it. No — by now the dogs and the swift birds have surely stripped the flesh from his bones, or the fish have eaten him in the sea, and his bones lie heaped on some beach, buried deep in the sand. He is dead and gone, and grief is all he has left his friends — me most of all. Wherever I go I will never find so kind a master again, not even if I went back to my own father and mother, in the land where I was born and raised. And I do not grieve for them as I grieve for him — though I long to lay eyes on them again in my own country. No, it is Odysseus, gone from us, that I ache for; even though he is not here, stranger, I shrink from speaking his name, so dearly did he love me and care for me; I call him my dear master still, far away as he is.”

Then long-suffering Odysseus answered him: “My friend, since you are so set against it, so sure in your heart that he will never come home, I will not merely say it — I will swear it: Odysseus is coming back. And pay me for the news only when he stands here in his house; then give me a good cloak and shirt to wear. Till then I will take nothing, for all my need — for I hate, like the gates of death itself, the man who lets his poverty drive him to lies. Now I call Zeus to witness, first of the gods, and this table of guest-friendship, and the hearth of Odysseus that I have come to: everything will fall out as I tell you. Within this very year Odysseus will be here — as this moon dies and the next one rises, he will come home and take his revenge on every man who is dishonoring his wife and his son.”

And you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd: “Old man, I will never pay you for that news, and Odysseus will never come home. Drink your wine in peace, and let us turn to something else. Don’t keep bringing this back to me; it stabs at my heart whenever anyone speaks of my honored master. We will let your oath be. But I do wish he would come — as Penelope wishes it, and old Laertes, and godlike Telemachus. It is the boy I grieve for now, Odysseus’s son. The gods were raising him like a young tree, and I thought he would grow to be no worse a man than his father, in build and in face — but some god, or some man, has unsettled his wits, and he has gone off to sacred Pylos after news of his father, while the proud suitors lie in ambush for his voyage home, hoping to wipe the line of godlike Arceisius out of Ithaca and leave it without a name. But let him go — let him be taken, or let him slip past them, if the hand of Zeus is held over him to keep him safe. And now, old man, tell me your own troubles. Tell me the truth, so I may know it well: who are you, and where do you come from? Where is your city, your family? What ship brought you here, and how did the crew land you on Ithaca, and who did they claim to be? For you surely did not come on foot.”

Then resourceful Odysseus answered him: “I will tell you the whole of it, plainly. If only we had food and wine enough to last, and could sit here at our ease in the hut, feasting in quiet while others did the work — I could talk for a whole year and not come to the end of all the sorrows the gods have laid on me.

“I am a Cretan by birth. My father was a man of substance, with many true-born sons by his wife, but I was the son of a bought woman, a slave he kept for his bed. Still, Castor son of Hylax — that was my father, a man the Cretans honored like a god for his wealth, his good fortune, and his fighting sons — he raised me no differently from his lawful children. But when death carried him down to the house of Hades, his proud sons divided the estate and cast lots for their shares, and they handed me almost nothing, only a roof and a scrap of land. Yet by my own worth I married into a wealthy family, for I was no fool and no coward in war. All that is past me now — but look at the stubble and you can guess the standing grain; hard times enough have stripped me bare. Ares and Athena had made me brave and gave me the nerve to break a battle line. When I picked my best men for an ambush, sowing the seeds of the enemy’s ruin, I never once thought of death; I would be the first to leap out and run my spear through any man who could not outrun me. That was the sort of soldier I was. But farm work was never to my taste, nor the quiet of a settled house where children are raised. What I loved were the oared ships and the wars, the polished javelins and the arrows — grim things, that make other men shudder. But one man loves one thing, another loves another; this was the life the gods turned my heart to. Before the Greeks ever sailed for Troy, I had nine times led men and ships against foreign coasts, and great wealth came to me from it — I took my pick of the plunder first, and much more fell to me by lot afterward. My house grew quickly, and I became a man feared and respected among the Cretans.

“But when far-seeing Zeus devised that terrible voyage, the one that loosened the knees of so many men, then the people called on me and on famous Idomeneus to lead the ships to Ilium, and there was no refusing — the weight of the people’s blame would have crushed us. There we Greeks fought nine long years, and in the tenth we sacked the city of Priam and sailed for home, and a god scattered us over the sea. Then Zeus in his wisdom set sorrow in my path. Just one month I stayed, taking my joy in my children, my wife, and all I had. After that the longing came on me to fit out ships and make a raid on Egypt, with a good crew. I manned nine ships, and the men flocked to fill them. For six days my trusty companions feasted, and I gave them many victims, both to sacrifice to the gods and to make their own meal; on the seventh we put out from Crete and ran before a fair, fresh North Wind, as easy as a boat going downstream. Not one ship was harmed; we sat at our ease, unhurt and well, and let the wind and the steersmen hold our course. On the fifth day we reached the flowing river of Egypt, and there I anchored my curved ships. I told my faithful crews to stay by the ships and guard them, and I sent scouts ahead to high points to spy out the land.

“But my men gave way to their own reckless greed. They fell at once upon the rich Egyptian fields, carrying off the women and little children and cutting down the men. The cry of it ran swiftly to the city, and at the first light of dawn the people came pouring out, until the whole plain was full of footmen and horsemen and the glitter of bronze. Then Zeus who delights in thunder broke my men in panic; not one of them dared stand and face the enemy, for ruin had closed in on every side. There the Egyptians killed many of us with the sharp bronze, and led the rest off alive, to slave for them. But Zeus himself put a thought into my mind — and how I wish I had died there in Egypt instead, met my fate then, for worse was still waiting for me. I tore the well-made helmet from my head, slid the shield from my shoulder, dropped the spear from my hand, and ran straight out in front of the king’s chariot. I caught his knees and kissed them, and he took pity, and spared me; he lifted me weeping into his own chariot and brought me home. Many of his men charged at me with their ash spears, raging to cut me down, but he held them off, in fear of the wrath of Zeus, the protector of strangers, who is angered most of all by cruel deeds.

“There I stayed seven years, and gathered a great store of wealth among the Egyptians, for every man gave me something. But when the eighth year came round, there arrived a certain Phoenician, a greedy, scheming rogue, a man who had already done the world much harm, and with his smooth tongue he talked me into sailing with him to Phoenicia, where his house and his property lay. I stayed there with him a full year. But when the months and the days had run their course and the seasons came round again to the same point, he put me aboard a ship bound for Libya — telling me a lie, that I was to help him carry a cargo there, but really meaning to sell me off and pocket a great price for me. I went with him, for all my suspicions; there was nothing else I could do.

“The ship ran before a fair, fresh North Wind, out over the open water above Crete, and Zeus plotted the death of every man aboard. For once we had left Crete behind and could see nothing but sky and sea, the son of Cronus hung a black cloud over the hollow ship, and the water went dark beneath it. Then Zeus thundered and hurled his bolt full into the ship, and she reeled and shuddered, struck by the lightning, and the air filled with the reek of sulfur. The men were flung out into the sea, and like sea-crows they were tossed on the waves around the black ship, and the god took from them their day of homecoming. But Zeus himself put the ship’s great mast into my hands, even in the depth of my despair, so that I might yet escape destruction. I wrapped my arms around it and drifted before the deadly winds. Nine days I drifted; on the tenth, in the black of night, a great rolling wave carried me to the land of the Thesprotians. There their king, the hero Pheidon, took me in and asked no payment, for his own son came on me, broken with cold and exhaustion, and raised me up by the hand and led me to his father’s house, where they gave me a cloak and a shirt to wear.

“And there it was I first heard of Odysseus. The king told me he had given him welcome and a guest’s honor, on his way home to his own land. He showed me, too, the treasure Odysseus had gathered — bronze and gold and worked iron — enough to keep a man’s house for ten generations, so great was the store he had left in the halls of King Pheidon. As for the man himself, the king said he had gone to Dodona, to learn the will of Zeus from the god’s great oak with its high crown of leaves — to ask whether, after so long an absence, he should come home to Ithaca openly, or in secret. And the king swore to me, pouring the wine of an oath in his own house, that the ship was launched and the crew standing ready to carry Odysseus to his own country. But he sent me off first, for it happened that a Thesprotian ship was sailing for Dulichium, that island rich in wheat, and he ordered the crew to bring me safe to King Acastus.

“But those men chose instead a wicked plan against me, to sink me into the very pit of misery. When the ship had run well out from land, they set about making a slave of me. They stripped off the cloak and shirt I wore and threw me these filthy rags you see me in now. And toward evening, when they came to the fields of sunlit Ithaca, they lashed me fast in the ship’s hull with a stout twisted rope and went ashore for their supper by the water. But the gods themselves loosed my bonds with ease. I wound my rags about my head, slid down the smooth steering-oar, lowered my chest into the sea, and struck out, rowing with both hands, until I was well clear of them and came ashore where the trees stood thick. There I crouched low and hid, while they ran searching with great cries; but when it seemed no use to hunt any further, they went back to the hollow ship. So the gods themselves hid me, with no trouble at all, and led me here, to the farm of a man who knows what is right — for it seems my time to die has not yet come.”

And you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd: “Poor unlucky stranger — you have stirred my heart, telling me all this, the long tale of your sufferings and your wanderings. But there is one part of it that does not ring true, and you will never get me to believe it: what you said about Odysseus. Why should a man like you tell such an empty lie? I know the truth of my master’s homecoming for myself — that the gods, every one of them, hated him; for they did not let him fall at Troy, or die in the arms of his friends once the fighting was done. The whole Greek army would have raised him a mound, and he would have won great glory for his son in days to come. Instead the storm-winds have snatched him away without glory, and no man knows where.

“As for me, I keep clear of all that, here with my pigs. I never go down to the town unless wise Penelope sends for me, when some traveler brings word. Then they crowd around the man and question him — both those who ache for the long-lost king, and those who are glad to eat his house up for nothing. But I gave up asking such questions long ago, ever since an Aetolian fooled me with a tale. He had killed a man and wandered far over the earth, and at last he came to my hut, and I gave him a kind welcome. He swore he had seen Odysseus among the Cretans, with Idomeneus, repairing the ships a storm had wrecked, and said he would be home by summer, or by harvest-time, with all his men and a great mass of wealth. So you too, unlucky old man, since some god has brought you to my door — do not try to win me with these stories, do not soothe me with empty hopes. It is not for any such reason that I will honor you and treat you well, but out of fear of Zeus, the god of strangers, and out of pity for you yourself.”

Then resourceful Odysseus answered him: “You have a heart that is hard to convince, I see. Even my oath could not win you over; you will not believe. Well then, let us strike a bargain, and let the gods on Olympus be our witnesses. If your master comes home to this house, you will give me a cloak and a shirt and send me on to Dulichium, where I want to go. But if he does not come, as I say he will, then set your men on me and fling me from the top of that great cliff — so the next beggar will think twice before going about with lies.”

And the loyal swineherd answered him: “Stranger, a fine name for goodness and decency I would win for myself, now and ever after, if I first took you into my hut and gave you a welcome, and then turned and killed you and tore the dear life out of you. I would have to pray to Zeus, son of Cronus, with a clean conscience after that. But it is suppertime now, and I hope my men will be in soon, so we can cook a good supper here in the hut.”

While they were talking, the swineherds came up driving the pigs, and shut them into their pens for the night, and a tremendous squealing rose as the sows were herded into their sties. Then the loyal swineherd called to his men: “Bring in the best of the boars, so I can sacrifice it for this stranger from far away — and we will get some good of it ourselves, who have toiled so long over these pigs while other men devour what our labor earns, and pay nothing.”

With that he split firewood with the pitiless bronze, while the others led in a fat five-year-old boar and stood it by the hearth. The swineherd did not forget the immortals, for his heart was sound; he began by cutting bristles from the head of the white-tusked boar and throwing them into the fire, and he prayed to all the gods that Odysseus might come home again. Then he struck the boar with a billet of oak he had kept back from the splitting, and it dropped, and the others cut its throat and singed it and quickly carved it up. The swineherd laid raw strips from every limb on a coat of fat, sprinkled them with barley meal, and threw them on the fire; the rest they sliced small, spitted, roasted carefully, drew off the spits, and heaped on platters. Then the swineherd stood to share it out, for he was a fair-minded man, and divided the whole into seven portions: one he set aside, with a prayer, for the nymphs and for Hermes, son of Maia; the rest he dealt out to each man there. And he honored Odysseus with the long cuts of the chine, the back of the white-tusked boar, and gladdened his master’s heart, so that resourceful Odysseus spoke up: “Eumaeus, may you be as dear to Father Zeus as you are to me, for honoring a wretch like me with the best you have.”

And you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd: “Eat, my poor friend, and take what pleasure you can in what is set before you. The god gives one thing and holds back another, just as he pleases in his heart — for he can do all things.”

With that he made a burnt offering of the first cuts to the everlasting gods, poured a libation of gleaming wine, set the cup in the hands of Odysseus the sacker of cities, and sat down to his own share. Mesaulius set out the bread — a man the swineherd had bought from the Taphians with his own goods while his master was away, telling neither his mistress nor old Laertes. So they reached out to the good things spread before them, and when they had put away all desire for food and drink, Mesaulius cleared away the bread, and they made for their beds, heavy and content with the feast.

Now the night came on, cruel and moonless. Zeus rained the whole night through, and a great West Wind blew, the wind that always brings wet. So Odysseus, to test the swineherd — to see whether the man who cared for him so well would strip off his own cloak and give it to him, or order one of his men to — said: “Listen to me now, Eumaeus, and all of you. I will tell you something, with a boast in it; it is the wine that draws me on, the heady wine, which sets even a steady man singing and laughing softly, and stirs him up to dance, and lets slip a word that had been better left unsaid. But I have started, so I will speak it out. I wish I were still young and my strength unbroken, as I was the day we laid an ambush below the walls of Troy. Odysseus and Menelaus led it, and I was a third in command, for they themselves chose me. When we had come up under the steep wall of the city, we crouched in the marsh among the dense reeds and brush, hunched under our shields. The night turned bitter as the North Wind came down, and snow fell on us cold as frost, and the ice crusted thick on our shields. The others all had cloaks and shirts and slept soundly enough, their shields drawn up over their shoulders; but I, like a fool, had left my cloak with my men when I set out, never thinking I would be cold, and I had come along with nothing but my shield and a bright war-belt. When the night was two-thirds gone and the stars had wheeled on past their height, I nudged Odysseus, lying close beside me, with my elbow, and he came awake at once.

“‘Royal son of Laertes,’ I whispered, ‘Odysseus of the many schemes — I will not be much longer among the living. This cold is killing me. I have no cloak; some god tricked me into coming with only my shirt, and now there is no escape.’

“That was what I said, and the thought came to him at once — for he was a man who was never at a loss, in fighting or in counsel. He spoke low, with his mouth close to my ear: ‘Quiet now, in case some other of the Greeks should hear you.’ Then he propped his head on his elbow and called out: ‘Listen, my friends. A dream from the gods came to me in my sleep. We have gone too far from the ships. I wish someone would go and tell Agamemnon, shepherd of the people, to send more men up here to us from the camp.’

“At that, Thoas, son of Andraemon, sprang up at once, flung off his purple cloak, and went running toward the ships — and I lay down in his cloak, glad of it, and slept warm until Dawn came on her golden throne. I wish I were as young now, my strength as whole, as I was then; then one of you swineherds would lend me a cloak here at the farm, both for kindness and for the respect owed a good fighting man. As it is, men look down on me for the poor rags on my back.”

And you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd: “Old man, that was a fine story you told, and not a word of it wasted or off the mark. So you shall lack neither clothing nor anything else that a hard-pressed suppliant has a right to ask — for tonight. But in the morning you must shake your own rags about you again, for we have no spare cloaks and shirts to hand out here; each man has only the one. When Odysseus’s own dear son comes home, he will give you a cloak and a shirt and send you wherever your heart is set on going.”

With that he rose and made up a bed for Odysseus close to the fire, throwing down the skins of sheep and goats, and Odysseus lay down on it, and Eumaeus spread over him a great thick cloak that he kept by for a change, to put on when the weather turned truly foul. So Odysseus slept there, and the young men slept beside him. But the swineherd would not lie down to rest away from his pigs, and he made ready to go out — and Odysseus’s heart warmed to see how well the man looked after his master’s goods, even with his master gone. First Eumaeus slung the sharp sword over his broad shoulders, then wrapped himself in a thick cloak against the wind, took up the skin of a great well-fed goat, and caught up a sharp javelin, to keep off dogs and men. Then he went out to lie down where his white-tusked boars were sleeping, under a hollow rock that gave them shelter from the North Wind.
