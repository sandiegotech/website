The Odyssey, Book XIII

The Return to Ithaca

Book XIII13 of 24

So Odysseus finished, and a hush held the whole hall. They sat spellbound in the shadowy galleries, still under the charm of his story. At last Alcinous spoke.

“Odysseus,” he said, “now that you have reached my high house with its bronze floor, I am sure of one thing: you will get home this time without being driven off course again, however much you have suffered before. And to the rest of you — you who come to my halls night after night to drink the king’s wine and listen to the bard — I have something to ask. Our guest’s chest is already packed with the woven clothing and the worked gold and all the other gifts our Phaeacian counselors have brought him. Let us add to it. Let each of us give him, besides, a great tripod and a cauldron. We will make good the cost later from the people at large; no one man should have to carry a gift this generous alone.”

They all thought this well said, and went home to sleep, each to his own house. When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, they hurried down to the ship with their gifts of gleaming bronze. Alcinous himself went aboard and stowed everything snugly under the benches with his own hands, so nothing would shift and foul the rowers when they bent to their work. Then they came back to the palace and set about a feast. Alcinous sacrificed an ox to Zeus, the dark-clouded son of Cronus, who is lord over all. They burned the thigh-pieces and sat down to a fine meal, and Demodocus, the bard the people loved, sang to them. But Odysseus kept turning his head toward the blazing sun, willing it to set, so hungry was he to be gone. As a man who has driven a pair of oxen all day through a fallow field aches for his supper, and his knees buckle under him as the light fails and he heads home to eat — just so welcome to Odysseus was the going down of the sun. At once he spoke to the Phaeacians, and most of all to Alcinous their king.

“King Alcinous, most honored of all this people, and all of you — pour your offering and send me on my way in peace, and farewell. Everything my heart wanted, you have given: an escort home, and these gifts, which I pray the gods in heaven will let me turn to good. May I come home to find my faithful wife unharmed and my own people safe and well. And may all of you whom I leave behind here bring lasting joy to your wives and your children. May the gods grant you every blessing, and may no trouble ever come upon your people.”

They all approved his words, and agreed their guest should have his escort, for he had spoken well and fairly. Then Alcinous called to his steward: “Pontonous, mix the wine in the great bowl and carry it round to every man in the hall, so that we may pray to Father Zeus and send our guest home to his own land.”

Pontonous mixed the honeyed wine and served it to each in turn. From where they sat, every man poured out his offering to the blessed gods who hold the wide heaven; but Odysseus rose to his feet, set the two-handled cup in Queen Arete’s hands, and spoke to her with winged words:

“Fare well, my queen, all your days — until old age comes upon you, and death, which waits for everyone. I am going now. Be happy here in your house, with your children and your people, and with Alcinous the king.”

With that he crossed the threshold. Alcinous sent a herald to walk with him down to the swift ship and the shore of the sea, and Arete sent serving-women after him — one carrying a fresh-washed cloak and tunic, one his strong chest of treasure, and a third bearing bread and red wine. When they came to the ship at the water’s edge, the crew took it all and stowed it aboard, every scrap of food and drink. For Odysseus himself they spread a rug and a linen sheet on the half-deck at the stern, so he might sleep soundly through the crossing. Then he too climbed aboard and lay down, without a word. They took their places at the oar-pins, in their proper order, and slipped the cable from the pierced stone where it was made fast. The moment they leaned back and the oars threw up the spray, a sleep dropped over Odysseus’s eyes — a deep, sweet, unbroken sleep, the very likeness of death.

The ship ran on. As a team of four stallions on a plain springs forward all together under the lash and devours the road, so her stern lifted and the dark wave of the loud-roaring sea boiled huge behind her. Steady and sure she held her course; not even a hawk, the swiftest thing that flies, could have kept pace beside her. So she cut through the waves at a run, carrying a man whose mind was a match for the gods — a man who had borne so much, so many wars of men and the bitter waves he had cut across, and who now slept on, still as death, forgetting all that he had suffered.

When the brightest of the stars rose, the one that comes to herald the light of early Dawn, the seafaring ship drew in to the island. There is a harbor in Ithaca that belongs to Phorcys, the old man of the sea, where two headlands run out sheer and shelving toward each other and close off the mouth; they break the great waves that the hard winds roll in from outside, so that once a ship is within, she can ride at anchor without even a line to hold her. At the head of the bay stands a long-leaved olive tree, and close by it a cave, shadowy and lovely, sacred to the nymphs men call the Naiads. Inside are great mixing-bowls and two-handled jars of stone, where the bees store their honey, and tall stone looms where the nymphs weave their sea-purple cloth, a marvel to look on, and springs that never run dry. The cave has two gates: one to the north, the way down for mortal men, and one to the south, the holier way, by which no man may enter — it is the road of the gods.

Into this harbor they rowed, for they knew it of old. The ship ran in so hard she drove half her length up onto the sand, such was the strength of the arms that had driven her. The men stepped out and first of all lifted Odysseus from the hollow ship, rug, linen sheet and all, and laid him down on the sand, still deep in sleep. Then they unloaded the treasure the Phaeacians, moved by gray-eyed Athena, had given him for the voyage home, and stacked it all together at the foot of the olive, well back from the road, in case some traveler should pass and rob him before he woke. That done, they turned for home.

But Poseidon had not forgotten the threats he had once made against Odysseus, and now he went to learn the mind of Zeus. “Father Zeus,” he said, “I shall never again be honored among the deathless gods, when even mortals show me no respect — these Phaeacians, who are my own blood. I always meant Odysseus to reach home, once he had suffered his full measure; I never robbed him of his return outright, since you had promised it with a nod of your head. But look — they have ferried him asleep across the sea in their swift ship and set him down in Ithaca, and loaded him with gifts past counting, more bronze and gold and woven goods than he could ever have carried home from Troy with his fair share of the plunder.”

Zeus, who gathers the clouds, answered him: “What are you saying, shaker of the earth, so wide in strength? The gods do not slight you. It would be a strange and ugly thing to deny respect to one so old and great. But if any mortal, trusting in his own strength, fails to honor you — you are always free to punish him, now or later, as you see fit. Do whatever your heart desires.”

“Then I would do it now, dark-clouded lord,” said Poseidon, the earth-shaker, “if I did not fear your anger and hold back from it. As things are, I mean to wreck that fine Phaeacian ship on her way home from the escort, out on the misty sea, so they will stop ferrying strangers ever after — and I will throw a great mountain round their city to hide it.”

“My friend,” Zeus answered, “this is how it seems best to me. When all the people are watching from the town as she runs in, turn her to stone close offshore, still in the shape of a ship, so that everyone stands amazed — and then wall their city round with the mountain.”

When Poseidon the earth-shaker heard this, he went off to Scheria, the land of the Phaeacians, and waited there. The seafaring ship came on, driving in fast and close, and the earth-shaker came up to her, struck her flat with the palm of his hand, and rooted her where she stood, turned all to stone. Then he was gone.

Up and down the shore the Phaeacians spoke to one another, the long-oared seamen famous for their ships, glancing at the man beside them: “Who in the world has rooted our swift ship in the sea, on her way home? We could see every plank of her a moment ago.” So they said, not one of them knowing what had happened. But Alcinous rose and spoke among them.

“Ah — now an old prophecy my father told me has come home at last. He used to say Poseidon would turn his anger on us for ferrying every man so safely across the sea, and that one day he would wreck a fine Phaeacian ship coming home from her escort, and throw a great mountain round our city. So the old man said, and now it is all coming true. Listen to me, and do as I say. From this day on, when strangers come to us, we give no more escorts home. And let us sacrifice twelve choice bulls to Poseidon, and pray he takes pity on us, and does not wall our city in with the mountain.” The people were afraid, and made the bulls ready. So the leaders and counselors of the Phaeacians stood about the altar and prayed to Poseidon, lord of the sea.

And just then Odysseus woke on his own native ground. He had been away so long that he did not know it again — and more than that, Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus, had poured a mist all around him, so that no one would know him, and she could tell him everything herself, with neither his wife nor his townsmen nor his friends recognizing him before the suitors had paid for all their crimes. So the whole land looked strange to its own king — the long straight paths, the safe harbors, the steep cliffs, the trees in their leaf, all of it changed. He sprang up, stared at the country of his birth, and groaned. He struck both thighs with the flat of his hands and cried out in despair.

“Poor wretch that I am — whose country have I come to now? Are they cruel here, savage, lawless — or are they kind to strangers, with the fear of the gods in their minds? And all this treasure — where do I carry it? Where do I even go? I wish I had stayed there among the Phaeacians. I might have gone on instead to some other great king, who would have taken me in and sent me home. As it is, I do not know where to put any of this, and I cannot leave it lying here for the first man who passes to carry off. So the Phaeacian leaders were not so wise or just after all. They have set me down in the wrong land. They swore they would bring me back to Ithaca, and they have not done it. May Zeus, who watches over those who beg for shelter, who sees all men and punishes the wrongdoer, pay them back for this. Well — let me count my goods and see whether the crew sailed off with any of it.”

He counted the lovely tripods and the cauldrons, the gold, the fine woven clothing — nothing was missing. But still he grieved for his own land, and dragged himself along the shore of the sounding sea, sick at heart. Then Athena came to him, in the form of a young man, a shepherd, but with the soft look of a prince’s son, a fine cloak doubled over his shoulders, sandals on his bright feet, and a spear in his hand. Odysseus was glad to see her, and went straight up to her and spoke to her with winged words.

“Friend — you are the first soul I have met in this country, so I greet you, and I beg you, do me no harm. Save these goods of mine, and save me too; I come to you as I would come to a god, and I take you by the knees. Tell me truly, what land is this? What people? Is this an island, or the shore of some mainland that runs down to the sea?”

Gray-eyed Athena answered him: “You must be a simple man, stranger, or come from very far away, to ask what country this is. It is hardly a place without a name. Crowds of men know it — both those who face the dawn and those who live toward the dark and the setting sun. It is rough country, no good for driving horses, and not a wide island either; but it is far from poor. It grows grain beyond measure, and wine, and the rain and the dew never fail it; it is good for goats and good for cattle, there is timber of every kind, and the watering-places never run dry all year. And so, stranger, the name of Ithaca has traveled even as far as Troy — which they say lies a long way off from this land of the Greeks.”

So she spoke, and long-suffering Odysseus was glad — glad to stand again in the land of his fathers, now that Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus, had named it. But even now he did not tell the truth. He held the word back on his lips and spun, out of the deep cunning that was always working in him, a false tale.

“I heard of Ithaca,” he said, “even over in Crete, far across the sea — and now here I am, with all this treasure. I left as much again behind for my children, but I am running for my life. I killed a man: Orsilochus, son of Idomeneus, the fastest runner in all of broad Crete. He tried to rob me of the spoils I had carried home from Troy, the spoils I had suffered so much for, cutting my way through the wars of men and the bitter waves — all because, he said, I would not serve his father at Troy as his man, but led a company of my own. So I lay in wait for him by the road with one comrade, and as he came in from the fields at nightfall I ran him through with my bronze spear. The night was black; no one saw it; no one ever knew I had taken his life. Once he was dead I went down to a ship and begged some Phoenicians — I paid them well from my plunder — to take me aboard and set me down at Pylos, or in Elis where the Epeans rule. But the wind blew them off their course, hard, however they fought it — they had no wish to cheat me — and brought us here in the night. We rowed in any way we could, and though we were starving not a man of us thought of supper; we just stumbled ashore and lay down where we fell. I was worn out, and a sweet sleep took me at once. They lifted my goods from the ship and set them beside me here on the sand, and then they sailed away for Sidon — and left me here, sick at heart.”

So he spoke, and gray-eyed Athena smiled. She stroked him with her hand, and changed before his eyes into a woman, tall and beautiful, skilled in the loveliest work, and her words came warm and laughing.

“He would have to be a thief and a sharp dealer indeed,” she said, “who could get past you in any kind of trickery — even a god, if a god took you on. You incorrigible man — endlessly clever, never tired of scheming. Even here, on your own soil, you will not lay down the lies and the cunning stories you love from the bottom of your heart. Enough — let it go. We are two of a kind, you and I, both old hands at this. Of all men alive you are far the best at laying plans and spinning words, and I am famous among all the gods for my wits and my craft. And still you did not know me — Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus — I, who stand beside you and guard you through every trouble, and who made all the Phaeacians love you so. I have come now to weave a plan with you, and to help you hide the treasure that the Phaeacians, by my design, sent you home with, and to tell you all the trials you must bear inside your own house. Endure them. You have no choice. And tell no one, man or woman, that you are home from your wandering. Bear every insult in silence, and let men do their worst.”

Odysseus, the man of many turns, answered her: “It is hard, goddess, for any mortal who meets you to know you, however much he knows of the world — you take so many shapes. But this I know well: you were good to me once, in the years we Greeks fought before Troy. Yet from the day we sacked Priam’s high city and went to our ships, and a god scattered the fleet of the Greeks — from that day, Athena, I never saw you, never once felt you come aboard my ship to ward off my grief. I wandered on with a broken heart until the gods set me free at last and I reached the rich land of the Phaeacians, where you put courage in me and led me into their town. Now, by your own father’s name, I beg you, tell me the truth — for I cannot believe I am really home in Ithaca. This is some other country, and you are toying with me, telling me what you do not mean, to make a fool of me. Tell me honestly: have I truly come home to my own land?”

Gray-eyed Athena answered: “That is always the turn your mind takes — and that is exactly why I cannot abandon you in your troubles. You are quick, and shrewd, and steady-minded. Any other man, home at last from such long wandering, would have rushed straight to his house to see his wife and children. But not you — you would rather hold back and test them all first, and put your own wife to the proof, your wife who sits in your halls, wasting away, weeping out the long days and nights. As for my not coming to you — I was never in doubt; I always knew in my heart you would come home, though you would lose every one of your men along the way. But I did not want to set myself against Poseidon, my father’s brother, who nursed his anger against you for blinding his own son. Come, then — I will show you the shape of the land, and you will believe me. This is the harbor of Phorcys, the old man of the sea; here at its head is the long-leaved olive, and close by, the shadowy, lovely cave sacred to the nymphs men call the Naiads — the same vaulted cave where you burned many a fine offering to them. And there, above, is the wooded mountain of Neriton.”

As she spoke the goddess scattered the mist, and the land stood clear. Then long-suffering Odysseus rejoiced, glad of his own country, and bent and kissed the grain-giving earth. At once he lifted his hands and prayed to the nymphs: “Naiad nymphs, daughters of Zeus — I never thought to see you again. Now I greet you with all my love, and I will bring you gifts as I did in the old days, if Zeus’s daughter, who drives the spoils of war, will be kind and let me live, and bring my dear son up to be a man.”

“Take heart,” said gray-eyed Athena. “Do not let that trouble you now. What matters first is to stow your treasure deep in the sacred cave, where it will be safe; then we will plan how all the rest may go best.”

With that the goddess went down into the shadowy cave to find the safest hiding-places, while Odysseus carried it all in — the gold, the bronze, the well-made clothing the Phaeacians had given him. They stowed it carefully away, and Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, set a stone across the mouth. Then the two of them sat down at the foot of the sacred olive and laid their plans for the death of the overweening suitors.

Gray-eyed Athena spoke first: “Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes — think now how you will lay hands on these shameless men who have lorded it over your house three years, courting your faithful wife, pressing their gifts on her, while she longs only for you and weeps. She gives each of them hope, and sends each man his promise, but her heart is set on other things.”

Odysseus, the man of many turns, answered her: “To think of it — I would have died in my own house the wretched death of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, if you had not told me all of this in time, goddess. Come then, weave the plan that will pay them back. Stand at my side and breathe the same fierce courage into me as on the day we tore down the bright crown of Troy’s towers. Be with me now as you were then, gray-eyed one, and I would fight three hundred men at once — with you beside me, lady, and your help.”

“I will be with you,” said gray-eyed Athena. “I will not lose sight of you once we set our hands to this work — and I think more than one of these men who are eating up your living will spatter the wide floor with his blood and brains. But first I will so disguise you that no one will know you. I will shrivel the smooth skin on your supple limbs, strip the fair hair from your head, and dress you in rags that would make any man who saw you turn away. I will dim those fine eyes of yours, until you seem a wretched thing in the sight of all the suitors, and of your wife, and of the son you left at home. Then go first to the swineherd, the man who keeps your pigs. He has always been loyal to you, and he loves your son and your steady Penelope. You will find him out by the pigs, where they feed by Raven Rock and the spring of Arethusa, fattening on acorns and dark water, the things that put good fat on a pig. Stay with him there. Sit, and ask, and learn how everything stands, while I go to Sparta, to the land of lovely women, to call your son Telemachus home — for he went off to Lacedaemon, to Menelaus, to ask after you, to learn whether you were still alive.”

Odysseus, the man of many turns, answered her: “Then why did you not tell him, when your own mind knew it all? Was it so that he too should suffer, wandering the barren sea, while strangers devour his living?”

Gray-eyed Athena answered: “Do not trouble yourself too much about him. I sent him myself, so that he would win a good name for the going. He is in no hardship; he sits at ease in the house of Menelaus, with plenty of everything around him. True, there are young men lying in wait for him in a black ship, meaning to kill him before he reaches home — but I do not think they will. The earth will close over more than one of those who are eating up your living, before that happens.”

As she finished, Athena touched him with her wand. She shriveled the smooth skin on his supple limbs and stripped the fair hair from his head; she wrapped the skin of an old man over his whole body, and dimmed his two fine eyes. She threw a poor cloak about him and a ragged tunic, filthy and grimed with smoke, and over it the great worn hide of a swift deer; and she gave him a staff, and a battered beggar’s bag full of holes, slung from a twisted cord. So the two of them laid their plans, and then they parted — and the goddess went off to bright Lacedaemon to fetch the son of Odysseus home.
