# The Odyssey, Book II

Telemachus Calls the Assembly

When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, Telemachus rose from his bed and dressed. He bound the fine sandals on his feet, slung his sword across his shoulder, and walked out of his room looking like a god. At once he sent the heralds out with their clear-voiced call, to summon the long-haired Greeks to assembly. They made the call, the men gathered, and when they were all together Telemachus went to the meeting place, spear in hand — not alone, for two swift hounds went trotting at his heels. And Athena poured such grace over him that the whole crowd marveled as he passed; and when he took his seat, his father’s seat, even the gray old elders drew back and made room.

Aegyptius spoke first — a man bent double with age, who had seen everything in his long life. One of his sons, Antiphus, had sailed with Odysseus to Troy, the land of fine horses, and the savage Cyclops had killed him in the cave, made his last grim meal of him. He had three sons still living: two who worked the family land, and a third, Eurynomus, who was one of the suitors. But the father never got over Antiphus, and he was weeping for him still as he began to speak.

“Men of Ithaca, hear me. Not once since the day Odysseus sailed away in his hollow ships have we held a council or an assembly. So who has called us together now? What young man, or what old one, felt the need so badly? Has someone caught word of an army marching on us, and wants to give us warning? Or is there some other public matter to lay before us? Whoever he is, I think him a good man, and I hope Zeus grants him whatever his heart desires.”

Telemachus took the old man’s words for a good sign, and could sit still no longer — he was bursting to speak. He rose in the middle of the gathering, and the herald Peisenor, a wise man, put the speaker’s staff into his hand. Then he turned to Aegyptius first: “Old sir, that man is not far off — you’ll soon see for yourself. It was I who called you here, for the grief that falls hardest falls on me. I’ve caught no word of any army marching on us, nothing to warn you of, and I have no public business to lay before the council. My trouble is my own — two evils that have come down on my house. The first is that I have lost my noble father, who was once king over all of you here, and who was gentle to every one of you, like a father. The second is worse, and it is fast ruining everything I have. The sons of the leading men among you are courting my mother against her will — and they shrink from going to her father Icarius, and let him weigh the matter, set the bride-price, and give his daughter to the man he favors. No: day after day they camp in my house, slaughtering my oxen and sheep and fat goats, feasting, swilling down my bright wine with never a thought for the cost. There is no fortune on earth that can stand it. We have no Odysseus now to drive this ruin from our doors, and I am not the man to do it myself. I’ll never be his equal, hard as I might try — but I would defend myself if I had the strength, for what is being done here is past all bearing. My house is being shamed, eaten down to nothing.

“Feel the outrage of it yourselves, then, and be ashamed before the people who live around us. And fear the anger of the gods, lest they turn in fury at these wrongs. I beg you, by Zeus and by Themis, who breaks up the assemblies of men and calls them to order — hold back, my friends, and leave me to my grief alone! Unless, that is, my brave father Odysseus once did some harm to the Greeks, and you are paying it back on me, by setting these men on. As for me — I’d rather you devoured my stores and my cattle yourselves. If you ate them, there might one day be a reckoning: I’d go through the town pressing my claim, demanding payment, until I got back everything I’d lost. But as it stands, there is no remedy, and you have heaped grief on me past all cure.”

So he spoke, in a rush of anger, and he dashed the staff to the ground and burst into tears. Pity ran through the whole assembly. The others all sat silent — not one had the heart to answer Telemachus with a hard word — all but Antinous, who spoke out: “Telemachus, you young hothead, what a speech! Trying to shame us, to pin the blame on us! It is not the suitors who are at fault — it’s your own mother, who is cunning beyond all women. Three years now, and the fourth wearing on, she has been leading us by the nose. She gives every one of us hope, sends each man messages and promises — and means not a word of any of it. Her heart is set on something else entirely.

“And here’s the trick she played on us. She set up a great loom in her hall and began to weave — a fine, wide web, enormous work — and she said to us: ‘Young men, my suitors, since noble Odysseus is dead, you are eager for the wedding, I know; but wait, be patient, until I finish this cloth — I don’t want all my spinning to go to waste — a shroud for the hero Laertes, against the day that death’s long grief comes and takes him. The women of Ithaca would never forgive me if a man who won so much should lie in his grave without a shroud.’ That is what she said, and our proud hearts gave way. So by day she would weave at the great web, and every night, by torchlight, she would unravel what she’d done. For three years the trick held, and we never saw through it. But when the fourth year came around with the turning seasons, one of her women — a girl who knew the whole of it — told us, and we caught her in the act of pulling apart that gleaming cloth. So she had to finish it, whether she wanted to or not.

“So here is the suitors’ answer to you — and hear it plainly, you and all the Greeks: send your mother away, and make her marry whichever man her father picks, and she agrees to. But if she means to go on grinding down the sons of the Greeks much longer, trading on the gifts Athena gave her — her rare skill, her cleverness, her scheming mind, the like of which we have never heard, not even in the famous women of the old days, in Tyro or Alcmene or fair-crowned Mycenae, none of whom had a mind to match Penelope’s — well, this once her scheming will not serve her. As long as she keeps the mind the gods have put in her now, just so long will we keep eating down your house and your living. She wins herself great fame by it; you are the one who pays. And we will not go back to our own lands, here or anywhere else, until she chooses one of us and marries him.”

And thoughtful Telemachus answered him: “Antinous, how can I drive the mother who bore me, who raised me, out of this house, against her will? My father is far away — alive or dead, I do not know. And it would go hard with me to pay back Icarius the great bride-price I’d owe him if I sent his daughter home of my own accord. Her father would deal harshly with me, and the gods would deal worse — for my mother, leaving this house, would call down the Furies on me. The world would turn against me. No — I will never give that order. If your own consciences are stung by this, then leave my house. Feast somewhere else, work through your own provisions, taking turns from house to house. But if you’d sooner keep grinding down one man’s living and pay nothing for it — then strip it bare. I will cry out to the everlasting gods, and pray that Zeus pays you back for what you’ve done. Then you’ll die inside these very halls, and no one left to avenge you.”

So spoke Telemachus, and Zeus who sees far sent down two eagles, flying from a high mountain peak. For a while they rode the wind, gliding wing to wing, holding their lordly course. But when they came right over the loud assembly, they wheeled, beating their thick wings, and looked down on the heads of the men below with death in their eyes. Then they tore at each other with their talons — clawing each other’s cheeks and necks — and swept off to the right, over the houses of the town. The men stared in wonder at the birds, and asked one another what it could mean. And the old hero Halitherses, son of Mastor, spoke among them — for no man of his time came close to him at reading birds and telling the things that fate had fixed. With good will he spoke out, and said:

“Hear me now, men of Ithaca — and I speak above all to the suitors, for it is on them this disaster rolls. Odysseus will not be far from his own people much longer. Even now he is close, sowing death and doom for these men — and not for them alone; many another of us who live in Ithaca will suffer for it. So let us think, while there’s still time, how to put a stop to all this — or let the suitors stop it themselves, which would be far better for them. I am no untried prophet; I know what I am saying. Everything I foretold for that man has come to pass — when the Greeks set sail for Troy, and crafty Odysseus with them, I said he would suffer terribly, lose every one of his companions, and come home in the twentieth year, unknown to all. And now, look — it is all coming true.”

Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, rounded on him: “Off home with you, old man, and prophesy to your own children — or it may go badly for them. When it comes to omens, I can read them far better than you. Plenty of birds wheel about under the sun, and most of them mean nothing at all. Odysseus is dead, far away — and I only wish you had died with him, instead of standing here croaking out your prophecies and stoking the boy’s anger, which burns hot enough already. No doubt you’re hoping he’ll hand you some gift for your household. Well, I’ll tell you, and it will come to pass: if you, an old man who ought to know better, talk this young one into something rash, the first to suffer for it will be Telemachus himself — he’ll get nothing out of it, with all of us against him. And on you, old man, we’ll lay a fine so heavy you’ll groan to pay it; the loss will sit hard on you. As for Telemachus, here in front of you all, this is my counsel to him: tell your mother to go back to her father’s house. They’ll make the wedding there and lay out all the gifts a beloved daughter deserves. Before that, I do not think the sons of the Greeks will give up their wearisome suit — for we fear no one, least of all Telemachus, however long his speeches run, and we care nothing for your fortune-telling, old man. It comes to nothing and only makes us hate you more. We’ll go on devouring his living, and pay back not a thing, so long as Penelope keeps the Greeks dangling over this marriage. Day after day we wait, contending for a prize so rare, and never go courting the other women each of us might fittingly wed.”

Then thoughtful Telemachus answered him: “Eurymachus — and the rest of you proud suitors — I’ll plead with you no more; I have said all I have to say. The gods know how things stand now, and so do all the Greeks. Give me one thing only: a fast ship and a crew of twenty men, to carry me there and back. I’m going to Sparta and to sandy Pylos, to ask after my father, gone so long. Someone may tell me something, or I may catch a word from Zeus, the kind that so often carries news to men. If I hear my father is alive and on his way home, then I’ll hold out one more year, hard as it is. But if I hear he is dead, gone from this world, then I’ll come home, heap up a mound for him, give him the full funeral honors he deserves, and hand my mother to a new husband.”

With that he sat down. Then Mentor rose to speak — an old friend of faultless Odysseus, the man to whom Odysseus, sailing away, had handed his whole household, charging them all to obey the old man and keep everything safe. With good will he spoke out among them: “Hear me now, men of Ithaca. I hope no sceptered king is ever again kind, or gentle, or fair-minded — I hope every one of them, from now on, is cruel and crooked in his dealings. For not a single man here remembers godlike Odysseus, who ruled over you and was as gentle as a father. I do not blame the suitors so much, for their violence at least is out in the open: they are gambling with their own heads, betting that Odysseus will never come back, and so they eat up his house by force. It is the rest of you I cannot stomach — sitting there in silence, never once speaking out to stop them, when you are many and they are few.”

Then Leocritus, son of Euenor, answered him: “Mentor, you reckless fool, what are you saying — setting the people on us to hold us back? It is a hard fight, against odds like these, to come between men and their dinner. Why, even if Odysseus of Ithaca came home himself and found us proud suitors feasting in his hall, and his heart burned to drive us out of it — even so his wife, for all her longing to see him, would take no joy in his return: he’d meet an ugly death there, taking on so many men at once. You’re talking nonsense. Come now — break this up. Everyone back to his own work. And let Mentor and Halitherses, old friends of his father, speed this boy on his journey — if he goes at all. But my guess is he’ll sit right here in Ithaca, waiting for news to come to him, and never make the voyage.”

On that he broke up the assembly, and the people scattered, each to his own house, while the suitors went back to the house of godlike Odysseus.

But Telemachus walked off alone, down to the shore. He washed his hands in the gray seawater and prayed to Athena: “Hear me, you god who came to my house yesterday and told me to take a ship across the misty sea and search out word of my father, gone so long. I want to obey — but the Greeks are blocking my way, and the suitors most of all, in their cruelty and pride.”

So he prayed, and Athena came up close, taking the shape and the voice of Mentor, and her words flew straight to him: “Telemachus, you’ll be no fool and no coward from this day on, if even a little of your father’s spirit runs in you — and what a man he was at seeing through whatever he said or set his hand to. This voyage of yours will not come to nothing. But if you were not truly his son, born of Penelope, then I’d have no hope you would ever bring it off. Few sons turn out the equals of their fathers; most fall short, and only a handful surpass them. But since you’ll be neither fool nor coward from now on, and Odysseus’ own wisdom is not lost in you, I have good hope you’ll see this through. So put the suitors and their scheming out of your mind — there is no sense or justice in them, and they cannot see the death and the dark fate standing close, ready to fall on them all in a single day. The voyage you long for will not be long delayed. I was such a friend to your father that I myself will find you a fast ship and sail with you. Go home now, and move among the suitors. Make your stores ready, pack everything in good vessels — the wine in jars, the barley meal, the marrow of men’s strength, in stout leather bags. I’ll go through the town and gather a willing crew. There are many ships in sea-girt Ithaca, old and new; I’ll look them over and choose you the best. We’ll fit her out fast and put to sea.”

So spoke Athena, daughter of Zeus, and Telemachus did not linger once he had heard the goddess. He went home with a heavy heart and found the proud suitors there in his courtyard, skinning goats and singeing pigs over the fire. Antinous came straight up to him, laughing, and caught his hand in his own: “Telemachus, you fiery young speechmaker — let it go, all of it, the hard words and the hard feelings. Eat and drink with us, the way you used to. The Greeks will see to everything for you — a ship and a picked crew — so you can sail to holy Pylos and get news of your noble father.”

And thoughtful Telemachus drew his hand away and answered: “Antinous, there is no eating in peace with men like you, no taking any pleasure in it. Wasn’t it enough that you wasted so much of what was mine, all the good things, while I was still a boy? Now I am grown, and I’ve learned the truth by listening to others, and the spirit has risen in me. I’ll do all I can to bring ruin down on you — whether I go to Pylos or stay right here among my own people. I am going, and the voyage I speak of will not be in vain — though I go as a passenger, with no ship and no crew of my own, since that is how you suitors thought best to leave me.”

So he spoke, and snatched his hand from Antinous’ grip. All through the house the suitors went on with their feast, and they mocked him as they worked, throwing taunts. One brash young man would say: “There’s no doubt Telemachus is plotting our murder. He’ll bring back fighting men from sandy Pylos, or from Sparta — he’s set his heart on it. Or maybe he means to sail to Ephyra, that rich land, and come home with deadly poison to drop in the wine-bowl and finish us all.” And another would say: “Who knows — once he’s off on his hollow ship, he may wander far from his friends and die, just like his father. Then we’d have all the more to do, splitting his goods among us — though we’d leave the house itself to his mother and the man who marries her.”

So they talked. But Telemachus went down to his father’s storeroom, a high, broad chamber where gold and bronze lay heaped, and chests of clothing, and a great store of fragrant olive oil. There stood jars of old wine, sweet and unmixed, a drink fit for a god, ranged in order along the wall, waiting for the day long-suffering Odysseus might come home at last. The room was shut with close-fitted double doors, and night and day the housekeeper kept watch over it: Eurycleia, daughter of Ops, son of Peisenor — careful and wise in all things. Telemachus called her into the storeroom and said: “Nurse, draw me off some wine in jars — sweet wine, the best after the vintage you’re keeping for my poor father, in case that man, escaping death and doom, ever comes home. Fill me twelve jars and seal them all with lids. And pour out barley meal into stitched leather bags — twenty measures of ground grain. Tell no one. Have it all put together; I’ll come for it this evening, once my mother has gone up to her room to sleep. I am going to Sparta and to sandy Pylos, to see what I can learn of my dear father’s return.”

When she heard him, the dear old nurse Eurycleia cried out, and the tears came, and she spoke from the heart: “Dear child, how could such a thing get into your head? Where would you go, across the whole wide world — you, an only son, and so beloved? Noble Odysseus is dead and gone, far from his country, somewhere among strangers. And the moment your back is turned, these men will be plotting against you, scheming to kill you by a trick and split everything you own among themselves. Stay here, with what is yours. There is no need for you to suffer, wandering over the barren sea.”

And thoughtful Telemachus answered her: “Take heart, nurse — there’s a god behind this plan. But swear to me you’ll say nothing of it to my mother until eleven or twelve days have passed, or until she misses me herself and hears that I’ve gone. I don’t want her spoiling her lovely face with tears.” So he spoke, and the old woman swore a great oath by the gods to keep silent. And when she had sworn it and the oath was done, she drew off the wine into the jars and poured the barley meal into the stout leather bags, while Telemachus went back inside to the suitors.

Then gray-eyed Athena thought of the next thing. Taking the form of Telemachus, she ranged through the town, going up to each man of the crew and telling them to gather at the fast ship by nightfall. And she asked Noemon, the fine son of Phronius, for a swift ship, and he gave it gladly. The sun went down and the streets grew dark. She hauled the ship to the water, stowed in her all the gear that well-benched ships carry, and brought her to rest at the harbor’s mouth. The good crew gathered around, and the goddess heartened every man.

Then gray-eyed Athena thought of one more thing. She went to the house of godlike Odysseus and poured sweet sleep over the suitors, fuddling them as they drank, knocking the cups from their hands. They could sit no longer over their wine; they shuffled off into the town to find their beds, their eyes already heavy with sleep. Then Athena, in the shape and voice of Mentor, called Telemachus out of his hall: “Telemachus, your men are aboard and at the oars, waiting for the word from you. Come, let’s not put off the voyage.”

With that, Pallas Athena led the way, swiftly, and Telemachus followed in the god’s footsteps. When they came down to the ship and the sea, they found the long-haired crew waiting on the shore, and Telemachus, in his strength, said to them: “This way, friends — let’s bring the stores aboard. They’re all set out together in the hall. My mother knows nothing of it, nor do the serving women — only one of them.” So he spoke, and led the way, and they came after. They carried it all down and stowed it in the well-benched ship, just as Odysseus’ dear son directed. Then Telemachus stepped aboard, with Athena going before him, and she took her seat in the stern, and he sat down beside her. The men loosed the cables, climbed aboard, and settled at the oars. And gray-eyed Athena sent them a fair wind, a fresh West Wind singing over the wine-dark sea. Telemachus called out to the crew to lay hold of the rigging, and they obeyed. They raised the fir mast, stepped it in its hollow socket, and made it fast with the forestays; then they hauled up the white sail with twisted oxhide ropes. The wind bellied out the sail, and the dark wave roared loud about the stem as the ship ran on, racing over the swell, holding her course. They made the tackle fast all through the swift black ship, and then they set out mixing-bowls brimful of wine and poured offerings to the deathless gods who live forever — but above all to the gray-eyed daughter of Zeus. And so, all night long and into the dawn, the ship cut on through the sea.
