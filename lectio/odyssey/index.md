# The Odyssey

Homer

- 800–701 BCE
- Western tradition
- Epic poem
- Lectio Edition 2026

Homer's poem of the long way home. Odysseus spends ten years getting back from Troy to his wife, his son and his island. Under the monsters and the gods it is about cunning, endurance and the pull of home.

## The story is easy. The 1900 file is not.

The free Odyssey is Samuel Butler's prose of 1900. Butler is readable, but he calls the hero Ulysses and the goddess Minerva, and he flattens the repeated lines that give the poem its sound.

The Lectio Edition tells all twenty-four books in current English. It keeps the Greek names and the fixed epithets, and where Homer repeats a line word for word, so does the English. Butler stays in the app beside it.

It was made to be read alongside the Lectio Iliad. The two share their names, their epithets and their spelling.

## How it begins.

> **Book I · Athena Visits Ithaca**

> Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turns — the one who wandered far and long after he sacked the holy city of Troy. He saw the cities of many peoples and learned how they thought; he suffered deep grief on the open sea, fighting for his own life and for the safe homecoming of his men. But he could not save them, hard as he tried — they died by their own reckless folly, those fools, when they devoured the cattle of Hyperion the Sun, who took from them the day of their return. Of these things, goddess, daughter of Zeus, begin where you will, and tell us too.

> By now everyone who had escaped a violent death — in the war or on the sea — was home. Only Odysseus was still kept from his wife and his own country, held back by the nymph Calypso, a queen among goddesses, who wanted him for a husband and kept him deep inside her hollow cave. And when at last, as the years rolled on, the time came that the gods had spun for him to return to Ithaca, his trials were still not over, not even among his own people. The gods pitied him now — all of them but Poseidon, who stayed furious with godlike Odysseus and would not let him reach his homeland.

The first 227 words of the Lectio Edition, as they stand in the bundle the app reads.

## What this edition decided.

The review swept all twenty-four books for each of these and corrected every departure it found.

- Gods and heroes keep their Greek names, in the familiar Latinized spelling: Odysseus, Athena, Telemachus, Eurycleia. The draft had mixed spellings such as Antinoos and Antinous. One form now holds.
- The army is "the Greeks", as in the Iliad.
- Fixed epithets have one form each: "winged words", "gray-eyed Athena", "Zeus who gathers the clouds", "earth-shaker".
- Lines Homer repeats word for word are rendered word for word. Penelope's shroud speech is the same in Books II, XIX and XXIV.
- Homeric idioms are kept where English can carry them and translated where it cannot.
- Modern idioms with the wrong sense came out: "make your play", "good luck to you", "last supper". So did leftover archaisms such as "trumpery" and "steading".

### Where it differs from the older translations

The edition records 72 divergences: places where it departs from the older English, each with its reason. Here are 5, with 1 correction from the review added. The full record is in the app, one tap from the text.

### πολύτροπος (*polytropos*)

Book I, line 1

**Older:** "that ingenious hero" (Butler)

**Lectio:** the man of many turns

The word means both much-traveled and quick to turn in thought. Older versions pick one sense. The phrase holds both.

### πολύτλας δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς

Throughout

**Older:** "the much-enduring noble Odysseus"

**Lectio:** long-suffering Odysseus

His standing epithet. Endurance is what defines him, and the reader should hear it each time Homer says it.

### Οὖτις (*Outis*)

Book IX, lines 366 to 414

**Older:** "Noman" (Butler, Pope)

**Lectio:** Nobody

Odysseus names himself Nobody, so that when the Cyclops shouts that Nobody is killing him, his neighbors go home. The name has to be the same word in the cave and in the cry, and it has to be ordinary English.

### ἐπάρουρος ἐὼν θητευέμεν ἄλλῳ

Book XI, lines 489 to 491

**Older:** "a paid servant in a poor man's house" (Butler)

**Lectio:** I would rather be a hired hand, working another man's land

Achilles among the dead takes back the creed of the Iliad. The Greek is exact: a hired field hand serving a man who has no land of his own. He is free, and he is at the bottom. "Servant" is too vague.

### τέτλαθι δή, κραδίη

Book XX, line 18

**Older:** "Heart, be still, you had worse than this to bear" (Butler)

**Lectio:** Endure, my heart. You have borne worse than this.

The verb is from the same root as his epithet. He is ordering himself to be what his name says he is.

### ἔπεα πτερόεντα

Throughout

**Older:** varied

**Lectio:** winged words

The draft gave this formula several ways. Homer gives it one way. Eleven places were corrected.

### Still open

What the first review left for the editor to decide or restore.

- The editor's own read of all twenty-four books.
- Seven lines about Helen in Penelope's speech (Book XXIII) were missing and have been restored. Ancient critics doubted these lines. The edition has no note saying so yet.
- Two translator's notes that had been printed as Homer's text, in Books V and XIV, are set aside. The first belongs in the margin notes. The second made a false claim and must be reworded before it goes anywhere.
- A passage in Book XVII repeats one in Book IV and is still worded differently. Matching them is optional.
