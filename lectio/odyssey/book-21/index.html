The Odyssey, Book XXI

The Contest of the Bow

Book XXI21 of 24

Now Athena put it into the mind of wise Penelope to set the suitors a contest with the bow and the gray iron axes, there in the halls of Odysseus — the beginning of their slaughter. She climbed the high stair of the house and took in her strong hand the storeroom key, a fine thing of bronze with an ivory handle. Then she went, with her maids behind her, to the inmost storeroom at the back of the house, where her husband’s treasures lay: gold, and bronze, and wrought iron. And there too lay the springing bow, and the quiver full of arrows, every one of them heavy with grief to come.

He had been given that bow long ago, in Lacedaemon, by Iphitus son of Eurytus, a man like the gods. The two of them had fallen in together at Messene, in the house of Ortilochus. Odysseus had come there to collect a debt owed him by the whole people, for the Messenians had carried off three hundred sheep from Ithaca in their ships, shepherds and all. He was still a young man then; his father and the other elders had sent him on the long journey to get them back. Iphitus had come for his own loss — twelve brood mares, and the sturdy mule-foals running with them. Those mares would be the death of him in the end. For when he went to the house of Heracles, that stubborn-hearted son of Zeus who did such mighty deeds, Heracles killed him under his own roof — killed a guest, with no fear of the gods’ eyes upon him and no shame for the table he had set before the man — and kept the mares for himself. It was while Iphitus was hunting for those mares that he met Odysseus, and gave him the great bow that mighty Eurytus had carried in his day and left to his son when he died. Odysseus gave him in return a sharp sword and a strong spear, the first tokens of a friendship between guest and host — though they never came to sit at each other’s tables, for Heracles killed Iphitus first. That bow Odysseus never took to Troy. He had kept it at home, a keepsake of a friend he honored, and there it stayed while he sailed to war.

Penelope came to the storeroom and stepped up onto the oak threshold, which a carpenter had once planed smooth and true to the line, and fitted with doorposts and hung with shining doors. Quickly she slipped the strap from the door-handle, drove the key home, and shot back the bolts. The doors flew open with a groan like a bull bellowing in a meadow — so the great bolts gave way under the key. She climbed onto the high platform where the chests stood, holding sweet-smelling clothes, and reached up and lifted the bow down from its peg, still in the bright case that sheathed it. Then she sat with it across her knees and wept aloud, and drew her husband’s bow out of the case. And when she had had her fill of tears and grieving, she went back to the hall, to the proud suitors, carrying the springing bow and the quiver with its many arrows of grief. Her maids came after her, bearing a chest packed with the iron and bronze her husband had won as prizes. When she reached the suitors, that lady among women drew her bright veil before her face and took her stand by a pillar that held up the roof, a faithful maid on either side of her. And she spoke out to the suitors:

“Hear me, you proud suitors. You have fallen on this house and eaten and drunk in it without end, all the long while its master has been gone, and the only excuse you can put forward is that you want to marry me. Well — here is the prize, and here it is set before you. I bring out the great bow of godlike Odysseus. Whoever among you strings it most easily, in his bare hands, and shoots an arrow clean through all twelve axes — him I will follow. I will leave this house that took me as a bride, this fine house, so full of the goods of living, that I think I will remember even in my dreams.”

So she spoke, and told Eumaeus, the loyal swineherd, to set the bow and the gray iron before the suitors. And Eumaeus wept as he took them and laid them down. Close by, the herdsman wept too at the sight of his master’s bow. But Antinous turned on them both and snapped: “You fool farmhands — no thought in your heads beyond the day in front of you. Why are you blubbering, you two, working up grief in your mistress, when her heart is heavy enough already with a husband lost? Sit and eat in silence, or take your crying outside and leave the bow here for us — the suitors — to settle. No light test, this, the stringing of such a bow. There is not a man in this whole company the equal of Odysseus. I saw him myself, I remember him, though I was only a child.”

So he said. But all the while his own heart was sure he would string the bow and send the arrow through the iron — he, who was to be the first to taste an arrow from the hands of Odysseus, the man he was dishonoring in his own hall, and stirring up the rest to dishonor too.

Then Telemachus spoke out, in his clear young voice. “Well now — Zeus himself must have stripped me of my wits. Here is my own dear mother, sensible as she is, saying she will go off after another man and leave this house, and I stand here laughing, light-hearted, like a fool. Come then, suitors. The prize is set before you, a woman with no equal anywhere — not in all the land of the Greeks, not in sacred Pylos, not in Argos, nor Mycenae, nor here in Ithaca, nor on the dark mainland. You know it as well as I do; what need is there for me to praise my own mother? Come, no more excuses, no more putting it off. String the bow, and let us see. I will try the bow myself, for that matter. If I can string it and shoot through the iron, then it will not break my heart to watch my mother leave this house with a stranger — not when I am left behind, man enough to take up the contests my father won.”

With that he sprang up, threw off his crimson cloak, and lifted the sword from his shoulders. First he set the axes up, all twelve, digging one long trench for them and lining them straight to a measured cord, then stamping the earth firm around them. Everyone watching was amazed to see how neatly he set them in their row, though he had never once seen it done. Then he went and stood on the threshold and put the bow to the test. Three times he strained at it, hauling with all his strength, hungry to bend it; and three times he had to let it go, though in his heart he had not given up hope of stringing it and shooting through the iron. And the fourth time, pulling hard, he would have strung it — but Odysseus caught his eye and stopped him, for all his eagerness. So the boy spoke out among them again:

“It seems I am to be a weakling all my days, and good for nothing. Or else I am still too young, with no strength yet in my arms to fend off a man who picks a fight with me. Come then, you others, stronger than I am — try the bow, and let us see this contest through.” And he set the bow down on the floor, leaning it against the smooth, close-fitted doors, propped the swift arrow against the bow’s fine tip, and sat back down in the seat he had risen from.

Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, said to them: “Stand up and come forward, all of you, one after another, from left to right — starting from the place where the wine is poured.” So said Antinous, and his words pleased them. The first to rise was Leiodes, son of Oenops. He was their priest at the sacrifices, and always sat in the inmost corner, by the fine mixing-bowl; and he was the only one of them who hated their reckless ways, and was sickened by the rest. Now he was the first to take up the bow and the swift arrow. He went and stood on the threshold and tried the bow — and could not bend it. Before he could, his soft, unworked hands grew tired on the string. And he said to the suitors: “My friends, I cannot string it. Let another man take it. This bow is going to take the life and the spirit out of many a great man here — and better to die than to go on living and miss the very thing we have all gathered and waited so long for. There is a man among us even now, hoping in his heart, longing to marry Penelope. But once he has handled this bow and seen what it is, let him go court some other fine Greek woman with his gifts, and let Penelope marry the man who brings the most, the one it is fated she will wed.”

So he spoke, and set the bow down, leaning it against the smooth doors, the swift arrow propped against its fine tip, and sat back in his seat. But Antinous rounded on him: “Leiodes — what is this that has slipped past the wall of your teeth? Hard words, ugly words; it makes me angry to hear them. This bow is going to take the spirit out of great men, is it — just because you cannot string it? No, your mother never bore you to be a bowman. But there are others here who will string it soon enough.”

Then he called out to Melanthius the goatherd: “Quick now, Melanthius — light a fire in the hall, set a big bench beside it and a fleece over the bench, and bring out a great cake of fat from the stores inside. We young men will warm the bow and grease it well, and then try it again, and bring the contest to its end.” So Melanthius lit the tireless fire, drew up a bench and threw a fleece across it, and brought out a great cake of fat from within. The young men warmed the bow and tried it — and not one of them was strong enough; they fell far short of stringing it. But Antinous still held back, and so did Eurymachus, godlike Eurymachus, the two ringleaders, far the best of them all.

Now the swineherd and the cowherd went out of the house together, and behind them went godlike Odysseus himself. When they were past the gates and out in the courtyard, he spoke to them gently, drawing them close: “Cowherd — and you, swineherd — there is something I could say to you. Or should I keep it to myself? No, my heart tells me to speak. What sort of men would you be, if it came to standing by Odysseus — if some god brought him home, suddenly, out of nowhere? Would you fight for the suitors, or for Odysseus? Tell me, just as your heart and spirit move you.”

And Philoetius, the loyal cowherd, answered: “Father Zeus — if only you would grant it, that the man come home, and some god guide him here. Then you would see what these hands of mine can do.” And Eumaeus prayed the same to all the gods, that wise Odysseus might come home to his own. So when Odysseus knew their minds for certain, he said to them: “He is here — the man himself. It is I. After twenty years of suffering I have come home to the land of my fathers. And I see that of all my servants, you two alone have longed for my return — for I have heard no other praying that I might come back. So I will tell you the truth, the way it will be. If a god strikes down these proud suitors under my hand, I will find wives for you both, and give you each goods of your own and a house built close to mine, and from that day you will be to me brothers and companions of Telemachus. And here — let me show you a clear sign, a thing you cannot doubt, so you will know me in your hearts. This scar — the wound a boar gave me with its white tusk, the day I hunted on Mount Parnassus with the sons of Autolycus.”

And with that he drew his rags aside from the great scar. The two of them looked, and knew it, and broke into weeping, and threw their arms around wise Odysseus, and kissed his head and his shoulders in welcome; and Odysseus kissed their heads and their hands. The sun would have gone down on their weeping, had he not checked them and said: “Enough crying now, or someone may come out from the hall and see us and carry word inside. Go back in, but one at a time, not together — I first, you after. And here is the sign between us. The suitors, every one of them, will refuse to let the bow and quiver be put into my hands. But you, good Eumaeus, carry the bow through the hall all the same and place it in my hands; and tell the women to bolt the close-fitting doors of their quarters. If any of them hears groaning, or the crash of men fighting through the house, she is not to come out — she is to stay where she is, in silence, at her work. And you, Philoetius — your task is the gate of the courtyard. Lock it, bar it fast, and tie it shut on the instant.”

So speaking he went back into the well-built house and sat down again in the seat he had left. And his two servants came in behind him, one after the other.

By now the bow was in the hands of Eurymachus, who was turning it this way and that in the heat of the fire to warm it. Even so he could not string it, and his proud heart groaned within him. He heaved a deep sigh and said: “This is bitter — bitter for me, and for us all. I do not grieve so much for the marriage, hard as that is — there are other Greek women enough, here in sea-girt Ithaca and in the other towns. What cuts me is this: that we should fall so far short of godlike Odysseus in strength that we cannot even string his bow. The shame of it will follow us, for men not yet born to hear of.”

But Antinous answered him: “Not so, Eurymachus — and you know it yourself. Today is a feast day of Apollo, holy across the land. Who would be bending a bow today? Set it down quietly. The axes can stand where they are; no one is going to walk into the house of Odysseus and carry them off. Let the wine-steward come round and pour for the offerings, and we will let the bow rest. In the morning we will have Melanthius bring in goats, the best of all the herds, and burn the thigh-pieces to Apollo the great archer — and then we will try the bow again, and bring the contest to its end.”

So said Antinous, and his words pleased them. The stewards poured water over their hands, and boys filled the bowls to the brim with wine and served it round to every man, the first drops poured out for the gods. And when they had made their offerings and drunk their fill, then crafty Odysseus, with a plan turning in him, spoke up: “Hear me, you suitors of the famous queen. I will say what the heart in me moves me to say — and most of all to Eurymachus and to Antinous, who spoke just now, and rightly. Lay the bow down for tonight; leave the matter to the gods, and in the morning the god will give the victory to whomever he chooses. But for now, hand me the polished bow, so that I may test my hands and my strength among you — to see whether the old power is still in these bent limbs, or whether the road and the rough years have worn it clean away.”

At that they were all furious, afraid he might actually string the polished bow. And Antinous turned on him and said: “You miserable stranger — there is not a wit left in you. Be content that you are allowed to eat in peace among your betters, with a fair share like the rest of us and nothing held back, and to sit and hear us talk — when no other beggar or stranger is let near our conversation. It is the wine that has run away with you, the honeyed wine that ruins any man who gulps it down past measure. Wine was the ruin of the centaur Eurytion, in the house of great-hearted Pirithous, the day he came among the Lapiths. When the wine had blinded his mind, he ran wild and did foul things under Pirithous’s roof. The heroes there sprang on him in their fury, dragged him to the porch, and sheared off his ears and his nose with the pitiless bronze; and off he went, raving, carrying his ruin in a darkened mind — and from that came the long war between men and centaurs. But he brought it down on himself, first of all, by drowning his wits in drink. So I tell you plainly: it will go hard with you if you string that bow. You will get no mercy from anyone in this hall. We will ship you straight off, in a black hull, to King Echetus, the mangler of all mankind, and there will be no saving you. Drink, then, and hold your peace, and pick no quarrels with men younger than yourself.”

Then wise Penelope said to him: “Antinous, it is neither right nor decent to abuse a guest of Telemachus, whoever comes to this house. Do you really suppose that, if this stranger trusts his hands and his strength and manages to string the great bow of Odysseus, he will take me home and make me his wife? Not even the man himself nurses such a hope in his heart. So let none of you eat his dinner here with that weighing on him. It does not bear thinking of.”

And Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered her: “Daughter of Icarius, wise Penelope — none of us thinks this man will carry you off. That would be unthinkable. What we shrink from is the talk of men and women, the chance that some lesser man among the Greeks will go about saying: ‘See — far weaker men than the hero are courting his wife, and not one of them could string his polished bow. Yet some drifting beggar came to the house and strung it easily, and shot clean through the iron.’ That is what they will say, and it will be our disgrace.”

But wise Penelope answered: “Eurymachus, there can be no good name among men for those who devour and dishonor the house of a great man. Why should you turn this into a disgrace? The stranger here is a big, well-made man, and he says his father was a man of standing. So give him the polished bow, and let us see. I tell you this, and it will be done: if he strings it, and Apollo grants him the glory, I will clothe him in a fine cloak and tunic, and give him a sharp javelin to keep off dogs and rough men, and a two-edged sword, and sandals for his feet, and send him on wherever his heart desires to go.”

Then Telemachus, with his good sense, said to her: “Mother, when it comes to the bow, no Greek has more right than I to give it or refuse it to whom I please — not one of all who rule in rocky Ithaca, or in the islands toward horse-grazing Elis. None of them will force my hand against my will, not even if I choose to give this stranger the bow outright, to carry away with him. So go to your room and see to your own work, the loom and the distaff, and keep the maids at their tasks. The bow is the men’s concern — and mine above all, for the rule of this house is mine.” And she went back in, astonished, taking her son’s strong words to heart. She climbed to her upper room with her women, and there she wept for Odysseus, her dear husband, until Athena cast sweet sleep over her eyelids.

Now the loyal swineherd had taken up the bow and was carrying it across, when the suitors all raised an uproar at him through the hall, and one of those arrogant young men cried out: “Where are you taking that bow, you wretched swineherd — out of your mind, are you? Soon the swift dogs you raised yourself will eat you out among your pigs, alone, with no man near, if Apollo and the other gods are kind to us.” At that Eumaeus took fright at the shouting that broke out all around him, and set the bow down then and there. But Telemachus shouted across at him, hard, from the other side: “Bring the bow on, old friend — you will be sorry soon if you listen to all of them. Young as I am, I will chase you back to the fields with a hail of stones, for I am the stronger. If only I were as far above all these suitors here in the house, in strength of hand, I would soon send a few of them packing, sick and sorry, out of our home — for the mischief they mean.”

So he spoke, and all the suitors laughed merrily at him, and let go of their hard temper toward Telemachus. The swineherd carried the bow on through the hall and put it in the hands of wise Odysseus. Then he called the nurse Eurycleia aside and said: “Eurycleia, Telemachus orders you to bolt the close-fitting doors of the women’s quarters. If any of them hears groaning, or the crash of men fighting inside, she is not to come out — she is to stay where she is, in silence, at her work.” So he spoke, and she answered not a word, but went and bolted the doors of the fair women’s hall.

And Philoetius slipped out without a word and locked the gates of the walled courtyard. There was a ship’s cable of papyrus fiber lying under the colonnade; with it he lashed the gates fast, then came back in and sat down again in the seat he had left, his eyes on Odysseus. And Odysseus by now had the bow in his hands and was turning it every way, testing it up and down, to see whether the worms had eaten into the horn while its master was far away. And one suitor would glance at the man beside him and say: “This one is a real connoisseur of bows — a cunning hand. No doubt he has one like it stored at home, or means to make one — the way the rascally old beggar turns it over in his hands.” And another of the arrogant young men would say: “May he have just as much luck in everything else as he is ever going to have in stringing that bow.”

So the suitors talked. But Odysseus, the man of many turns, lifted the great bow and looked it over end to end. And as a man skilled in the lyre and in song easily slips a new string onto its peg, looping the twisted sheep-gut fast at either end, so without effort Odysseus strung the great bow. Then he took it in his right hand and tried the string; and it sang sweetly under his touch, with a voice like a swallow’s. A sharp grief ran through the suitors, and the color drained from every face. And Zeus thundered loud, sending forth his sign. Long-suffering, godlike Odysseus rejoiced that the son of crooked-counseling Cronus had sent him an omen.

He took up one swift arrow that lay bare on the table beside him — the rest were still inside the hollow quiver, though the Greeks would taste those soon enough. He laid it against the bridge of the bow, drew back the string and the arrow’s notch, all the while sitting just as he was in his seat, and let fly, aiming true. He did not miss a single axe of all of them: through the first ring, and on through every one, the heavy bronze-tipped arrow drove its way clean out the other side. Then he turned to Telemachus and said: “Telemachus, the stranger sitting in your hall has not shamed you. I did not miss the mark, and I did not labor long to string the bow. My strength is still whole in me, whatever the suitors say to mock me. But now it is time to make the Greeks their supper, while the daylight lasts — and then the rest, the song and the lyre, that are the crown of any feast.” And as he spoke he gave the sign with his brows. Telemachus, the dear son of godlike Odysseus, slung his sharp sword over his shoulder, closed his hand on his spear, and took his stand beside his father’s seat, armed in the gleaming bronze.
