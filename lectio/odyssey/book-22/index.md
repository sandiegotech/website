# The Odyssey, Book XXII

The Killing of the Suitors

Then Odysseus tore the rags from his body and leapt to the great stone threshold, the bow in his hand and the quiver full of arrows at his side. He poured the arrows out at his feet and said, “That contest is finished. Now I will see whether I can hit a mark no man here has hit before — and whether Apollo will grant it to me.”

And with that he leveled a bitter arrow at Antinous. The man was just then reaching for a fine two-handled gold cup, lifting it to drink, with no thought of death in his head. Who, at a feast among so many, would imagine that one man, however hard, would stand alone against the whole crowd and bring him down? The arrow caught Antinous square in the throat, and the point drove clean through the soft of his neck. He pitched sideways. The cup dropped from his hand, and a thick jet of blood came spurting from his nostrils. His foot kicked out and overturned the table, and the bread and the roasted meat slid off and were fouled on the floor.

The suitors broke into an uproar when they saw the man go down. They sprang up from their seats in a panic, every one of them, and their eyes went searching along the walls — but there was no shield anywhere, no spear to lay a hand on. And they turned on Odysseus in their fury: “Stranger, you will pay for shooting men. You have killed the finest young man in Ithaca, and it is the last shot you will ever take. The vultures will have you.”

So they raged, for they still believed he had hit Antinous by accident. The fools did not understand that the noose had already drawn tight around every neck in the room. Odysseus looked at them with a black stare and said: “Dogs. You told yourselves I would never come home from Troy. So you ate my house bare, you forced my serving-women to your beds, and you courted my wife while I was still alive — with no fear of the gods who hold the wide sky, and no fear of the anger of men to come. Now the day of reckoning has caught up with the whole pack of you.”

The blood drained out of their faces as he spoke, and every man looked about him for a way to run. Only Eurymachus found his voice. “If you are truly Odysseus, then every word of it is just. The Greeks have done you great wrong, here in your halls and out on your lands. But the man who was the head and front of it all lies dead already — Antinous. He drove the whole thing. It was never the marriage he cared for, not really; he had another aim, one that Zeus has now denied him. He meant to kill your son and make himself the king of Ithaca. He has met the death he had coming. So spare the rest of us, your own people. We will make it all good. Every one of us will pay you back for what we ate and drank in your house — a fine worth twenty oxen apiece, in bronze and in gold, and we will keep on paying until your heart is satisfied. No one could blame you for your anger before that.”

Odysseus answered with the same black stare. “Eurymachus, not if you handed me every last thing you own — all you have now and all you could ever scrape together — would I hold back my hands from killing until the suitors have paid in full for everything they did. Now you have a choice in front of you. Stand and fight, or run for your lives. But I do not think a single one of you will get away.”

Their knees went weak where they stood. But Eurymachus spoke up again: “Friends, this man will not stop. He has the bow and the arrows, and he will plant himself on that threshold and shoot us down to the last man unless we stop him. So set your minds on fighting. Draw your swords. Hold the tables up against his arrows. Then rush him all together — drive him off the threshold and out the doorway, and break into the town and raise the alarm. He will not loose many more shots after that.”

And as he spoke he drew his sharp bronze sword, two edges to it, and threw himself at Odysseus with a terrible cry. In the same instant Odysseus let fly. The arrow took Eurymachus in the chest beside the nipple and buried itself in his liver. The sword fell from his hand. He doubled over the table and crashed down across it, spilling the food and the two-handled cup onto the floor, and he beat his forehead against the ground in his agony and thrashed the chair with his heels — until the dark closed over his eyes.

Then Amphinomus drew and charged straight at Odysseus, to try and force him from the door. But Telemachus was the quicker. He drove his spear into the man’s back from behind, between the shoulders, and the point came out through his chest. He went down hard and struck the floor full with his face. Telemachus sprang back at once, leaving the spear standing in the body — he was afraid that if he stooped to wrench it free, one of the Greeks might run in and cut him down or stab him while he was bent over. So he ran to his father’s side and said quickly: “Father, let me bring you a shield and a pair of spears and a bronze helmet for your head. I will arm myself as well, and carry armor out to the swineherd and the herdsman. We are better off in armor.”

“Run and fetch it,” Odysseus said, “while I still have arrows to hold them off. Once they are gone they will rush me and force me from the door, and I will be one man alone.”

Telemachus did as his father told him and went to the storeroom where the war-gear was kept. He took out four shields, eight spears, and four bronze helmets with horsehair crests, and carried them back at a run. He armed himself first, and then the herdsman and the swineherd put on the bronze and took their stand beside Odysseus. And Odysseus, as long as the arrows lasted, kept shooting the suitors down one after another, and they fell in heaps. When the last arrow was spent he stood the bow against the doorpost by the wall, slung a four-layered shield across his shoulders, set a strong helmet on his head — the horsehair crest nodding down over it — and took up two heavy bronze-tipped spears.

Now in the wall of the hall there was a small raised door, a vent, and at the end of the threshold a way out into a narrow passage, shut by a close-fitting door. Odysseus posted the noble swineherd to guard it, standing close by, since there was only one way in. But Agelaus called out to the others: “Can no one get up to that vent and shout to the town what is happening here? One alarm and help would come, and we would soon finish this man and his shooting.”

“That cannot be done, Agelaus,” said Melanthius the goatherd. “The mouth of the narrow passage is too close to the courtyard gate — one good man there could hold off an army. But I know what I can do. I will bring you arms from the storeroom. That is surely where Odysseus and his son have hidden them.”

And with that the goatherd Melanthius slipped up through the back passages of the house to the storeroom. There he chose twelve shields, twelve helmets, and twelve spears, and carried them back as fast as he could and handed them around to the suitors. The heart in Odysseus sank when he saw the men buckling on the armor and shaking their long spears. He understood at once how much the danger had grown, and he said to Telemachus, “One of the women in the house is helping them against us — or else it is Melanthius.”

“The fault is mine, father, mine alone,” Telemachus answered. “I left the storeroom door standing open, and they kept a sharper watch than I did. Go, Eumaeus, shut it fast — and find out whether it is one of the women doing this, or Melanthius son of Dolius, which is what I think.”

While they were speaking, Melanthius went up to the storeroom a second time, to fetch more armor. But the swineherd saw him and said to Odysseus at his side: “Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes, there he goes — that scoundrel Melanthius, the one we suspected, off to the storeroom again. Tell me plainly: if I get the better of him, shall I kill him, or shall I bring him here to you, to pay for all the wrongs he has done in your house?”

“Telemachus and I will hold the suitors here, however hard they press,” Odysseus said. “You two go and bind his hands and his feet behind his back, throw him into the storeroom, and make the door fast. Then run a rope around his body and haul him up a tall pillar to the rafters, and leave him there alive — to suffer a long time before he dies.”

They went and did exactly as he said. They reached the storeroom unheard, for Melanthius was deep inside it rummaging for arms, and they took their stand on either side of the door and waited. Out he came at last, a helmet in one hand and in the other an old shield, dry-rotted and gone soft, that Laertes had carried in his young days and laid aside long ago, the straps perished. The two of them seized him, dragged him back inside by the hair, and threw him down on the floor. They wrenched his hands and feet up behind his back and lashed them there, hard and cruel, as Odysseus had ordered, and they ran a rope about him and hauled him up the high pillar to the rafters. And you, Eumaeus, swineherd, stood over him and jeered: “Now you can keep watch through the whole night, Melanthius, stretched on the soft bed you have earned. You will not miss the dawn coming up from the streams of Ocean — your hour for driving in the goats to feast the suitors.”

So they left him there, racked in his bonds. They put their own armor back on, shut the storeroom door, and went back to take their stand beside Odysseus — the four of them now in the hall, full of fight and fury. And the suitors against them were still many, and still dangerous. Then Athena came, in the shape and voice of Mentor. Odysseus was glad to see her and called out, “Mentor, stand by me. Remember your old companion, and all the good I did you. We are the same age, you and I.” But all the while he knew well enough it was Athena, marshaler of armies.

From across the hall the suitors raised an outcry when they saw her, and Agelaus shouted his threats: “Mentor, do not let Odysseus talk you into siding with him against us. This is how it will go. When we have killed those two, father and son, we will kill you with them. You will pay for what you mean to do with your own head. And once you are dead we will take everything you have, indoors and out, and throw it in with the goods of Odysseus. We will not let your sons live on in your house, nor your daughters, nor will your wife walk the streets of Ithaca again.”

That only made Athena angrier, and she rounded on Odysseus with hard words — to test him: “Odysseus, where is the strength and the daring that was in you for nine long years at Troy, fighting over white-armed Helen? You killed your men by the score in those days, and it was your scheme that brought down the wide streets of Priam’s city. How is it that here, on your own ground, with your own house and your own goods to fight for, you flinch at standing up to these suitors? Come here beside me, friend, and watch. You will see how Mentor son of Alcimus repays a kindness in the thick of his enemies.”

But she would not yet hand him the victory outright. She wanted to go on proving the courage and the strength of Odysseus and his brave son. So she flew up to a crossbeam in the smoke-blackened roof and perched there, looking down — in the likeness of a swallow.

Now the suitors were led by Agelaus son of Damastor, and by Eurynomus, Amphimedon, Demoptolemus, Pisander son of Polyctor, and Polybus. These were the best of the men still on their feet and fighting for their lives; the rest had already gone down under the arrows. Agelaus called to them: “Friends, the man will have to give over soon. Mentor has flown off after all his big talk and left them standing there alone at the door. So do not throw all at once. Let six of you cast first, and pray that Zeus lets you bring Odysseus down. Once he falls, the others are nothing to fear.”

So they threw, the six of them, hard and eager. But Athena turned every spear aside. One struck the doorpost of the strong-built hall, one the close-fitting door, and another’s heavy bronze head buried itself in the wall. When the four had let the volley go by, Odysseus said to his men: “Friends — now it is our turn. Throw into the middle of them, before they add our deaths to all the rest of the harm they have done.”

They took aim straight ahead and threw. Odysseus killed Demoptolemus, Telemachus killed Euryades, the swineherd killed Elatus, and the herdsman Philoetius killed Pisander. The four of them bit the floor together, and as the rest fell back into a corner of the hall the four attackers rushed in and pulled their spears free from the bodies of the dead.

Then the suitors threw again, hard and eager — and again Athena turned most of it aside. One spear struck a pillar of the hall, one the close door, another’s bronze point lodged in the wall. Still, Amphimedon grazed Telemachus across the wrist, just skimming the skin, and Ctesippus reached over the swineherd’s shield to nick his shoulder, though the spear flew on and dropped to the floor. And the four threw back into the press of suitors. Odysseus struck Eurydamas, Telemachus struck Amphimedon, the swineherd struck Polybus. Then the herdsman Philoetius caught Ctesippus full in the chest, and called out over him: “Foul-mouthed son of Polytherses — no more of your big crude talk now. Leave the last word to the gods. They are far stronger than men. Take that as my gift in return for the ox-hoof you threw at Odysseus when he was begging through his own house.”

So spoke the herdsman of the cattle. And Odysseus closed in and ran the son of Damastor through with his spear, and Telemachus drove his spear into the belly of Leocritus son of Evenor, so that it came out the other side and the man dropped face-down on the floor. Then Athena, from her place on the rafter, lifted high the aegis that destroys men — and the hearts of the suitors broke. They scattered down the hall like a herd of cattle that the darting gadfly has maddened in the long warm days of spring. And as vultures with hooked beaks and crooked talons come stooping down out of the hills on the smaller birds, who flee low across the plain with no cover above them and no fight in them, and the vultures fall on them and kill, and there is no help and no escape, and men look on and delight in the chase — so the four fell on the suitors and cut them down through the hall, this way and that. A hideous groaning rose as the skulls were struck in, and the whole floor ran with blood.

Then Leiodes ran and caught Odysseus by the knees, pleading: “Odysseus, I beg you, take pity on me. I never did wrong to any of the women in your house, in word or in deed, and I tried to hold the others back from it. They would not listen. So they have come to this end for their own wickedness. But I was only their priest at the offerings, and I did nothing — and now I am to lie among them and die, with no thanks for any good I did.”

Odysseus looked down at him grimly. “If you were truly their priest at the offerings, then many a time you must have prayed in this house that my homecoming would never come, and that my wife would be yours and bear your children. For that you will not escape death.” And with that he caught up a sword from the floor where Agelaus had let it fall in his dying, and struck Leiodes across the back of the neck. The head was still speaking when it rolled in the dust.

But Phemius the bard, son of Terpes, who had been made to sing to the suitors against his will, was still trying to save his life. He stood near the vent with his clear-toned lyre in his hands, torn between two thoughts — whether to slip out of the hall and crouch at the great altar of Zeus in the courtyard, where Laertes and Odysseus had burned the thighs of many oxen, or to go straight to Odysseus and clasp his knees. In the end he judged it better to clasp the knees of Odysseus. So he laid the lyre down on the floor between the mixing-bowl and the silver-studded chair, and he ran to Odysseus, took hold of his knees, and begged: “Odysseus, I beg you, have pity on me, spare me. You will grieve for it afterward if you kill a singer — one who sings for gods and men alike. I am self-taught; a god has planted every kind of song in me, and I could sing for you as for a god. Do not be in such a hurry to take off my head. Your own son Telemachus will tell you the truth of it — that I never came to your house willingly, never wanted to sing to the suitors after their feasts. They were too many and too strong. They forced me.”

Telemachus heard him, and stepped quickly to his father’s side. “Hold — this man is innocent. Do him no harm. And let us spare Medon the herald too, who looked after me all through my boyhood — unless Philoetius or Eumaeus has already killed him, or he has crossed your path while you were storming through the hall.”

Medon caught the words. He was crouched under a chair where he had hidden, wrapped in a fresh-flayed ox-hide, and now he threw off the hide, sprang out, and clasped Telemachus by the knees. “Here I am, my dear lord — hold your hand, and tell your father, before he kills me in his rage at the suitors for eating his house bare and slighting you the way they did.”

Odysseus smiled at him and said, “Take heart. My son has saved you, and brought you out alive — so that you may know in your own bones, and tell other men, how much better a good deed fares in the end than an evil one. Now go out of the hall and sit in the courtyard, clear of the killing, the two of you, the bard and the herald, while I finish my work here inside.”

The pair went out into the courtyard as fast as they could and sat down by the great altar of Zeus, glancing about them, still half-expecting death. And Odysseus searched the whole hall, looking to see whether any man was left alive and hiding. But he found them all fallen in the blood and the dust, the whole crowd of them — like fish that the fishermen have dragged up out of the gray sea in the meshes of their net and poured out on the curve of the shore, where they lie heaped on the sand, gasping for the saltwater, while the burning sun takes the life out of them. So the suitors lay heaped, one against another.

Then Odysseus said to Telemachus, “Go and call the nurse Eurycleia. There is something I must tell her.” Telemachus went and knocked at the door of the women’s quarters and called, “Up, old mother, come out. You who keep watch over all the serving-women of the house — my father wants you. He has something to say.”

Eurycleia did not stop to answer. She opened the doors of the hall and came, with Telemachus leading the way. And she found Odysseus among the bodies of the slain, spattered with blood and gore — like a lion that has just fed on an ox of the field, his chest and his jaws on both sides smeared red, a thing terrible to look on. So was Odysseus fouled, from his feet to his head and his hands. When she saw the dead, and all that blood, she opened her mouth to cry aloud in triumph, for she saw the greatness of what had been done. But Odysseus stopped her, held her back from it, and said: “Keep your joy in your heart, old woman. Do not cry out. It is no holy thing to gloat over slain men. The doom of the gods and their own foul deeds brought these men down. They honored no one who came among them, low or high. So they have come to a hard end for their own wickedness. Now tell me which of the women in the house have shamed themselves, and which are guiltless.”

“I will tell you the truth, my child,” the nurse answered. “There are fifty serving-women in the house, women we trained in their work — to card the wool and to bear their lot as slaves. Of those, twelve in all have gone the way of shamelessness, with no respect for me, or even for Penelope. Telemachus they obeyed, for he is only lately grown and his mother never let him give orders to the women. But let me go up now and tell your wife everything. Some god has laid sleep on her.”

“Do not wake her yet,” said Odysseus. “First send me the women who have disgraced themselves.”

So Eurycleia went off through the hall to call the women and bring them to him. And Odysseus called Telemachus and the herdsman and the swineherd to him and said: “Begin carrying out the dead, and make the women help you. Then sponge down the fine chairs and the tables with water. And when you have set the whole hall in order, take the women out, between the round storehouse and the wall of the courtyard, and put them to the sword — every one — until you have driven the life out of them, and the memory of the love they made in secret with the suitors.”

The women came down together, in a huddle, weeping bitterly. First they carried the dead out and set them down under the gateway, propped against one another, while Odysseus stood over them and drove them at the work, and they bore the bodies out under his eye. Then they scoured the tables and the chairs with water and sponges, while Telemachus and the two herdsmen scraped the floor clean of the caked blood and the filth, and the women carried it all out of doors. When the whole hall was clean and in order, they led the women out and hemmed them into the narrow ground between the round storehouse and the courtyard wall, with no way through. And Telemachus said, “I will not give these women a clean death — not after the shame they heaped on my head and my mother’s, lying with the suitors.”

And with that he made a ship’s cable fast to a great pillar and looped it high around the round storehouse, drawn taut, so that none of them could reach the ground with her feet. As thrushes with their long wings, or doves, come flying into a snare set across a thicket, and find a cruel bed where they had thought to roost — so the women hung in a row, the nooses about their necks, to die the most pitiful death. Their feet kicked out a little while. It did not last long.

Then they brought Melanthius out through the hall into the courtyard. There they cut off his nose and his ears with the pitiless bronze, and tore out his manhood to throw raw to the dogs, and in their fury hacked off his hands and his feet.

When that was done they washed their own hands and feet and went back into the house to Odysseus, for the work was finished. And Odysseus said to the dear nurse Eurycleia, “Bring sulfur, old woman — it cleanses every pollution — and bring me fire, so that I may purify the hall. And go tell Penelope to come down, with her women, and have all the serving-women of the house come too.”

“All that you say is true, my child,” Eurycleia answered. “But let me first bring you a shirt and a cloak to put on. It is not right that you should stand in your own hall in these rags any longer.”

“Light me the fire first,” said Odysseus. So she brought the fire and the sulfur, and Odysseus purified the whole hall, and the house, and the courtyard. Then the old woman went through the house to call the loyal women and tell them all that had happened, and they came out of their quarters with torches in their hands and crowded around Odysseus, welcoming him home — taking his hands, kissing his head and his shoulders. And a sweet longing to weep came over him, for in his heart he knew every one of them.
