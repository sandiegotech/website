The Odyssey, Book XV

Telemachus Sails Home

Book XV15 of 24

Meanwhile Athena went to the fair city of Sparta, to tell the son of Odysseus that it was time to come home. She found Telemachus and Pisistratus bedded down in the forecourt of Menelaus’s house. Pisistratus was deep in sleep, but Telemachus could find no rest the whole night through, lying awake with his thoughts running on his lost father. Athena came and stood close over him and said:

“Telemachus, you should not be wandering so far from home any longer, leaving your goods behind and such insolent men loose in your house. Be careful they do not divide everything you own among themselves and eat it up, and your whole journey come to nothing. Press Menelaus to send you off at once, while you can still find your faithful mother under your own roof. Her father and her brothers are already urging her to marry Eurymachus, who is outbidding every other suitor with his gifts, piling up the bride-price higher and higher. See that she carries nothing valuable out of the house against your will. You know how a woman’s heart is: once she marries again, she sets herself to build up the house of her new husband, and forgets the children of the man she loved before, and the husband himself, now that he is dead — she no longer remembers him or cares. So go home, and put all you have in the charge of the most trustworthy of your women, until the gods show you a wife of your own. And there is one more thing you must take to heart. The chief men among the suitors are lying in wait for you in the strait between Ithaca and rugged Same, meaning to kill you before you can reach home. But I do not think they will. Sooner than that, the earth will close over some of the very men who are now eating up your house. Keep your good ship well clear of the islands, and sail on through the night; the god who watches over you will send a wind at your back. And the moment you touch Ithaca, send your ship and your crew on to the town, but go yourself, first of all, to the swineherd who keeps your pigs. He is loyal to you. Pass the night there with him, and send him into the town to tell Penelope you are home from Pylos, safe and sound.”

With that she went back to Olympus. Telemachus dug his heel into Pisistratus to wake him, and said, “Up, Pisistratus, son of Nestor — yoke the horses to the chariot. We have to be on our way.”

But Pisistratus answered, “Telemachus, in whatever hurry we may be, we cannot drive through the dark. It will be daybreak before long. Wait until Menelaus, that great spearman, has brought his gifts and set them in the chariot, and sent us off with a kind word. A guest remembers all his days the host who showed him friendship.”

As he spoke, Dawn came up on her golden throne, and Menelaus, who had already risen and left Helen’s side, came toward them. When Telemachus saw him, he pulled on his shirt in haste, threw his great cloak around his shoulders, and went out to meet him. “Menelaus,” he said, “royal son of Atreus, let me go back now to my own country. My heart aches to be home.”

And Menelaus answered, “Telemachus, I will not keep you against your will, since you are set on going. I have no patience with a host who fawns on his guest beyond all measure, nor with one who is cold to him — the right way lies between. It is just as wrong to push a man out the door when he wants to stay as to hold him back when he longs to go. Treat your guest well while he is under your roof, and speed him on when he wishes to leave. Only wait until I have brought you fine gifts and laid them in the chariot for you to see with your own eyes, and I will have the women set out a good meal from what we have in the house. There is honor in it, and good sense too — a man should eat before he sets out on so long a road. And if you have a mind to make a circuit through Hellas and the heart of Argos, I will yoke my own horses and be your guide through every town. No one will send us away empty-handed; each will give us something to carry off — a bronze tripod, or a cauldron, a pair of mules, or a cup of gold.”

“Menelaus,” Telemachus replied, “I want to go straight home. When I sailed I left no one to guard what is mine, and I am afraid that while I hunt for my father I will lose my own life, or come back to find some treasure of mine gone from the house.”

When Menelaus heard that, he told his wife and her women at once to set out a good meal from what was in the house. Then Eteoneus, son of Boethous, came in, just risen from his bed nearby, and Menelaus told him to light the fire and roast the meat, and he did as he was told. Menelaus himself went down to his fragrant, vaulted storeroom — not alone; Helen went with him, and Megapenthes. When they came to the place where his treasures were kept, he chose a two-handled cup for himself to give, and told his son Megapenthes to carry up a mixing-bowl of silver. Helen went to the chests where she kept the embroidered robes she had made with her own hands, and lifted out the one that was largest and richest in its needlework. It shimmered like a star, and it had lain at the very bottom of all the rest. Then they came back through the house to Telemachus, and Menelaus said: “Telemachus, may Zeus, the loud-thundering lord of Hera, bring you home to your heart’s desire. Of all the treasures stored in my house, I give you the finest and the most precious. I give you this mixing-bowl, worked all of silver, its rim finished in gold — the work of Hephaestus’s own hands. Phaedimus, the hero, king of the Sidonians, gave it to me when his house took me in on my way home. And now I would have it be yours.”

With that he placed the two-handled cup in Telemachus’s hands, while strong Megapenthes set the gleaming silver bowl before him, and fair Helen came near, the robe ready in her arms.

“I too have a gift for you, dear child,” she said, “to remember the hands of Helen by — for your bride to wear on the day of your wedding. Until then let your mother keep it in her own room. And may you come with joy to your strong house and your own native land.”

So she put the robe in his hands, and he took it gladly. Pisistratus laid the gifts in the basket of the chariot, gazing at each in wonder. Then Menelaus led the two of them indoors, and they took their seats on benches and chairs. A maidservant poured water for their hands from a fine golden jug into a silver basin, and drew up a polished table beside them. The grave housekeeper set out bread and many good things, giving freely of all she had, while Eteoneus carved the meat and served their portions and the son of Menelaus poured the wine. So they reached out for the good things spread before them, and when they had put away all desire for food and drink, Telemachus and the splendid son of Nestor yoked the horses, mounted the inlaid chariot, and drove out through the gateway and the echoing colonnade. Menelaus came after them, gold cup in his right hand, brimming with wine, so they might pour an offering before they went. He stood before the horses and drank their health: “Farewell to you both, young men, and give my greeting to Nestor, shepherd of the people — for he was as kind to me as a father, all the years we Greeks were fighting at Troy.”

“We will be sure,” said wise Telemachus, “to tell him every word of it the moment we see him, royal son of Atreus. And I wish I were as certain of finding Odysseus home again when I reach Ithaca, so I could tell him how warmly you received me, and show him all the fine gifts I carry away.”

Even as he spoke, a bird flew past on the right — an eagle, with a great white goose in its talons, a tame one snatched up from the yard, and the men and women came running and shouting behind it. The eagle drew close and swept off to the right, in front of the horses, and at the sight of it their spirits lifted and the heart grew glad in every breast. Then Pisistratus, son of Nestor, spoke first: “Tell us, Menelaus, royal son of Atreus — has the god sent this sign for us, or for you?”

Menelaus, dear to Ares, turned it over in his mind, searching for the right and fitting word — but Helen, in her long robes, was too quick for him. “Hear me,” she said, “and I will read it as the gods have put it in my heart, and as I believe it will come to pass. As this eagle came down from the mountain where it was bred and where its nestlings are, and seized the goose that was fattened in the house, so Odysseus, after long wandering and much suffering, will come home and take his revenge — or perhaps he is home already, and even now sowing ruin for every one of the suitors.”

“May Zeus, the loud-thundering lord of Hera, grant it so,” said Telemachus. “If he does, I will pray to you as I would to a god, all my days at home.”

With that he laid the whip to the horses, and they sprang forward at a gallop through the town and out into open country. All day long they kept the yoke shaking on their necks, until the sun went down and the roads grew dark. Then they came to Pherae, to the house of Diocles, son of Ortilochus, whose father was Alpheus, and there they slept the night, and he gave them a guest’s welcome. When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, they yoked the horses again, mounted the inlaid chariot, and drove out through the gateway and the echoing colonnade. Pisistratus touched the horses with the whip, and they flew on, glad to run, and soon came in sight of the high citadel of Pylos. Then Telemachus said:

“Pisistratus, will you do this one thing I ask of you, and promise it? We can claim a friendship that runs back to our fathers; we are of the same age; and this journey has bound us closer still. So do not carry me past my ship, son of Nestor — set me down there. If you take me to your father’s house, his kindness will keep me, for he will press me to stay against my will, and I have to get home without delay.”

Pisistratus thought it over in his heart, how best to do as he was asked, and in the end he judged it wisest to turn the horses toward the swift ship on the shore. There he set down the fine gifts in the stern — the gold and the woven cloth that Menelaus had given. Then he urged Telemachus on: “Go aboard now, quickly, and tell your men the same, before I can reach home and bring my father word. I know him too well in my heart: he will never let it rest. He will come down here himself to fetch you, and I tell you, he will not go back empty-handed. He will take it hard.”

With that he drove his fine-maned horses back to the city of the Pylians, and soon reached home. And Telemachus called his men together and gave the order: “Set everything in order aboard the ship, men, and let us be on our way.” So he spoke, and they listened and obeyed; quickly they went aboard and took their places at the oars.

But while Telemachus was busy at this, pouring an offering and praying to Athena in the stern, there came up to him a man from a far country — a seer, in flight out of Argos, for he had killed a man. He was of the line of Melampus, who had once lived in Pylos, the land of flocks; a rich man, with a great house among the Pylians, until he was driven into exile by Neleus, the proudest of living men, who seized his wealth and held it a full year by force. All that year Melampus lay a prisoner in the halls of Phylacus, suffering bitter pains, for the sake of the daughter of Neleus and the heavy madness a goddess, the dread Fury, had laid upon his heart. But he came through it with his life, drove the lowing cattle from Phylace to Pylos, paid back proud Neleus for the wrong, and won the woman as a bride for his brother. Then he left for another country, for horse-pasturing Argos — for it was fated he should live there and rule over many. There he married, built his high-roofed house, and fathered two strong sons, Antiphates and Mantius. Antiphates fathered great-hearted Oicles, and Oicles fathered Amphiaraus, rouser of armies, whom both Zeus and Apollo loved with all manner of love. Yet he never crossed the threshold of old age, but died at Thebes, betrayed for a woman’s bribe. His sons were Alcmaeon and Amphilochus. Mantius fathered Polypheides and Cleitus. Golden-throned Dawn carried off Cleitus for his beauty, to dwell among the immortals; and Apollo made noble-hearted Polypheides the finest prophet of all mankind, now that Amphiaraus was dead. He quarreled with his father and went away to Hyperesia, and there he made his home and prophesied to all.

It was this man’s son, Theoclymenus by name, who now came and stood before Telemachus as he poured his offering and prayed by the black ship. “Friend,” he said, “since I find you making sacrifice in this place, I beg you, by these offerings and by the god you make them to, and by your own life and the lives of the men who follow you — tell me the truth, and hide nothing. Who are you, and where do you come from? Where is your city, and who are your people?”

And wise Telemachus answered, “Stranger, I will tell you the whole truth. I am from Ithaca, and my father is Odysseus — if ever such a man lived. But by now he has met some wretched end. That is why I have taken these men and this black ship, and gone in search of word of my father, so long away.”

Then Theoclymenus, godlike man, answered him: “I too am far from my own land, an exile, for I killed a man of my own people. He has many brothers and kinsmen in Argos, the land where horses graze, and great is their power among the Greeks. To escape death at their hands, I am running for my life, doomed now to wander the earth. Take me aboard your ship, for I come to you a fugitive and a suppliant — do not let them kill me. I am sure they are on my track.”

“I will not turn you away from my good ship, if you wish to come,” said wise Telemachus. “Come, then, and in Ithaca you shall have such welcome as we can give.”

With that he took Theoclymenus’s bronze spear and laid it along the deck of the curved ship, then went aboard himself and sat down in the stern, and had Theoclymenus sit beside him. The men cast off the stern cables, and Telemachus called to them to lay hold of the rigging, and they bent to it briskly. They stepped the pine mast in its socket in the cross-beam, made it fast with the forestays, and hauled up the white sail with twisted oxhide ropes. And Athena sent them a fair wind, blowing keen and strong out of the high air, so the ship might run her swiftest course over the salt sea. So they passed Crouni and the fair streams of Chalcis.

The sun went down, and the roads of the world grew dark. The ship ran on toward Pheae, driven hard by the wind of Zeus, and on past bright Elis, where the Epeans hold sway. From there Telemachus steered for the Pointed Isles, his heart hanging between escape and capture.

Meanwhile Odysseus and the loyal swineherd were taking their supper in the hut, and the other men supped with them. When they had put away all desire for food and drink, Odysseus began to test the swineherd — to see whether he would still treat him with such warmth and press him to stay at the farm, or send him off to the town. So he said:

“Listen to me now, Eumaeus, and all of you. In the morning I mean to be gone, to go down and beg through the town, so as to be no more burden to you or your men. Give me your good advice, then, and a guide to set me on the road. Once I am there I will have to make my own way, going door to door, in hope that someone will give me a cup and a crust. I would go to the house of Odysseus too, and carry word to wise Penelope, and mingle with the overweening suitors, to see whether out of all that plenty they might give a man a meal. I would do whatever work they wanted, and do it well. For let me tell you — and mark it — by the grace of Hermes the guide, who lends beauty and a good name to the labor of every man’s hands, there is no one alive who could match me as a servant: at laying fresh logs on a steady fire, at splitting dry kindling, at carving and roasting meat, at pouring the wine — all the work that lesser men do for their betters.”

It troubled you to the heart to hear this, Eumaeus, swineherd, and you said: “Friend, what in the world put such a notion into your head? You are bent on getting yourself killed outright, if you mean to go in among that crowd of suitors, whose violence and arrogance reach the iron sky. They take no men like you to serve them. Their servants are young, and well turned out in fine cloaks and shirts, with their hair always sleek and their faces handsome; and the polished tables groan with bread and meat and wine. No — stay here with us. No one finds you a trouble, not I, nor any of my men. And when the dear son of Odysseus comes home, he will give you a cloak and a shirt for your back and send you wherever your heart is set on going.”

Then long-suffering Odysseus answered him: “Eumaeus, may you be as dear to Father Zeus as you are to me, for sparing me the misery of the road and its hard living — there is nothing worse for a man than wandering. Still, for the sake of the cursed belly men bear hard troubles — any man on whom wandering comes, and want, and grief. But now, since you keep me here and bid me wait for the young master, tell me about the mother of godlike Odysseus, and his father, whom he left on the threshold of old age when he sailed for Troy. Are they still alive and looking on the light of the sun? Or are they dead now, and gone down into the house of Hades?”

And the swineherd, that leader of men, answered: “I will tell you the whole truth of it. Laertes is still living, but day after day he prays the gods to let the life slip out of his body in his own house, for he grieves without end for his son gone away, and for the wise wife he married, whose death wounded him most of all and brought old age upon him before his time. She died of sorrow for her glorious son — a cruel way to die; may no friend of mine who has dealt kindly by me come to such an end. As long as she lived, for all her grief, it was a comfort to me to go and ask after her, because she raised me herself, alongside her own daughter, long-robed Ctimene, the youngest child she bore. We grew up side by side, and she gave me almost the same honor she gave her own. But when we both came to the age that all of us long for, they married Ctimene to a man in Same and were paid a great bride-price for her; and as for me, my mistress dressed me well, in a fine cloak and shirt, with sandals for my feet, and sent me out to the farm — yet she loved me more than ever in her heart. All that is gone from me now. But the blessed gods have made my work prosper here, in the place I tend; I have enough to eat and drink, and to share with anyone worth honoring. From my mistress, though, there is no kind word to be had these days, and no kind deed, now that ruin has fallen on the house and arrogant men are in it. Servants long to speak with their mistress face to face, and learn how things stand, and eat and drink in the hall, and carry a little something back to the country with them — the kind of thing that warms a servant’s heart.”

Then resourceful Odysseus answered him: “Think of it — you were only a small child, Eumaeus, when you were carried so far from your home and your parents. Tell me, and tell me the truth. Was it that the broad-streeted city your father and mother lived in was sacked and overthrown? Or did enemies catch you alone among the sheep or the cattle, run you down to the ships, and sell you off to the master of this house, who paid them a fair price for you?”

And the swineherd, that leader of men, answered: “Stranger, since you ask me this, then sit quiet, take your ease, and drink your wine, and listen. The nights now are endless. There is time for sleep, and time to sit up and take pleasure in talk; you have no need to go to bed before the hour — too much sleep is a weariness too. As for the rest, let any man whose heart bids him go off and sleep, and at first light eat his breakfast and follow the master’s pigs out to pasture. But the two of us will sit here in the hut, eating and drinking and easing our hearts with the memory of each other’s hard sorrows — for a man who has suffered much and wandered far comes in time to take pleasure even in his griefs once they are well behind him. Now, here is the answer to what you ask.

“There is an island called Syrie — you may have heard the name — out beyond Ortygia, there where the sun turns. It is not thickly settled, but the soil is good: rich in cattle and in sheep, full of wine, deep in wheat. Famine never comes to those people, nor any of the hateful sicknesses that fall on us poor mortals; but when the men of the city grow old, Apollo of the silver bow comes with Artemis, and brings them down with his painless arrows. There are two cities on it, and the whole land is divided between them, and over both of them my father was king — Ctesius, son of Ormenus, a man like the immortals.

“To that island there came one day some Phoenicians, famous seafarers, but greedy men, with a black ship full of countless trinkets. Now there was a Phoenician woman in my father’s house, tall and beautiful and skilled at fine work, and these sly Phoenicians led her astray. One of them lay with her by the hollow ship while she was washing clothes, and turned her heart with smooth words — the kind of thing that bends even an honest woman’s mind. Then he asked her who she was and where she came from, and she pointed to my father’s high-roofed house and said: ‘I am proud to say I come from Sidon, rich in bronze, and I am the daughter of Arybas, a man rolling in wealth. But Taphian pirates snatched me up one day as I came in from the fields, and carried me here over the sea, and sold me to the master of this house, who paid them a good price for me.’

“Then the man who had lain with her said: ‘Would you like to come home with us now, and see the high house of your father and mother again, and your parents themselves? For they are still alive, and they are spoken of as rich.’

“And the woman answered: ‘I would, if you sailors will first swear me a solemn oath to bring me home unharmed.’

“So she spoke, and they all swore it as she asked. When they had sworn and finished the oath, the woman spoke again: ‘Keep it quiet now. Let none of your men speak to me if he meets me in the street, or at the well — someone might go and tell the old man, and then in his suspicion he would clap me in cruel chains and plot the deaths of you all. So hold your peace, and get your wares bought quickly. And when your ship is loaded with cargo, send word to me at the house, and I will bring whatever gold I can lay my hands on. And there is one more fare I can pay you, gladly. There is a child of the master’s in the house, in my charge — a clever little thing, who already runs out of doors at my side. I will bring him aboard for you, and he will fetch you a great price, wherever you sell him among strangers.’

“With that she went back into the great house. The Phoenicians stayed on a whole year among us, gathering a heavy cargo into the hollow ship. When at last it was loaded and ready to sail, they sent a man to carry word to the woman. A cunning fellow he was; he came to my father’s house with a golden necklace strung here and there with amber beads, and while my mother and her women in the hall passed it from hand to hand, gazing at it and bidding for it, he gave the woman a quiet nod, and went off back to the hollow ship. Then she took me by the hand and led me out through the doors. In the porch she came on the cups and the tables where the men who waited on my father had been feasting — but they had just gone off to a sitting of the council and the assembly — so she snatched up three goblets and hid them in the fold of her dress and carried them off, and I went with her in my innocence. The sun went down, and all the roads grew dark, and we came quickly to the fine harbor where the Phoenician ship lay. They put us aboard and sailed out over the watery ways, and Zeus sent a wind at their backs. Six days we sailed, by day and by night alike; but when Zeus, son of Cronus, brought round the seventh day, Artemis the archer struck the woman down, and she crashed into the bilge like a sea-bird plunging, and they threw her overboard to the seals and the fishes, and I was left there grieving, all alone. The wind and the waves carried them on and brought them to Ithaca, and there Laertes bought me, paying for me out of his own goods. And that is how I first set eyes on this country with these eyes of mine.”

Then royal Odysseus answered him: “Eumaeus, you have stirred my heart to the depths, telling over all the sorrows you have borne. Yet truly Zeus has set good beside your evil: for all your suffering, you came into the house of a kind master, who sees to it that you have your bread and wine, and you live a good life — while I am still wandering city to city, with no end to it.”

So they talked, the two of them, and slept only a little, no more — for soon the bright Dawn was up. Meanwhile Telemachus and his men, off the coast, slackened the sails, brought down the mast, and rowed the ship in to her mooring. They threw out the anchor-stones, made fast the stern cables, then went ashore on the breaking surf, made their meal, and mixed the gleaming wine. When they had put away all desire for food and drink, wise Telemachus spoke: “Take the ship on round to the town, the rest of you. I am going up to the herdsmen and my farms. In the evening, when I have looked over my holdings, I will come down to the town, and in the morning I will set before you a good feast of meat and sweet wine to reward you for the voyage.”

Then godlike Theoclymenus said: “And what of me, dear child? Whose house am I to go to, among the men of rocky Ithaca? Or shall I go straight to your house and your mother?”

“At any other time,” wise Telemachus answered, “I would have sent you to our own house, where you would want for no welcome. But just now it would be the worse for you — I will be away, and my mother will not see you. She rarely shows herself to the suitors in the hall, but keeps to an upper room, weaving at her loom and away from them. Still, I can name you a man whose house you may go to — Eurymachus, the splendid son of wise Polybus, whom the people of Ithaca look upon now almost as a god. He is by far the best of them, and the most determined to wed my mother and take the place of Odysseus. But Olympian Zeus, up in his high heaven, knows whether a day of reckoning will not come down on them before any wedding.”

Even as he spoke, a bird flew past on the right — a hawk, the swift messenger of Apollo. In its claws it held a dove, and tore at it, and the feathers fell to the ground between Telemachus and the ship. Then Theoclymenus drew him aside, away from the men, clasped his hand, and said: “Telemachus, that bird did not fly past on your right without a god behind it. I knew, the moment I looked at it, that it was a sign of fortune. There is no line in all Ithaca more kingly than your own. You will hold the power, you and your house, forever.”

“Stranger,” wise Telemachus answered, “I only wish your words may come to pass. Then you would soon know my friendship, and so many gifts from my hand that any man who met you would call you blessed.”

Then he turned to Piraeus, a man he trusted: “Piraeus, son of Clytius, of all the men who came with me to Pylos you have served me the most faithfully. Take this stranger now to your own house, and treat him with all honor and kindness, until I come for him.”

And Piraeus, that great spearman, answered: “Telemachus, stay away as long as you like. I will see to him, and he shall lack nothing a guest may ask for.”

With that he went aboard, and ordered the men to embark and cast off the cables, and they went up and took their places at the oars. But Telemachus bound his fine sandals on his feet and took up his strong spear, tipped with sharp bronze, from the deck of the ship. They loosed the cables, thrust off from shore, and made for the town, as Telemachus had told them. And he strode on, fast and far, until he came to the farm where his great droves of swine were penned, and where the loyal swineherd slept among them — the man who, of all his master’s servants, cared the most faithfully for what was his.
