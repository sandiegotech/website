The Odyssey, Book V

Calypso, and the Raft

Book V5 of 24

Now Dawn rose from her bed beside Tithonus, to bring her light to gods and mortals alike, and the gods sat down in council, and among them Zeus the lord of thunder, who is mightiest of all. And Athena began to tell them of the many troubles of Odysseus, for it weighed on her that he was still in the nymph Calypso’s house.

“Father Zeus,” she said, “and all you other blessed gods who live forever — let no sceptered king ever again be kind, or gentle, or fair-minded. Let every one of them be cruel from now on, and rule by force. For not a single soul among the people Odysseus once ruled remembers him now — and he ruled them as gently as a father. There he lies, suffering terribly on an island, in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who keeps him against his will. He has no way home — no ships, no crew to carry him over the wide back of the sea. And now they mean to kill his only son as well, when he comes home, for the boy has gone to sandy Pylos and to Sparta, to ask after his father.”

“My child,” Zeus who gathers the clouds answered her, “what a thing to let past your lips. Wasn’t this your own plan — that Odysseus should come home and pay these men back? And as for Telemachus, you can see him safely home yourself; you have the power. Bring the boy back to his own land untouched, and let the suitors come sailing home empty-handed, their murder undone.”

Then he turned to his dear son Hermes. “Hermes — you are our messenger in all things — go to the lovely-haired nymph and tell her our firm decision: that long-suffering Odysseus is to go home. He goes with no escort, neither of gods nor men. On a raft of bound timbers he is to suffer for twenty days and reach Scheria, the rich land of the Phaeacians, who are close kin to the gods. They will honor him like one of the immortals and send him on by ship to his own country, with more bronze and gold and fine cloth than he would ever have carried home from Troy, had he come back unharmed with his full share of the spoils. For it is his fate to see his own people again, and stand once more in his high-roofed house, in the land of his fathers.”

So he spoke, and Hermes the runner, the slayer of Argos, did not disobey. At once he bound beneath his feet the beautiful sandals, immortal and golden, that carry him over the water and the endless earth as fast as the wind. And he took up the wand he uses to charm men’s eyes shut, or to wake the sleeping when he pleases. With it in his hand he flew, swooping down past Pieria from the high air to the sea, and skimmed the waves like a gull that hunts the fish in the deep folds of the restless water and wets its thick wings in the spray. On and on he went over the endless swell, until he came to that far-off island. There he stepped from the violet sea onto dry land and walked until he reached the great cave where the lovely-haired nymph made her home. And he found her there.

A great fire was burning on the hearth, and the sweet smell of split cedar and juniper drifted far across the island as it burned. Inside, Calypso was singing in a beautiful voice, working her loom, driving the golden shuttle back and forth. Around the cave a wood had grown up thick and green — alder, black poplar, and sweet-smelling cypress, where long-winged birds roosted: owls and hawks and the chattering sea-crows whose work is on the water. A garden vine, heavy with grapes, ran trailing around the mouth of the cave, and four springs in a row poured out their clear water, each one channeled off in a different direction, and all around soft meadows of violet and wild parsley were in bloom. Even a god, coming upon that place, would stop and marvel, and feel the heart lift within him. And so Hermes, the keen-eyed slayer of Argos, stood and gazed his fill — and then went inside.

Calypso, fair among goddesses, knew him the moment she saw his face, for the gods all know one another, no matter how far apart they live. But she did not find brilliant Odysseus within; he was sitting out on the shore, as always, breaking his heart with tears and groans, staring through his weeping eyes at the barren sea. She gave Hermes a bright, shining chair and asked him: “Hermes — honored friend, and always welcome, though you come to me so seldom — why have you come now? Tell me what’s on your mind. My heart says do it, if I can, and if it can be done at all. But first come in, and let me set something before you.”

With that the goddess drew up a table laden with ambrosia and mixed him the red nectar, and the slayer of Argos ate and drank his fill. Then, when he had supped, he answered her. “You ask me, goddess to god, why I have come, and I will tell you straight, since you ask it. Zeus ordered me here; it was none of my own doing. Who would choose to cross all this endless salt water, where there is no city of men to offer the gods their sacrifices and choice hecatombs? But no god can slip past the will of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, or set it aside. He says you have here the unluckiest of all the men who fought nine years around King Priam’s city and sailed home in the tenth, after they had sacked it. On the way home they wronged Athena, and she raised wind and high seas against them, so that all his brave companions were lost, and he alone was carried here by wind and wave. Zeus commands you to send this man on his way at once. It is not his fate to die here, far from his own people. He is to see them again, and stand in his high-roofed house in the land of his fathers.”

Calypso, fair among goddesses, shuddered with rage at his words. “You are cruel, you gods,” she cried, “and grudging beyond all others. You cannot bear to see a goddess take a mortal man to her bed and live with him openly as her husband. So it was when rosy-fingered Dawn took Orion for herself — you gods who live in ease could not rest until chaste Artemis came to Ortygia and shot him down with her gentle arrows. And so again when fair-haired Demeter gave in to her heart and lay with Iasion in a thrice-plowed fallow field — Zeus heard of it soon enough and struck the man dead with his blazing bolt. And now you grudge me this — that I keep a mortal man here. I saved him myself, when he came drifting astride a single keel, all alone, after Zeus had split his fast ship with a bright bolt and shattered it in the middle of the wine-dark sea. All his brave companions drowned; he alone was carried here by wind and wave. I took him in. I loved him, and cared for him, and meant to make him deathless, never to grow old, all his days. But no god can slip past the will of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, or set it aside. So let him go, if that is what Zeus demands — out over the barren sea. But I cannot send him off myself; I have no ships, no crew to carry him over the water. Still, I will gladly give him what counsel I can, and hide nothing, so he comes home to his own land unharmed.”

Then the runner, the slayer of Argos, answered: “Send him off, then, and now — and beware the wrath of Zeus, or he will turn on you one day in anger.” And with that the mighty slayer of Argos was gone.

And the queenly nymph went looking for great-hearted Odysseus, now that she had heard the word of Zeus. She found him sitting on the shore, his eyes never dry, his sweet life draining away as he mourned for home — for the nymph no longer pleased him. By night, true, he still slept beside her in the hollow cave, willing no longer, lying beside a goddess who willed it. But by day he sat out on the rocks and the sand, breaking his heart with tears and groans and grief, staring through his weeping eyes at the barren sea. The goddess came close and spoke to him. “My poor man, you needn’t sit here grieving any longer, wasting your life away. I am letting you go now, of my own free will. So come — take a bronze axe, cut long timbers, and build yourself a broad raft. Fit an upper deck on it, high enough to carry you safe across the misty sea. And I will stock it with bread and water and red wine to keep the hunger off, and put clothes on your back, and send a fair wind behind you, so you reach your own land unharmed — if that is the will of the gods who hold the wide heaven, who are stronger than I am, and know these things better.”

Long-suffering, brilliant Odysseus shuddered as he heard her, and spoke to her with winged words: “Goddess, you’re plotting something here — this is no help home. You tell me to cross the great gulf of the sea, that hard and terrible water, on a raft — when not even a trim, fast ship runs that course gladly, even with a fair wind from Zeus. No. I will not set foot on a raft unless you bring yourself to swear me a great oath, goddess, that you are scheming no fresh harm against me.”

Calypso, fair among goddesses, smiled at that and stroked him with her hand, and said his name. “You are a rogue, and no fool, to think of asking such a thing. Then let the earth be my witness, and the wide heaven above, and the down-flowing water of the Styx — which is the greatest, most dreadful oath the blessed gods can swear — that I am scheming no fresh harm against you. No, I have in mind for you only what I would plan for myself, if ever I came to such straits. My own heart is fair-minded; it is not made of iron. There is pity in me.”

With that the queenly goddess led the way, quickly, and the man followed in the god’s footsteps. They came to the hollow cave, the goddess and the man, and he sat down on the chair that Hermes had risen from, and the nymph set out before him all kinds of food, the things that mortal men eat and drink. She took her own seat facing godlike Odysseus, and her handmaids set ambrosia and nectar before her, and the two of them reached out and helped themselves to the good things laid ready. And when they had taken their fill of food and drink, Calypso, fair among goddesses, began. “Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes — so you mean to go home, to your own dear country, this very moment? Go, then, and I wish you well, even so. But if you only knew, in your heart, how much suffering you are fated to endure before you reach that land, you would stay here with me and keep this house, and be deathless — for all your longing to see your wife again, the one you ache for, day after day. And yet I think I am no worse than she is — not in form, not in stature. It would hardly be fitting for a mortal woman to match an immortal in body or in beauty.”

And Odysseus of the many schemes answered her: “Lady goddess, don’t hold this against me. I know it well myself — wise Penelope is nothing beside you to look at, in stature or in beauty. She is mortal, and you are deathless, and never grow old. Even so, all my longing, all my days, is to go home and see the day of my return. And if some god should wreck me out on the wine-dark sea, I will bear it. The heart in my breast is hardened to suffering — I have endured so much already, so much hard labor on the waves and in the war. Let this come too, with the rest.”

So he spoke, and the sun went down, and the dark came on, and the two of them went back into the deep of the hollow cave and took their pleasure of love, side by side.

When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, Odysseus pulled on his tunic and his cloak, and the nymph wrapped herself in a long silver robe, fine-woven and graceful, and clasped a beautiful golden belt about her waist, and drew a veil over her head. Then she turned her mind to speeding great-hearted Odysseus on his way. She gave him a great axe that fit his grip, the bronze keen on both edges, with a fine handle of olive-wood set firmly in it; and a smooth-finished adze besides. Then she led him to the far end of the island, where the tall trees grew — alder, black poplar, and fir that reached the sky, long dead and dry, and seasoned, so they would ride light on the water. When she had shown him where the tall trees stood, Calypso, fair among goddesses, went home, and he set to cutting timber, and the work went fast. He felled twenty trees in all and dressed them with the bronze, planed them skillfully, and trued the edges to a chalk-line. Then the goddess brought him augers, and he bored the timbers through and fitted them to one another, and joined them tight with pegs and lashings. As wide as a shipwright skilled in his craft will lay out the hull of a broad cargo ship, so wide did Odysseus build his broad raft. He fixed the deck on close-set ribs and finished it off with long gunwale planks. He set up a mast, and a yard to cross it, and shaped a steering-oar to hold his course. He fenced the whole length of it with woven willow against the seas, and threw down a good ballast of brush. Then Calypso, fair among goddesses, brought cloth for the sail, and he made that well too, and bound the braces and halyards and sheets in place. And at last, with levers, he hauled the raft down to the bright sea.

By the fourth day he had the whole of it finished, and on the fifth Calypso, fair among goddesses, sent him from the island, after she had bathed him and dressed him in sweet-smelling clothes. She put aboard one skin of dark wine and a second, larger one of water, and a bag of provisions besides, and many good things to eat. And she sent a fair, warm wind after him, and great-hearted Odysseus spread his sail to it with joy, and sat by the steering-oar and held his course like a master. Sleep never fell on his eyes; he kept them fixed on the Pleiades, and on Boötes that sets so late, and on the Bear — which men also call the Wain — that wheels forever in its place, watching Orion, and alone takes no part in the baths of Ocean. For Calypso, fair among goddesses, had told him to keep that star on his left hand as he crossed the sea. Seventeen days he sailed the open water, and on the eighteenth the shadowy mountains of the Phaeacian land rose up, where it stood nearest to him, lifting like a shield out of the misty sea.

But lord Poseidon, the shaker of the earth, on his way back from the Ethiopians, caught sight of him far off, from the mountains of the Solymi. He saw Odysseus sailing the sea, and a great rage swelled in him, and he shook his head and muttered to his own heart: “So this is it — the gods have changed their minds about Odysseus while I was away among the Ethiopians. He’s already close to the Phaeacian shore, where it is his fate to slip the great net of suffering that has him fast. But I think I can still give him his fill of trouble.”

With that he gathered the clouds together and seized his trident in both hands and churned the sea, and roused the blasts of every wind that blows, and hid land and water alike in cloud, and night came rushing down out of the sky. The East Wind and the South Wind clashed together, and the hard-blowing West and the North that is born in the clear air rolling a great wave before it. And the knees of Odysseus went slack, and his heart with them, and he groaned to his own great spirit: “What sorrow — what is to become of me now? I’m afraid the goddess told it true, every word, when she said I’d fill my cup of suffering at sea before I ever reached home. Now all of it is coming to pass. Look at the clouds Zeus is wreathing the whole sky in — and the sea churned wild, and the gusts of every wind hurling down at once. There’s no escape now; my ruin is certain. Three times blessed, four times blessed, were the Greeks who died back then on the wide plain of Troy, doing the work of the sons of Atreus. I wish I had died there, met my end on the day the Trojans came at me in their thousands, around the dead body of Achilles. I’d have had my funeral rites, and the Greeks would have carried my name down the years. Instead it is my fate to be taken by a wretched death.”

As the words left him a great wave crashed down from above, terrible, driving hard, and spun the raft around. He was flung far overboard, and the steering-oar tore from his hands, and the fury of the warring winds snapped his mast clean in the middle, and the sail and the yard went flying far out into the sea. The water held him under a long time; he could not fight back up at once against the rush of the great wave, for the clothes Calypso had given him dragged him down. At last he broke the surface and spat out the bitter brine that streamed in rivers from his head. Battered as he was, he did not forget his raft. He lunged through the waves and caught hold of it and pulled himself up into the middle of it, and so he kept clear of death. The great swell carried the raft this way and that on the current, the way the North Wind in autumn drives the thistledown across a field, the tufts clinging together — so the winds bore it back and forth across the sea, now the South Wind tossing it to the North to carry, now the East giving it over to the West to chase.

But Ino saw him — the daughter of Cadmus, Ino of the slim ankles, who is also called Leucothea. She was once a mortal of human speech, but now in the salt depths she holds her share of honor among the gods. She pitied Odysseus as he drifted in his misery, and rose from the water like a sea-gull on the wing, and settled on the raft, and spoke to him. “My poor man — why is Poseidon the earth-shaker so set against you, sowing all this trouble in your path? Yet he will not destroy you, for all his fury. Do as I tell you — you seem to have your wits about you. Strip off those clothes, leave your raft to the winds, and swim with your arms for the Phaeacian shore, where it is your fate to come through. Here — take this veil of mine and stretch it under your chest. It is immortal; you need fear nothing now, no suffering, no drowning. But the moment your hands touch land, untie it and fling it far out into the wine-dark sea, well clear of the shore, and turn your face away.” With that the goddess gave him the veil, and dove back down into the heaving sea like a gull, and the dark water closed over her.

But long-suffering, brilliant Odysseus was torn, and groaned to his own great spirit: “No — I’m afraid this is one of the immortals weaving me another trap, telling me to leave my raft. I won’t do it, not yet. That land where she says I’ll be safe — I saw with my own eyes it’s still a long way off. Here’s what I’ll do, and I think it’s best: as long as the timbers hold together, I’ll stay aboard and ride out the worst. But the moment the sea smashes the raft to pieces, then I’ll swim for it — there’s nothing better I can think of than that.”

While he was still turning it over in his mind, Poseidon the earth-shaker raised up a great wave, terrible and crushing, towering over him, and brought it down. As a wild gust scatters a dry heap of chaff and flings it every way, so the long timbers flew apart. Odysseus threw himself astride a single beam and rode it like a man on horseback, and stripped off the clothes Calypso had given him, and wound Ino’s veil beneath his chest, and plunged headlong into the sea, his arms spread to swim. And lord Poseidon, the shaker of the earth, saw him do it, and shook his head, and muttered to his own heart: “There — now drift in misery across the sea, until you fall in with men whom the gods have favored. Even so, I don’t think you’ll find your suffering too light.” With that he whipped his fine-maned horses on and drove to Aegae, where his glorious palace stands.

But Athena, daughter of Zeus, had other plans. She bound back the paths of all the other winds and ordered them to rest, to fall still and sleep — all but the swift North Wind, which she roused, and she broke the waves down ahead of brilliant Odysseus, so that he might reach the Phaeacians, who love their oars, and slip past death and doom.

For two nights then, and two days, he drifted on the heavy swell, and again and again his heart looked his own death in the face. But when fair-haired Dawn brought on the third day, the wind died and a windless calm came over the sea, and there, as he was lifted high on a great swell and peered ahead with straining eyes, he saw land close by. As welcome as the first sign of life is to children, when their father lies sick, racked with long agony, wasting under some cruel god’s hand, and then the gods set him free of the evil — that welcome was the sight of land and trees to Odysseus, and he swam hard for it, longing to set his feet on solid ground. But when he had come within shouting distance of the shore, he heard the boom of the surf against the rocks. Great waves were thundering in against the dry land with a terrible roar, and everything was sheeted in spray. There were no harbors there to hold a ship, no roadstead — only headlands jutting out, and reefs, and cliffs.

And the knees of Odysseus went slack, and his heart with them, and he groaned to his own great spirit: “What now? Zeus has let me see the land I’d given up all hope of, and I’ve cut my way clear across this gulf — but there’s no way out of the gray water, no foothold. Sharp rocks ahead, and the surf roaring all around them, and the cliff rising up sheer and smooth, with deep water hard against it, no place to stand on my two feet and pull free of this ruin. If I try to climb out, a great wave will lift me and dash me against the jagged rock, and my struggle is for nothing. But if I swim on further, looking for a gently shelving beach, or a quiet harbor, I’m afraid the storm will snatch me up again, groaning, and carry me back out over the teeming sea — or some god will send a great monster up against me from the deep, the kind that famed Amphitrite breeds by the thousand. For I know how the great earth-shaker hates me.”

While he was still turning it over in his mind, a great wave carried him in against the rugged shore. There he would have been flayed, his bones broken, had not gray-eyed Athena put it into his heart what to do. He lunged with both hands and caught the rock and clung to it, groaning, until the great wave had passed. So he rode that one out — but then the backwash struck him and flung him far out to sea again. As when an octopus is torn from its hole and the pebbles cling thick to its suckers, so the skin was stripped from his strong hands against the rock, and the great wave closed over him. And there, against all his fate, poor Odysseus would have died, had not gray-eyed Athena given him a clear head. He fought up clear of the surf where it pounded the shore, and swam along outside it, his eyes always on the land, looking for a beach that shelved gently or a harbor out of the sea’s reach. And as he swam he came to the mouth of a fair-running river, and that, he saw, was the best place of all — smooth, no rocks, and sheltered from the wind. He felt the river’s current running and he prayed to it in his heart:

“Hear me, lord, whoever you are. I come to you as the answer to all my prayers, fleeing the sea and Poseidon’s threats. Even the deathless gods honor the man who comes to them lost and wandering, the way I come now to your stream and your knees, after so much suffering. Take pity on me, lord. I am your suppliant, and I claim it.”

So he prayed, and the river-god at once held back his current and stilled the waves, and made the water smooth before him, and brought him safe to land at the river’s mouth. There at last his knees gave way, and his strong hands, for the sea had broken him utterly. His whole body was swollen, and the sea-water ran streaming from his mouth and nose, and he lay there with no breath in him and no voice, worn out past all enduring. But when at last he caught his breath and the spirit gathered again in his heart, he untied the goddess’s veil from his body and let it fall into the seaward-running river, and a great wave bore it back down the current, and Ino caught it in her hands at once. Then he turned from the river, sank down in the reeds, and kissed the grain-giving earth.

And still, in his misery, he groaned to his own great spirit: “What now — what’s to become of me, here at the end of everything? If I keep watch by the river through the long, anxious night, the bitter frost and the soaking dew may finish me off, weak as I am, breath barely in me — and a cold wind blows up off a river toward dawn. But if I climb the slope to the shadowy woods and lie down to sleep in the thick brush, even if the cold and the weariness let me be and sweet sleep comes — I’m afraid I’ll be left as prey, a meal for the wild beasts.”

In the end this seemed the better course. He went up toward the woods and found a thicket on rising ground, in a clearing near the water. There he crept in under two olive trees grown from a single root — one a wild olive, the other a cultivated one. No force of the wet, blustering wind could reach through them, no ray of the blazing sun, no soaking rain, so closely had they grown into each other. Odysseus crept in under them, and at once he raked together a wide bed for himself, for there was a great drift of fallen leaves there, enough to cover two men, or three, in the hard weather of deep winter. Brilliant, long-suffering Odysseus saw it and was glad, and lay down in the middle and heaped the fallen leaves over himself. As a man on a lonely farm, far from any neighbor, will bury a glowing brand in the black ashes to save the seed of fire, so he would not have to go begging a light from somewhere else — so Odysseus buried himself in the leaves. And Athena poured sleep over his eyes, and closed his lids, to release him as quickly as she could from his bone-deep weariness.
