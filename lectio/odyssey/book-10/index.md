# The Odyssey, Book X

Aeolus, the Laestrygonians, and Circe

From there we came to the island of Aeolia, where Aeolus lived, the son of Hippotas, dear to the immortal gods. It is a floating island, ringed all around with a wall of unbreakable bronze, and the rock of it rises sheer out of the sea. Aeolus has twelve children inside that hall — six daughters and six sons, all grown — and he gave his daughters to his sons to be their wives. They live beside their dear father and their gentle mother, with feasting laid before them that never ends. All day long the house is thick with the smell of roasting meat, and the courtyard rings with it; and at night each of them sleeps beside his own wife, on a bed spread with blankets, in the well-made hall. These were the people we had come among.

Aeolus welcomed me as a guest and kept me a full month, asking me about everything — Troy, the Greek ships, how we sailed for home. I told him the whole of it, just as it happened. And when at last I asked him to send me on my way, he made no difficulty; he set about it gladly. He flayed the hide of a nine-year-old ox and made it into a bag, and into that bag he bound the ways of the howling winds — for Zeus had made him the winds’ keeper, to rouse them or still them as he pleased. He stowed the bag in the hold of my ship and lashed its mouth shut with a bright silver cord, so that not even a breath could slip out from any side. The one wind he left free was the West Wind, to blow us home. But it came to nothing in the end — and the ruin we brought down was our own.

Nine days we sailed, nine days and nights together, and on the tenth our own land at last came up over the water — so near that we could see men tending their watch-fires along the shore. And just then, dead-tired, I fell asleep. I had held the steering-oar myself the whole way, never trusting it to any of the men, so we might come home the faster. But while I slept the crew began to talk among themselves. They had it that I was carrying gold and silver home in the bag, the gift of great-hearted Aeolus, and one would turn to the man beside him and say: “It’s a wonder, the way everyone loves this man and heaps him with honor, whatever land he comes to. Look at the fine plunder he’s bringing back from Troy — and the rest of us, who came every weary mile he did, going home empty-handed. And now Aeolus has loaded him with all this on top of it, out of pure friendship. Come — quick — let’s see what’s in it. How much gold and silver is tied up in that bag?”

That was the talk, and the bad counsel won. They opened the bag — and out roared every wind at once. The storm caught us and swept us back out to sea, away from home, the men weeping. I woke to it, and for a moment I weighed in my heart whether to go over the side and drown then and there, or hold on and bear it and stay among the living. I held on. I bore it. I wrapped myself in my cloak and lay down in the ship while the wild gale carried the whole fleet back, my men groaning, all the way to the island of Aeolus.

We went ashore and drew fresh water, and the men ate their meal there by the ships. When we had eaten, I took one herald and one of the crew and went straight up to the house of Aeolus — and there I found him at the feast, with his wife and his children. We came in and sat down as suppliants on the threshold, by the doorpost, and they stared at us, astonished. “Odysseus — how is this?” they said. “What god has set himself against you? We sent you off with such care, to bring you home to Ithaca, or wherever in the world you wished to go.”

That is what they said, and I answered, sick at heart: “My own crew undid me — they and a cruel sleep. Make it good for me, my friends; you have the power, if you will.”

I pleaded as gently as I could, but they sat in silence. Only their father answered, and his words were hard: “Off this island — now — you lowest thing alive. It is not for me to take in and send onward a man the blessed gods hate. Get out. You come here cursed by heaven.” And with that he drove me from his door, and I went grieving.

From there we sailed on, heavy at heart. The spirit went out of the men at the oars, worn down by the cruel rowing, for now there was no wind at all to help them, and the fault was our own folly. Six days we toiled, days and nights together, and on the seventh we came to the high citadel of Lamus — to Telepylus, the city of the Laestrygonians. It is a strange country: there the night and the day run so close together that a herdsman driving his flocks in at dusk calls out to a herdsman driving his out, and that one calls back. A man who could go without sleep might earn double wages there — one for keeping the cattle, one for pasturing the white sheep — for the paths of the night and the day lie near each other.

We came into a fine harbor, ringed all around by sheer cliffs, with two headlands running out to make a narrow mouth between them. There my captains brought all their ships inside and moored them close together, gunwale to gunwale, for inside that water no wave ever rose, great or small; it lay always dead calm. But I alone kept my black ship outside, at the very point, and made her fast to the rock there. Then I climbed a high crag to look around. I could see no plowed field, no cattle, no sign of men — only smoke going up from somewhere over the land. So I sent two of my men, with a third to go with them, to find out who lived in that country.

They went ashore and followed a smooth road, the one the wagons take, hauling timber down from the high hills into the town. Outside the walls they met a girl drawing water — the tall daughter of Antiphates the Laestrygonian. She had come down to the clear spring of Artacia, where the people fetch their water, and the men went up and asked her who was king here, and over what people he ruled. She pointed them at once to her father’s high-roofed house. But when they went in they found his wife — a woman huge as a mountain peak — and the sight of her froze their blood.

Straightaway she shouted for her husband, Antiphates, from the meeting-place, and he came, and his welcome was death. He snatched up one of my men on the spot and made his meal of him. The other two sprang up and ran for their lives, back to the ships — but Antiphates raised the alarm through the town, and the mighty Laestrygonians came running from every side, thousands of them, more like giants than men. They tore great boulders from the cliffs, each the load of a man, and hurled them down on us; and there rose up from the ships a horrible din — timbers splintering, men dying — as the giants speared them like fish and carried them off to make their grim feast. While they were killing my men there in the deep harbor, I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh, slashed through the cables of my dark-prowed ship, and shouted to my own crew to throw their backs into the oars and pull, if they wanted to live. In terror of death they pulled as one, and my ship leapt clear of the overhanging rocks, out to open water. But all the rest were lost there together — every one.

On from there we sailed, grieving in our hearts, glad to be alive and yet sick for the friends we had lost. And we came to the island of Aeaea, where Circe lived — Circe of the lovely braids, a dread goddess with a human voice, own sister to Aeetes, whose mind runs to ruin. Both of them were children of Helios, who gives light to mortals, born to him by Perse, the daughter of Ocean. There we brought our ship in to the shore without a sound, into a sheltered cove — some god was guiding us. We landed, and for two days and two nights we lay where we were, worn out in body and worn out in spirit, eating our hearts with grief.

But when fair-haired Dawn brought up the third morning, I took my spear and my keen sword and climbed up from the ship to a high lookout, hoping to catch some sign of men at work, or the sound of voices. And standing on that rocky height I saw it — smoke going up from Circe’s house, far off through the dense scrub and the forest. I turned it over in my mind, whether to go straight on and learn more, now that I had seen the smoke. And in the end it seemed the better course to go back down to the ship and the shore, give the men their meal, and send a party to find out, rather than go alone.

On my way down, near the ship, some god took pity on me there in my loneliness, and sent a great stag with towering antlers right across my path. The heat of the noon had driven him down out of the woodland pasture to drink at the river, and as he came I struck him square in the spine, and the bronze point drove clean through, and he dropped in the dust with a bellow, and his life flew out of him. I set my foot on him, pulled the spear from the wound, and laid it on the ground; then I gathered twigs and willow shoots and twisted a good rope, a fathom long, and lashed the great beast’s four feet together. I slung him round my neck and carried him down to the ship, leaning on the spear, for he was far too big to balance on one shoulder with a hand — he was an enormous creature. I threw him down in front of the ship and roused the men with cheerful words, going from one to the next: “Friends — beaten as we are, we are not going down to the house of Hades before our day. Not while there’s meat and drink still in the ship. Up, now — let’s not waste away with hunger.”

At that they took heart. They threw off their cloaks there on the shore and stood marveling at the stag, for he was a splendid thing. And when they had feasted their eyes, they washed their hands and set about a glad feast. So all that day, till the sun went down, we sat there filling ourselves with meat past counting and sweet wine; and when the sun set and the dark came on, we slept by the breaking surf. But when the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, I called them all together and spoke to them: “Friends — listen to me, hard as things stand. We have no idea, men, where the darkness lies or where the dawn, where the sun goes down to the world below or where it climbs again. Let us think, while there’s still time, whether there’s any way out — and I can see none. I climbed to a rocky height and looked: this is an island, low-lying, with the endless sea drawn round it like a ring. And in the middle of it I saw smoke, going up through the thick woods.”

At that their hearts broke in them. They remembered everything — Antiphates the Laestrygonian, and the savage, lawless Cyclops — and they wept aloud, the great tears falling. But there was nothing to be won by crying. So I counted them off into two companies and set a leader over each: one I took myself, and the other I gave to godlike Eurylochus. Then we shook the lots in a bronze helmet, and out jumped the lot of brave Eurylochus. So off he went, weeping, and with him twenty-two of the men — and the rest of us were left behind, weeping too.

Deep in the glens they came on Circe’s house, built of dressed stone on open, commanding ground. All around it prowled wolves of the mountain and lions — creatures she herself had bewitched, drugging them with her wicked potions. They did not set upon the men; they came up, fawning, wagging their long tails, the way dogs crowd around their master when he comes home from a feast, for he always has some good thing for them in his hands. Just so those great-clawed wolves and lions fawned about my men — and the men were terrified, face to face with such monsters. They stopped in the gateway of the lovely-haired goddess, and from inside they could hear Circe singing in a beautiful voice as she worked at her great loom, weaving a web wide and shimmering and delicate, the kind of fine, radiant work that only the hands of a goddess make. Polites spoke up first — a leader of men, and dearest to me, the one I trusted most: “Friends, someone inside is working a great loom and singing — beautifully — and the whole place rings with it. Goddess or woman, let’s call out to her.”

So they called, and she came. She opened the bright doors and asked them in, and they all followed her, knowing no better — all but Eurylochus, who hung back, suspecting a trap. She led them in and seated them on chairs and benches, and stirred up for them a brew of cheese and barley meal and pale honey in Pramnian wine — but into the food she mixed her cruel drugs, to wipe their homeland clean out of their minds. When they had drunk it down, she struck them with her wand and herded them into the pens of her pigsty. They had the heads of pigs now, the bristles, the grunting, the whole shape of them — but the mind inside was still a man’s, unchanged. So they were penned, weeping, while Circe threw them acorns and beechmast and the red cornel-berries, the food of pigs that wallow in the mud.

Eurylochus ran straight back to the swift black ship to bring word of his comrades and their bitter fate. But for all his trying he could not get a word out — the grief had struck him dumb, his eyes brimming, his heart set only on weeping. We pressed him with questions, every one of us amazed, until at last he found his voice and told us how the others were lost: “We went off through the oak woods, brilliant Odysseus, as you ordered, and deep in the glens we found a fine house of dressed stone, on open ground. Someone was inside, working a great loom and singing in a clear, high voice — a goddess, or a woman — and the men called out to her. She came at once, opened the shining doors, and asked them in, and the whole crowd of them followed her, knowing no better. But I stayed where I was; I smelled a trap. And every one of them vanished. Not one came out again, though I sat a long, long time and watched.”

When I heard that, I slung my great bronze-studded sword across my shoulders, took up my bow, and told Eurylochus to lead me back the way he had come. But he threw both arms around my knees and begged me, the words pouring out of him: “Don’t make me go back there, my lord — leave me here. I know it: you’ll never come back yourself, and you’ll never bring one of the others with you. Let’s get away, the rest of us, now, while we can. We may still save our lives.”

And I said to him: “Eurylochus — then stay here yourself, eating and drinking beside the black ship. But I am going. There is a hard necessity laid on me.” And with that I left the ship and the sea behind me and went up inland.

I had come through the sacred glens and was near the great house of Circe, the witch of many drugs, when Hermes met me — Hermes of the golden wand — in the very road. He had the look of a young man with the first down on his lip, in the fairest bloom of his youth. He took me by the hand and spoke my name: “Where are you off to now, you luckless man, alone, over these hills, in a country you don’t know? Your men are penned in Circe’s sty, over there, shut up like swine in their close pens. Have you come to set them free? I tell you, you won’t get away yourself — you’ll stay there with the rest. But never mind. I’ll save you from it; I’ll bring you through. Here — take this herb, a good one, and keep it on you when you go to Circe’s house, and it will turn the day of ruin from your head.

“Now I’ll tell you every trick of Circe’s deadly craft. She’ll mix you a brew, and slip her drugs into the food — but she won’t be able to charm you, for this good herb I’m giving you won’t let her. Here is what to do. The moment Circe strikes you with her long wand, draw the sharp sword from beside your thigh and rush at her as if you meant to run her through. She’ll shrink back in terror and ask you into her bed — and you must not refuse a goddess’s bed, not if you want her to free your men and take care of you. But make her swear first the great oath of the blessed gods that she’ll plot no new harm against you — or else, once she has you stripped, she’ll unman you and leave you good for nothing.”

And as he spoke, the slayer of Argos pulled the herb out of the ground and showed me what it was. Its root was black, but the flower was white as milk. The gods call it moly. It is hard for mortal men to dig — but the gods can do anything. Then Hermes was gone, away over the wooded island, back toward high Olympus, and I went on to the house of Circe, and my heart was dark with many thoughts as I walked.

I stood at the gates of the lovely-haired goddess and called, and she heard me and came at once, opened the bright doors, and asked me in; and I followed, sick at heart. She brought me to a beautiful chair, silver-studded, with a footstool under my feet, and mixed me a brew in a golden cup to drink — and into it she slipped her drug, for her mind was set on harm. She gave it to me, and I drank, and it did not charm me. Then she struck me with her wand and said, “Off to the sty now — go wallow with the rest of your friends.” But I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh and rushed at her as if to kill her, and she gave a great scream and ducked under the blade and caught me by the knees, and the words came pouring out: “Who are you? Where do you come from — what city, what people? It is a marvel: you drank my drugs and they did not charm you, and no other man alive has ever withstood them, once they passed his lips. There is a mind in you no spell can touch. You must be Odysseus, the man of many turns — the one Hermes of the golden wand always told me would come here, on his way home from Troy in his swift black ship. Come, then — sheathe your sword. Let us two go up into my bed, and lie together, and learn to trust each other there.”

But I answered her: “Circe — how can you ask me to be gentle with you, when in this very hall you have turned my men into swine? And now you have me here, and your mind is set on treachery, asking me to your bed, so that once you have me stripped you can unman me and leave me nothing. No — I will not go up into your bed unless you bring yourself to swear me a great oath, goddess, that you’ll plot no new harm against me.” So I spoke, and at once she swore the oath I asked. And when she had sworn it and the oath was done, then I went up into the beautiful bed of Circe.

Meanwhile her four handmaids were at their work about the hall — the spirits of the springs and the woodland groves and the sacred rivers that run down to the sea. One spread the chairs with fine purple cloths and laid a smooth carpet beneath. Another drew up silver tables before them and set out baskets of gold. A third mixed sweet wine with water in a silver bowl and ranged the golden cups. And the fourth carried in water and lit a great fire beneath a tall cauldron until the water grew hot. When it sang in the gleaming bronze, she set me in the bath and washed me, easing the soul-deep weariness out of my limbs with water tempered to my liking, pouring it over my head and shoulders. And when she had bathed me and rubbed me with rich oil, she dressed me in a fine cloak and tunic and led me to a chair, a beautiful one, silver-studded, with a footstool for my feet. A maid brought water in a lovely golden pitcher and poured it over my hands into a silver basin, and drew a polished table up beside me. A grave housekeeper set out bread and many good things, and pressed me to eat. But I had no heart for it. I sat with my mind elsewhere, brooding, sensing trouble still.

When Circe saw me sitting there, not reaching for the food, deep in my grief, she came close and spoke winged words: “Odysseus, why do you sit like this, like a man struck dumb, eating your own heart and touching neither food nor drink? Do you still think I mean some treachery? You have no cause to fear. I have already sworn you the great oath.”

And I answered her: “Circe — what man with any sense of right could bring himself to eat or drink before he had seen his comrades free, here, with his own eyes? If you truly mean me well, set them loose. Let me see my faithful friends.” So I spoke, and Circe walked out through the hall with her wand in her hand, opened the doors of the sty, and drove them out — and they came, in the likeness of nine-year hogs. They stood there facing her, and she went down the line and rubbed each one with another drug, and the bristles the first vile potion had grown dropped away, and they were men again — younger than before, and taller, and finer to look at. They knew me, and each one caught my hand, and such a sweet weeping rose among them that the whole house rang with it; and even the goddess was moved. She came and stood beside me and said: “Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes — go down now to your swift ship and the sea. First haul the ship up onto the dry land, and stow all your gear and your cargo away in the caves. Then come back here, and bring your faithful comrades with you.”

I agreed, and went down to the swift ship and the shore, and there I found the men by the ship, grieving, the tears running down. The way calves around the farmyard come bounding to meet the cows when they trail home full-fed to the pen — leaping, mooing, the byres ringing, the fences no longer able to hold them — so my men, the moment they laid eyes on me, came crowding round me weeping; and to their hearts it felt as if they had come home, home to rugged Ithaca, to the very town where they were born and raised. And through their tears they cried out: “You’re back — and to us, brilliant Odysseus, it’s as if we’d reached Ithaca, our own land. But tell us — how did the rest of our company die?”

I spoke to them gently: “First let’s drag the ship up onto the land, and stow our gear and all our cargo in the caves. Then come with me, all of you, and you’ll see your comrades in Circe’s holy halls, eating and drinking with plenty before them.” At that they were ready to come at once — all but Eurylochus, who tried to hold them back, and his words were urgent: “Where are we rushing, you poor fools? Why are we so hungry for our own ruin — going down to Circe’s house, where she’ll turn the lot of us into pigs, or wolves, or lions, and pen us to guard her great hall by force? It will be just like the Cyclops, when our friends went into that cave, and this same reckless Odysseus led them — it was his folly that got them killed.”

When I heard that, I half drew the long sword from beside my thigh, ready to strike his head off and roll it in the dust, close kin of mine though he was by marriage. But the men crowded round and held me back, every one of them pleading: “Born of the gods — let this man stay behind, if you will, to guard the ship. But lead the rest of us to Circe’s holy house.” So they spoke, and went up from the ship and the sea. And Eurylochus did not stay behind by the ship after all; he came along too, cowed by the hard edge in my voice.

Meanwhile Circe had been taking kind care of the others, back at her hall. She had them bathed and rubbed with oil, dressed in soft fleecy cloaks and tunics; and we found them all at the feast, in good comfort. When the men saw one another face to face and knew each other, they wept for joy until the whole house echoed with it. Then the lovely goddess came up to me and said: “Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes — no more of this weeping now. I know it myself, all you have suffered on the teeming sea, all the harm cruel men did you ashore. Come — eat, drink, until the spirit rises again in your chests, the spirit you had when you first sailed from your own land, from rugged Ithaca. As it is, you are worn thin and faint of heart, your minds always on the bitterness of your wandering; there is no joy left in you, for you have suffered so much.”

So she spoke, and our proud hearts said yes. And there we stayed, day after day, a whole year round, feasting on meat past counting and sweet wine. But when the year had turned full circle — the seasons coming round, the moons waning away, the long days running out — then my faithful men called me aside and said: “My lord — it is time, at last, to remember your own country, if it is fated you should be saved and reach your high-roofed house and the land of your fathers.”

So they spoke, and my proud heart agreed. So all that day, until the sun went down, we sat feasting on meat past counting and sweet wine; and when the sun had set and the dark came on, the men lay down to sleep through the shadowy halls. But I climbed into Circe’s lovely bed, and clasped her knees, and the goddess heard me out as I begged her: “Circe — keep now the promise you made me, to send me home. My heart is set on it, and so are my men’s; they wear me down with their grieving, whenever you are out of earshot.”

And the lovely goddess answered me at once: “Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes — you shall not stay on in my house against your will. But there is another journey you must make first. You have to go down to the house of Hades and dread Persephone, to consult the ghost of Tiresias of Thebes, the blind seer, whose mind is sound even in death. To him alone, dead as he is, Persephone has left his wits whole; the other shades go flitting about like shadows.”

When I heard that, the heart broke in me. I sat there on the bed and wept; I had no more wish to live, no wish to see the light of the sun. But when I had wept and writhed my fill, I said to her: “Circe — who is to guide me on that road? No man has ever yet reached the house of Hades in a black ship.”

And the lovely goddess answered me at once: “You will need no guide. Step the mast, spread the white sail, and sit still; the breath of the North Wind will carry you there of itself. When your ship has crossed the stream of Ocean, you will come to a low shore and the groves of Persephone — tall poplars, and willows that drop their fruit unripe. Beach your ship there, by the deep eddies of Ocean, and go on yourself to the moldering house of Hades. There the river Pyriphlegethon and the Cocytus, which is an arm of the water of the Styx, run into Acheron, and there is a rock where the two roaring rivers meet.

“Go up close to that, hero — listen well — and dig a pit, a forearm’s length each way, and pour around it an offering to all the dead: first honey mixed with milk, then sweet wine, and the third time water, and scatter white barley meal over it all. Pray hard to the strengthless heads of the dead, and vow that when you reach Ithaca you will sacrifice a barren heifer, your best, in your halls, and heap the fire with good things; and to Tiresias alone, apart, vow a black ram, the finest in your flocks. When you have prayed to the famous nations of the dead, then sacrifice a ram and a black ewe, turning their heads down toward Erebus while you yourself look away, back toward the streams of the river. Then the souls of the dead and gone will come thronging up. Call to your men, and have them flay and burn the sheep that lie there killed by the pitiless bronze, with prayers to the gods — to mighty Hades and dread Persephone. And you yourself, draw the sharp sword from beside your thigh and sit there, and do not let the strengthless heads of the dead come near the blood until you have asked Tiresias all you would know. The seer will come to you soon, leader of men, and he will tell you the way and the measure of your road, and how you are to make your passage home over the teeming sea.”

She had hardly finished when golden Dawn came up. Circe dressed me in cloak and tunic, and threw about herself a long silvery robe, fine and graceful, and clasped a beautiful golden belt at her waist, and veiled her head. Then I went through the hall and roused the men, standing over each one with gentle words: “No more sweet sleep now. Up — we are going. Lady Circe has told me the whole of it.” And their proud hearts agreed.

Yet even then I did not lead them all away unharmed. There was one of us, Elpenor — the youngest of all, no great fighter, and none too steady in his wits. He had wandered off to sleep apart from the rest, up on the flat roof of Circe’s house, looking for the cool, heavy with wine. He heard the noise and the bustle of the men setting out, and started up all at once, and clean forgot to go back down by the long ladder. He pitched headlong from the roof, and his neck snapped at the spine, and his soul went down to the house of Hades.

When the rest were gathered, I spoke to them: “You think, no doubt, that we are bound now for home, for our own dear country. But Circe has set us another road — down to the house of Hades and dread Persephone, to consult the ghost of Tiresias of Thebes.” At that the heart broke in them; they sank down where they stood, groaning, and tore their hair. But no good came of their grieving. And as we made our way down to the swift ship and the shore, weeping, the great tears falling, Circe had gone on ahead and tethered a ram and a black ewe close by the ship — slipping past us easily, unseen. For who can lay eyes on a god going to and fro, when the god does not wish to be seen?
