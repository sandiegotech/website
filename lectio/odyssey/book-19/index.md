# The Odyssey, Book XIX

By the Fire, and the Scar

Odysseus stayed behind in the hall, turning over in his mind how, with Athena’s help, he might bring the suitors down. At last he said to Telemachus: “We must carry the weapons out of here and stow them away inside. When the suitors miss them and ask, put them off with some excuse. Tell them you have moved the arms out of reach of the smoke, that they are not what they were when your father sailed but have grown dull and grimed with soot. And add something heavier than that: say you are afraid Zeus may set them quarreling over their wine and they will turn the iron on each other, fouling both the feast and the courtship — for the iron itself draws a man on.”

Telemachus thought his father spoke well, so he called the nurse Eurycleia and said: “Nurse, keep the women shut in their quarters while I carry my father’s arms down into the storeroom. No one tends them now that he is gone; they have gathered soot all through my boyhood. I mean to put them where the smoke cannot reach them.”

“I only wish, child,” Eurycleia answered, “that you would take the whole house into your own hands at last and look after everything yourself. But who is to go with you and light your way? You will not let the maids carry a torch for you.”

“The stranger,” said Telemachus. “A man who eats my bread can earn it, wherever he comes from.”

Eurycleia did as she was told and bolted the women inside. Then Odysseus and his son fell to it, carrying the helmets and the bossed shields and the long spears within; and Athena went before them holding a golden lamp that filled the room with a soft, marvelous light. Telemachus stopped and stared. “Father,” he whispered, “my eyes are seeing a great wonder. The walls, the rafters and crossbeams, the pillars that bear them — everything glows as if a fire were running over it. There is surely a god here, one of those who hold the wide heaven.”

“Hush,” said Odysseus. “Hold your tongue and ask nothing. This is the way of the gods who live forever. Go now and lie down. Leave me here to talk with your mother and the maids. She will be heavy with grief, and there is much she will want to ask me.”

So Telemachus went by torchlight across the court to the room where he always slept, and there he lay until morning. And Odysseus was left alone in the hall, still turning over in his mind how, with Athena’s help, he might bring the suitors down.

Then Penelope came down from her room, lovely as Artemis or golden Aphrodite. They drew up for her, near the fire in her usual place, a chair inlaid with whorls of silver and ivory — the work of the craftsman Icmalius, who had built a footstool into it, all of a piece, and spread it thick with a fleece. There she sat, and the maids came out of the women’s quarters to join her. They began clearing away the tables where the shameless suitors had dined, carrying off the leavings of bread and the cups they had drunk from, and they raked the dead coals out of the braziers and piled them high with fresh wood for light and warmth. And Melantho started in on Odysseus a second time. “Still here, stranger?” she said. “Do you mean to slink about the house all night and spy on the women? Get out, you wretch, and be glad of the supper you’ve had — or you’ll have a firebrand at your back to drive you through the door.”

Odysseus gave her a dark look. “My good woman,” he said, “why come at me so hard? Because I am filthy, and dressed in rags, and have to beg my way through the world like other tramps? Need does that to a man. I too was rich once, with a fine house of my own, and in those days I gave to many a wanderer like the one I am now, whoever he was and whatever he needed. I had servants past counting and all the comforts of a man called wealthy. Then Zeus took it from me — such was his will. So take care, woman, that you do not someday lose the high place you flaunt now over the other maids; take care your mistress does not turn against you in her anger, or that Odysseus does not come home — for there is still a hope of it. And even if he is dead, as you suppose, there is his son Telemachus, whom Apollo keeps, and nothing the women do amiss in this house escapes him now. He is no longer a boy.”

Penelope heard him, and turned on the maid. “You brazen creature,” she said, “do not think your insolence is hidden from me — you will pay for it with your own head. You knew well, for I told you so myself, that I meant to see the stranger and ask him about my husband, who is the reason I live in such endless grief.”

Then she said to her head housekeeper Eurynome: “Bring a chair, and a fleece to lay on it, so the stranger can sit while he tells his story and listens to what I have to say. I want to question him.”

Eurynome brought the chair at once and spread a fleece on it, and when Odysseus had sat down Penelope began: “Stranger, the first thing I will ask is this — who are you, and where do you come from? Where is your city, and who are your people?”

“Lady,” Odysseus answered, “who on the whole wide earth could find a word of blame for you? Your fame goes up to the very floor of heaven. You are like some blameless king who fears the gods and holds the right, ruling a great and warlike people: under him the dark earth bears its wheat and barley, the trees hang heavy with fruit, the flocks bring forth without fail, the sea gives up its fish — all because he governs well, and his people prosper under him. So ask me anything else here in your house, but do not press me about my birth and my country, or you will fill me with fresh grief over the memories. I am a man weighed down with sorrow, and it is not fitting to sit weeping and wailing in another’s house — there is no good in grieving on and on without end. One of your women might take it ill, or you yourself, and say my eyes swim because I am heavy with wine.”

“Stranger,” Penelope answered, “whatever grace I had of face or form, the gods stripped from me the day the Greeks set sail for Troy and my husband went with them. If he were to come home and take up my life again, I would be better thought of, and show a fairer face to the world. As it is, I am bowed down with sorrow; some god has heaped grief on grief upon me. All the chiefs who hold power in the islands — Dulichium, Same, wooded Zacynthus — and the lords of rocky Ithaca too, are courting me against my will and bleeding my house white. So I can pay no heed to strangers, or to suppliants, or to heralds who serve the people; I do nothing but ache for Odysseus. They press me to marry, and I have had to spin out my tricks. First some god put it in my heart to set up a great loom in my hall and begin weaving a wide web of fine thread. And I said to them: ‘Young men, my suitors, since noble Odysseus is dead, you are eager for the wedding, I know; but wait, be patient, until I finish this cloth — I don’t want all my spinning to go to waste — a shroud for the hero Laertes, against the day that death’s long grief comes and takes him. The women of Ithaca would never forgive me if a man who won so much should lie in his grave without a shroud.’ So I spoke, and their pride gave way. Then by day I would weave at the great web, and by night, with torches set beside me, I would unravel it again. Three years I fooled them and kept them believing it. But when the fourth year came round with the turning seasons, and the months waned and the long days were done, those worthless girls, my own maids, gave me away, and the suitors broke in and caught me at it, and raged at me — so I was forced to finish it, willing or not. And now I can find no way out of this marriage, no further dodge. My parents are pressing me hard to take a husband, and my son chafes at the way these men devour his living, for he sees it all clearly now; he is a grown man, well able to keep his own house, and the gods have given him a good heart. But for all that — tell me who you are, and where you come from. You were not born of the oak of old stories, or sprung from a rock.”

Then Odysseus answered: “Honored wife of Odysseus, since you will not let it go, I will tell you — though it costs me something, as it must cost any man who has been an exile as long as I have, and suffered among as many peoples. Still, I will answer what you ask. There is a land called Crete, in the middle of the wine-dark sea, fair and fertile, ringed with water — a crowded land, with ninety cities, and many tongues mingled in it: there are Achaeans, and the true-born Cretans, and Dorians in their three tribes, and the noble Pelasgians. The great city among them is Cnossus, where Minos was king, who every ninth year took counsel with mighty Zeus himself. Minos was the father of Deucalion, and Deucalion was my father, for he had two sons — Idomeneus and myself. Idomeneus sailed for Troy in the curved ships, and I, the younger, am called Aethon; my brother was the elder and the better man. It was in Crete that I saw Odysseus and gave him welcome, for a storm had driven him off his course on his way to Troy, carrying him past Cape Malea and into Amnisus, by the cave of Eileithyia, where the harbors are hard to enter and he barely rode out the gale. The first thing he did was come up into the town and ask for Idomeneus, calling himself his sworn and honored friend — but Idomeneus had already sailed for Troy, ten or twelve days before. So I took him into my own house and gave him a guest’s full welcome, for I had plenty of everything. I fed his crew from the town’s stores, and gathered up wine and oxen for their sacrifices, until they had eaten and drunk their fill. The Greeks stayed with me twelve days, for a great north wind was blowing, so fierce a man could hardly keep his feet on land — some unfriendly god had raised it. On the thirteenth the wind dropped, and they put out to sea.”

So he went on, telling lie after lie that sounded like the truth; and Penelope wept to hear it, for her heart was melting. As the snow melts on the high mountains when the West Wind has heaped it up and the East Wind thaws it, until the rivers run full and overflow their banks — so her fair cheeks ran with weeping for the husband who sat there beside her all the while. Odysseus pitied his wife as she wept, but he held his eyes steady as horn or iron in their lids; he did not let so much as a flicker show, so cunningly did he master his own tears. And when she had eased her heart with weeping, she turned to him again: “Now, stranger, I think I will test you, and see whether it is true that you took my husband and his men into your house, as you say. Tell me, then — what kind of clothes did he wear, and what sort of man was he to look at, and the companions with him?”

“Lady,” Odysseus answered, “it is hard to say after so long. It is twenty years now since he left my country and went his way. But I will tell you the picture my mind still holds. Odysseus wore a thick wool cloak, dyed sea-purple and lined double, and it was fastened with a brooch of gold, with two sheaths for the pin. On the face of it was worked a marvel: a hound holding a dappled fawn pinned beneath its forepaws, gripping it as it struggled — and everyone wondered at the workmanship, the way the hound, all of gold, throttled the fawn while the fawn writhed with its hooves to be free. I noticed the tunic against his skin too, smooth as the skin off a dried onion, soft as that, and shining like the sun; the women could not take their eyes from it. But I tell you, and mark it well — I cannot say whether Odysseus wore these things from home, or whether one of his comrades gave them to him aboard ship, or some host on his travels made him the gift; for he had friends everywhere, and few among the Greeks were his equal. I myself gave him a bronze sword, and a fine double cloak of sea-purple, and a tunic that fell to his feet, and I saw him off to his ship with every honor. He had a herald with him, a little older than himself, and I can tell you his look as well: round-shouldered, dark-skinned, with thick curling hair. His name was Eurybates, and Odysseus held him closer than any of the others, because the man’s mind ran with his own.”

At this Penelope was moved more deeply still, for she knew the signs Odysseus laid before her were true beyond doubt. And when she had wept her fill she said to him: “Stranger, I pitied you before, but now you will be honored and made welcome in my house. It was I who gave him those clothes. I took them from the storeroom and folded them myself, and I pinned on the bright brooch to set them off. But I shall never welcome him home again. It was a cruel fate that sent him sailing off to that city I will not even name — the name of ruin.”

Then Odysseus answered: “Honored wife of Odysseus, do not waste your fair face and wear out your heart any longer weeping for your husband — though I will not blame you for it. Any woman grieves for the man she married and loved and bore children to, even were he a lesser man than Odysseus, who they say was like the gods. But dry your tears now and hear me, for I will tell you the truth and hide nothing: I have heard, lately and close at hand, that Odysseus is alive and on his way home. He is among the Thesprotians, in their rich country, and bringing back great treasure that he has gathered up and down the land. He lost his ship and all his men on the wine-dark sea, leaving the island of Thrinacia, for Zeus and the Sun were angry that his crew had slaughtered the cattle of the Sun, and every man of them went down. But Odysseus clung to the keel and the waves carried him to the shore of the Phaeacians, who are close kin to the gods. They honored him as though he were a god himself, gave him many gifts, and would have brought him home safe of their own accord. Odysseus would have been here long ago, except that he judged it wiser to go on from land to land, gathering wealth — for no man alive is as cunning as he is; there is no one to match him. Pheidon, king of the Thesprotians, told me all this, and swore to me, pouring the wine of his oath, that the ship was hauled down to the water and the crew was ready to bring Odysseus home to his own country. But me he sent off first, for a Thesprotian ship happened to be sailing for Dulichium, where the wheat grows. He showed me the treasure Odysseus had heaped up — enough lying in King Pheidon’s house to keep a man’s family ten generations. As for Odysseus, the king said, he had gone to Dodona to learn the will of Zeus from the great oak, whether after so long away he should come back to Ithaca openly or in secret. So you may be sure he is safe and near, and will be here soon — he cannot stay away from his own much longer. And I will give you my oath on it: let Zeus be my witness, first and highest of the gods, and this hearth of Odysseus to which I have come — everything I have told you will come to pass. This very year Odysseus will be here, between the waning of this moon and the rising of the next.”

“May it be so,” Penelope answered. “If your words come true, you will have such gifts and such goodwill from me that everyone who meets you will call you blessed. But my own heart tells me how it will go. Odysseus will not come home, and you will not get your escort either, for there are no longer any masters in this house such as Odysseus was — if ever there truly was such a man — to welcome an honored stranger and speed him on his way. Even so — wash his feet, you maids, and make him a bed: a couch with rugs and bright blankets, so he can lie warm until the golden Dawn. And early in the morning bathe him and rub him with oil, so he can sit at meat in the hall beside Telemachus. It will be the worse for any of those hateful men who vexes him — say what they like, they will get nothing more done in this house. For how, stranger, are you to know whether I am better than other women in heart and in good sense, if I let you sit at my table unwashed and dressed in rags? Our lives are short. A hard man, who deals hardly, is cursed by all while he lives and mocked when he is dead; but a man who is just, and acts justly — strangers carry his name far over the earth, and many call him good.”

Odysseus answered: “Honored wife of Odysseus, I have had no use for rugs and bright blankets since the day I first lost sight of the snowy mountains of Crete and went down to my ship. I will lie as I have lain through many a sleepless night before. Night after night I have passed in some rough bed, waiting for the holy Dawn. And as for washing my feet — I want none of it. No young girl of those who serve your house is going to lay a hand on my feet. But if there is some old woman here, faithful, who has borne as much trouble in her life as I have in mine, I will let her wash them.”

“Dear guest,” Penelope said, “of all the strangers who have ever come to my house from far away, not one has spoken so wisely and so well as you. There is an old woman here, faithful and full of good sense — the same who took my poor husband into her arms the night he was born, and nursed him at her breast. She is feeble now, but she shall wash your feet.” Then she called out: “Come, Eurycleia, and wash this man — he is your master’s age. Odysseus must by now have hands and feet much like his, for hard trouble ages a man fast.”

At those words the old woman hid her face in her hands and broke into tears, and her grief poured out of her: “Oh, my child, what can I do for you! Surely Zeus has hated you above all men, god-fearing as your heart was. No mortal ever burned the Thunderer so many rich thigh-bones, or gave him such fine offerings, praying to reach a green old age and see your own son grow into a man — and yet he, of all men, you he has shut out from the day of his homecoming. I dare say the women in some far palace where Odysseus has come are jeering at him now, the way these shameless sluts here have jeered at you, and no wonder you would not let them touch you and add their insults to it. But I will wash your feet gladly — for Penelope’s sake, and for your own, for you have stirred my heart to its depths. And hear this, for there is something in it: many a worn stranger has come to this house in his trouble, but never yet, I swear, have I seen one so like Odysseus — your build, your voice, the very set of your feet.”

“Old woman,” Odysseus answered, “that is what everyone says who has seen us both — that we are wonderfully alike. You have an eye, to have marked it.”

Then the old woman took the gleaming basin she used for washing feet and poured in cold water, a good deal of it, and added hot to warm it. Odysseus sat by the hearth, but he turned himself quickly toward the dark, for it had come to him all at once that when she took his leg in her hands she might know the old scar, and the whole truth would come spilling out. And so it was: the moment she came near and laid hold of her master, she knew the scar — the wound a boar had given him, years before, with its white tusk, the day he hunted on Mount Parnassus with the sons of his grandfather Autolycus. Autolycus was the finest thief and the best swearer of oaths in all the world; the god Hermes himself had given him that gift, for Autolycus burned for him the pleasing thigh-bones of lambs and kids, and the god went gladly at his side. Once, when Autolycus had come to the rich land of Ithaca, he found his daughter’s child newly born; and when supper was done Eurycleia set the baby on his knees and said: “Autolycus, find a name yourself now for your daughter’s son. He is a child long prayed for.”

And Autolycus answered: “My daughter, and my son by marriage, give him the name I tell you. I have come here a man who has been angered at many in my time — men and women both, the whole world over. So let the boy’s name be Odysseus: the child of anger. And when he is grown and comes to his mother’s great house on Parnassus, where my wealth lies, I will give him a share of it and send him home rejoicing.”

So when Odysseus was grown he went to Parnassus to claim the gifts. Autolycus and his sons took his hand and welcomed him warmly, and his grandmother Amphithea threw her arms around him and kissed his head and his two bright eyes. Autolycus called on his sons to make the meal ready, and they did as he asked, and led in a five-year-old bull, flayed it and dressed it, and cut it into joints, and carved the joints small and ran them on the spits and roasted them through, and shared out the portions. So all day long until the sun went down they feasted, and no man wanted for his fair share. And when the sun had set and darkness came on, they lay down and took the gift of sleep.

When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, the sons of Autolycus went out to hunt with their hounds, and noble Odysseus went with them. They climbed the steep, wooded flank of Parnassus and soon came up into its windy folds; and just as the sun, fresh-risen from the deep, smooth flood of Ocean, began to strike the fields, the beaters came down into a glen. The hounds ran ahead, casting for the scent, and behind them came the sons of Autolycus, and among them Odysseus, close on the dogs, swinging a long spear. There, in a thicket so dense the wet winds could not pierce it nor the bright sun get through, with the fallen leaves lying deep on the ground, a great boar lay in his lair. He heard the tramp of the men’s feet and the baying of the hounds closing in, and he charged out to meet them, his bristles standing up along his spine, fire flashing from his eyes, and stood at bay. Odysseus was the first to drive in, lunging with the long spear to kill — but the boar got there first and gored him above the knee, ripping a long furrow of flesh as he came in sideways, though the tusk did not reach the bone. Odysseus caught him through the right shoulder, and the bright point drove clean out the other side, and the boar dropped in the dust with a scream and his life went out of him. The sons of Autolycus saw to the carcass, and bound up the wound of faultless, godlike Odysseus with care, and stanched the dark blood with a charm, and got him home to their father’s house. And when Autolycus and his sons had healed him well, they sent him back to his own land of Ithaca with splendid gifts, glad of him and he of them. His father and mother were overjoyed to see him, and asked him about everything — how he had come by the scar — and he told them the whole of it: how the boar had ripped him with its white tusk when he was up on Parnassus hunting with the sons of Autolycus.

This was the scar the old woman now ran her flat hands over, and knew, and let go his foot. The leg dropped into the basin; the bronze rang, and it tipped, and the water went pouring out across the floor. Joy and grief seized her at once, and her eyes filled with tears, and her voice caught in her throat. She reached up and took Odysseus by the chin and said: “You are Odysseus, my own dear child — you are, and I never knew you, not until I had my hands on the body of my master.”

And she looked toward Penelope, to tell her that her own husband was here in the house. But Penelope could not meet her eyes or take in what was passing, for Athena had turned her mind away. Then Odysseus, with his right hand, caught the old woman by the throat, and with his left he pulled her close, and said: “Nurse — do you want to destroy me, you who nursed me at your own breast, now that after twenty years of pain I have come home at last to my own country? You have found me out; a god has put it in your heart. Then be silent. Let no one else in the house hear a word of it. For I tell you this, and it will be done: if the god gives these proud suitors into my hands, I will not spare you, my nurse though you are, when I come to kill the other women in this house.”

“My child,” Eurycleia answered, “what a thing to say! You know how firm I am — nothing can bend me or break me. I will hold as hard as stone, as iron. And hear me on this, and keep it close: when the god has put the proud suitors under your hand, I will tell you which of the women in the house have shamed themselves, and which are blameless.”

“Nurse,” Odysseus answered, “why should you name them? There is no need. I will mark them well enough myself, every one. Keep silent, and leave the rest to the gods.”

At that the old woman went off through the hall to fetch fresh water, for the first had all been spilled. And when she had washed him and rubbed him with oil, Odysseus drew his chair nearer the fire again to warm himself, and hid the scar under his rags. Then Penelope began to speak to him once more.

“Stranger,” she said, “there is one small thing more I would ask you. It is near the hour of rest — at least for anyone whom sweet sleep can take in spite of grief. But to me the gods have given a sorrow past all measure. By day, even at my work, looking after the house and the women, I find no comfort but in mourning and tears; and when night comes, and the others go to their beds, I lie awake, and my heart is torn by trouble that will not let go. As the daughter of Pandareus, the pale-brown nightingale, sings out her sweet song in the new spring, perched in the thick leaves of the trees, and pours it out in run after trembling run, mourning her own child Itylus, the son of King Zethus, whom she killed by mischance with the bronze — so my own heart goes this way and that, torn whether I should stay here with my son and keep all safe: my house, my servants, my high roof, out of respect for my marriage bed and the word of the people; or whether I should go now with the best of these Greeks who court me in the hall and bring the richest gifts. While my son was still a child and had no understanding, he would not hear of my leaving his father’s house; but now that he is grown a man, he begs me to go, fretting at the way the suitors eat up his inheritance. But listen now to a dream of mine, and read it for me if you can. I keep twenty geese about the house, that eat their grain soaked in water, and I love to watch them. I dreamed a great eagle with a hooked beak swooped down off the mountain and broke all their necks and killed them, and they lay scattered dead about the hall, and the eagle soared back up into the bright sky. And I wept and wailed there in my dream, and the fair-haired women of the town gathered round me, while I cried my heart out because the eagle had killed my geese. Then he came back and settled on a jutting beam of the roof, and checked my grief, and spoke to me in a human voice: ‘Take heart, daughter of far-famed Icarius. This is no dream but a waking truth, and it will come to pass. The geese are the suitors; and I, who was the eagle, am your own husband, home again at last, and I will bring a foul death down on every one of them.’ At that I woke, and looked, and there were my geese in the yard at their grain, just as before.”

“Lady,” Odysseus answered, “there is no twisting this dream to mean anything else, since Odysseus himself has told you how he will make it good. The suitors’ death is clear, every one of them; not one will get away from his fate.”

“Stranger,” Penelope answered, “dreams are slippery things, hard to read, and not all of them come true for the people who see them. There are two gates through which our fleeting dreams pass: one is made of horn, the other of ivory. The dreams that come through the carved ivory are deceivers, carrying words that come to nothing; but those that come through the polished horn bring true things to pass, for the one who sees them. I do not think my own strange dream came through there — much as my son and I would welcome it if it did. And one thing more I will tell you, and hold it in your heart: the dawn is coming now whose evil light will cut me off from the house of Odysseus. For I am going to set the suitors a contest, the axes. Odysseus used to stand twelve axe-heads in a row down the hall, like the ribs set up to build a ship, and then stand well back and send an arrow clean through the whole twelve. Now I will set the suitors to do the same; and whichever of them strings the bow most easily in his hands and shoots an arrow through all twelve axes, with him I will go, leaving behind this house of my marriage, so fine a house, so full of good things — the house I think I shall remember even in my dreams.”

Then Odysseus answered: “Honored wife of Odysseus, do not put off this contest in your house. Odysseus will be here, the man of many turns, before these men can take that polished bow and string it and shoot the iron through.”

“Stranger,” Penelope answered, “if you would only sit here in my hall and go on talking to me, sleep would never fall on my eyes. But no one can do without rest forever; the gods have set every mortal his measure of all things, even of sleep, here on the grain-giving earth. So I will go up now to my own room and lie down on that bed of mourning, the bed I have soaked with my tears since the day Odysseus sailed away to that city of ruin I will not name.”

So she went up to her bright upper room, not alone, for her women went with her; and there she wept for Odysseus, her dear husband, until Athena cast sweet sleep over her eyelids.
