# The Odyssey, Book VIII

The Games of the Phaeacians

When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, mighty Alcinous rose from his bed, and so did Odysseus, sacker of cities. Alcinous led the way to the Phaeacian meeting-place, which was laid out beside the ships, and there the two of them sat down side by side on benches of polished stone. Meanwhile Pallas Athena went up and down the town in the likeness of one of wise Alcinous’ heralds, busy on her plan to bring Odysseus home. She went up to each man in turn and said: “Come now, you captains and counselors of the Phaeacians — come to the assembly, all of you, and hear the stranger who has just come to the house of King Alcinous after long wandering on the sea. He looks like one of the immortal gods.”

With these words she set them all astir, and quickly the seats and the standing-room filled with the gathering crowd. Many a man marveled at the sight of clever Odysseus, for Athena had shed a wondrous grace over his head and shoulders, and made him look taller and broader than before, so that he might win the goodwill of the Phaeacians and come through all the trials of strength to which they would challenge him. When they had all come together, Alcinous stood and spoke:

“Hear me, captains and counselors of the Phaeacians, and let me say what is on my mind. This stranger — I do not know who he is — has come wandering to my house from somewhere out of the East or the West. He asks for passage home, and begs us to make it certain. So let us speed him on his way, as we have sped others before him; for no one who ever came to this house has had to linger long, grieving for want of an escort. Come — let us drag a black ship down to the bright sea, one that has never yet made a voyage, and pick fifty-two of our finest young men to crew her. When you have lashed the oars to the thole-pins, leave the ship and come up to my house and make ready a feast in haste; I will provide for all of you. These are my orders to the young men who will sail. As for you lords who carry the staff, come to my fine halls, so that we may welcome the stranger there — I will take no refusal. And call in godlike Demodocus, the bard; the god has given him the gift of song above all others, to gladden us with whatever his heart moves him to sing.”

So he spoke, and led the way, and the staff-bearing lords followed, while a herald went to fetch the divine singer. The fifty-two chosen oarsmen went down to the shore of the restless sea, as they had been told, and when they reached the ship and the water they dragged the black hull into the deep, set the mast and the sails inside her, lashed the oars in their leather loops in good order, and spread the white sails aloft. They moored her well out from the land, and then came up to the great house of wise Alcinous. The galleries and the courts and the walled grounds were thronged with the crowds that gathered, young and old alike. For them Alcinous slaughtered a dozen sheep, eight white-tusked boars, and two shambling oxen; these they skinned and dressed and made ready, and laid out a glad feast.

Then the herald came near, leading the faithful bard, Demodocus, whom the Muse had loved above all men, though she had given him both good and evil: she had taken the light from his eyes, but granted him the gift of sweet song. Pontonous set a silver-studded chair for him in the middle of the guests, leaning it against a tall pillar; and the herald hung the clear-toned lyre on a peg above his head, and showed him with his hands how to reach for it. Beside him he set a fine table and a basket of bread, and a cup of wine, to drink whenever his heart desired.

So they reached out their hands to the good things spread before them, and when they had taken their fill of food and drink, the Muse stirred the bard to sing of the famous deeds of men — a tale whose fame had then climbed all the way to the wide heaven: the quarrel between Odysseus and Achilles, son of Peleus, and the savage words they had hurled at each other one day as they sat at a rich feast in honor of the gods. Agamemnon, king of men, had been glad in his heart to see the best of the Greeks at each other’s throats; for Phoebus Apollo had foretold it to him at holy Delphi, when he crossed the stone threshold to consult the oracle. There, by the will of Zeus, had begun the rolling wave of ruin that broke over Greeks and Trojans alike.

So the famous bard sang, but Odysseus took his great purple cloak in his strong hands and drew it up over his head and hid his handsome face, for he was ashamed to let the Phaeacians see the tears welling under his brows. Each time the divine singer paused, Odysseus would wipe the tears away, lower the cloak from his head, and lift his two-handled cup and pour an offering to the gods. But every time the bard began again — for the Phaeacian lords kept urging him on, they loved his song so — Odysseus would cover his head and weep. No one in all that company noticed his tears but Alcinous, who sat close beside him and caught the deep, heavy sighs he was heaving. At once the king spoke up to the Phaeacians, who love their oars: “Hear me, captains and counselors of the Phaeacians. By now our hearts have had their fill of the shared feast and of the lyre, the feast’s true companion. Let us go out now and try our hand at every kind of contest, so that when our guest reaches home he can tell his friends how far we outstrip all other men as boxers and wrestlers, as jumpers and as runners.”

With that he led the way, and the others followed. A herald hung the clear-toned lyre back on its peg, took Demodocus by the hand, and led him out of the hall, along the same road that the noblest Phaeacians were taking to look on at the games. They went out to the gathering-ground, and a great crowd, past counting, came after them, and many fine young men stood up to compete. Acroneus rose, and Ocyalus and Elatreus, Nauteus and Prymneus, Anchialus and Eretmeus, Ponteus and Proreus, Thoon and Anabesineus, and Amphialus, son of Polyneus, son of Tecton. Up too stood Euryalus, son of Naubolus, the equal of murderous Ares himself, and the best-looking man among all the Phaeacians but for flawless Laodamas. And three sons of great Alcinous rose as well: Laodamas, Halius, and godlike Clytoneus.

First they ran the foot-race. The line was drawn for them from the post, and they all flew forward together, raising the dust across the plain. Godlike Clytoneus came in first, far ahead — by as much ground as a team of mules can break in a fallow field, that far he left the rest behind and reached the crowd. Next they tried the hard work of wrestling, and there Euryalus came out best of all. Amphialus was the finest at the jump; at the discus, no one came near Elatreus; and at boxing, Laodamas, the noble son of Alcinous, was the best. And when they had all gladdened their hearts with the games, Laodamas, son of Alcinous, said: “Friends, come, let us ask the stranger whether he is skilled in any sport. He is no mean figure to look at — his thighs and his calves, his two hands, his thick neck, all of him is built for strength. And he is not past his prime; it is only that he is broken by his many hardships. There is nothing, I tell you, like the sea for grinding a man down, however strong he may be.”

And Euryalus answered him: “Laodamas, you have spoken well, and to the point. Go up to him yourself, then, and put it to him.”

When the good son of Alcinous heard this, he went and stood in the middle of the crowd and said to Odysseus: “Come, sir, you too — will you try your hand at our games, if you have any skill in them? And surely you must; there is no glory for a man in all his days greater than what he wins with his own hands and feet. Come, then, and make a trial of it, and shake the sorrow from your heart. Your journey home will not be long delayed now; the ship is already drawn down to the water, and the crew is ready.”

Then resourceful Odysseus answered him: “Laodamas, why do you taunt me with this? My heart is set on grief, not games. I have suffered more than I can say, and I sit here among you now as a suppliant, begging your king and your people to send me home.”

But Euryalus spoke up and threw the insult in his face: “No — I can see it now: you are no man for the kind of contests men love. You are some skipper of a tramping merchant crew, a man who minds his cargo and keeps his eye on his freight and his greedy profits. There is nothing of the athlete about you.”

Then resourceful Odysseus looked at him from under his brows and said: “Stranger, that was an ugly thing to say. You talk like a reckless fool. So it is — the gods do not give every grace to every man, not beauty, not brains, not eloquence all at once. One man is poor enough to look at, but the god crowns his words with such grace that men gaze at him with delight; he speaks on without a stumble, with a winning modesty, and stands out in any gathering, and when he walks through the town men look on him as on a god. Another has the beauty of the immortals, but no grace is set as a crown upon his words. That is your case. You are splendid to look at — a god could not improve you — but your wits are worthless. You have stirred the heart in my breast with your rude, ill-judged talk. I am no stranger to games, whatever you think — no, I was among the best of them while I still had my youth and the strength in my hands. But now I am held down by hardship and grief; I have come through much, cutting my way through wars among men and across the cruel waves. Still, for all I have suffered, I will try your contests. Your words have bitten deep, and you have goaded me to it.”

He sprang up, cloak and all, without even stripping, and seized a discus — a bigger one, more massive, far heavier than the ones the Phaeacians threw among themselves. He whirled it back and let it fly from his great hand, and the stone hummed in the air; and the long-oared Phaeacians, those famous seamen, cringed down to the ground beneath the rush of it as it flew. Easily, smoothly, it sailed past the marks of every other throw. Athena, taking the form of a man, marked the spot where it had fallen, and called out to him: “Stranger, even a blind man could find your mark by feeling for it, for it is not lost in the crowd of the others — it lies far out, ahead of them all. Take heart in this contest, at least. No Phaeacian will reach this throw, or beat it.”

So she spoke, and long-suffering, brilliant Odysseus was glad, glad to find a true friend among the onlookers, and now he spoke to the Phaeacians with a lighter heart: “Match that, young men, if you can — and I think I will throw another, as far or farther. As for the rest, if any man’s heart and spirit move him, let him come and try me — you have stung me past bearing — at boxing, at wrestling, at the foot-race, I care not which. Any man of you all but Laodamas; for he is my host, and who would fight against the man who has befriended him? Only a fool, a man worth nothing, would challenge his host to a contest in a foreign land; he would be cutting the ground from under his own feet. But of the rest I refuse no one and make light of none; I will meet any man face to face and learn what he is made of. There is no sport men know that I am bad at. I know my way well with the polished bow: in a press of enemies, I would be the first to find my man with an arrow, though a whole crowd of comrades stood beside me shooting too. Of all the men now alive on the earth and eating bread, Philoctetes alone outshot me, when we Greeks drew the bow before Troy. But I will not set myself against the men of long ago — against Heracles, or against Eurytus of Oechalia, who would draw the bow even against the immortals. That is how great Eurytus came to his early death; he never reached old age in his halls, for Apollo killed him in anger, because the man had dared to challenge him at the bow. And with a spear I can throw farther than any other man can shoot an arrow. Only at the foot-race do I fear that one of you Phaeacians might pass me; the sea has worn me down past all decency, for there was no steady food aboard for me — and so my limbs are slack.”

So he spoke, and they all fell silent. Only Alcinous found his voice and answered him: “Stranger, there is no offense in anything you say. You wish to show the prowess that is in you, since this man stood up beside you in the games and insulted you — and no one who knew how to speak with sense would ever belittle your prowess. But now hear me, so that you may tell your story to some other hero when you are home, dining in your own hall beside your wife and children, and so remember the skill that is ours, the deeds that Zeus has set in our hands ever since our fathers’ time. We are no great boxers, no, nor wrestlers, but we are quick of foot, and the finest of seamen, and dear to us forever are the feast, the lyre, the dance, fresh clothes to wear, the warm bath, and our beds. Come, then — let those of you who are the best dancers of the Phaeacians begin the dance, so that when our guest reaches home he can tell his friends how far we surpass all other men, at sailing and at running, at the dance and at song. And someone go quickly and fetch Demodocus his clear-toned lyre; it is lying somewhere in my halls.”

So godlike Alcinous spoke, and a herald rose to bring the hollow lyre from the king’s house. And the nine chosen judges stood up, the public officers who saw to everything at the games; they smoothed the dancing-floor and opened a fair, wide ring. The herald came back with the clear-toned lyre for Demodocus, who stepped into the middle, and around him stood young men in the first bloom of their strength, masters of the dance. They struck their feet on the holy floor, and Odysseus watched the flashing of their feet and marveled in his heart.

Then, striking the strings, the bard lifted his voice in a beautiful song — of the love of Ares and Aphrodite of the lovely crown, and how they first lay together in secret in the house of Hephaestus. Many were the gifts Ares gave her, and he shamed the bed and the marriage of lord Hephaestus. But Helios the Sun saw them at their love and went and told him. And when Hephaestus heard the bitter news, he went to his forge with murder in his heart, and set his great anvil on its block, and hammered out chains that could not be broken or loosed, to hold the two of them fast where they lay. And when he had forged his snare, raging at Ares, he went into the room where his own bed stood and bound the netting of chains all around the bedposts, and let many of them hang down from the great roof-beam above — fine as a spider’s web, so fine that no one could see them, not even one of the blessed gods, so cunningly had they been made. When he had spread the whole trap over the bed, he made as if to set out for Lemnos, that strong-walled town that of all places on earth he loved the best. And Ares of the golden reins kept no idle watch; the moment he saw the great craftsman Hephaestus go, he made for the famous god’s house, hot with longing for Aphrodite of the lovely crown.

She had only just come home from her father, the mighty son of Cronus, and had sat down. Ares came into the house and took her by the hand and spoke to her, calling her by name: “Come, my love, let us go to bed and take our pleasure lying down. Hephaestus is not at home; by now, I think, he has gone off to Lemnos, among the Sintians, whose speech is harsh and strange.”

So he spoke, and to her it seemed a welcome thing to lie with him. So the two of them went to the bed and lay down — and around them the chains that cunning Hephaestus had forged came crashing down, so that they could not move a limb or raise themselves at all, and they knew, too late, that there was no escaping. And the famous god with the crooked feet came near; he had turned back before reaching the land of Lemnos, for Helios the Sun had kept his watch and brought him word. Sick at heart, he came to his own house, and stood there in the doorway, and a wild fury seized him. He gave a terrible shout, and cried out to all the gods:

“Father Zeus, and all you other blessed gods who live forever — come here and see a thing to laugh at, a thing past all bearing: how Aphrodite, the daughter of Zeus, dishonors me always, lame as I am, and gives her love to deadly Ares, because he is handsome and sound of foot, while I was born a cripple. And no one is to blame for that but my own two parents — would they had never had me! Come and look where the two of them lie, in love, climbed into my own bed; it cuts me to the heart to see it. And yet I do not think they will want to lie like that much longer, in love as they are. Soon enough they will lose all desire to sleep — but the trap and the chains will hold them fast, until her father pays me back every last gift I handed over for this shameless girl. His daughter is beautiful, yes — but she cannot keep faith.”

So he spoke, and the gods came thronging to the bronze-floored house. Poseidon came, who holds the earth in his arms; and Hermes came, the bringer of luck; and lord Apollo came, who works from far off. But the goddesses, every one of them, stayed home for shame. So the gods, the givers of good things, stood there in the doorway, and unquenchable laughter broke out among the blessed gods as they looked on the craft of clever Hephaestus. And one of them would glance at the god beside him and say:

“Bad deeds don’t prosper. The slow catches the swift — see how Hephaestus, slow as he is, and lame, has caught Ares with his craft, the fastest of all the gods on Olympus. Now Ares owes the fine an adulterer pays.”

So they spoke to one another. But lord Apollo, son of Zeus, said to Hermes: “Hermes, son of Zeus, runner and giver of good things — would you be willing to lie under all those crushing chains, if you could sleep at the side of golden Aphrodite?”

And the keen-eyed slayer of Argos answered him: “If only it could be so, lord Apollo, archer from afar! Let there be three times as many chains, past counting, and let all of you gods look on, and all the goddesses too — I would still sleep at golden Aphrodite’s side.”

So he spoke, and laughter rose among the deathless gods. But Poseidon did not laugh; he kept pleading with Hephaestus, the great craftsman, to set Ares free. He spoke to him with winged words: “Let him go, and I give you my word that he will pay you in full, all that is right and fitting, before the deathless gods.”

But the famous god with the crooked feet answered him: “Poseidon, who holds the earth — do not ask this of me. The pledges of a worthless man are worthless to hold. How could I bind you before the deathless gods, if Ares should slip away and leave both his debt and his chains behind him?”

Then Poseidon the earth-shaker answered him: “Hephaestus — if Ares does run off and leave his debt unpaid, I myself will pay you.” And the famous god with the crooked feet replied: “Then it is not right, and it is not possible, to refuse you.” With that the mighty Hephaestus loosed the chains, and the two of them, the moment they were free of that strong bond, sprang up at once and were gone — Ares away to Thrace, and laughter-loving Aphrodite to Cyprus, to Paphos, where she has her sacred ground and her altar fragrant with incense. There the Graces bathed her and rubbed her with the immortal oil that glistens on the everlasting gods, and dressed her in lovely clothes, a wonder to behold.

So sang the famous bard, and Odysseus was glad at heart to hear it, and so were the long-oared Phaeacians, those famous seamen.

Then Alcinous called on Halius and Laodamas to dance by themselves, for there was no one who could match them. They took a fine ball, a purple one that skilled Polybus had made for them, and one of them would arch backward and fling it high toward the shadowy clouds, while the other would leap clear of the ground and catch it easily before his feet came down again. And when they had shown what they could do throwing the ball straight up, they began to dance on the bounteous earth, tossing it back and forth between them, while the rest of the young men stood about the ring and beat the time, and a great drumming of feet rose up. Then brilliant Odysseus said to Alcinous: “Lord Alcinous, most renowned of men — you told me your dancers were the best in the world, and now I see it is the plain truth. The wonder of it holds me as I watch.”

So he spoke, and the mighty king Alcinous was delighted, and at once said to the Phaeacians, who love their oars: “Hear me, captains and counselors of the Phaeacians. This stranger seems to me a man of fine good sense. Come, then — let us give him a guest-gift, as is only right. Twelve lords hold sway and rule among our people, and I am the thirteenth. Let each of us bring him a fresh cloak and a tunic and a talent of fine gold, and let us gather all of it together quickly, so that the stranger may take it in his own hands and go to his supper with a glad heart. And as for Euryalus — let him make it right with the man himself, with words and with a gift, for what he said was out of all bounds.”

So he spoke, and they all agreed and gave the order, and each sent his herald off to fetch the gift. Then Euryalus answered the king and said: “Lord Alcinous, most renowned of men — I will make it right with the stranger, as you ask. I will give him this sword, all of bronze, with a hilt of silver, and a scabbard of fresh-sawn ivory closing round it. It will be worth much to him.”

With that he put the silver-studded sword into the hands of Odysseus and spoke to him with winged words: “Good fortune to you, honored stranger. If any harsh word slipped from me, let the storm-winds catch it up and carry it off, and may the gods grant that you see your wife again and reach your own land, for you have been long away from your dear ones, and have borne much sorrow.”

And resourceful Odysseus answered him: “And good fortune to you too, my friend, and may the gods grant you every blessing. And may you never, in days to come, miss this sword you have given me with your words of peace.” So he spoke, and slung the silver-studded sword about his shoulders.

The sun went down, and the splendid gifts began to arrive, as the proud heralds carried them to the house of Alcinous. There the sons of flawless Alcinous took them and laid the lovely treasures before their honored mother. The king led the way, and the others followed, and they came in and sat down on the high seats. Then mighty Alcinous said to Arete: “Wife, bring out the finest chest we have, and lay in it a fresh cloak and a tunic. And heat a cauldron of water over the fire for the stranger, so that when he has bathed and seen all the gifts that the noble Phaeacians have brought packed away in good order, he may take his pleasure in the feast and in the singing that follows. And I will give him this cup of mine, beautiful and golden, so that he may remember me all his days, whenever he pours an offering in his hall to Zeus and the other gods.”

So he spoke, and Arete told her serving-women to set a great three-legged cauldron over the fire as fast as they could. They set the cauldron for the bath on the blazing fire, poured the water in, and piled on wood to make it burn, and the flame licked round the cauldron’s belly and the water grew hot. Meanwhile Arete brought a beautiful chest from her own room for the stranger, and packed into it the fine gifts of clothing and gold that the Phaeacians had given, and added a cloak and a good tunic of her own, and spoke to him with winged words: “See to the lid yourself now, and tie it fast with a good knot, so that no one robs you on the way, when you are sleeping your sweet sleep aboard the black ship.”

When long-suffering, brilliant Odysseus heard this, at once he fitted the lid in place and tied it fast with a cunning knot that the lady Circe had once taught him. And straightway the housekeeper called him to step into the bath and wash. Glad was his heart at the sight of the warm bath, for such comforts had not often come his way since he left the home of lovely-haired Calypso — though there, while it lasted, he had been cared for like a god. When the serving-women had bathed him and rubbed him with oil, and dressed him in a fine cloak and tunic, he came out of the bath and went to join the men over their wine. And Nausicaa, with her god-given beauty, stood beside the pillar that held up the roof of the hall, and marveled at Odysseus as her eyes fell on him. She spoke to him with winged words: “Farewell, stranger. And when you are home in your own land, remember me sometimes, for it is to me, first of all, that you owe the price of your life.”

And resourceful Odysseus answered her: “Nausicaa, daughter of great-hearted Alcinous — may Zeus grant it, the loud-thundering lord, husband of Hera, that I reach my home and see the day of my return. Then, even there, I will pray to you as to a god, all my days; for it was you, my lady, who gave me back my life.”

He spoke, and sat down on the chair beside King Alcinous. By now they were serving out the portions and mixing the wine, and a herald came near, leading the faithful bard, Demodocus, honored by all the people. He seated him in the middle of the company, leaning the chair against a tall pillar. Then resourceful Odysseus called to a herald, cutting off a portion of the chine of a white-tusked boar — and plenty of it, with the rich fat still on either side: “Herald, here, take this meat to Demodocus, so that he may eat; I would show him my goodwill, grieving as I am. Among all the men who walk the earth, bards are owed their share of honor and respect, for the Muse has taught them the paths of song, and she loves the whole tribe of singers.”

So he spoke, and the herald carried it over and put it into the hands of the hero Demodocus, who took it and was glad at heart. Then they reached out their hands to the good things spread before them, and when they had taken their fill of food and drink, resourceful Odysseus said to Demodocus: “Demodocus, I praise you above all mortal men. Either the Muse, daughter of Zeus, has taught you, or Apollo himself — for you sing the fate of the Greeks so truly, all that they did and suffered and toiled through, as though you had been there yourself, or heard it from one who was. Come now, change your song, and sing of the building of the wooden horse, which Epeus made with Athena’s help — the cunning thing that brilliant Odysseus brought up into the citadel as a trap, after he had filled it with the men who sacked Troy. Sing me that, sing it as it truly happened, and I will declare to all the world that the god has freely given you the gift of holy song.”

So he spoke, and the bard, stirred by the god, took up the tale and let his song flow — beginning where the Greeks had boarded their well-benched ships and sailed away, after setting fire to their own huts, while the others, with famous Odysseus, sat already hidden inside the horse in the Trojans’ own meeting-place; for the Trojans themselves had dragged it up into their citadel. So it stood there, and the people sat around it arguing endlessly, of three minds about what to do: whether to split the hollow timber open with the pitiless bronze, or to haul it to the high rock and hurl it down the cliff, or to let it stand as a great offering to win the gods’ favor — and that was the way it ended, for it was fated that the city should fall once it had taken in that great horse of wood, where all the best of the Greeks sat waiting to bring death and doom upon the Trojans. And he sang how the sons of the Greeks poured out of the horse, leaving their hollow ambush, and sacked the steep city. He sang how this man and that laid waste the town from one quarter to another, and how Odysseus went like the war-god Ares, with godlike Menelaus, to the house of Deiphobus. There, he sang, Odysseus dared his most terrible fight, and won it in the end through the help of great-hearted Athena.

All this the famous bard sang. But Odysseus felt the grief melt his heart, and the tears ran down and wet his cheeks. He wept the way a woman weeps, throwing her arms about her own dear husband, who has fallen in front of his city and his people, fighting to keep the pitiless day from his town and his children. She has seen him gasping in his death, and she pours herself over him, wailing aloud, while behind her the enemy strike her back and her shoulders with their spear-shafts and lead her away into slavery, to a life of toil and misery, and her cheeks waste with the most pitiful grief — even so pitifully did the tears run down from under Odysseus’ brows. And again no one in all the company noticed his weeping but Alcinous, who sat close beside him and caught the deep, heavy sighs he was heaving. At once the king spoke up to the Phaeacians, who love their oars:

“Hear me, captains and counselors of the Phaeacians. Let Demodocus hold back the clear-toned lyre now, for his song does not please everyone here alike. From the moment we sat down to supper and the divine singer began, our guest has never once left off his bitter grieving; some great sorrow has surely closed over his heart. So let the bard leave off, and let us all be glad together, hosts and guest alike; that is the better way. For all of this — the escort and the gifts of friendship we so gladly give — is in honor of our guest, and any man with even a grain of sense knows that a suppliant and a stranger are to be treated like a brother.

“And so, my friend, do not hide from me, with cunning thoughts, the thing I am about to ask you; it is far better to speak it out. Tell me the name your father and mother called you back home, and the people of your town and the country round about; for no one among men, whether highborn or low, is altogether without a name once he is born — every parent gives a child a name at birth. And tell me your land, your people, and your city, so that our ships may carry you there with their own minds set on the course. For the Phaeacian ships have no pilots, and no steering-oars such as other ships have; they know of themselves the thoughts and the wishes of men, and they know all the cities and rich fields of every people, and they cross the gulf of the sea at the greatest speed, wrapped in mist and cloud, and never any fear of harm or wreck. And yet I once heard my father Nausithous say that Poseidon held a grudge against us, because we give safe passage to every man without harm — that one day he would shatter a fine Phaeacian ship as it came home from such an escort, out on the misty sea, and would throw up a great mountain to hide our city. So the old man said; the god may bring it to pass, or leave it undone, as it pleases his heart.

“But come now, tell me this, and tell me truly: where have your wanderings carried you? What countries of men have you seen — their great cities, the people themselves? Who among them were cruel and savage and lawless, and who were kind to strangers and god-fearing of mind? And tell me too why you weep and grieve in your heart when you hear the fate of the Greeks who went to Troy. The gods themselves brought all that to pass; they spun out the death of those men so that there might be a song for those yet to be born. Did some kinsman of yours, by marriage, fall before Troy — a brave man — a son-in-law, or a father-in-law, the dearest of those who are bound to us outside our own blood? Or was it some comrade, dear to your heart, a man of valor? For a true friend, sound in his thinking, is no less dear to a man than his own brother.”
