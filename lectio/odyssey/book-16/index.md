# The Odyssey, Book XVI

Father and Son

At first light Odysseus and the swineherd had a fire going in the hut and were getting breakfast ready, for they had already sent the herdsmen out with the pigs. When Telemachus came up the path, the dogs did not bark — they fawned around him, crowding close. Odysseus heard footsteps and saw the dogs go quiet and friendly, and he said to Eumaeus: “Eumaeus, someone is coming — a man of yours, I think, or someone the dogs know. They are not barking; they are making up to him.”

The words were scarcely out of his mouth when his own son stood in the doorway. Eumaeus jumped up, startled, and the bowls he had been mixing wine in dropped from his hands as he rushed to his master. He kissed his head and both his shining eyes and both his hands, and wept for joy. As a father who has waited ten long years welcomes home an only son, the child of his old age, come back at last from a far country and all its hardship — so the swineherd threw his arms around Telemachus and kissed him all over, as a man kisses one back from the dead. The tears ran down, and he poured out his love: “So you are home, Telemachus, light of my eyes. When I heard you had sailed for Pylos I was sure I would never see you again. Come in, dear child, and sit, so I can have my fill of looking at you, now you are back under my roof. You don’t come out to us herdsmen often — you keep close to the town. I suppose you’d rather have your eye on that crowd of suitors and what they are at.”

“As you say, old friend,” Telemachus answered. “But I came for your sake — to see you, and to learn whether my mother is still at home, or whether some man has finally married her, and the bed of Odysseus lies empty now, gathering cobwebs.”

“She is still in the house,” Eumaeus said, “breaking her heart, doing nothing but weep, day and night without end.”

He took the young man’s spear, and Telemachus stepped across the stone threshold and came in. Odysseus rose from his place to give it up to him, but Telemachus stopped him. “Stay where you are, stranger,” he said. “I’ll find another seat — there’s a man here who will lay one for me.” So Odysseus sat down again, and Eumaeus spread fresh green brushwood on the floor and threw a sheepskin over it for Telemachus. He set out platters of the cold meat left from the day before, heaped the baskets with bread as fast as his hands could move, mixed wine in a bowl of ivy-wood, and took his own seat facing Odysseus. They reached for the good things spread before them, and when they had eaten and drunk their fill, Telemachus said to Eumaeus: “Old friend, where is this stranger from? What crew brought him to Ithaca, and who did they say they were? For he plainly did not come on foot.”

And you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd: “My boy, I’ll tell you the whole truth. He says he is a Cretan, and that he has wandered far and wide over the earth. Just now he is on the run from a Thesprotian ship, and he has come to my hut for shelter. I put him in your hands — do with him as you like; he is your suppliant now.”

“That is a hard thing you have laid on me, Eumaeus,” Telemachus said. “How can I take this stranger into my house? I am young still, and have no strength of my own to fall back on if a man rises against me. My mother cannot make up her mind — whether to stay here and keep the house, out of respect for her husband’s bed and the talk of the people, or to go at last with the best of the men who court her, the one whose offer is highest. But since the stranger has come to your station, I’ll give him a good cloak and shirt to wear, a sword, and sandals for his feet, and send him on wherever his heart is set. Or keep him here with you, if you’d rather, and I’ll send out clothes and food, so he is no drain on you and your men. Only I will not have him go near the suitors — they are too far gone in insolence, and they would abuse him in some way that would cut me to the heart. One man can do nothing against numbers, however brave he is; they will always be too many for him.”

Then long-suffering Odysseus spoke up. “Friend — for I think it is right that I should say something — it tears at me to hear how these suitors carry on, riding roughshod in the house of a man like you, and you so young. Tell me: do you take it lying down, or have your own people turned against you, set on by some god? Or is it your brothers you blame — for those are the men a person leans on when a real quarrel comes? I tell you, if I were as young as you and had the heart I have now — if I were the son of long-suffering Odysseus, or were the man himself, come home from his wandering — I would let a stranger strike my head from my shoulders before I would go on bearing this. I would rather walk into that hall and be the death of every last one of them. And if their numbers beat me down, single-handed, I would still rather die fighting in my own house than stand by, day after day, and watch this — guests shoved and insulted, the serving-women hauled about for sport, wine running to waste, bread squandered, all of it for nothing, for an end that will never come.”

Telemachus answered, “Then I’ll tell you the truth of it, stranger. There is no quarrel between me and my people, and I have no brothers to blame — for Zeus has made ours a line of single sons. Arceisius fathered Laertes alone, and Laertes fathered only Odysseus, and Odysseus left only me behind in his house, with no good he ever had of me. That is why the place is overrun now with men who mean it harm: every lord of the islands round us — Dulichium, Same, wooded Zacynthus — and every man of weight in Ithaca itself, all of them courting my mother and eating the house bare. She neither says no outright nor brings the thing to an end, and so they devour what is mine, and before long they will devour me with it. But all that rests on the knees of the gods. Eumaeus, old friend — go quickly to the town and tell Penelope I am safe and back from Pylos. Tell her alone, and no one else, and come straight back. There are many here who would do me harm.”

And you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd: “I understand. You needn’t say it twice. But tell me — as I’m going that way, shall I take word to poor Laertes too? He used to keep an eye on the work of his farm in spite of all his grief for Odysseus, and eat and drink with the servants in the house when he had a mind to. But they say that from the day you sailed for Pylos he has not eaten or drunk as a man should, nor looked to his fields — he only sits and weeps, the flesh wasting off his bones.”

“That grieves me,” Telemachus said, “and I’m sorry for him — but we must leave him to himself for now. If men could have everything as they wished, the first thing I’d ask for is my father’s return. Give your message and hurry back; don’t turn aside to the farm to tell Laertes. Ask my mother to send one of her women out to him quietly, with the news.”

So he sped the swineherd on his way. Eumaeus bound the sandals to his feet and set off for the town. Athena watched him well clear of the hut, and then drew near in the shape of a woman — tall, beautiful, skilled in fine work. She stood at the entrance, in plain sight of Odysseus, but Telemachus did not see her and did not know she was there, for the gods do not show themselves to everyone. Odysseus saw her, and so did the dogs; they did not bark, but slunk off whimpering to the far side of the yard. She gave him a sign with her brows, and Odysseus came out and stood before her beyond the wall. Then she said:

“Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes — now is the time. Speak to your son and keep him in the dark no longer. Lay your plans for the killing of the suitors, the two of you together, and then make for the town. I will not be far behind — I am hungry for the fight myself.”

With that she touched him with her golden wand. First she settled a fresh-washed cloak and a tunic about his shoulders. Then she made him taller to look on, and young again; she gave him back his dark color, filled out his cheeks, and let the beard grow black once more around his chin. Then she was gone, and Odysseus went back into the hut. His son stared, astonished, and turned his eyes away, afraid that he was looking on a god.

“Stranger,” he said, “you are not the man you were a moment ago. Your clothes are different, the very color of your skin is changed. Surely you are one of the gods who hold the wide sky. Be kind to me, then — I will give you sacrifices that please you, and gifts of worked gold. Only spare me.”

And long-suffering Odysseus said, “I am no god. Why do you take me for one? I am your father — the man you grieve for, the man for whose sake you bear so much at the hands of lawless men.”

And as he spoke he kissed his son, and the tears he had held back so long ran down at last and fell to the ground. But Telemachus could not yet believe it was his father, and he said: “You are not Odysseus, not my father — some god is mocking me with false hope, to make my grief the heavier afterward. No mortal man could do this by his own wit, make himself old and young from one breath to the next, unless a god stood at his side. A moment ago you were old and dressed in rags, and now you are like the gods who hold the sky.”

Odysseus answered, “Telemachus, it is not right for you to wonder so at your own father here before you, or to stand back in such amazement. No other Odysseus will ever come; I am he. This is the man — after long wandering and much suffering, home in the twentieth year to the land of his fathers. What astonishes you is the work of Athena, who drives the spoil — she makes me what she will, for the gods can do as they please. One hour she makes me a beggar, the next a young man in fine clothes. It is an easy thing for the gods who hold the sky to lift a man up or to bring him low.”

He sat down, and Telemachus threw his arms around his father and wept aloud, and Odysseus wept with him. They cried more shrilly than birds — sea-eagles, or vultures with hooked talons, whose young the country folk have stolen from the nest before they could fly — so bitterly did they let the tears fall from under their brows. And the sun would have gone down on their weeping, but Telemachus broke off and asked: “In what ship, then, dear father, did your crew bring you here to Ithaca? Who did they say they were? For you cannot have come on foot.”

“I’ll tell you the truth, my son,” said long-suffering Odysseus. “It was the Phaeacians who brought me — great seafarers, who give safe passage to any man who reaches their shores. They carried me over the water fast asleep and set me down in Ithaca, with gifts to send me off: bronze, gold, and woven cloth. By the gods’ grace those things lie hidden in a cave. And I have come here now at Athena’s word, so the two of us can plan the death of our enemies. So tell me the suitors — count them, name them — that I may know who they are and how many. Then I can turn it over in my mind and judge whether the two of us can stand against them alone, or whether we must look for others to fight beside us.”

Telemachus answered, “Father, I have always heard of your name — your strength in the field, your judgment in council. But this is too great a thing you speak of; the wonder of it stops me cold. Two men cannot stand against so many strong ones. The suitors are not ten, not twice ten, but many times that. Hear their count: from Dulichium, fifty-two picked young men, with six servants; from Same, twenty-four; from Zacynthus, twenty of the Greeks; from Ithaca itself, twelve, all of the best blood. With them are the herald Medon, a bard, and two men skilled at carving meat. If we go against all of these inside the hall, I am afraid your homecoming and your revenge will end in bitterness. Think hard whether there is anyone who might come and stand with us.”

“Listen, then,” said long-suffering Odysseus, “and think this over. Do you reckon Athena and Father Zeus enough for us, or shall I cast about for some other ally too?”

“Those two you have named are powerful helpers,” Telemachus said. “They sit high among the clouds, and they hold sway over all others — gods and men alike.”

“They will not hold back long from the fight,” Odysseus said, “once the suitors and we two come to the test of arms in my own hall. Now, then: go home at daybreak and move among the suitors as you always do. Later the swineherd will bring me into the town, looking the part of a wretched old beggar. When you see them abusing me, harden your heart against my suffering — even if they drag me feet-first through the house or throw things at me, watch and do nothing. Speak to them gently, if you must, and tell them to stop; but they will not listen, for their day of reckoning is upon them. And mark this well, fix it in your mind: when Athena puts it in my heart, I will give you a nod. When you see it, gather up every weapon in the hall and stow it away in the inner storeroom. When the suitors miss the arms and ask, put them off with a soft answer — say you have moved them out of the smoke, that they are no longer what they were when Odysseus sailed away, but blackened and fouled with soot; and say too that you fear Zeus may set the men quarreling over their wine and turn them on each other, with arms at hand to shame the feast and the wooing — for the iron itself draws a man on. But leave a sword and a spear for each of us, and two oxhide shields we can snatch up in an instant; Zeus and Athena will quiet the rest of them soon enough. And one more thing — if you are truly my son and of my blood, let no one learn that Odysseus is in the house. Not Laertes, not the swineherd, not one of the servants, not even Penelope. Only you and I will sound out the women, and try some of the menservants too, to see who still honors us and whose hand is against us.”

“Father,” Telemachus said, “you will come to know my heart in time, and find no slackness in it. But I don’t think this part of the plan will serve either of us — turn it over again. It would take us a long while to go round all the farms and test the men, and the whole time the suitors will be wasting your house, at their ease and without a qualm. Try the women, by all means, to learn who has been false and who is clear of it. But I would not go round trying the men just now. Leave that for later — if you really have some sign from Zeus that he is with us.”

So the two of them talked on. Meanwhile the ship that had carried Telemachus and his crew from Pylos reached Ithaca. They ran her up onto the beach inside the harbor, and their attendants took the gear from them and bore the gifts to the house of Clytius. Then they sent a herald ahead to Penelope, to tell her Telemachus was in the country but had sent the ship round to the town, so that the queen would not be afraid and weep. This herald and Eumaeus met on the way, both bound on the same errand to Penelope. When they reached the house, the herald spoke out among the serving-women: “Your son, my queen, is home from Pylos.” But Eumaeus went up close to Penelope and told her privately all that her son had bidden him say. Then, his message given, he left the house and its yards and went back to his pigs.

The suitors were stunned and sullen at the news. They came out past the great wall of the court and sat down in council by the gate. Eurymachus, son of Polybus, spoke first. “Friends, this is a serious thing Telemachus has brought off — this voyage. We were certain it would come to nothing. Quick, let us run a ship down to the water, get a crew aboard, and send word to the men out there to come home at once.”

He had hardly finished when Amphinomus, turning where he sat, caught sight of the ship already inside the harbor, the crew furling her sail and laying up the oars. He laughed and said, “No need to send any message — they are here. Some god told them, or else they saw the other ship slip past and could not catch her.”

At that they rose and went down to the shore. The crew hauled the ship up onto the land, and their attendants took the gear from them, and they all went together to the place of assembly — and they let no one else, young or old, sit in with them. Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke. “Curse it — see how the gods have saved this man from ruin. Day after day we kept lookouts posted along the windy headlands, and at sundown we never went ashore to sleep but lay out at sea in the ship all night, watching for dawn, hoping to catch him and make an end of him. And some god has carried him home in spite of us. Well, then — let us plan his death here. Telemachus must not slip us, for our whole business will never come off while he lives. He is sharp, and the people are by no means all on our side. We had better move before he can call the Greeks to assembly — and he will lose no time, raging as he is; he will stand up before them all and tell how we plotted his murder and missed. They will not love us for it, once they know. They may turn on us, and drive us out of our own land into exile. So let us take him first — out on his farm, away from the town, or on the road in. Then we divide his goods among us and leave the house to his mother and whatever man marries her. But if this does not sit well with you, and you would have Telemachus live and keep his father’s estate, then let us stop gathering here and eating him out of house and home. Let each man court Penelope from his own hall, with his own gifts, and she can marry the one who gives the most, the man who is fated to win her.”

They all sat silent until Amphinomus rose to speak. He was the son of Nisus, son of King Aretias, and he stood first among the suitors who came from the wheat-rich, grassy island of Dulichium; of all of them, his talk pleased Penelope most, for there was decency in him. He spoke plainly, with their good in mind: “Friends, I am against killing Telemachus. It is a terrible thing to spill the blood of a man of royal line. Let us first ask the will of the gods. If the oracles of Zeus approve it, I will kill him with my own hand and call on every one of you to do the same; but if the gods warn us off, then I say hold back.”

His words pleased them, and they rose at once and went to the house of Odysseus, where they took their usual seats.

Now Penelope made up her mind to show herself to the suitors. She had learned of the plot against her son, for the herald Medon had overheard them at it and told her. She went down to the hall with her women, and when she came among the suitors she stood by a pillar of the roof, a fine veil before her face, and rounded on Antinous: “Antinous — you reckless schemer, you man of malice — they say you are the best speaker and the best counsel of any man your age in Ithaca, and you are nothing of the kind. Madman, why do you plot the death of Telemachus? Why do you trample on suppliants, whose witness is Zeus himself? It is a vile thing, to lay traps for one another. Have you forgotten how your own father once fled to this house, in terror of the people? They were enraged at him — he had gone off with Taphian pirates and raided the Thesprotians, who were at peace with us; the people would have torn him apart and devoured all he had. It was Odysseus who held them back and stayed their fury. And this is the house whose goods you now devour without paying, whose wife you court, whose son you would murder — breaking my heart. Stop it, I tell you, and make the others stop as well.”

Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered her: “Take heart, Queen Penelope, daughter of Icarius, and do not trouble yourself over this. The man is not born — never will be — who will lay a hand on your son Telemachus while I am alive and see the light of day. I tell you plainly, and it will come to pass: that man’s dark blood will run down my spear. Many a time the city-sacker Odysseus took me on his knees and held the roast meat to my mouth and the red wine to my lips. So of all men Telemachus is the dearest to me, and he has nothing to fear from us suitors — though if death comes to him from the gods, no man can turn it aside.” So he spoke, to soothe her — but in his own heart he was plotting the boy’s death.

Penelope went back up to her bright room and wept for her husband, until Athena cast sweet sleep upon her eyes. At evening the swineherd came back to Odysseus and his son. They had just killed a year-old pig and were busy getting supper ready; and Athena came up to Odysseus, struck him with her wand, made him an old man again, and put the rags back on him — so that Eumaeus would not know him on sight and fail to keep the secret, but run to tell Penelope.

Telemachus spoke first. “So you’re back, Eumaeus. What is the word in the town? Have the suitors come in from their ambush yet, or are they still out there, waiting to take me on my way home?”

And you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd: “It never crossed my mind to ask about that, going through the town — I was set on giving my message and getting back as fast as I could. But a herald from the men who had sailed with you fell in with me; he was the first to bring the news to your mother. This much I can tell you, though, for I saw it with my own eyes: I had just climbed to the crest of Hermes’ hill above the town when I saw a ship putting into the harbor, with a crowd of men aboard her, and a press of shields and spears. I took them for the suitors — but I cannot say for certain.”

At that Telemachus smiled across at his father, careful that Eumaeus should not see. Then, when their work was done and the meal laid, they ate, and no man’s share fell short — each had his fill. And when they had eaten and drunk all they wanted, they lay down and took the gift of sleep.
