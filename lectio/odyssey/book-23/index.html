The Odyssey, Book XXIII

The Sign of the Bed

Book XXIII23 of 24

Eurycleia went up the stairs laughing, to tell her mistress that her dear husband was home at last. Her old knees turned young again, and her feet were light with joy. She bent over Penelope’s head and spoke: “Wake up, dear child. Wake and see with your own eyes the thing you have longed for all these years. Odysseus is home. He has come at last, and he has killed the suitors who ate up the house and tormented your son.”

“Dear nurse,” Penelope answered, “the gods have driven you out of your mind. They can make a wise woman foolish and lift a foolish one into sense, and they have unsettled you, who were always so steady. Why do you mock me, with grief enough on me already, waking me out of the sweetest sleep I have known since the day my husband sailed for that city I cannot bring myself to name? Go back down to the women’s room. If any other woman had roused me with such nonsense, I would have sent her off with a scolding she would remember. Your age alone protects you.”

“I am not mocking you, dear child,” said Eurycleia. “It is the truth. Odysseus is home. He was the stranger they all abused in the hall. Telemachus knew it all along, but he kept his father’s secret, so that the two of them could take their revenge on those proud men.”

At that Penelope sprang up from the bed and threw her arms around the old woman, weeping for joy. “Then tell me, dear nurse,” she said, “if he has truly come, how did one man bring down the whole shameless crowd of them? There were always so many.”

“I did not see it,” Eurycleia answered. “I do not know. I only heard the groaning of men being killed. We sat huddled in a corner of the women’s room with the doors shut fast, until your son came to fetch me, sent by his father. And there I found Odysseus standing among the dead, and they lay all around him on the hard ground, one upon another. It would have warmed your heart to see him, splashed with blood and filth, like a lion over its kill. Now the bodies are heaped together by the courtyard gate, and he has lit a great fire to purify the house with sulphur. He sent me to bring you. Come, so that the two of you may be glad together at last. Your heart’s long wish has come true: your husband is home, and he has found you and your son alive, and has paid the suitors back in his own halls for all the wrong they did.”

“Dear nurse, do not be so sure of your joy yet,” said Penelope. “You know how everyone in this house would welcome Odysseus home — I most of all, and the son the two of us share. But what you tell me cannot be true. It is some god who has killed the suitors, angry at their pride and their cruelty; for they respected no one in the world, high or low, who came near them, and their wickedness has brought them down at last. As for Odysseus, he is dead, far from the land of the Greeks. He will never come home again.”

“My child, what a thing to say,” the nurse answered. “Your husband is here, at his own hearth this very moment, and still you swear he will never come. But here is one more proof. When I washed him, I felt the scar the wild boar gave him long ago. I wanted to tell you, but he clapped his hand over my mouth — that was his cunning — and would not let me. Come down with me. And I will stake my own life on it: if I have deceived you, kill me by the cruelest death you can think of.”

“Dear nurse, however wise you are, you cannot hope to read the purposes of the gods,” Penelope said. “Still, let us go down to my son, so that I may see the suitors dead and the man who killed them.”

So she came down from her room, and all the way her heart was divided — whether to keep her distance and question her husband, or go straight to him, take his hands, and kiss him. But when she had crossed the stone floor of the hall, she sat down across from Odysseus, in the firelight, against the far wall. He sat by one of the great pillars, his eyes on the ground, waiting to see what his wife would say to him now that she saw him with her own eyes. For a long while she sat in silence, lost in wonder. One moment she looked him full in the face and seemed to know him; the next, the filthy rags threw her off, and she could not. At last Telemachus could bear it no longer, and turned on her.

“Mother — though you are too hard for me to call you that — why do you keep away from my father like this? Why not sit beside him and speak to him, ask him your questions? No other woman alive would hold off from her husband, come home at last after twenty years and everything he has suffered. But your heart was always colder than stone.”

“My son,” Penelope answered, “I am so stunned that I have no words — I cannot ask him anything, I cannot even meet his eyes. But if this truly is Odysseus, home again, the two of us will know each other well enough soon. There are signs between us that no one else knows, signs hidden from everyone in the world.”

Odysseus smiled at that, and said to his son: “Telemachus, let your mother test me however she likes. She will be sure of me soon enough. For now she turns away, because I am caked in dirt and dressed in rags, and she cannot believe it is me. But let us think what to do next. When a man has killed even one other man — one who leaves few friends behind to avenge him — he still has to flee his country and abandon his own people. And we have struck down the flower of Ithaca, the props of the whole town. Think on that.”

“Look to it yourself, father,” said Telemachus. “They say there is no mind in the world like yours, that no other man can match you. We will follow you with all our hearts, and we will not fail you, as long as the strength is in us.”

“Then here is what seems best to me,” Odysseus said. “Wash now, and put on clean shirts, and have the women dress as well. Let Phemius take up his lyre and lead off a dance, so that anyone passing in the street, or any neighbor who hears, will think there is a wedding in the house. Word of the suitors’ deaths must not run through the town before we have slipped away to my own land out in the country. There we will see what plan the gods give us.”

They did just as he said. The men washed and put on their shirts; the women made ready; Phemius took up his lyre and woke in them all a hunger for song and stately dancing. The great house rang with the beat of dancing feet, and out in the street people said to one another, “So the queen has married at last. The faithless woman — she had not the heart to keep her husband’s house safe until he came home.” That was their talk; they had no idea what had truly happened inside.

Meanwhile the housekeeper Eurynome bathed Odysseus and rubbed him with oil and dressed him in a fine cloak and shirt. And Athena poured grace and beauty over him: she made him taller to look on, and stronger, and set the hair thick and curling on his head, like blossoms of hyacinth. As a craftsman skilled by Hephaestus and Athena in every art lays gold over silver and makes a thing of beauty, so the goddess gilded his head and shoulders with grace. He came from the bath looking like one of the deathless gods, and went and sat down again across from his wife, in the seat he had left. “You strange woman,” he said. “The gods have given you a harder heart than they gave any other woman alive. No other wife could keep herself away from her husband, come home at last after twenty years and everything he has suffered. Nurse — make up a bed for me. I will sleep alone. Her heart is iron.”

“You strange man,” Penelope answered. “I am not too proud, and I do not think too little of you. But I know very well what you were like the day you sailed from Ithaca. — Eurycleia, make up the strong bed for him, outside the bedchamber my husband built with his own hands. Carry the bed out here, and pile it with fleeces and good blankets and warm coverlets.”

She said it to test him, and Odysseus flared up at his faithful wife. “Woman,” he said, “that word cuts me. Who has moved my bed? It would be hard for the best craftsman alive — unless a god came down and shifted it with his own hand. No living man, however young and strong, could pry that bed from its place. There is a great sign built into it, a marvel I made myself, and no one else. A long-leaved olive tree was growing inside the courtyard, in full life, thick as a pillar. I built my bedchamber around that tree, and walled it in with close-set stone, and roofed it over, and hung strong doors that shut tight. Then I cut away the leafy crown of the olive, and squared the trunk up from the root, and smoothed it true with the adze, and drew the line down it, and made of it the standing bedpost. Around that post I built the rest of the bed, and inlaid it with gold and silver and ivory, and stretched across it oxhide thongs dyed glowing red. That is the sign I give you. And what I do not know, woman, is whether my bed still stands where it stood — or whether some man has cut through the olive’s root and carried it off.”

At that — the sure sign she alone could know — Penelope’s knees went loose, and her heart with them. She knew him now, past all doubt. She burst into tears and ran straight to him, threw her arms around his neck, and kissed his head. “Do not be angry with me, Odysseus,” she wept. “In all things you were the wisest of men. The gods gave us sorrow; they grudged us the years of growing old side by side. Do not hold it against me now, that I did not run to you the moment I saw you. My heart was cold with dread — that some man might come and trick me with a smooth story. There are so many cruel and scheming men in the world. Helen of Argos herself, daughter of Zeus, would never have lain in love with a man from a foreign land if she had known that the warlike sons of the Greeks would bring her home again to her own country. It was a god who drove her to that shameful act; she had not taken to heart, before, the blindness that was the start of sorrow for us too. But now you have told the sure signs of our bed, which no living soul has seen but you and I, and Actoris, the maid my father gave me when I came here, who keeps the door of our room — now, hard as I was to win over, I doubt you no longer.”

Her words broke something open in him, and he wept, holding his dear and faithful wife. As welcome as land is to swimmers, when Poseidon has smashed their ship far out in the heaving sea, and only a few struggle in out of the gray water to the shore, their bodies crusted with salt, and they climb out glad to be alive and free of death — so welcome to her was the sight of her husband, and she would not loosen her white arms from his neck. And rosy-fingered Dawn would have risen on their weeping, had Athena not thought of one more thing. She held the long night back at the edge of the world, and kept golden Dawn waiting beside the streams of Ocean, and would not let her yoke the swift horses, Lampus and Phaethon, that carry the morning to mankind.

At last Odysseus said, “My wife, we have not yet come to the end of all our trials. There is still a long, hard labor left for me, and I must see it through. So the ghost of Tiresias told me, the day I went down into the house of Hades to ask the way home for myself and my men. But come now, let us go to bed, and take the sweet gift of sleep together.”

“Your bed is ready whenever you wish it,” Penelope said, “now that the gods have brought you home to your own house and your own land. But since some god has put this in your mind, tell me about the labor still to come. I will have to learn of it sooner or later — better to hear it now.”

“You strange woman, why press me to tell you?” Odysseus answered. “Still, I will not hide it. Though it will give you no joy — it gives me none. Tiresias told me to take a well-cut oar and carry it on my shoulder, on and on, until I come to men who know nothing of the sea, who eat their food unsalted, who have never seen ships with their painted cheeks, or the oars that are the wings of a ship. And he gave me a sign I could not miss: when another traveler, meeting me on the road, says I have a winnowing-fan on my shoulder, then I am to plant my oar in the ground and make fine offerings to lord Poseidon — a ram, a bull, and a boar that mounts the sows — and then go home and offer sacred hecatombs to all the deathless gods who hold the wide heaven, each in his turn. And death, he said, will come to me far from the sea — a gentle death that will take me when I am worn out in a sleek old age, with my people prospering around me. All this, he said, would surely come to pass.”

“If the gods mean to grant you a kinder old age,” said Penelope, “then there is hope you will find some rest from your troubles at the last.”

So the two of them talked. Meanwhile Eurynome and the nurse made up the bed with soft coverlets by torchlight. When it was ready, the old nurse went off to her rest, and Eurynome lit them to their room and left them. And gladly they came together again to the rites of the bed they had known of old. As for Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd, they ended the dancing, and the women left off too, and they lay down to sleep through the shadowed halls.

When Odysseus and Penelope had taken their fill of love, they turned to the joy of talking, each telling the other everything. She told all she had borne in the house, watching that crowd of ruinous men slaughter her cattle and her sheep and drain cask after cask of wine, all on her account. And Odysseus told her all the grief he had dealt others and all he had suffered himself, every hardship of it, and she listened with delight and would not let sleep touch her eyes until he had told it to the end.

He began with the Cicones, and how from there he came to the land of the Lotus-eaters; all that he did to the Cyclops, and how he made him pay for the brave companions he had eaten without mercy; how he came to Aeolus, who took him in kindly and sped him on, though it was not yet his fate to reach home, for a storm caught him up and carried him back out over the fish-filled sea, groaning; how he came to Telepylus of the Laestrygonians, who destroyed his ships and their crews, all of them — only Odysseus escaped, in his black ship; the cunning of Circe and all her arts; how he sailed down to the dank house of Hades to question the ghost of Tiresias of Thebes, and saw there all his old companions, and the mother who bore him and raised him; how he heard the Sirens singing on and on, and came to the Wandering Rocks, and dread Charybdis, and Scylla, whom no man ever passed unharmed; how his crew killed the cattle of the Sun, and Zeus struck the swift ship with a smoking thunderbolt, so that all his good men died together and he alone lived on; how he reached the island of Ogygia and the nymph Calypso, who kept him in her hollow cave and longed to make him her husband, and would have made him deathless and ageless forever, but never could win his heart; and how at last, after all his suffering, he came to the Phaeacians, who honored him like a god and sent him home in their ship with gifts of bronze and gold and clothing in plenty. This was the last thing he told her before a sweet sleep came over him and loosed the cares from his heart.

And Athena thought of one more thing. When she judged that Odysseus had had his fill of his wife and of sleep, she roused golden Dawn from the streams of Ocean to bring her light to mankind. Odysseus rose from the soft bed and said to Penelope, “My wife, we have both had our long share of trials — you here, weeping for my return, and I held back from home, year after year, aching to reach it. Now that we are together again at last, take care of all the goods that are left in the house. As for the flocks the suitors have wasted, I will take many myself in raids, and the Greeks shall make good the rest, until my pens are full again. But now I am going out to the wooded farm, to see my father, who has grieved so long for me. And to you, wise as you are, I give one word: at sunrise the news will spread that I have killed the suitors. So go up to your room with your women, and sit there, and see no one, and ask nothing.”

With that he buckled on his armor. He woke Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd and had them all take up their weapons, and they armed. Then they opened the gates and went out, Odysseus leading. It was full daylight by now, but Athena wrapped them in darkness and led them quickly out of the town.
