# The Odyssey, Book XI

The Land of the Dead

“When we had come down to the sea and the ship, the first thing we did was drag her into the bright water and step the mast and set the sails. Then we took the sheep and put them aboard, and climbed in ourselves, heartsick and weeping. And Circe, that dread goddess with a human voice, sent us a good companion behind the dark-prowed ship — a fair wind that filled the sail and held steady, so that we had nothing to do but sit while the wind and the helmsman kept her straight. All day long the sail strained as she ran on across the open sea, until the sun went down and all the roads of the world grew dark.

“So we came to the deep-running stream of Ocean, the river that circles the edge of the world, and to the land and the city of the Cimmerians, a people wrapped forever in mist and cloud. The bright sun never looks down on them with his rays — not when he climbs into the starry sky, not when he turns again from heaven toward the earth — but a deadly night is stretched over those wretched people without end. There we ran the ship aground and put the sheep ashore, and walked along the stream of Ocean until we reached the place Circe had named.

“There Perimedes and Eurylochus held the victims fast, while I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh and dug a pit, a forearm’s length each way. Around it I poured an offering to all the dead — first with milk and honey, then with sweet wine, and a third time with water — and over it I scattered white barley. And I prayed long to the strengthless heads of the dead, vowing that when I came home to Ithaca I would sacrifice my best barren heifer in my own halls and heap the fire with rich gifts; and to Tiresias alone I promised, apart, a ram all black, the finest in my flocks. When I had prayed and made my vows to the great company of the dead, I took the two sheep and cut their throats over the pit, and the dark blood ran down.

“Then up out of Erebus they came swarming, the spirits of the dead and gone — brides and unmarried boys and old men worn down by their long labor; tender girls with grief still fresh in their hearts; and many men cut down in battle, run through by bronze spears, their armor still dark with blood. From every side they crowded the pit with an eerie crying, and a pale terror took hold of me. But I called to my men and told them to flay the sheep that lay there killed by the pitiless bronze, and burn them, and pray to the gods — to mighty Hades and to dread Persephone. And I myself sat with my drawn sword and would not let the powerless ghosts come near the blood until I had questioned Tiresias.

“The first to come was the spirit of my comrade Elpenor. We had not yet buried him under the wide earth; we had left his body behind us in Circe’s hall, unwept and unburied, because other work pressed hard on us. I wept when I saw him, and pity took hold of me, and I spoke to him with winged words: ‘Elpenor, how did you come down here into the gloom and the dark? You came faster on foot than I did in my black ship.’

“And he answered me with a groan: ‘Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes — a god’s cruel doom undid me, and too much wine. I had bedded down on Circe’s roof, and never thought to climb down again by the long ladder. I fell headlong; my neck snapped at the spine, and my soul went down to the house of Hades. Now I beg you, by those you left behind, by your wife, by the father who raised you when you were small, and by Telemachus, the only son you left in your halls — for I know that when you leave this place you will put in again at the island of Aeaea with your trim ship — there, my lord, remember me. Do not sail off and leave me unwept and unburied, or I may become a cause of the gods’ anger against you. Burn me with all the armor that is mine, and heap me a mound on the shore of the gray sea, so men in the days to come will know my sorry story. Do this for me — and fix upon my grave the oar I pulled at while I lived, when I rowed among my friends.’

“And I answered him: ‘All this, poor friend, I will do. I will not fail you.’ So we two sat there trading our sad words — I on the one side, holding my sword out over the blood, and the ghost of my comrade speaking on from the other.

“Then came the spirit of my own dead mother, Anticleia, the daughter of great-hearted Autolycus. I had left her living when I sailed for sacred Troy. I wept to see her, and pity went through me — but even in all my grief, I would not let her come near the blood before I had questioned Tiresias.

“Then came the spirit of Theban Tiresias, his golden staff in his hand. He knew me and spoke: ‘Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes — why have you come, you luckless man, leaving the light of the sun to look on the dead and this joyless place? Stand back from the pit and lower your sharp sword, so I may drink the blood and tell you the truth.’

“So I drew back and slid the silver-studded sword into its sheath. And when he had drunk the dark blood, the faultless prophet spoke to me at last.

“‘You are searching for your sweet homecoming, glorious Odysseus,’ he said, ‘but a god will make it hard. For I do not think you will slip past the earth-shaker, who has set his heart in anger against you, enraged that you blinded his dear son. Even so, you and your men may still come home, for all your suffering — if you can master your own hunger and theirs when first your ship touches the island of Thrinacia, your escape from the violet sea. For there you will find grazing the cattle and the fat flocks of Helios the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things. Leave them unharmed, keep your mind fixed on your homecoming, and you may yet reach Ithaca, hard as the road will be. But if you harm them, then I foretell ruin — for your ship and for your men. And even if you save yourself, you will come home late and broken, having lost all your companions, in a stranger’s ship — and you will find trouble waiting in your house: insolent men devouring your goods, courting your godlike wife, pressing their gifts on her.

“‘But you will make those men pay for their outrages when you come. And after you have killed the suitors in your halls — by cunning or in the open, by the sharp bronze — then you must go out again. Take a well-cut oar and carry it on your shoulder until you come to men who know nothing of the sea, who eat their food unsalted, who have never seen ships with their painted cheeks or the oars that are the wings of a ship. And I will give you a sign so clear you cannot miss it. When another traveler, meeting you, says you have a winnowing-fan on your strong shoulder — then plant your oar in the ground and make fine offerings to lord Poseidon: a ram, a bull, and a boar that mounts the sows. Then go home and offer sacred hecatombs to the deathless gods who hold the wide heavens, to all of them, each in his turn. And death will come to you, far from the sea — a gentle death that will take you down when you are worn out in a sleek old age, your people prospering around you. All this I tell you is the truth.’

“And I answered him: ‘Tiresias, surely the gods themselves have spun this out as my fate. But tell me this, and tell me true. I see the spirit of my dead mother here. She sits near the blood in silence, and cannot bring herself to look her own son in the face or say a word to him. Tell me, my lord — how can I make her know me for who I am?’

“And he answered me at once: ‘That is an easy thing to tell you. Whichever of the dead you let come near the blood will speak to you, and speak the truth. But the one you refuse will turn and go back again.’ And with that the spirit of lord Tiresias went back into the house of Hades, his prophecy spoken.

“But I stayed where I was until my mother came up and drank the dark blood. At once she knew me, and her grief broke into words: ‘My child, how did you come down here, into all this darkness, and you still alive? It is hard for the living to look on these things. Between us and you lie great rivers and dreadful waters — Ocean first of all, which no man can cross on foot, but only in a well-built ship. Have you come here from Troy, wandering all this long while with your ship and your men? Have you not yet reached Ithaca, not yet seen your wife in your own halls?’

“And I answered her: ‘Mother, I had no choice — I had to come down to the house of Hades to ask the spirit of Theban Tiresias. I have not yet come near Greece, not yet set foot on my own soil. I have been wandering in misery from the first day I followed great Agamemnon to Ilios, the land of fine horses, to fight the Trojans. But tell me this, and tell me true. What death laid you low? Was it a long sickness, or did Artemis the archer come with her gentle arrows and bring you down? And tell me of my father, and of the son I left behind. Is my honor still safe in their hands, or has some other man taken it, thinking I will never come home? And tell me what my wedded wife intends, what is in her mind. Does she still hold to me and guard all that is mine — or has one of the Greeks, the best of them, married her by now?’

“And my honored mother answered me: ‘She holds out still, her heart enduring, there in your halls. Her nights and her days waste away in sorrow and weeping. No man has yet taken your fine kingship; Telemachus holds your lands in peace and sits at the public feasts that a man who deals out justice should attend, for everyone invites him. But your father stays out in the country and never comes to town. He has no bed now, no blankets, no bright coverlets. In winter he sleeps in the house where the servants sleep, in the ashes by the fire, with poor rags on his body. And when summer comes and the rich harvest-time, he lies anywhere on the ground on the slope of his vineyard, on fallen leaves, grieving, nursing the sorrow in his heart, longing for you to come — and a hard old age has come on him. That is how I too died and met my fate. It was not the keen-eyed archer Artemis who shot me down with her gentle arrows in my own halls, nor any sickness that wastes the body and drains the spirit away. It was longing for you, my glorious Odysseus — your kindness and your counsel — that took the sweet life out of me.’

“So she spoke, and I longed in my heart to hold the spirit of my dead mother. Three times I started toward her, my whole heart aching to take her in my arms; three times she slipped through my hands like a shadow, like a dream. And the grief in me grew sharper, and I spoke to her with winged words: ‘Mother, why won’t you stay for me, when I long to hold you — so that even here in the house of Hades we might throw our arms around each other and take some cold comfort in our shared sorrow? Is this only some phantom that dread Persephone has sent me, to make me weep and grieve all the more?’

“And my honored mother answered: ‘Oh my child, unluckiest of all men born — Persephone, the daughter of Zeus, is not deceiving you. This is the way of mortals when they die. The sinews no longer hold the flesh and the bones together; the strong fury of the burning fire destroys all that, as soon as the life leaves the white bones, and the soul flutters off like a dream and is gone. But hurry back to the light now, with all speed. And keep all this in mind, so you may tell it to your wife in the days to come.’

“So we two spoke together. And then the women came — for dread Persephone drove them up to me, all the wives and daughters of the great men of old. They crowded around the dark blood, and I wondered how I might question them each in turn. This seemed the best plan: I drew the long sword from beside my sturdy thigh and would not let them all drink the dark blood at once. So they came up one by one, and each told me her birth, and I questioned them all.

“The first I saw was high-born Tyro, who said she was the daughter of faultless Salmoneus and the wife of Cretheus, son of Aeolus. She had fallen in love with a river, the god Enipeus, far the loveliest of the rivers that run on the earth, and she used to walk by his beautiful waters. There Poseidon, who holds and shakes the earth, took the river’s shape and lay with her where the eddying stream runs into the sea. A dark wave reared over them, high as a mountain, curving to hide the god and the mortal woman. He loosed her maiden girdle and shed sleep over her. And when the god had finished the work of love, he took her by the hand and said: ‘Be glad, woman, of this love. As the year comes round you will bear glorious children, for the bed of a god is never barren. Care for them and raise them. Now go home, and hold your tongue, and tell no one — for know that I am Poseidon, the earth-shaker.’ With that he sank beneath the heaving sea. And she conceived and bore Pelias and Neleus, who both grew to be strong servants of mighty Zeus — Pelias rich in flocks, living in spacious Iolcus, and Neleus in sandy Pylos. And to Cretheus this queen of women bore others: Aeson, and Pheres, and Amythaon who fought from his chariot.

“After her I saw Antiope, the daughter of Asopus, who boasted she had slept in the very arms of Zeus, and bore him two sons, Amphion and Zethus. They were the first to found the seat of seven-gated Thebes and to wall it round, for strong as they were, they could not hold open Thebes without its walls. Next I saw Alcmene, the wife of Amphitryon, who lay in the arms of great Zeus and bore unconquerable, lion-hearted Heracles; and Megara, daughter of proud Creon, whom the tireless son of Amphitryon took as his wife.

“And I saw the mother of Oedipus, lovely Epicaste, who did a monstrous thing without knowing it — she married her own son. He had killed his own father, and married her, and the gods soon made it known to men. Yet for all his anguish he went on ruling the Thebans in their lovely land, by the cruel design of the gods; but she went down to the house of Hades, the strong gatekeeper, when she had knotted a high noose to a beam and hanged herself, mastered by her grief — and she left him agonies past counting, all that a mother’s avenging Furies bring to pass.

“And I saw beautiful Chloris, whom Neleus once married for her beauty, after he had paid a fortune in bride-gifts. She was the youngest daughter of Amphion, son of Iasus, who once ruled with power in Minyan Orchomenus. She was queen in Pylos and bore him splendid children — Nestor, and Chromius, and lordly Periclymenus — and besides them she bore stately Pero, a wonder to all who saw her, whom every man around courted. But Neleus would give her only to the man who could drive off the cattle of mighty Iphicles from Phylace — a heavy task. A faultless seer alone promised to do it, but a god’s harsh fate bound him fast, and the rough herdsmen and their chains. Yet when the months and the days had run their course and the seasons came round again, mighty Iphicles set him free, once he had told out all the oracles. And so the will of Zeus was done.

“And I saw Leda, the wife of Tyndareus, who bore him two strong-hearted sons — Castor, breaker of horses, and Polydeuces, mighty with his fists. The living earth holds them both, and yet they live: even under the ground Zeus gives them honor. One day they live and the next day they are dead, each in his turn, and they have won honor like the gods’.

“After her I saw Iphimedeia, the wife of Aloeus, who said she had lain with Poseidon. She bore two sons, but their lives were short — godlike Otus and far-famed Ephialtes, the tallest men the grain-giving earth ever raised, and the most beautiful by far, after famous Orion. At nine years old they were nine cubits across and nine fathoms tall, and they threatened to bring the roar of raging war up to the immortals on Olympus. They strained to pile Mount Ossa on Olympus, and leafy Pelion on Ossa, to make a stairway into heaven — and they would have done it, had they only reached the full measure of their strength. But the son of Zeus, whom fair-haired Leto bore, destroyed them both before the first down ever curled on their cheeks or covered their chins with a young beard.

“And I saw Phaedra, and Procris, and lovely Ariadne, the daughter of merciless Minos, whom Theseus once carried off from Crete toward the high ground of sacred Athens — but he had no joy of her, for first Artemis killed her on sea-girt Dia, on the word of Dionysus. And I saw Maera, and Clymene, and hateful Eriphyle, who took gold as the price of her own husband’s life.

“But I could never name them all, all the wives and daughters of heroes that I saw; the immortal night would run out first. It is time now to sleep, whether I go down to the swift ship and the crew, or stay here. My setting-out will be in your hands, and the gods’.”

So he spoke, and they all sat hushed and still, held in a spell through the shadowy hall. Then white-armed Arete was the first to break the silence: “Phaeacians, what do you think of this man — his looks, his stature, the steady mind within him? He is my own guest, but every one of you shares the honor of it. So do not be in a hurry to send him on, and do not stint your gifts to a man in such great need. You have many treasures in your halls, by the gods’ good grace.”

Then the old hero Echeneus spoke up, the eldest among the Phaeacians: “My friends, what our wise queen says is not wide of the mark or far from our own thinking — so do as she says. But the word and the work both rest with Alcinous here.”

And Alcinous answered him: “Then let it stand as she has said, as surely as I am alive and rule over the Phaeacians who love the oar. Let our guest, for all his longing to be home, hold on until tomorrow, until I have made the whole gift ready. His passage will be the care of all of us, and mine most of all, for the power in this land is mine.”

And Odysseus, the man of many turns, answered him: “Lord Alcinous, most renowned of all your people — if you bade me wait here even a whole year while you readied my passage and your splendid gifts, I would gladly do it. It would be far better to reach my own dear country with fuller hands; I would be more honored, more loved, by every man who saw me come home to Ithaca.”

And Alcinous answered him: “Odysseus, looking at you, we do not take you for a cheat or a liar, the kind the dark earth breeds in such numbers, men who spin lies out of nothing a man could ever see. There is a grace on your words, and a sound mind within, and you have told your tale with the skill of a singer — the heavy sorrows of yourself and all the Greeks. But tell me this now, and tell me true. Did you see any of your godlike companions there, who went with you to Troy and met their fate? The night is long, beyond all measure; it is not yet time to sleep in the hall. Tell me these wonders. I could hold out until bright dawn, if only you were willing to go on telling me your sorrows here.”

And Odysseus, the man of many turns, answered him: “Lord Alcinous, most renowned of all your people — there is a time for long telling, and a time for sleep. But if you still long to hear, then I will not hold back from telling you of things still more pitiful than these — the sorrows of my companions who died afterward, who came safe out of the Trojans’ bitter war only to die on their return, by the will of a wicked woman.

“When holy Persephone had scattered the spirits of the women this way and that, there came the grieving spirit of Agamemnon, the son of Atreus, and around him gathered all the others who had died with him in the house of Aegisthus, meeting their fate there. He knew me at once, when he had drunk the dark blood. He wept aloud, the warm tears falling, and reached out his arms to me, longing to hold me — but the strength was gone, the firm power that had once filled his supple limbs. I wept when I saw him, and pity went through my heart, and I spoke to him with winged words: ‘Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men — what fate of pitiless death brought you down? Did Poseidon raise a dreadful blast of cruel winds and break you on the sea? Or did enemies cut you down on dry land while you were rounding up their cattle and fine flocks, or fighting for their city and their women?’

“And he answered me at once: ‘Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes — no, Poseidon did not break me on the sea with a blast of cruel winds, and no enemies cut me down on dry land. It was Aegisthus who plotted my death and my doom — he and my own accursed wife. He asked me to his house, he feasted me, and he killed me as a man kills an ox at its manger. So I died, the most pitiable death, and around me my companions were slaughtered without mercy, like white-tusked pigs cut down for a wedding feast or a banquet in a rich man’s house. You have seen many men killed, in single combat and in the press of battle — but your heart would have ached most of all to see how we lay among the wine-bowls and the laden tables, all through the hall, and the whole floor running with blood. The most pitiful cry I ever heard was the voice of Cassandra, Priam’s daughter, whom treacherous Clytemnestra killed at my side. I lifted my hands and beat the ground as I died with the sword in me; but the brazen-faced woman turned away and would not, though I was on my way to Hades, close my eyes with her hands or shut my mouth. There is nothing more dreadful, nothing more shameless, than a woman who can set her heart on a deed like this — as she did, plotting that monstrous thing, the murder of the husband of her youth. I thought I would come home to the welcome of my children and my servants — but she, with her heart bent on utter evil, has poured shame on herself and on all the women yet to be born, even on one who does what is right.’

“And I answered him: ‘It is true — far-thundering Zeus has always borne a terrible hatred against the line of Atreus, working through the schemes of women. For Helen’s sake so many of us died, and Clytemnestra laid her trap for you while you were far away.’

“And he said: ‘So you too — never be soft, even with your own wife. Do not tell her everything in your mind. Tell her one thing, and keep the rest hidden. And yet you, Odysseus, will not be murdered by your wife — Penelope, Icarius’ daughter, is too wise, too sound of heart for that. We left her a young bride when we marched off to war, with an infant at her breast, a boy who must be sitting now among grown men. Lucky child — his father will come home and see him, and he will throw his arms around his father, as a son should. But my wife would not even let me feast my eyes on my own son; she killed me first. And here is one more thing — take it to heart. Bring your ship in to your own dear land in secret, not in the open, for there is no trusting women anymore. But tell me this now, and tell me true — have you heard anything of my son, still living? In Orchomenus, perhaps, or sandy Pylos, or with Menelaus in broad Sparta? For surely my Orestes is not yet dead and gone.’

“And I answered him: ‘Son of Atreus, why ask me this? I do not know whether he is alive or dead, and it is a poor thing to speak words into the wind.’

“So we two stood trading our sad words, grieving, the warm tears falling. And then there came the spirit of Achilles, son of Peleus, and the spirits of Patroclus and faultless Antilochus and of Ajax, who in build and in beauty was the finest of all the Greeks after the matchless son of Peleus. The spirit of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus knew me, and grieving he spoke to me with winged words: ‘Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes — reckless man, what greater thing than this could your heart still devise? How did you dare come down to Hades, where the dead live on without their wits, the worn-out husks of men who have done with their labor?’

“And I answered him: ‘Achilles, son of Peleus, far the strongest of all the Greeks — I came needing Tiresias, to see if he could tell me any way to reach rocky Ithaca. For I have not yet come near Greece, nor set foot on my own land. I have nothing but trouble. But you, Achilles — no man was ever more blessed than you, nor ever will be. While you lived we Greeks honored you like a god, and now that you are here you rule with great power among the dead. So do not grieve at your dying, Achilles.’

“And he answered me at once: ‘Don’t gild death for me, glorious Odysseus. I would rather be a hired hand, working another man’s land — a man with no portion of his own, scraping by — and be alive, than be lord over all the wasted dead. But tell me of my son, my proud boy. Did he follow the war as a leader, or not? And tell me of faultless Peleus, if you have heard — does he still keep his honor among the Myrmidons, or do they scorn him now through Hellas and Phthia, because old age has him by the hands and feet? For I am no help to him under the sun’s light, the man I was on the wide plain of Troy, when I cut down the best of their army defending the Greeks. If I could come, even for a moment, to my father’s house with that strength still in me, I would give any man who shames him and bars him from his honor good reason to dread these hands of mine.’

“And I answered him: ‘Of faultless Peleus I have heard nothing. But of your dear son Neoptolemus I will tell you all the truth, as you ask. It was I myself who brought him over from Scyros in my trim, hollow ship, to join the well-greaved Greeks. Whenever we held council before Troy, he was always the first to speak, and he never missed the mark; only godlike Nestor and I outdid him. And when we fought on the Trojan plain, he never hung back in the crowd of men, but always charged far out in front, yielding to no one in his fury. He killed many men in the dread press of battle — I could not name them all, all the host he cut down defending the Greeks — but I will tell you how he brought down the hero Eurypylus, son of Telephus, with the bronze, and many of the Ceteians around him fell, all for a woman’s bribes. He was the handsomest man I ever saw, after godlike Memnon. And when we, the best of the Greeks, were climbing into the horse that Epeus built, and the whole thing was left in my hands — to open our hiding-place or to keep it shut — then the other leaders and counselors of the Greeks were wiping the tears from their eyes, and every man’s legs were shaking under him. But him I never once saw turn pale in his handsome face, or wipe a tear away. He kept begging me to let him burst out of the horse, gripping his sword-hilt and his heavy bronze spear, mad to fall on the Trojans. And when we had sacked Priam’s high city, he went aboard his ship with his full share and a fine prize, not a scratch on him — never struck by a flung spear, never wounded in close fighting, the way men so often are in war, when Ares rages without rule.’

“So I spoke, and the spirit of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus went off with long strides across the field of asphodel, glad that I had told him his son was a man of valor.

“The other spirits of the dead and gone stood grieving around me, and each one asked after those it loved. Only the spirit of Ajax, son of Telamon, held off, apart, still burning over the victory I had won against him at the ships, in the contest for the arms of Achilles. His honored mother had set them up as the prize, and the sons of the Trojans and Pallas Athena were the judges. I wish I had never won such a contest — for it laid in the earth so great a man, Ajax, who in build and in deeds was the finest of all the Greeks after the matchless son of Peleus. I called out to him gently: ‘Ajax, son of faultless Telamon — even in death, will you not let go your anger at me over that cursed armor? The gods set it there as a grief to the Greeks; in you we lost so great a tower of strength. We mourned you, when you died, as bitterly as we mourned Achilles himself. No one is to blame but Zeus, who hated the Greek army with a terrible hatred, and fixed your doom. Come here, my lord, and listen — master that fury, that proud spirit of yours.’

“He gave me not one word in answer, but turned and went off into Erebus, to join the other spirits of the dead. Even so, angry as he was, he might still have spoken to me, or I to him — but the heart in me longed to see the other spirits of the dead.

“And there I saw Minos, the glorious son of Zeus, his golden staff in his hand, seated and giving judgment over the dead, while around him they pressed their cases, sitting and standing through the wide-gated house of Hades. After him I saw huge Orion, herding across the field of asphodel the wild beasts he had killed himself on the lonely hills, a club of solid bronze in his hands, unbroken forever.

“And I saw Tityus, the son of Earth, stretched out on the ground, covering nine acres where he lay. Two vultures, one on either side, sat tearing at his liver, plunging their beaks into the flesh, and he could not beat them off — because he had laid hands on Leto, the honored consort of Zeus, as she went up to Pytho through the lovely fields of Panopeus.

“And I saw Tantalus too, in his cruel torment, standing in a pool that lapped at his chin. He strained with thirst, and could never reach the water to drink: every time the old man bent to it, the water would drain away and vanish, and the black earth would show at his feet, dried up by some god. And tall trees leaned their fruit down over his head — pears and pomegranates, apples gleaming bright, sweet figs and ripe olives — but whenever the old man stretched up to take them, the wind would toss the branches away to the shadowy clouds.

“And I saw Sisyphus too, in his agony, heaving a monstrous stone with both his hands. He would brace himself, hands and feet, and shove it up toward the crest of a hill — but just as he was about to push it over the top, the weight would turn it back, and down to the plain the shameless stone would come crashing again. And he would dig in once more and strain to push it up, the sweat pouring from his limbs, the dust rising from his head.

“And after him I saw mighty Heracles — his phantom, that is, for the man himself is among the immortal gods, taking his ease at their feasts, with lovely-ankled Hebe for his wife, the daughter of great Zeus and golden-sandaled Hera. Around him the dead made a din like startled birds, scattering in every direction. He came on like black night, his bow stripped bare, an arrow on the string, glaring fearsomely as if forever about to shoot. And across his chest was a terrible baldric, a strap of gold worked with marvels — bears and wild boars and lions with flashing eyes, and battles and slaughter and the killing of men. May the craftsman who put his art into that belt never make another like it. He knew me the moment he laid eyes on me, and grieving he spoke winged words: ‘Royal son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many schemes — poor man, so you too drag out a hard fate, the same I bore under the light of the sun. I was a son of Zeus, son of Cronus, and yet my suffering had no end. I was bound in service to a man far worse than myself, who laid the heaviest labors on me. He even sent me down here once, to bring up the hound of Hades — for he thought he could set me no harder task than that. But I carried the hound up out of Hades, with Hermes and gray-eyed Athena to guide me.’

“With that he went back into the house of Hades, but I stayed on where I was, in case some other of the heroes of old should come — and I would have seen still more of the men of long ago whom I longed to see, Theseus and Pirithous, the glorious children of the gods. But before that, the numberless tribes of the dead came swarming around me with an eerie cry, and a pale dread seized me — that holy Persephone might send up out of Hades the head of the Gorgon, that monstrous, fearful thing. So I went straight back to my ship and told my men to climb aboard and loose the cables. They went quickly to their places at the oars, and the current carried us down the stream of Ocean — rowing at first, and then a fair wind came on.”
