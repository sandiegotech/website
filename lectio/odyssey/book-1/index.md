# The Odyssey, Book I

Athena Visits Ithaca

Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turns — the one who wandered far and long after he sacked the holy city of Troy. He saw the cities of many peoples and learned how they thought; he suffered deep grief on the open sea, fighting for his own life and for the safe homecoming of his men. But he could not save them, hard as he tried — they died by their own reckless folly, those fools, when they devoured the cattle of Hyperion the Sun, who took from them the day of their return. Of these things, goddess, daughter of Zeus, begin where you will, and tell us too.

By now everyone who had escaped a violent death — in the war or on the sea — was home. Only Odysseus was still kept from his wife and his own country, held back by the nymph Calypso, a queen among goddesses, who wanted him for a husband and kept him deep inside her hollow cave. And when at last, as the years rolled on, the time came that the gods had spun for him to return to Ithaca, his trials were still not over, not even among his own people. The gods pitied him now — all of them but Poseidon, who stayed furious with godlike Odysseus and would not let him reach his homeland.

But Poseidon had gone off to the distant Ethiopians — the Ethiopians who live at the ends of the earth, split in two, half toward the setting sun and half toward the dawn — to accept a great offering of bulls and rams, and there he sat, enjoying the feast. The rest of the gods, meanwhile, gathered in the halls of Olympian Zeus. And the father of gods and men spoke first, for his mind was on Aegisthus, on noble Aegisthus whom Agamemnon’s son, far-famed Orestes, had killed. With that in mind, he spoke out to the immortals:

“It’s a marvel — how mortals are forever blaming the gods. They say their troubles come from us, when in fact it’s their own reckless folly that brings them grief beyond their share. Look at Aegisthus. He took Agamemnon’s lawful wife for himself and killed the man when he came home — and he knew it would destroy him, because we warned him. We sent Hermes, the keen-eyed slayer of Argos, to tell him: don’t kill the man, don’t court his wife, because Orestes will come for vengeance once he’s grown and longs for his own land. Hermes told him this for his own good — but he wouldn’t listen. And now he has paid for it, all at once, in full.”

Then gray-eyed Athena answered him: “Father of us all, son of Cronus, highest of the mighty — that man got exactly what he deserved, and so should anyone who does what he did. But it’s not Aegisthus I’m thinking of. My heart aches for Odysseus, that wise, unlucky man, kept so long from everyone he loves on a lonely island ringed by the sea, the very navel of the waters. It’s a wooded island, and a goddess lives there — daughter of cruel Atlas, who knows the depths of every sea and holds up by himself the great pillars that keep the earth and sky apart. His daughter holds that grieving, broken man, and works on him day after day with soft, coaxing words to make him forget Ithaca. But Odysseus, longing only to see the smoke rising from his own land, wants to die. And still your heart doesn’t turn, Olympian. Didn’t Odysseus please you, sacrificing beside the Greek ships on the wide plain of Troy? Why are you still so set against him, Zeus?”

And Zeus who gathers the clouds answered her: “My child, what a thing to let past your lips. How could I ever forget godlike Odysseus? No mortal alive has a finer mind, and no one has given more generously to the immortal gods who hold the wide heavens. No — it’s Poseidon who circles the earth who keeps up this relentless rage, all because of the Cyclops whose eye Odysseus blinded: Polyphemus, mightiest of them all, son of Poseidon by the nymph Thoosa, daughter of Phorcys, lord of the restless sea. Ever since Odysseus put out his eye, Poseidon the earth-shaker won’t kill him, but drives him wandering far from home. Still — let all of us put our heads together and find him a way back. Poseidon will have to let his anger go. He can’t hold out alone, against the will of every god.”

Then gray-eyed Athena answered him: “Father of us all, son of Cronus, highest of the mighty — if it truly pleases the blessed gods now that wise Odysseus should come home, then let us send Hermes the runner, the slayer of Argos, to the island of Ogygia, to tell the lovely-haired nymph straight out what we’ve decided: that long-suffering Odysseus is to set out for home. As for me, I’ll go down to Ithaca, to put fresh heart into his son. I’ll fill him with courage, call the long-haired Greeks to assembly, and have him speak his mind to the suitors — those men forever slaughtering his crowded flocks and his shambling, twist-horned cattle. And I’ll send him to Sparta and to sandy Pylos to ask after his dear father’s homecoming, so the world may speak well of him.”

So she spoke, and she bound beneath her feet the beautiful sandals, immortal and golden, that carry her over the water and the endless earth as fast as the wind. And she took up her heavy spear, tipped with sharp bronze — massive, solid, strong, the spear with which she breaks the ranks of warriors who cross her, the goddess and daughter of a mighty father. Down from the peaks of Olympus she swept, and stood in Ithaca at the gate of Odysseus’ courtyard, on the threshold of the hall, the bronze spear in her hand — taking the form of a guest-friend, Mentes, the lord of the Taphians. There she found the proud suitors, sprawled on the hides of oxen they themselves had killed and eaten, amusing themselves with their game of counters in front of the doors. Heralds and quick servants worked around them — some mixing wine and water in the bowls, some wiping the tables down with their sponges and setting them out again, others carving up great portions of meat.

Godlike Telemachus saw her long before anyone else. He had been sitting among the suitors, sick at heart, picturing his noble father — how he might come back from somewhere and scatter these men through the house, and win back his own honor and rule his own estate. Lost in that thought among them, he caught sight of Athena and went straight to the gate, stung that a stranger had been left standing there at the door. He came up close, took her right hand, lifted the bronze spear from her, and spoke to her with winged words: “Welcome, stranger. You’ll be treated kindly here. Eat first, and then tell us what you need.”

With that he led the way, and Pallas Athena followed. When they were inside the high-roofed hall, he carried her spear to the tall pillar and set it in the polished rack, among the many spears of long-suffering Odysseus. Then he led her to a chair and seated her there, spreading a fine cloth beneath her, with a footstool under her feet. He drew up an inlaid bench for himself beside her, apart from the suitors, so his guest wouldn’t be sickened by the racket of those arrogant men and lose his appetite — and so he could ask, in private, about his absent father.

A maid came with water in a beautiful golden pitcher and poured it over their hands into a silver basin, and drew up a polished table beside them. A grave housekeeper brought bread and laid it out, and many good things besides, giving freely from her stores. A carver lifted platters of every kind of meat and set them down, and placed golden cups beside them, while a herald came round again and again to fill the cups with wine.

Then the proud suitors came in. One by one they took their seats on the chairs and benches. Heralds poured water over their hands, serving women heaped the baskets with bread, and young men filled the bowls to the brim with wine. The men reached out for the good things spread before them, and when they’d had their fill of food and drink, their hearts turned to other things — to song and dancing, the crowning glories of a feast. A herald put a beautiful lyre into the hands of Phemius, who sang for the suitors against his will. He struck the strings and lifted his voice in a lovely song — and just then Telemachus leaned in close to gray-eyed Athena, his head near hers, so the others wouldn’t hear, and said:

“Dear guest, I hope you won’t take offense at what I’m about to say. These men have it easy — music and song, all of it free, because they’re eating up another man’s living, a man whose white bones are lying somewhere in the rain, rotting on dry land or rolling in the surf of the sea. If they ever saw him set foot in Ithaca again, every one of them would pray for faster legs instead of more gold — gold couldn’t save them. But he’s gone, lost to some cruel fate, and there’s no comfort left for us, even when someone on earth claims he’s still coming. The day of his return is dead. Now tell me this, honestly — who are you? Where do you come from? Where is your city, your family? What ship brought you here, and what crew? How did they bring you to Ithaca, and who did they say they were? You surely didn’t come on foot. And tell me the truth: are you new to this house, or were you a friend of my father’s? Many men came to our door, for he went about among men a great deal, in his time.”

Then gray-eyed Athena answered him: “I’ll tell you, and I’ll tell you straight. I am Mentes, son of wise Anchialus, and I rule the Taphians, who love their oars. I’ve come ashore here with my ship and crew, sailing the wine-dark sea toward men of a foreign tongue — to Temesa, for copper, with a cargo of gleaming iron to trade. My ship is moored away from the town, off in the open country, in the harbor of Rheithron, under wooded Mount Neion. As for us, we’re guest-friends from way back, on our fathers’ side — go and ask the old hero Laertes, if you like. They tell me he doesn’t come to town anymore, but lives out in the country, suffering, with one old serving woman to set out his food and drink whenever the weariness drags at his limbs from hauling himself up and down his vineyard slope. I came because they said your father was home at last — but it seems the gods are still blocking his road. Brilliant Odysseus is not dead, not yet, somewhere on this wide earth. He’s alive, held back on some sea-ringed island in the middle of the deep, in the hands of rough, savage men who keep him there against his will. But let me tell you what the gods put into my heart — and I believe it will come true, though I’m no prophet and read no clear omen in birds. He won’t be away from his own land much longer. Even bound in iron chains, that man would find a way home — he’s that full of cunning. But tell me this, honestly: are you really Odysseus’ son, big as you are now? You look so like him about the head and the fine eyes — for we two met often, before he sailed for Troy, where the bravest of the Greeks went in their hollow ships. Since then, neither of us has laid eyes on the other.”

And thoughtful Telemachus answered her: “I’ll tell you the truth, stranger. My mother says I’m his son — but I don’t know it for myself. No man ever really knows his own father. I only wish I were the son of some lucky man who grew old among his own possessions. As it is, they tell me I come from the unluckiest man ever born — and since you ask, that’s my answer.”

Then gray-eyed Athena said: “Surely the gods haven’t doomed your line to vanish without a name — not while Penelope brings such a son as you into the world. But tell me this, honestly: what is this feast, this whole crowd? What do you need with it? Is it a banquet, a wedding? It’s clearly no meal where each man pays his share — they swagger through the house so insolently, gorging themselves. Any decent man walking in on all this disgrace would be sick at the sight.”

And thoughtful Telemachus answered her: “Stranger, since you ask me — once, while that man was still among us, this house was rich and honorable. But the gods turned against us and willed otherwise, and they have made him vanish more completely than any man before him. I wouldn’t grieve even his death so much, if he had gone down with his men in the land of the Trojans, or died in the arms of his friends once the fighting was done. Then all the Greeks would have raised a mound over him, and he’d have won great glory for his son to inherit. Instead the storm-winds snatched him away, no glory, no trace — gone, leaving me nothing but grief and weeping. And it isn’t only him I mourn for; the gods have piled other miseries on me besides. Every lord with power in the islands — in Dulichium, in Same, in wooded Zacynthus — and every man of rank in rocky Ithaca itself is courting my mother and wasting my house. She won’t refuse the hateful marriage outright, but she can’t bring herself to end it either. And so they devour my living, and soon enough they’ll tear me apart as well.”

Then Pallas Athena answered, stirred with anger: “Oh, how badly you need Odysseus home, to lay his hands on these brazen suitors. If only he could come now and stand at the outer gate of his house — helmet on, shield and two spears in hand, the man he was when I first saw him in our halls, drinking and enjoying himself! He had just come back from Ephyra then, from the house of Ilos son of Mermerus, where he’d gone in his fast ship to beg a deadly poison to smear on his bronze-tipped arrows. Ilos wouldn’t give it to him, out of fear of the everlasting gods, but my father gave him some, he loved him so. If only that Odysseus could meet these suitors now — every one of them would find a quick death and a bitter wedding.

“But that, of course, lies on the knees of the gods — whether he comes back and takes his revenge in his own halls, or not. What I urge you to think about now is how to drive these suitors out of the house. So listen, and take what I say to heart. Tomorrow morning call the Greek heroes to assembly, speak your mind to them all, and let the gods be your witnesses. Tell the suitors to scatter, each to his own home. And as for your mother — if her heart is set on marrying — let her go back to her powerful father’s house. They’ll arrange the wedding there and provide the gifts a beloved daughter should have. And here is my own clear advice for you: get the best ship you can, man it with twenty oarsmen, and go searching for your father, gone so long. Someone may tell you something, or you may catch a rumor sent by Zeus, the kind that most often brings men news. First go to Pylos and ask noble Nestor, then on to Sparta to fair-haired Menelaus, last of the bronze-armored Greeks to reach home. If you hear your father’s alive and on his way, then hold out one more year, hard as it is. But if you hear he’s dead, gone from this world, then come home, heap up a mound for him, give him the full funeral honors he deserves, and hand your mother to a new husband. And once you’ve seen all that done, search your mind and heart for a way to kill these suitors in your halls — by cunning or in the open. You’re too old now to act like a child. Haven’t you heard how the whole world praises Orestes for the glory he won, killing Aegisthus, the sly murderer of his famous father? You too, my friend — and I can see how tall and handsome you are — be brave, so that men in years to come will speak well of you. But now I must go down to my fast ship and my crew, who are surely fretting at the wait. Look to this yourself, and remember what I’ve told you.”

Then thoughtful Telemachus answered her: “Stranger, you’ve spoken to me as kindly as a father to his son, and I will never forget your words. But stay a little longer, eager as you are to be off. Bathe first, ease your heart, and then go back to your ship glad at heart, carrying a gift from me — something fine and precious, a keepsake to treasure, the kind of thing dear friends give one another.”

Then gray-eyed Athena answered him: “Don’t hold me back now — I’m anxious to be on my way. As for the gift your heart moves you to give me, save it for when I come again, to carry home. Choose a truly fine one, and you’ll get its worth in return.”

And with that gray-eyed Athena was gone, soaring up and away like a bird — but she had set strength and courage in his heart and made him think of his father more than ever. He turned it over in his mind and was struck with wonder, for he sensed she had been a god. Then, godlike as he was, he went straight back to the suitors.

The famous singer was still singing to them, and they sat in silence, listening, as he told the song of the Greeks’ bitter homecoming from Troy, the doom gray-eyed Athena had laid on them. And up in her room, wise Penelope, daughter of Icarius, heard his haunting song. She came down the high stairs of her house — not alone; two serving women went with her. And when that radiant woman reached the suitors, she stood by the doorpost of the great roofed hall, holding a shimmering veil before her face, a faithful maid on either side. And she wept, and spoke to the god-touched singer:

“Phemius, you know so many other songs that hold men spellbound — the deeds of gods and heroes that singers love to praise. Sing them one of those, and let them drink their wine in silence. But stop this one, this bitter song — every time, it breaks the heart in my breast, because grief beyond all bearing has fallen on me. I long without end for that man, always remembering him — so dear a man, whose fame fills Hellas and the heart of Argos.”

Then thoughtful Telemachus answered her: “Mother, why grudge the loyal singer the right to delight us however his spirit moves him? Singers aren’t to blame — Zeus is the one to blame, who hands out fortune to mortal men however he pleases. There’s nothing wrong in this man singing the hard fate of the Greeks. People always praise most warmly the newest song that reaches their ears. Steel your heart and listen. Odysseus wasn’t the only one who lost the day of his return at Troy; many another good man went down as well. Now go back inside and see to your own work — the loom, the distaff — and tell your women to get on with theirs. Speech will be the men’s concern, and mine above all, for the power in this house is mine.”

She went back into the house, amazed, and took her son’s strong words to heart. Climbing the stairs with her serving women to her room, she wept there for Odysseus, her beloved husband, until gray-eyed Athena cast sweet sleep over her eyes. Down below, the suitors broke into uproar through the shadowy halls, every man praying to lie in her bed.

Then thoughtful Telemachus rounded on them: “Suitors of my mother — you arrogant, overbearing men — let’s enjoy the feast for now, and no more shouting. It’s a fine thing to listen to a singer like this, with a voice that sounds like a god’s. But in the morning let’s all take our seats in assembly, so I can give you plain notice to clear out of this house. Feast somewhere else — go through your own provisions, taking turns from house to house. But if you think it’s better, easier, to keep grinding down one man’s living and pay nothing, then strip it bare. I’ll cry out to the everlasting gods, and pray that Zeus pays you back for what you’ve done. Then you’ll die inside these very halls, with no one left to avenge you.”

So he spoke, and they all bit their lips, stunned that Telemachus would dare to speak so boldly. Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, answered him: “Telemachus, the gods themselves must be teaching you this — to talk big and speak with such nerve. May the son of Cronus never make you king of sea-girt Ithaca, though it’s yours by birth, from your father.”

And thoughtful Telemachus answered him: “Antinous, you may not like what I’m about to say, but I’d gladly take that title too, if Zeus would grant it. Do you think being king is the worst thing that could happen to a man? It’s no bad thing at all — your house grows rich and you yourself are honored. But there are plenty of other Greek lords in sea-girt Ithaca, young and old, and any one of them might take that throne now that brilliant Odysseus is dead. Even so, I will be master of my own house, and lord over the slaves that godlike Odysseus won for me.”

Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered him: “Telemachus, which of us is to be king over the people of Ithaca lies on the knees of the gods. But you will surely keep your own possessions and rule your own house — and may no man ever come to drive you out by force and rob you, not while there’s a single soul living in Ithaca. But tell me, my good fellow, about this stranger. Where does the man come from? What land does he claim, what people, what family? Where are his fathers’ fields? Did he bring word of your father on his way home, or did he come here on some business of his own? He sprang up and was gone so suddenly, never waiting to be known. And yet, to look at him, he was no low-born man.”

And thoughtful Telemachus answered him: “Eurymachus, the day of my father’s return is gone for good. I’ll never trust another rumor, wherever it comes from, and I pay no attention to the prophecies my mother drags out of some seer she’s summoned into the house. As for the stranger — he was a guest-friend of my father’s from Taphos. He said he was Mentes, son of wise Anchialus, and that he rules the Taphians, who love their oars.” So he spoke — but in his heart he knew it had been an immortal goddess.

Then the suitors turned back to their dancing and the joy of song, and made merry, waiting for evening to come on. And while they were still enjoying themselves, dark night came down — and they went home, each to his own house, to sleep. Telemachus went to his own bedroom, built high in the beautiful court, with a clear view all around — and there he lay down, his mind churning with thoughts. Loyal Eurycleia walked ahead of him, carrying two blazing torches — Eurycleia, daughter of Ops, son of Peisenor. Laertes had bought her, long ago, with his own wealth, when she was just a girl, paying the price of twenty oxen for her. He honored her in his house as much as his own faithful wife, but he never took her to his bed, fearing his wife’s anger. She it was who carried the torches for him now, for she loved him more than any of the other serving women, and she had nursed him when he was small. He opened the door of his strongly built room, sat down on the bed, pulled off his soft tunic, and handed it to the wise old woman. She folded it and smoothed it and hung it on a peg beside the corded bedstead, then went out, pulling the door shut by its silver handle and sliding the bolt home with the strap. And there, all night long, wrapped in a fleece of wool, Telemachus lay turning over in his mind the journey gray-eyed Athena had set before him.
