The Odyssey, Book III

Nestor at Pylos

Book III3 of 24

As the sun climbed out of the lovely sea into the bronze sky, to bring its light to gods and mortals alike, they came to Pylos, the well-built city of Neleus. The people were down on the shore, sacrificing jet-black bulls to Poseidon, the dark-maned shaker of the earth. There were nine companies seated there, five hundred men in each, and each had brought nine bulls to offer. They were tasting the inner meats and burning the thigh-bones to the god just as Telemachus and his crew sailed in. The men furled the sails, brought the ship to anchor, and stepped ashore.

Athena led the way, and Telemachus came after her. Then she said to him: “Telemachus, there’s no place for shyness now, not even a little. You crossed the sea for this — to learn where your father lies and how he met his end. So go straight up to Nestor. Let’s see what he can tell us. Ask him to speak the truth, and he will — he’s far too good a man to lie.”

“But how, Mentor?” Telemachus answered. “How do I walk up to a man like that, and what do I even say? I’ve never been one for long speeches with anyone, and I’m ashamed to start by questioning a man so much older than I am.”

Then gray-eyed Athena said: “Some of it, Telemachus, your own heart will tell you what to say, and the rest a god will put into your mind. I’m sure of it — the gods have been with you from the day you were born until now.”

She went on quickly, and Telemachus followed in her steps until they reached the place where the men of Pylos were gathered. There sat Nestor with his sons, and all around them his people were busy with the feast — roasting some meat on the spits, threading more onto them. The moment they saw the strangers, the men crowded forward, clasped their hands in welcome, and waved them to seats. Nestor’s son Pisistratus reached them first; he took each by the hand and settled them on soft fleeces spread over the sand, beside his father and his brother Thrasymedes. Then he handed them their share of the inner meats and poured them wine in a golden cup, lifting it first to Athena, and he greeted her as he gave it:

“Stranger, say a prayer to Lord Poseidon — it’s his feast you’ve come to share. When you’ve prayed and made your offering, pass the cup to your friend so he can pour his too. He prays as well, I’m sure, for no man can live without the gods. But he’s the younger of you, near my own age, so I’ll give you the cup first.”

With that he put the golden cup in her hand. Athena was pleased — it was right and fitting of him to give it first to her — and she began to pray in earnest to Poseidon: “Hear me, Poseidon, holder of the earth, and don’t grudge us, for all our praying, the fulfilling of what we’ve come to do. First grant glory to Nestor and his sons, and then send the people of Pylos a fair return for this great offering. And last, grant Telemachus and me a safe passage and a good end to the errand that brought our swift ship here.”

So she prayed — and made it all come true herself. Then she handed the lovely cup to Telemachus, and Odysseus’ dear son prayed in his turn. When the outer meats were roasted and drawn off the spits, they cut everyone his portion and feasted well. And when they had all had their fill of food and drink, Nestor, the old knight of Gerene, began to speak.

“Now that our guests have eaten,” he said, “it’s a fair time to ask who they are. Tell us, then, strangers — who are you, and from what harbor have you sailed? Are you traders on some business? Or do you roam the sea like raiders, who risk their lives to bring ruin to other men?”

Telemachus answered him boldly now, for Athena herself had put courage in his heart to ask about his lost father and so win himself a name. “Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Greeks — you ask where we come from, and I’ll tell you. We come from Ithaca, below Mount Neion, and my errand is my own, not the people’s. I’m searching for word of my father, long-suffering Odysseus, who they say once sacked the city of Troy at your side. We know what fate found every other man who fought there — but of Odysseus the gods have hidden even the manner of his death. No one can tell us where he died: whether he fell on dry land, cut down by enemies, or was lost at sea in the waves of Amphitrite. So I come and clasp your knees, in the hope you’ll tell me how he met his cruel end — whether you saw it yourself, or heard it from some other wanderer. He was a man born to suffering; his mother bore him for grief. Don’t soften it out of pity for me. Tell me plainly, just as you saw it. If my brave father Odysseus ever served you well, by word or deed, in those hard years you Greeks suffered at Troy, remember it now, and tell me everything, exactly true.”

“My friend,” Nestor answered, “you bring back a time of great sorrow to my mind. You remind me of all we Greeks endured there — proud, untiring men — both on the sea, raiding wherever Achilles led us, and in the long fighting around King Priam’s great city. There our best men fell, every one of them. Ajax lies there, and Achilles, and Patroclus, who was like the gods in counsel, and my own dear son Antilochus — fast on his feet, and a fierce fighter. We bore so much more than that besides; no mortal tongue could tell the whole of it. You could sit here and question me for five years, six, and you’d grow weary and go home before I’d told you all the Greeks suffered. For nine long years we wove every kind of trap against them, and the son of Cronus barely brought it to an end. And through all of it, no man came close to your father in cunning. In every scheme, brilliant Odysseus was first — if you really are his son. I look at you and can hardly believe it; you even speak as he did. You’d never think a man so young could talk so like him. In all that time he and I were never once at odds, not in the assembly, not in the council — we were of one mind, one heart, and together we counseled the Greeks toward what was best.

“But when we had sacked Priam’s high city and were boarding our ships, and a god scattered the Greeks, then Zeus devised a bitter homecoming for them — for not all of them had been wise, or just. So many came to a hard end through the deadly rage of the gray-eyed daughter of a mighty father, who set the two sons of Atreus quarreling. The two of them called the whole army to assembly — recklessly, out of all order, at sundown, with the men heavy with wine. They said why they had summoned everyone: Menelaus urged them all to think of sailing home at once across the sea’s broad back. But that did not please Agamemnon, who wanted to hold the army there and offer sacred hecatombs to soothe Athena’s terrible anger. The fool — he never grasped that she would not be moved. The minds of the everlasting gods don’t turn so easily. So the brothers stood there hurling hard words at each other, until the Greeks leapt to their feet with a deafening roar, split clean in two over what to do.

“That night we slept on our anger, each side brooding against the other, for Zeus was readying our grief. In the morning some of us dragged our ships down to the bright sea and loaded our goods and our women aboard; the rest, about half, stayed back with Agamemnon. We sailed — and the ships ran well, for a god had smoothed the waves under us. At Tenedos we sacrificed to the gods, aching for home, but Zeus in his cruelty would not yet let us reach it; he stirred a second quarrel, and some of the men turned their curved ships around and went back — they sailed under Odysseus, wise and many-minded, to do Agamemnon’s bidding again. But I pressed on with all the ships in my company, for I could see a god was brewing trouble. The warlike son of Tydeus drove his men on too, and later Menelaus caught up with us at Lesbos, where we were debating the long course ahead — whether to sail north of rugged Chios, by the island of Psyra, keeping it on our left, or south of Chios, past the windy headland of Mimas. We asked the god for a sign, and he showed us one: cut straight across the open water to Euboea, and we’d soonest be out of danger. A fresh wind sprang up and carried us swiftly through the night to Geraestus, where we burned many thigh-bones to Poseidon, thankful to have crossed that great stretch of sea. On the fourth day the men of Diomedes, son of Tydeus, drew up their ships at Argos, while I held on for Pylos, and the wind never once failed me from the moment a god first sent it fair.

“So I came home, dear boy, knowing nothing of the others — who reached safety and who was lost. But the news that has come to my own halls since I returned, I’ll share with you, as is only right; I’ll keep nothing back. They say the Myrmidons came home safely under great Achilles’ son Neoptolemus, and so did Philoctetes, the noble son of Poias. Idomeneus brought all his men back to Crete — every one who’d survived the war, the sea took none of them. And of Agamemnon you’ll have heard, even off in your far corner of the world: how he came home, and how Aegisthus murdered him, and how Aegisthus paid for it, in the end, in full. There’s the great good of a man leaving a son behind him — for Orestes took his revenge on the killer, sly Aegisthus, who had cut down his glorious father. You too, my friend — and I can see you’re tall and handsome — be a brave man, so that men yet unborn will speak well of you.”

“Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Greeks,” Telemachus answered, “he took his revenge, and richly. The Greeks will carry his name down through all the years to come, a song for those still to be born. If only the gods would clothe me in that kind of strength — to pay back the suitors for the outrage they heap on me, plotting my ruin in their arrogance. But the gods have spun no such happiness for my father and me. Now there’s nothing for it but to endure.”

“My friend,” said Nestor, “since you bring it up yourself — they tell me a crowd of suitors is courting your mother, against your will, scheming in your own house. Tell me: do you bow to it of your own accord, or have the people of your land turned against you, swayed by some word from the gods? Who knows? Odysseus may yet come home and pay these brazen men back for their violence — alone, or with the whole army of the Greeks behind him. If only gray-eyed Athena would choose to love you as openly as she loved glorious Odysseus in the land of Troy — for I never saw the gods show such open favor to any man as Athena showed to your father — if she would care for you the way she cared for him, more than one of those suitors would forget all about marriage.”

“Old sir,” Telemachus answered, “I don’t think that will ever come to pass. It’s too great a thing to hope for. Wonder takes hold of me at the thought. Hope as I might, it would never happen for me — not even if the gods themselves willed it.”

Then gray-eyed Athena said to him: “Telemachus — what a thing to let slip past your teeth! A god, if he chooses, can bring a man safely home from the far ends of the earth. For myself, I’d rather suffer hardship after hardship and reach home at last than sail back quickly only to be killed at my own hearth, the way Agamemnon was, by the treachery of Aegisthus and his own wife. Death, though — that one thing the gods cannot turn aside, even from a man they love, once the cruel fate of the long sleep has him.”

“Mentor,” Telemachus answered, “let’s not talk of this anymore, grieved as we both are. There’s no more homecoming for my father; the deathless gods have long since fixed his death and his dark fate. But there’s something else I want to ask Nestor, for no man knows more than he does. They say he has been king through three generations of men, so that to look at him is almost to look at one of the immortals. Nestor, son of Neleus, tell me the truth: how did Agamemnon, lord of so wide a land, come to die? Where was Menelaus? And how did Aegisthus contrive to kill a far better man than himself? Was Menelaus somewhere far from Greece, wandering among other peoples, so that Aegisthus took heart and struck him down?”

“I’ll tell you the truth of it, child,” Nestor answered, “and you’ve nearly guessed how it happened. If Menelaus, coming home from Troy, had found Aegisthus alive in the house, there’d have been no mound heaped over him when he died — the dogs and the birds would have torn him out beyond the city walls, and not one woman would have wept for him, so monstrous was the thing he’d done. But we were over there, fighting hard at Troy, while he sat at his ease in the green heart of Argos, beguiling Agamemnon’s wife with his endless flattery.

“At first Clytemnestra would have nothing to do with his ugly scheme, for there was good in her by nature. And there was a singer at her side, a man Agamemnon had charged, when he sailed for Troy, to keep close watch over his wife. But when the gods had fixed her ruin, Aegisthus took the singer to a barren island and left him there to be the prey and plunder of the birds — and then she went willingly to the house of Aegisthus. He burned great offerings to the gods on their altars and hung the temples with woven cloth and gold, giddy that he’d brought off a thing he never dreamed he could.

“Meanwhile Menelaus and I were sailing home together from Troy, the best of friends. But when we reached Sunium, the sacred cape of Athens, Apollo struck down the helmsman of Menelaus’ ship with his gentle arrows — Phrontis, son of Onetor, who could hold a ship’s course in a rising gale better than any man alive. He died there with the steering-oar still in his hands, and Menelaus, desperate as he was to sail on, had to stop and bury his comrade and give him his rites. When he put to sea again and reached the cliffs of Malea in his hollow ships, Zeus laid a bitter plan against him: he loosed a screaming wind and lifted the waves up like mountains. There he split his fleet. Part of it he drove to Crete, where the Cydonians live by the streams of the Iardanus. There’s a sheer cliff running into the sea there, off the headland of Gortyn, and along that coast the south wind drives the great waves against the western point, toward Phaestus — and a small reef holds back a heavy sea. There the ships were flung onto the rocks and smashed, though the men barely saved their lives. The other five dark-prowed ships were carried by wind and water to Egypt, and there Menelaus wandered, gathering much gold and goods among men of a foreign tongue. And all that while Aegisthus was plotting his foul work back home. Seven years he ruled in golden Mycenae after he killed Agamemnon, and the people bent under him. But in the eighth year his ruin came: noble Orestes returned from Athens and killed the man who had killed his father, sly Aegisthus. Then he held a funeral feast for the Greeks of Argos, burying his hateful mother and the coward Aegisthus — and on that very day Menelaus came home, lord of the great war-cry, his ships loaded with all the treasure they could carry.

“So take my advice: don’t roam far from home for long, leaving your wealth behind you, and such overbearing men inside your house. They’ll devour everything you have and split it among themselves, and your whole journey will have been for nothing. Still — I urge you, by all means, go and see Menelaus. He’s only lately come home, from peoples so distant a man would lose all hope of return once the winds had blown him that far off course, across a stretch of sea so vast and terrible that not even the birds could fly it within the year. Go to him by sea, then, with your own ship and crew; or if you’d rather go by land, you can have a chariot and horses, and my own sons to escort you to Lacedaemon, where fair-haired Menelaus lives. Ask him to speak the truth, and he will — he’s far too good a man to lie.”

As he finished, the sun went down and the dark came on, and gray-eyed Athena said: “Old sir, you’ve spoken well, and it’s all just as it should be. Now have them cut out the tongues of the victims and mix the wine, so we can pour an offering to Poseidon and the other immortals, and then to bed — it’s the hour for it. The light is gone in the west, and it’s not right to sit long at a god’s feast; we should be on our way.”

So spoke the daughter of Zeus, and they obeyed her. Servants poured water over their hands, young men filled the bowls to the brim with wine, and after pouring a few drops into each man’s cup, served it round to all. They threw the tongues into the fire and rose to make their offerings, and when they had poured to the gods and drunk their fill, Athena and godlike Telemachus made to go back to their hollow ship. But Nestor stopped them on the spot, reproaching them:

“Zeus forbid, and the other deathless gods forbid, that you should leave my house to sleep aboard a ship — as though I were some pauper with no spare cloaks, no rugs in the place, no soft beds for myself and my guests to rest on! I’ve blankets in plenty, and fine cloaks. Never, while I’m alive, will the son of my dear friend Odysseus lie down on a ship’s deck — and after me my sons will keep the same open house for any stranger who comes.”

Then gray-eyed Athena answered him: “Well said, old sir, and Telemachus would do right to do as you ask. Let him go back with you and sleep in your house. But I’ll return to our black ship to hearten the crew and tell them how things stand. I’m the only older man among them; the rest are young, of Telemachus’ own age, who came along out of friendship. I’ll lie down by the ship tonight, and in the morning I’ll go to the Cauconians, where there’s an old debt owed me, and no small one. As for Telemachus — your guest now — send him on to Lacedaemon by chariot, with one of your sons, and give him your finest, fastest horses.”

With that gray-eyed Athena was gone — gone in the form of a sea-eagle — and awe took hold of all who saw it. The old man marveled at the sight; he took Telemachus by the hand and said: “My friend, I think you’ll grow to be no weak or worthless man, if the gods are already your escorts while you’re still so young. This was surely one of those who hold Olympus — none other than the daughter of Zeus, the glorious Tritoborn, she who honored your brave father above all the Greeks. Be gracious to us, my queen, and grant good fame to me, to my sons, and to my honored wife. In return I’ll offer you a yearling heifer, broad-browed and unbroken, never yet led under the yoke by any man. I’ll sheathe her horns in gold and offer her to you.”

So he prayed, and Pallas Athena heard him. Then he led the way to his own house, his sons and his sons-in-law behind him. And when they had come to the great hall and taken their seats on the chairs and benches, the old man mixed them a bowl of sweet wine that had stood eleven years before the housekeeper loosed the lid from the jar. As he mixed it he prayed long and poured offerings to Athena, daughter of Zeus who holds the storm-shield. And when they had poured and drunk their fill, they all went home to sleep, each to his own house. But Nestor, the old knight of Gerene, laid Telemachus down to sleep beneath the echoing portico, on a corded bedstead, and put Pisistratus beside him — that fine spearman, the only one of his sons still unmarried in the house. He himself slept in the inmost room of the high hall, with his wife the queen beside him, who made his bed.

When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, Nestor rose from his bed and went out to sit on the smooth stones, white and gleaming with oil, set before his lofty doors. Once Neleus had sat there, a counselor like the gods, but he had long since gone down to the house of Hades; now Nestor sat there in his turn, scepter in hand, watching over his people. His sons came from their rooms and gathered around him — Echephron, Stratius, Perseus, Aretus, and godlike Thrasymedes; sixth came the hero Pisistratus, and they brought Telemachus to sit among them. Then Nestor, the old knight of Gerene, spoke first:

“Quickly, my children — do as I tell you, so that before all the gods I may win the favor of Athena, who came to me in her own bright presence at the god’s rich feast. One of you go to the plain for a heifer, and have the herdsman drive it here at once. Another go down to noble Telemachus’ ship and bring up all his crew, leaving only two behind to mind the vessel. And send one to fetch the goldsmith Laerces, to lay the gold around the heifer’s horns. The rest of you stay here with me. Tell the women inside to make ready a fine feast through the halls, to set out seats and firewood, and to bring fresh spring water.”

So he spoke, and they all hurried to it. The heifer came in from the plain, and the crew came up from Telemachus’ swift, balanced ship. The smith came with the tools of his trade in his hands — anvil, hammer, and the well-made tongs he worked his gold with. And Athena herself came, to take her part in the offering. Old Nestor, the knight, gave out the gold, and the smith worked it carefully around the heifer’s horns, so the goddess would delight in the sight of it. Stratius and noble Echephron led her forward by the horns. Aretus came from the house carrying water in a flowered basin, with a basket of barley grain in his other hand. Thrasymedes, hardened in war, stood ready with a sharp axe to strike the heifer down, and Perseus held the bowl to catch the blood. Then Nestor, the old knight of Gerene, began the rite — washing his hands, scattering the barley, and praying long to Athena as he cut a tuft from the heifer’s head and threw it on the fire.

When they had prayed and flung the barley grain, the son of Nestor, brave Thrasymedes, stepped up and struck. The axe cut clean through the tendons of the neck and the heifer’s strength gave way. The women raised their cry — Nestor’s daughters and his sons’ wives, and his honored wife Eurydice, eldest daughter of Clymenus. Then the men lifted the heifer’s head from the wide-wayed earth and held it up, and Pisistratus, leader of men, cut her throat. When the dark blood had run out and the life had left her bones, they cut her up at once. They sliced out the thigh-bones in the proper way, wrapped them in a double layer of fat, and laid raw flesh on top; the old man burned them on split wood and poured bright wine over them, while the young men stood by with five-pronged forks in their hands. When the thigh-bones were burned and they had tasted the inner meats, they carved the rest into small pieces, ran them onto the spits, and roasted them, holding the sharp spits steady over the flame.

Meanwhile Polycaste, Nestor’s youngest daughter, lovely as the gods, bathed Telemachus. When she had washed him and rubbed him with olive oil, she dressed him in a fine tunic and cloak, and he stepped from the bath looking like a god, and went to sit beside Nestor, shepherd of the people. When the outer meats were roasted, they drew them off the spits and sat down to the feast, and worthy stewards waited on them, filling their golden cups again and again with wine. And when they had had their fill of food and drink, Nestor, the old knight of Gerene, spoke first: “My sons, bring out the fair-maned horses for Telemachus, and yoke them to the chariot, so he can be on his way.”

So he spoke, and they did just as he said, and harnessed the swift horses to the chariot. The housekeeper packed bread and wine and good things to eat, the kind of fare kings’ sons live on. Telemachus stepped up into the beautiful chariot, and Nestor’s son Pisistratus, a leader of men, took his place beside him and gathered the reins in his hands. He flicked the horses to a start, and they flew off, not unwilling, out into the open country, leaving the high city of Pylos behind. All day long they shook the yoke across their necks, and the sun went down and the roads grew dark. So they came to Pherae, to the house of Diocles, the son of Ortilochus, whom Alpheus had fathered. There they spent the night, and Diocles treated them as a host should. And when the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, they yoked the horses again and mounted the inlaid chariot, and drove out through the gateway and the echoing portico. Pisistratus flicked the horses on, and they flew, not unwilling — and soon they came to the wheat-bearing plain, and there they drove on to their journey’s end, so well did the swift horses carry them. And the sun went down and the roads grew dark.
