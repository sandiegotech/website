The Odyssey, Book XVII

The Beggar at His Own Door

Book XVII17 of 24

When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, Telemachus bound the sandals to his feet and took up a strong spear that sat well in his hand, for he meant to go into the city. “Old friend,” he said to the swineherd, “I am off to the town now, to show myself to my mother — she will not stop grieving, I know, until she has seen me with her own eyes. As for this poor stranger, take him into the town and let him beg his bread there from anyone willing to give him a crust and a drink. I have troubles enough of my own; I cannot carry every man’s. If that offends him, the worse for him — I would rather say plainly what I mean.”

“I have no wish to stay either, sir,” said Odysseus. “A beggar does better in the town than out here; whoever cares to can hand him something. I am too old to wait on a master’s word at a country station. Let your man do as you have told him; only let me warm myself a little by the fire first, and let the day take on some heat, then take me down. My clothes are wretchedly thin, and on a frost like this morning’s I would freeze on the road — and you say the city lies some way off.”

At that Telemachus strode out through the yards, his mind already on the day he would pay the suitors back. When he reached the house he stood his spear against a pillar of the colonnade, crossed the stone floor, and went in.

Old Eurycleia, his nurse, saw him before anyone else. She was busy spreading the fleeces over the chairs, and she burst into tears as she ran to him; the other maids crowded round too, kissing his head and shoulders in their welcome. Then Penelope came out of her room, lovely as Artemis or golden Aphrodite, and wept as she threw her arms about her son. She kissed his forehead, and both his beautiful eyes, and the words broke from her: “Light of my eyes, you are home — I had given up ever seeing you again. To think you slipped away to Pylos without a word to me, without my leave. But come, tell me everything you saw.”

“Mother, do not scold me,” Telemachus answered, “and do not stir up my heart when I have only just come through such danger. Wash, put on fresh clothes, go up to your room with your women, and vow whole and ample sacrifices to all the gods, if only Zeus will grant us the day of our revenge. I must go now to the place of assembly, to bring in a guest who came back with me from Pylos. I sent him on ahead with my crew, and told Piraeus to take him home and care for him until I could come for him myself.”

She took her son’s words to heart. She washed, put on fresh clothes, and vowed whole and ample sacrifices to all the gods, if only they would grant her the day of revenge upon the suitors.

Telemachus took up his spear and went out through the colonnade — not alone, for his two quick hounds went with him; and Athena poured such grace about him that every eye turned in wonder as he passed. The suitors gathered round him with welcome on their lips and malice in their hearts, but he gave them no opening, and went to sit instead with Mentor, Antiphus, and Halitherses, old friends of his father’s house, who pressed him to tell them all that had befallen him. Then Piraeus came up, bringing Theoclymenus, whom he had escorted through the town to the assembly, and Telemachus went straight to them. Piraeus spoke first: “Telemachus, send some of your women to my house to fetch away the gifts Menelaus gave you.”

“Piraeus, we cannot tell how things will fall out,” Telemachus answered. “If the suitors kill me in my own house and carve up my goods among them, I would far rather you kept those gifts than that any of them got their hands on them. But if I bring those men down instead, then gladly bring the gifts to me — and I will thank you for it.”

With that he led Theoclymenus home. When they came to the house they laid their cloaks over the benches and went to bathe; and when the maids had washed and oiled them and dressed them in clean tunics and cloaks, they took their places at the table. A serving-woman brought water in a fine golden jug and poured it over their hands into a silver basin, and drew up a polished table beside them; and the housekeeper set out bread and a generous share of all there was in the house. Across from them sat Penelope, leaning against a pillar of the hall and turning her spindle. They reached out to the good things spread before them, and when they had eaten and drunk their fill, Penelope said:

“Telemachus, I am going up now to lie down on that bed of mine — that grieving bed I have wet with my tears since the day Odysseus sailed for Troy with the sons of Atreus. But before the suitors come back into this house, you have still not told me plainly: did you hear anything, anywhere, of your father’s coming home?”

“Then I will tell you the truth, mother,” her son replied. “We went to Pylos, to Nestor, and he took me into his house and made me as welcome as a man would welcome his own son come home after long years away — he and his sons both. But he said he had not heard one word, from any living soul, of Odysseus, whether he was alive or dead. So he sent me on with a chariot and horses to Menelaus. There I saw Helen, for whose sake the Greeks and the Trojans alike, by the will of heaven, bore so much. Menelaus asked me what had brought me to Lacedaemon, and I told him the whole of it; and he said: ‘Shameless cowards — to think they’d lie down in the bed of a man like that! It’s as if a deer had laid her newborn fawns to sleep in a lion’s den and gone off to graze the wooded glens, and the lion came home to his lair and tore them apart, both of them, in a moment — that’s how Odysseus will deal with those men, and bring their ugly fate on them. Father Zeus, and Athena, and Apollo — if only the Odysseus who once stood up in lovely Lesbos and wrestled the son of Philomeleides and threw him down so hard that all the Greeks cheered — if only that Odysseus could meet these suitors now. Every one of them would find a quick death and a bitter wedding. But as for what you ask me and beg me to tell — I won’t twist it aside or deceive you. Of all the old sea-god told me, I’ll hide nothing. He said he had seen Odysseus on an island, in bitter grief, in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who keeps him there against his will; and he cannot win his way home, for he has no ship and no crew to carry him over the wide sea.’ That was what Menelaus told me. When I had heard it I sailed for home, and the gods sent a fair wind and brought me safely back.”

His words stirred Penelope to the heart. Then Theoclymenus spoke to her: “Honored wife of Odysseus, Telemachus does not see clearly into these things. Hear me, then, for I read them surely and will hide nothing. Let Zeus be my witness above all the gods, and this table of guest-friendship, and the hearth of Odysseus to which I have come — Odysseus is already here, in his own country. Whether he sits still or moves from place to place, he is learning the whole of this evil, and for every one of these suitors he is planting the seed of their ruin. Such was the omen I read aboard ship, and I told it then to Telemachus.”

“May it be so,” Penelope answered. “If your words come true, you will have such gifts and such goodwill from me that everyone who meets you will call you blessed.”

So they talked together. Meanwhile, out in front of the house, the suitors were hurling the discus and the throwing-spear at a mark on the leveled ground, as insolent as ever. But when it came time for the meal, and the flocks were driven in from the country all around with their herdsmen as always, Medon — the one servant they liked best, who waited on them at table — called out: “Young masters, you have had your sport; come inside now and let us see to the meal. There is nothing wrong with dinner at dinnertime.” They broke off their games at his word and went in, and laid their cloaks over the benches and chairs, and butchered fat sheep and goats and pigs and a full-grown heifer, and so made ready to feast.

Out at the farm, Odysseus and the loyal swineherd were setting off for the town. “Stranger,” Eumaeus said, “I suppose you are still set on going down today, the way my master told you to — though for my own part I would rather have kept you here to watch the place. But I dare not cross him; he would only scold me later, and a master’s tongue is a hard thing to bear. Let us be off, then. The day is at its height; before long it will be evening, and you will feel the cold worse than now.”

“I know it, I take your meaning,” answered Odysseus. “There is no need to say more. Let us go. But if you have a stick cut and ready, give it to me to lean on — you tell me the road is rough.”

So he slung his shabby, torn old beggar’s bag over his shoulder by its cord, and Eumaeus gave him a staff that suited his hand; and the two set out, leaving the dogs and the herdsmen to mind the place. The swineherd led, and his master came behind, leaning on his staff like some broken-down old tramp, his body wrapped in rags. They worked their way down the rough, steep track, and as they neared the city they came to the fountain — the fair-flowing fountain Ithacus, Neritus, and Polyctor had built, where the townsfolk drew their water. A ring of water-loving poplars grew all around it, and the cold water came falling from the rock above; and over the spring stood an altar to the nymphs, where every traveler stopped to make an offering. There Melanthius, son of Dolius, overtook them, driving down the pick of his goats for the suitors’ table, two herdsmen with him. The moment he saw Eumaeus and Odysseus he let fly at them with foul, abusive language that stirred Odysseus to anger.

“There they go,” he sneered, “one rogue leading another — heaven matches like to like. Where do you think you are dragging this disgusting beggar, master swineherd? A thing to turn your stomach at the table. He is the sort to slouch from door to door rubbing his shoulders on the posts, whining for scraps — never for swords or cauldrons like a man, only for whatever filth will fill his belly. Hand him to me to mind my station and I will set him to mucking out the pens and bringing fodder to the kids, and he can fatten his shanks on whey to his heart’s content. But no — he has learned only idleness, and would not stoop to honest work; he would rather beg his bread round the whole town to stuff that bottomless gut of his. And I tell you, and it will surely come to pass: if he sets foot in the house of Odysseus, the stools will fly at his head from every hand and break it before they throw him out.”

And as he passed he kicked Odysseus on the hip, for sheer mindless spite — but Odysseus held his ground, not so much as swaying from the path. For a moment he weighed it: whether to leap on Melanthius and beat the life out of him with his staff, or seize him and dash his head against the ground. But he mastered it, and held it down in his heart. The swineherd, though, looked the man full in the face and rebuked him, and lifting up his hands he prayed aloud: “Nymphs of the fountain, daughters of Zeus — if ever Odysseus burned you the rich thigh-bones of lambs and kids, hear me now: bring him home, let some god guide him here. He would soon scatter these swaggering airs of yours, the way you strut up and down the town while your flocks go to ruin under your worthless keeping.”

“Listen to the cur,” Melanthius the goatherd shot back, “and the mischief he barks. One of these days I will load you onto a ship and carry you off to some far country and sell you for a tidy sum. As for that one — I only wish I were as sure that Apollo would strike Telemachus dead today, or the suitors would, as I am sure that Odysseus is never coming home.”

With that he left them to follow at their own pace, and went on ahead, and was soon at his master’s house. He went in and took his seat among the suitors, across from Eurymachus, who favored him above the rest; and the servers set a portion of meat before him, and the housekeeper laid out bread for him to eat. By now Odysseus and the swineherd had reached the house and halted outside it, and the sound of the lyre came drifting out, for Phemius was just lifting his voice to sing to the suitors. Then Odysseus took the swineherd by the hand and said: “Eumaeus, this is surely the house of Odysseus — a man would know it among a thousand. One building runs on into the next; the courtyard is walled and topped with a coping; the great doors are double and well made; no man would carry this by storm. And there are many feasting within, I can tell — the smell of roast meat is everywhere, and I hear the lyre, which the gods have made the companion of a feast.”

And you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd: “You have read it rightly, as you read most things. But let us think now what we should do. Will you go in first among the suitors and leave me here, or shall I go ahead and you wait? Only do not wait long, or someone may catch sight of you loitering and let fly something at you, or drive you off. Think it over.”

“I understand and take your meaning,” Odysseus replied. “Go in first; leave me here. I am no stranger to blows and to things thrown at my head. I have been knocked about too long, in war and on the sea, to be unmade by one more. Let this go with the rest. The one thing a man cannot hide is the gnawing of a hungry belly — that is the enemy that brings men endless grief; it is for the belly that ships are launched and rigged to cross the barren sea and carry war to other men.”

As they were speaking, a dog that had been lying there lifted his head and pricked up his ears. This was Argos — the hound Odysseus had bred himself before he sailed for Troy, though he sailed before he had any joy of him. In the old days the young men would take him out after wild goats and deer and hares; but now, with his master gone, he lay neglected, cast off on a deep heap of dung — the droppings of the mules and oxen, piled before the gates until the field hands should come to cart it off and manure the wide estate. There Argos lay, riddled with ticks. The moment he caught sight of Odysseus standing near, he dropped both ears and wagged his tail — but he no longer had the strength to drag himself any closer to his master. And Odysseus, seeing him across the yard, turned his face aside and brushed away a tear, easily hiding it from Eumaeus, and said: “Eumaeus, it is a strange thing — a dog as fine as that lying on a dung-heap. He is well built; but I cannot tell whether he had the speed to match his looks, or whether he was only one of those table-dogs that men keep about them for show.”

And you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd: “That hound belonged to a man who died far from home. If he were now what he was in body and in work the day Odysseus left for Troy, you would soon marvel at his speed and his strength. No creature he started in the deep woods ever got away from him; he ran a scent like nothing else. But now he has fallen on hard days. His master is dead in some distant land, and the women give him no care. Servants stop doing honest work the moment their masters are no longer over them; for Zeus takes away half a man’s worth the day the yoke of slavery comes down on him.”

With that he went on into the house, into the hall where the suitors were, and Argos, in the very moment he had seen his master again in the twentieth year, was taken by the darkness of death.

Telemachus was the first by far to see Eumaeus come in, and he gave him a quick nod to come and sit by him. The swineherd looked about, found the stool the carver used when he was serving out the suitors’ portions, carried it over to Telemachus’s table, and sat down across from him; and a server set a portion of meat before him and gave him bread from the basket.

Right behind him came Odysseus, in the look of a wretched old beggar, leaning on his staff with the rags hanging off him. He sat down on the ash-wood threshold just inside the doorway, with his back against a post of cypress that the carpenter had once planed smooth and set true to the line. Telemachus took a whole loaf from the basket, and as much meat as his two hands would hold, and said to Eumaeus: “Carry this to the stranger, and tell him to make his round of the suitors and beg from each — a beggar cannot afford to be ashamed.” So Eumaeus went and stood by him: “Stranger, Telemachus sends you this, and says you are to go round the suitors and beg, for a beggar cannot afford to be ashamed.”

“King Zeus grant Telemachus every happiness,” Odysseus answered, “and give him all his heart desires.” Then he took what was sent him in both hands and set it down on the filthy old bag at his feet, and ate while the bard was singing; and as the song ended, so did his meal. The suitors broke into noise, and Athena came to Odysseus and stirred him to go begging crusts from each of them in turn, to learn which were decent men and which were not — though not one of them would she spare for it in the end. So he went round from left to right, holding out his hand to each as if he had begged all his life. Some pitied him and wondered who he might be and where he came from; and the goatherd Melanthius spoke up: “Suitors of my noble queen, I can tell you this much of the stranger — I have laid eyes on him before. The swineherd brought him here. But of the man himself, of where he is from, I know nothing.”

At that Antinous turned on the swineherd. “You prize fool,” he cried, “what did you mean, hauling this fellow into town? Have we not tramps and beggars enough plaguing us as we eat? Is it a small thing to you that they swarm here eating up your master’s living — and you must drag in one more?”

And you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd: “Antinous, your blood is good, but your words are not. Who goes out to fetch a stranger from abroad, unless it be one who serves the people — a seer, a healer of hurts, a worker in wood, or a singer who can charm us with his voice? Those men are welcome the world over; but no one calls in a beggar to eat him out of house and home. You are always the hardest of all the suitors on the servants of Odysseus, and on me above the rest — but I do not care, so long as Telemachus and wise Penelope still live in this house.”

“Hush,” said Telemachus, “do not answer him. Antinous has the cruelest tongue of all of them, and he eggs the others on.” Then he turned to Antinous: “How fatherly of you, Antinous, to look after my interests so — telling me to drive this stranger from my house with harsh words. Heaven forbid. Take something and give it to him yourself; I do not begrudge it — I am telling you to do it. Never mind my mother, nor any servant in the house of Odysseus. But you have no such thing in mind: you would far rather eat it yourself than hand it to another.”

“What a high-handed speech, Telemachus,” Antinous answered. “If every suitor gave him as much as I will, this house would not see his face again for three whole months.” And as he spoke he dragged out from under the table the footstool his sleek feet had been resting on, and brandished it. But the others all gave the beggar something and filled his bag with bread and meat; and he was about to go back to the threshold and eat what the suitors had given, when first he stopped before Antinous and said:

“Give me something too, friend. You do not look the poorest man here — you look a prince, the first among them; so you should give the most freely, and I will carry the fame of your bounty far and wide. I too was a rich man once, with a fine house of my own, and many a wanderer like this I gave to in those days, whoever he was and whatever he needed. I had servants without number and all the things that make a man’s life full and call him wealthy. But Zeus took it all from me — it was his will. He sent me off with a band of roving raiders to Egypt, a long voyage, and it was my undoing. I drew up my ships in the river of Egypt and bade my men stay close and stand guard while I sent scouts to look out from the high ground. But they would not hold to my orders; they followed their own greed and fell to plundering the Egyptian fields, killing the men and carrying off the women and children. The cry reached the city, and at first light the people poured out until the whole plain was thick with men on foot and in chariots, ablaze with bronze. Then Zeus broke my men’s nerve, and not one of them would stand and face the enemy, for ruin closed on us from every side. They cut down many of us with the bronze, and the rest they took alive to work as slaves. I was given to a stranger who happened by, to carry off to Cyprus — Dmetor, son of Iasus, a great lord there. And from there I have come here, by way of misery upon misery.”

Then Antinous said: “What spirit has sent us this plague to spoil our dinner? Stand off, out in the open of the court, or you will have your Egypt and your Cyprus over again, for the bold and shameless beggar you are. You have made your round of all of them, and they have given you freely — there is no holding back where there is plenty, and free with other men’s goods is an easy thing.”

Odysseus drew back, and said: “For shame — your looks are better than your heart. In your own house you would not spare a beggar so much as a grain of salt, you who sit here at another man’s table with plenty all around you, and cannot bring yourself to break off one piece of bread.”

That stung Antinous, and he glared at him: “Then I think you will not get out of this hall in one piece, after talking like that.” And he caught up the footstool and hurled it, and struck Odysseus on the right shoulder, high on the back. But Odysseus stood firm as a rock; the blow from Antinous did not so much as stagger him. He only shook his head in silence, brooding on his revenge. Then he went back to the threshold and sat down, and laid his full bag at his feet, and said:

“Hear me, you suitors of the great queen, while I say what my heart bids me. There is no grief, no smart, in being struck while a man fights for his own — for his cattle or his white-fleeced sheep. But Antinous has struck me on account of my belly, that cursed, ruinous thing that brings men so much trouble. Yet if there are gods anywhere for the poor, and powers that avenge them, then I pray the end of Antinous comes before his wedding day.”

“Sit and eat in silence, stranger, or take yourself elsewhere,” Antinous shouted. “One more word and the young men will drag you out by the hand and foot through the whole house and strip the skin from your bones.”

But this was too much even for the others, and one of the arrogant young men spoke up: “Antinous, it was an ugly thing to strike that luckless wanderer. You will pay for it, if he should turn out to be some god from heaven. For the gods take the likeness of strangers from far countries, and in every kind of shape they walk the cities of men, watching who does violence and who keeps the right.” So the suitors said, but Antinous paid them no mind. And Telemachus, though no tear fell from him, raged in his heart at the blow struck against his father, and only shook his head in silence, brooding on his revenge.

Now when Penelope, in her room, heard that the stranger had been struck in the hall, she said before her maids: “May Apollo strike you, Antinous, the same way.” And her housekeeper Eurynome answered, “If our prayers were granted, not one of them would live to see the sun rise.” “Nurse,” Penelope said, “I hate every one of them, for there is nothing in them but mischief — but Antinous I hate like black death itself. A poor luckless wanderer comes begging through the house for sheer need, and everyone gives him something to put in his bag; but Antinous lets fly a footstool and strikes him on the right shoulder.”

So she talked with her women, sitting in her own room, while down in the hall Odysseus was finishing his meal. Then she called the swineherd to her and said: “Eumaeus, go and tell the stranger to come to me. I want to welcome him and ask him whether he has heard or seen anything of my unhappy husband, for he has the look of a man who has wandered far.”

And you answered her, Eumaeus, swineherd: “If only these Greeks would hold their peace, my queen, you would be spellbound by the tale he tells. I had him three days and three nights in my hut — for that was the first place he came to, fresh from his ship — and even so he has not reached the end of his sorrows. The way a man hangs on the words of a bard taught by the gods, with the song spilling sweet from his lips, that is how I sat in my hut and hung on his words. He says there is an old guest-friendship between his house and the house of Odysseus; that he comes from Crete, where the line of Minos still lives; and that, driven from land to land by every kind of misfortune, he has heard word of Odysseus alive and near at hand, among the Thesprotians, and bringing home great wealth besides.”

“Call him here, then,” said Penelope, “that I may hear it from his own mouth. As for those men — let them take their pleasure indoors or out, it is all the same to them. They have nothing to grieve over: their own grain and wine lie untouched in their houses, with only the servants to use them, while they haunt our house day after day, slaughtering our oxen and sheep and fat goats for their feasts and swilling our wine without a care. No house could bear such waste; there is no Odysseus here now to fight off the ruin. But if he came home, he and his son would soon take their revenge.”

As she spoke Telemachus gave a sneeze so loud the whole house rang with it, and Penelope laughed and said to Eumaeus: “Go now and call the stranger in. Did you not hear how my son sneezed on my very words? It can only mean that death is coming to all the suitors, and not one of them will escape it. And mark this, lay it to your heart: if I am satisfied the stranger speaks the truth, I will dress him in a good cloak and tunic.”

When he heard this, Eumaeus went straight to Odysseus: “Stranger, my queen, the mother of Telemachus, has sent for you. Heavy as her grief is, she longs to hear whatever you can tell her of her husband; and if she is satisfied you speak the truth, she will give you a cloak and a tunic — the very things you most need. As for bread, you can fill your belly with that, begging round the town from any who will give.”

“I will tell Penelope nothing but the strict truth,” answered long-suffering Odysseus. “I know all about her husband; we have borne hard fortune together. But I am afraid of this savage pack of suitors, whose insolence reaches to the sky. Just now, as I went about the hall doing no harm to anyone, a man struck me a cruel blow, and neither Telemachus nor anyone else lifted a hand for me. So tell Penelope to be patient and wait in her room until the sun goes down. Then let her ask me about her husband’s return — but let her seat me close to the fire, for my clothes are wretched, as you know yourself, since it was you I first begged for help.”

The swineherd heard him and went back, and as he stepped over the threshold Penelope said: “Why do you not bring him, Eumaeus? Is he afraid of being ill-used, or only too shy to come into the house? A beggar cannot afford to be ashamed.”

And you answered her, Eumaeus, swineherd: “He speaks good sense, my queen — only what any man would think. He is keeping clear of those overbearing men. He asks you to wait until sundown; and it will be far better so, for then you can have him to yourself, to hear him and question him as you please.”

“He is no fool, whoever he is,” Penelope answered. “It may well be as he says, for there are no men in all the world so set on evil as these.” When she had spoken Eumaeus went back into the crowd of suitors, his errand done, and bent close to Telemachus so no one else could hear: “My dear lad, I will go back now to the pigs, to look after things there — your living and mine. You see to things here, but above all keep yourself safe; there are many in this house who wish you harm. May Zeus bring them to ruin before they bring it down on us.”

“So be it,” said Telemachus. “Have your meal first, then go. And in the morning come back, and bring the beasts for the day’s sacrifice. All the rest will be my concern, and the gods’.” At that Eumaeus sat down again, and when he had eaten and drunk his fill he left the courts and the hall behind, with the men still at their meat, and went back to his pigs. As for the suitors, they gave themselves over to dancing and song, for the day was wearing on toward evening.
