# The Odyssey, Book XII

The Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, and the Cattle of the Sun

“Once we were clear of the river Ocean and out on the open sea again, we came back to the island of Aeaea, where the early Dawn has her dancing-grounds and the sun comes up. There we ran the ship aground on the sand, climbed out onto the shore, and lay down to sleep, waiting for the bright morning.

“And when the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, I sent some of the men up to Circe’s house to bring back the body of Elpenor. We cut firewood and carried it to the highest point of the headland, and there, weeping, with the tears running down, we gave him his funeral rites. When the body and the armor had burned to ashes, we heaped him a mound and set a stone over it, and at the top we fixed the oar he had pulled at while he lived.

“Circe knew well enough that we had come back from the house of Hades, and she dressed herself and hurried down to us, her serving-women behind her, carrying bread and meat and gleaming red wine. She stood in the middle of us, that dread goddess, and spoke: ‘You reckless men, who went down alive to the house of Hades — so you will die twice, where other men die only once. Stay here now, eat your fill, and drink the day away; you sail at first light. And I will set you your course and make everything plain, so that no trap on the land or the sea catches you off your guard and brings you grief.’

“So we agreed, and we sat and feasted all day long until the sun went down. When the sun had set and the dark came on, the men lay down to sleep by the ship’s stern cables. But Circe took me by the hand and led me apart from my companions, and sat me down, and lay beside me, and asked me to tell her all that had happened. And I told her everything, in its order.

“Then the great goddess gave me my sailing directions. ‘That is done, then,’ she said. ‘Now listen to me, and a god will keep it in your mind for you besides. First you will come to the Sirens, who bewitch every man who comes near them. Whoever draws too close, in his ignorance, and hears the voices of the Sirens — never again will his wife and little children stand around him glad that he has come home. The Sirens enchant him with their clear song, sitting in a meadow, and around them is a great heap of bones, of men rotting away, the skin shriveling on them. Row hard past them. Soften beeswax and stop the ears of your men, so that none of the rest can hear. But you yourself, if you long to listen, have them bind you hand and foot in the swift ship, standing upright against the mast-step, with the rope-ends lashed to the mast, so you may hear the two Sirens’ voices and take your joy of it. And if you beg the men to set you free, if you order it — then let them tie you down all the tighter.

“‘When your men have rowed you past the Sirens, I cannot tell you outright which of two roads you must take. You will have to weigh it in your own mind; I will lay both before you. On the one side there are overhanging cliffs, and against them the great waves of dark-eyed Amphitrite come thundering. The blessed gods call these the Wandering Rocks. No bird can pass them — not even the shy doves that carry ambrosia to Father Zeus; the bare rock always snatches one of them away, and the Father sends another to keep their number whole. No ship of men that ever came there got clear again; the planks of the hull and the bodies of the crew are swept off together by the sea-surge and the storms of deadly fire. One ship alone of all that sail has ever passed that way — the Argo, that all men sing of, on her voyage home from Aeetes’ land; and even her the wave would have flung against the great rocks, except that Hera saw her through, for love of Jason.

“‘On the other road there are two cliffs. One of them reaches the wide sky with its sharp peak, and a dark cloud caps it always and never draws off, so the top is never clear, not even in the warm days of summer and harvest. No man could climb it or get a foothold there, not if he had twenty hands and twenty feet, for the rock runs up sheer, as smooth as if it had been polished. Halfway up the cliff there is a misty cave, facing the west and the dark of Erebus, and it is past this that you must steer your ship, glorious Odysseus. Not even a strong young archer, shooting from the deck, could reach that hollow cave with his arrow. Inside it Scylla lives, and her howling is dreadful. Her voice is no louder than a new-born puppy’s, but she herself is a monstrous, evil thing, and no one would be glad to look on her, not even a god who met her. She has twelve feet, all dangling, and six necks stretched out long, and on each a horrible head, and in each head three rows of teeth set thick and close, packed full of black death. Up to her middle she is sunk in the hollow cave, but she thrusts her heads out from the awful pit and fishes there, scouring the rock for dolphins and dogfish and any greater beast she can seize, of the thousands that loud-moaning Amphitrite feeds. No sailors have ever yet passed her and gone away unscathed in their ship; with each head she snatches a man and carries him off.

“‘The other cliff lies lower, Odysseus — you will see it — and close to the first; a man could shoot an arrow across between them. On it grows a great fig tree thick with leaves, and beneath it monstrous Charybdis sucks the black water down. Three times a day she spews it up, and three times she swallows it again, a terror to see. Pray you are not there when she swallows: not even the earth-shaker could save you from ruin then. No — keep hard against Scylla’s cliff and drive the ship past at full speed. It is far better to lose six men and keep the rest than to lose them all.’

“‘But surely, goddess,’ I said, ‘there must be some way to escape deadly Charybdis and still beat Scylla off when she comes for my men?’

“‘You hard, headstrong man,’ the goddess answered, ‘always reaching for the next fight, the next labor — you will not give way even to the deathless gods. Scylla is no mortal thing; she is an immortal horror, savage, monstrous, and not to be fought. There is no defense. The best thing is to flee her. If you waste time arming beside her rock, I am afraid she will dart out again and take six more of your men with her six heads. No — row past with all your strength, and call on Crataiis, Scylla’s mother, who bore her to be a plague to men. She will hold her back from a second strike.

“‘After that you will come to the island of Thrinacia, where the cattle of the Sun graze, and his fat flocks of sheep — seven herds of cattle, seven fine flocks of sheep, fifty in each. They never breed and they never die, and two goddesses herd them, the lovely-haired nymphs Phaethousa and Lampetie, whom shining Neaera bore to Helios who walks above us. Leave those cattle unharmed, keep your mind fixed on getting home, and you may all still reach Ithaca, hard as the way will be. But if you harm them — then I promise you the ruin of your ship and your men. And even if you yourself escape, you will come home late and broken, and every one of your companions lost.’

“By now Dawn had come up on her golden throne, and the great goddess went away inland. I went down to the ship and roused my men to climb aboard and loose the stern cables. They went quickly to their places and struck the gray sea with their oars. And behind our dark-prowed ship Circe sent us a friend, a fair wind that filled the sail and stayed steady at our back, so we had only to keep the gear in order and let the wind and the helmsman carry us on.

“Then, heavy at heart, I spoke to my men. ‘Friends,’ I said, ‘it is not right that only one or two of us should know what Circe foretold me. I will tell you, so that whether we live or die we go in with our eyes open. First she warned us of the wonderful song of the Sirens, and their meadow full of flowers. I alone am to hear them, she said — but you must bind me with a cruel knot, standing upright against the mast-step, with the rope-ends lashed to the mast, so I cannot stir. And if I beg you to let me go, if I order it — then bind me tighter still.’

“I had hardly finished telling them everything before our trim ship came swiftly to the island of the Sirens, for a fair wind drove her on. And then, all at once, the wind dropped; a dead calm came over the water, and some god smoothed the waves to stillness. The men got up and furled the sail and stowed it in the hollow ship, then sat to the oars and whitened the sea with the polished blades. Meanwhile I took a great round of beeswax and cut it small with the sharp bronze, and worked it in my strong hands until it grew soft under the pressure and the heat of the Sun, the lord Hyperion. I stopped the ears of every man in turn; and they bound me hand and foot in the ship, upright against the mast-step, and lashed the rope-ends to the mast, and sat down again and struck the gray sea with their oars. But when we were as far off as a man’s shout will carry, driving on hard, the Sirens did not fail to see our swift ship come near, and they raised their clear song:

“‘Come here to us, famous Odysseus, great glory of the Greeks — bring your ship in and listen to our two voices. No man has ever yet rowed past this place in his black ship until he has heard the honey-sweet song from our lips; he goes on his way with his heart gladdened, and knowing more than he did. For we know everything the Greeks and the Trojans suffered on the wide plain of Troy, by the will of the gods; and we know all that happens anywhere on the fruitful earth.’

“So they sang, in their lovely voices, and my heart ached to listen. I signaled the men with my brows to set me free; but they only bent harder to the oars, and Perimedes and Eurylochus stood up and bound me with still more ropes, tighter than before. Only when we had rowed well past, until we could no longer hear the Sirens’ voices or their song, did my faithful men take the wax from their ears and loose me from the mast.

“But the moment we left that island behind I saw smoke and a great surf ahead, and heard a roaring. The men were so afraid that the oars flew from their hands and trailed splashing in the sea, and the ship lay dead in the water, with no one rowing now. So I went up and down the ship and steadied every man with quiet words, standing over each one.

“‘Friends,’ I said, ‘we are no strangers to danger. This is nothing worse than when the Cyclops trapped us in his cave by brute force, and even there my courage and my counsel and my cunning brought us out. We will remember this too, one day. So now — every man do as I say. Keep your oars in the surf and dig hard at the deep sea; it may be Zeus will let us slip past this ruin and live. And you, helmsman — here is your order, and take it to heart, for the ship’s curved hull is in your hands. Steer her clear of that smoke and surf; hug the cliff, or before you know it she will swerve toward the other side and you will pitch us all to our deaths.’

“They did as I said, and quickly. But I never spoke of Scylla, the horror there was no fighting, for fear my men would let go their oars in terror and huddle below in the hold. And in this one thing I forgot Circe’s hard command — she had told me not to arm at all, but I put on my glorious armor, took up two long spears, and went to stand on the foredeck, for it was from there I thought I would first see rock-dwelling Scylla coming with death for my men. But I could not spot her anywhere, and my eyes ached with searching every fold of the shadowy cliff.

“So we sailed up the narrow strait, groaning, for Scylla was on one side, and on the other monstrous Charybdis sucked the salt sea down in her terrible way. When she spewed it up she seethed and churned like a cauldron over a great fire, and the spray flew high over the tops of both cliffs. When she swallowed the sea down again, all of her showed, churning within, and the rock around her roared horribly, and below, the sea-bed came into sight, black with sand. Green fear took hold of my men. We were staring at Charybdis, dreading our ruin from her — and in that moment Scylla snatched six of my companions out of the ship, the strongest hands I had. I turned to look at the swift ship and at my men, and I saw their hands and feet already overhead as she swung them up, and they cried out to me, calling my name one last time, in the agony of their hearts. As a fisherman on a jutting rock lets down his long pole, his bait on the ox-horn hook to trick the little fish, and flings each one he catches gasping onto the shore — just so she swung my men up, gasping, to the rocks, and there at her cave-mouth she devoured them while they screamed and stretched out their hands to me in their last dreadful struggle. That was the most pitiful thing my eyes ever saw in all my wanderings on the paths of the sea.

“When we had escaped the rocks and dread Charybdis and Scylla, we came at last to the fair island of the Sun-god, where the fine broad-browed cattle grazed, and the many fat flocks of Helios who walks above us. While I was still out at sea in the black ship I could hear the lowing of the cattle as they were stalled, and the bleating of the sheep, and the words of the blind prophet, Theban Tiresias, came back to me, and Circe’s warning too — both had told me again and again to keep away from the island of the Sun, who gives men their joy. So, heavy at heart, I spoke to my men. ‘Friends,’ I said, ‘worn out as you are, hear me. Tiresias foretold me a thing, and Circe warned me of it too: that our worst danger lies on the island of the Sun, who gives men their joy. Drive the black ship past it.’

“At that their spirit broke, and Eurylochus answered me with bitter words. ‘You are a hard man, Odysseus,’ he said. ‘Your strength is more than ours; your limbs never tire; you must be made all of iron. Here are your men worn down with toil and want of sleep, and you will not let us set foot on dry land — on an island ringed by the sea, where we could cook ourselves a good supper — but you order us to keep wandering through the running night, driven off across the misty sea. It is at night the cruel winds rise and wreck ships. How would any of us escape sudden death if a squall came up out of the south or the west, that smashes a ship in spite of the gods? No — let us give in to the dark night now and make our supper here by the swift ship, and at dawn we will go aboard again and put out on the wide sea.’

“So Eurylochus spoke, and the rest agreed. Then I knew for certain that some god was set on our ruin, and I answered him with winged words. ‘Eurylochus, I am one man, and you force my hand. But swear me a great oath, all of you: if we come on a herd of cattle or a fine flock of sheep, no man among you will be so blind, so mad, as to kill a single beast. Sit quiet, and eat the food the immortal Circe gave us.’

“So I spoke, and they swore the oath I asked. When they had sworn it through, we brought the trim ship to anchor in a curving cove beside a spring of fresh water, and the men climbed out and made their supper with care. And when they had eaten and drunk their fill, they fell to weeping, remembering their dear companions whom Scylla had snatched from the ship and devoured; and they wept until sweet sleep came over them.

“In the third watch of the night, when the stars had wheeled past their height, Zeus who gathers the clouds raised a screaming wind, a great storm, and hid the land and the sea alike in cloud; and night came down out of the sky. When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, we hauled the ship up into a hollow cave, where the sea-nymphs have their seats and their dancing-floors, and there I called the men together and spoke to them.

“‘Friends,’ I said, ‘there is food and drink enough in the swift ship. Keep your hands off these cattle, or it will go badly for us. They belong to a dread god, Helios, who sees all things and hears all things.’ So I spoke, and their proud hearts gave in.

“But for a whole month the south wind blew without a break, and after it no wind at all came except the south and the east. As long as the men had bread and red wine, they kept their hands off the cattle, for they wanted to live. But when all the ship’s stores were gone, hunger drove them to range the island after game, fishing with bent hooks, hunting birds, taking whatever came to hand — for their bellies were eaten hollow with want. Then one day I went off alone up the island to pray to the gods, hoping one of them might show me a way home. When I had crossed the island and got clear of my men, I found a place out of the wind, and there I washed my hands and prayed to all the gods who hold Olympus. And they shed sweet sleep over my eyes.

“And while I slept, Eurylochus was laying his ruinous plan before the men. ‘Hear me, friends,’ he said, ‘beaten down as you are. Every death is hateful to us poor mortals, but the worst of all is to die of hunger and meet your doom that way. Come — let us drive off the best of the Sun’s cattle and sacrifice them to the gods who hold the wide sky. And if we ever reach Ithaca, our own land, we will build the Sun a rich temple at once and fill it with fine offerings. But if he is angry over his straight-horned cattle and means to wreck our ship, and the other gods agree — then I would rather drink down one wave and die at once than waste away by inches on a desert island.’

“So Eurylochus spoke, and the rest agreed. At once they drove off the best of the Sun’s cattle from close by — for the fine broad-browed beasts were grazing not far from the dark-prowed ship — and they gathered around them and prayed to the gods, plucking the soft young leaves from a tall oak, since they had no white barley left aboard. When they had prayed, they cut the cattle’s throats and flayed them; they cut out the thigh-bones, wrapped them in a double layer of fat, and laid raw flesh on top. They had no wine to pour over the burning offering, so they made their libation with water as they roasted the innards. And when the thighs were burned and they had tasted the inner parts, they carved up the rest and ran it onto the spits.

“Just then sweet sleep left my eyes, and I went down to the swift ship and the shore of the sea. And as I came near the curved ship, the warm smell of roasting fat reached me. I groaned aloud and cried out to the deathless gods: ‘Father Zeus, and all you blessed gods who live forever — it was for my ruin you lulled me to sleep. See what a monstrous thing my men have dared while I was gone.’

“And Lampetie of the trailing robes ran swiftly to Helios who walks above us and told him we had killed his cattle. At once, in a fury, he spoke out among the immortals. ‘Father Zeus, and all you blessed gods who live forever — make the companions of Odysseus pay for what they have done. In their insolence they have killed my cattle, the cattle I loved to look on every time I climbed the starry sky and every time I turned back from heaven toward the earth. If they do not pay me a fitting price for them, I will go down to Hades and shine among the dead.’

“And Zeus who gathers the clouds answered him: ‘Helios, keep shining on, among the immortals and over the grain-giving earth for mortal men. As for those men, soon enough I will strike their swift ship with a bright bolt and shatter it to splinters in the middle of the wine-dark sea.’

“All this I had from Calypso of the lovely hair, who said she herself had heard it from the messenger Hermes.

“When I had come down to the ship and the sea, I went up to the men and rebuked them one by one. But there was no remedy now; the cattle were dead. And straightaway the gods began to show us signs and portents. The hides crawled along the ground; the flesh on the spits, cooked and raw alike, began to bellow, and there was a sound like the lowing of cattle all around us.

“For six days my faithful companions feasted on the best of the Sun’s cattle they had driven off. But when Zeus, the son of Cronus, brought on the seventh day, the wind dropped its raging at last, and we went aboard and put out on the wide sea, raising the mast and spreading the white sail. When we had left the island behind and no other land was in sight, nothing but sky and sea, the son of Cronus set a black cloud over the hollow ship, and the sea grew dark beneath it. She did not run on much longer. All at once a screaming west wind came on in a great storm, and the gale snapped both the forestays of the mast, and the mast fell backward, and all the rigging spilled into the bilge. The mast came down on the head of the helmsman in the stern and crushed every bone of his skull together, and he plunged from the deck like a diver, and his proud spirit left his bones.

“Then Zeus thundered and hurled his bolt into the ship at once. She reeled from end to end, struck by the lightning of Zeus, and filled with the reek of sulfur, and my men were flung out of her. They bobbed in the swell around the black ship like sea-crows, and the god took their homecoming from them.

“I myself kept pacing the deck until the surge tore the ship’s sides from her keel, and the bare keel went drifting on the swell. The mast had snapped off close to the keel, but a backstay of stout oxhide was still fastened to it. With that I lashed the keel and the mast together, and I sat astride them and let the deadly winds carry me where they would.

“Then the west wind dropped its raging, and the south wind came up quickly, bringing fresh dread to my heart — that it would carry me back to deadly Charybdis. All that night I was swept along, and at sunrise I came again to Scylla’s cliff and dread Charybdis, just as she was sucking the salt sea down. But I sprang up toward the tall fig tree and clung to it, hanging on like a bat, with nowhere to set my feet, nothing to climb on; the roots ran down far below, and the branches arched high overhead, long and great, shadowing all of Charybdis. So I held on, waiting for her to spit up the mast and the keel again — and I longed for them, and they came at last. At the hour when a judge rises from the assembly for his supper, after settling the many quarrels of young men at law, that was the hour when those timbers came up again out of Charybdis. I let go with hands and feet and dropped down with a splash, just beyond the long beams, and climbing onto them I rowed with my hands. And the father of gods and men did not let Scylla catch sight of me; otherwise I would never have escaped sheer death.

“From there I was carried for nine days, and on the tenth night the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia, where Calypso of the lovely hair lives, a dread goddess with a human voice. She took me in and was kind to me. But why tell that again? I told it all to you and your noble wife in your hall only yesterday, and it is a tiresome thing to say twice over what has already been said plainly.”
