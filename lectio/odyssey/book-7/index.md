# The Odyssey, Book VII

The Palace of Alcinous

So Odysseus waited there and prayed, while the girl drove on into the town. When she reached her father’s house she pulled up at the gate, and her brothers — handsome as gods — came crowding round her. They loosed the mules from the wagon and carried the clothes inside, and Nausicaa went to her own room, where an old serving woman, Eurymedusa of Apeira, lit the fire for her. Long ago she had been carried over the sea from Apeira and given to Alcinous as a prize of honor, for he was king over the Phaeacians and the people obeyed him as though he were a god. She had been Nausicaa’s nurse, and now she kindled the fire and brought the girl her supper in her room.

Then Odysseus rose to go on toward the town, and Athena wrapped a thick mist all around him, so that none of the proud Phaeacians who crossed his path might insult him or ask who he was. And just as he was about to enter the town, she came to meet him in the form of a young girl carrying a pitcher. She stopped right in front of him, and Odysseus said:

“My child, would you be kind enough to show me the house of King Alcinous? I’m a stranger here, down on my luck, and I don’t know a soul in your town or your country.”

Then gray-eyed Athena answered him: “Of course, good stranger, I’ll show you the house you’re looking for — it stands close by my own father’s. Only come quietly, and don’t say a word, and don’t look any man in the face or ask him questions. The people here have little patience with strangers and don’t take kindly to anyone from somewhere else. They’re a seafaring folk, and by the grace of Poseidon they sail the deep in ships swift as a thought, or as a bird on the wing.”

With that she led the way, and Odysseus followed in her steps; but not one of the Phaeacians could see him as he passed through their midst, for the great goddess in her care for him had hidden him in a thick cloud of darkness. He marveled at their harbors and their trim ships, at their meeting-places and the high city walls, fitted with palisades — a wonder to look on. And when they reached the king’s house, Athena said:

“This is the house, good stranger, that you asked me to show you. You’ll find a crowd of great men sitting at table inside, but don’t be afraid — go straight in. A bold man comes out ahead in everything, even one who arrives a stranger from far away. The first one you must find is the queen. Her name is Arete, and she comes of the very same line as her husband Alcinous. They both go back to Poseidon, who fathered Nausithous on Periboea, the loveliest of women — the youngest daughter of Eurymedon, who once ruled the reckless Giants, and brought his doomed people to ruin, and was destroyed himself.

“But Poseidon lay with his daughter Periboea, and she bore him a son, great-hearted Nausithous, who reigned over the Phaeacians. Nausithous in turn had two sons, Rhexenor and Alcinous. Apollo struck down the first while he was still a young bridegroom, with no son of his own — but he left a daughter, Arete, and Alcinous made her his wife, and he honors her as no other woman on earth is honored, of all who keep house under a husband’s roof.

“From the start she has been cherished with all his heart — by Alcinous himself, by their children, and by the whole people, who look on her as something more than mortal and salute her with their eyes whenever she walks through the city. For there is no better woman, in mind or in heart, and for those she favors she settles quarrels, even the quarrels of men. Win her good will, and you have every hope of seeing the ones you love again, and coming home at last to your own high-roofed house and your native land.”

With that gray-eyed Athena left Scheria and went out over the barren sea — to Marathon, and the wide streets of Athens, where she passed into the strong house of Erechtheus. But Odysseus came up to the house of Alcinous, and his heart turned over many things as he paused on the bronze threshold before crossing it, for a light shone through the high-roofed house of great-hearted Alcinous like the gleam of the sun or the moon. The walls ran in bronze from the doorway to the inmost room, and the cornice above them was a deep blue enamel. The doors were of gold, hung on silver posts that rose from a floor of bronze; the lintel above was silver, and the door-handle was gold.

On either side of the doorway stood dogs of gold and silver, which Hephaestus had made with all his cunning skill to guard the house of great-hearted Alcinous — deathless, and never to grow old, for all their days. Benches stood ranged along the wall, this side and that, from the threshold to the inmost chamber, spread with finely woven covers that the women of the house had made. Here the leading men of the Phaeacians would sit and eat and drink, for there was plenty the whole year round. And on their pedestals stood golden statues of young men, holding lit torches in their hands, to light the hall by night for those at the feast. Fifty serving women work in that house: some grind the golden grain at the millstones, others weave at the loom or sit and spin, their hands fluttering like the leaves of a tall poplar; and the linen they weave is so close that the oil runs off it. For just as the Phaeacian men are the finest sailors in the world, so their women are the finest at the loom — Athena has given them beyond all others a gift for the lovely work of her hands, and good sense as well.

Outside the courtyard gate, hard by the doors, lies a great orchard of four acres, with a hedge running all the way around it. Tall trees grow there in full leaf — pears and pomegranates and apples bright with fruit, sweet figs and olives in their prime. The fruit of these trees never spoils and never fails, winter or summer, the whole year through; the West Wind blows over them always, bringing some to bud while it ripens others. Pear ripens on pear, apple on apple, cluster on cluster of grapes, and fig on fig. There too is rooted a fruitful vineyard. Part of it lies open to the sun on level ground, drying in the heat; in another part the men are picking the grapes, and in another treading them out, while the near rows are still shedding their blossom and the far ones just beginning to darken. And along the lowest edge grow trim beds of every kind of flower, in bloom from one year’s end to the next. Two springs run through it: one is led in channels across the whole garden, while the other slips beneath the courtyard gate toward the high house itself, and from it the townspeople draw their water. Such were the splendid gifts the gods had heaped on the house of Alcinous.

There godlike Odysseus stood a while and gazed. And when his heart had taken its fill of wonder, he crossed the threshold quickly and went inside. He found the Phaeacian leaders and chief men pouring libations from their cups to keen-eyed Hermes, the slayer of Argos — the last offering they made each night before turning to sleep. Straight through the hall went Odysseus, still wrapped in the thick mist Athena had laid about him, until he reached Arete and King Alcinous. He threw his arms about Arete’s knees — and in that instant the magical mist fell away from him, and there he was, in plain sight. The whole company froze in silence, struck dumb at the sight of a man among them; and Odysseus began his plea at once.

“Arete,” he said, “daughter of great Rhexenor — after all I have suffered, I come as a suppliant to your knees, and to your husband, and to these men at your feast. May the gods grant them good lives, and may each pass on to his children the wealth in his halls and the honor the people have given him. But I beg you, see me sent home to my own land, and quickly — for I have been long away from my friends, and long in grief.”

With that he sat down on the hearth, in the ashes by the fire, and no one said a word. At last the old hero Echeneus spoke — the eldest of the Phaeacians, a fine speaker who knew the wisdom of long years — and out of an honest heart he said:

“Alcinous, this does you no credit — to let a stranger sit on the ground among the hearth-ashes while the rest of us hold back, waiting on your word. Come, raise the man up and seat him on a silver-studded chair, and tell your heralds to mix more wine, so we may pour an offering to Zeus who hurls the thunder, who walks beside suppliants, whom all must honor. And let the housekeeper give our guest a supper out of what the house has.”

When Alcinous heard this, the strong and sacred prince took wise Odysseus by the hand, raised him from the hearth, and gave him the chair of Laodamas — the favorite son who had been sitting beside him — bidding the boy give way. A serving woman brought water in a beautiful golden pitcher and poured it over his hands into a silver basin, and drew up a polished table beside him. A grave housekeeper brought bread and laid it out, with many good things besides, giving freely from her stores; and Odysseus, long-suffering and brilliant, ate and drank. Then Alcinous said to one of his men: “Pontonous, mix the bowl and serve it round the hall, so we may pour to Zeus who hurls the thunder, the protector of suppliants, whom all must honor.”

Pontonous mixed the honeyed wine and carried it round, giving each man his portion to pour. And when they had made their offerings and drunk their fill, Alcinous spoke out among them:

“Leaders and lords of the Phaeacians, hear me. You have feasted; now go home to your beds. In the morning we’ll call a larger gathering of the elders, give our guest the honor of a feast, and offer fine sacrifices to the gods. After that we’ll see to his passage home — so that with no toil or trouble of his own he may come, with our escort, swiftly and gladly back to his native land, however far off it lies; and may come to no harm or grief on the way, until he sets foot in his own country. There, once home, he must bear whatever fate and the heavy Spinners wove for him at his birth, the day his mother brought him into the world. But if he is one of the immortals come down from the sky, then the gods are doing something new with us — for till now they have always shown themselves to us plainly, whenever we offer them great sacrifices, and they feast at our side, sitting where we sit. And even if a lone traveler stumbles on one of them, they put on no disguise, for we are close kin to the gods, as the Cyclopes are, and the wild tribes of the Giants.”

Then Odysseus, full of schemes, answered him: “Alcinous, put that thought out of your mind. I am nothing like the immortals who hold the wide heaven, not in body or in spirit — I am only a mortal man. Think of those you know who carry the heaviest load of sorrow; those are the ones I would set myself beside in suffering. Indeed, I could tell you a longer tale of misery still, of all I have endured by the will of the gods. But for now, let me eat my supper for all my grief. There is nothing so shameless as the belly — it forces a man to take notice of it, no matter how worn down he is or how deep his sorrow runs. My heart is heavy with grief, yet the belly keeps ordering me to eat and drink, driving the memory of all I’ve suffered clean out of me, and demanding only to be filled. But you — at the first light of dawn, set about sending a poor man home to his own land, hard as my trials have been. And then let life leave me, if only I may first see my own house again, my servants, and my great high-roofed halls.”

So he spoke, and they all approved his words and called for the stranger to be given his escort home, since all he said was fair and right. Then, when they had poured their offerings and drunk their fill, each man went home to his own house to sleep, leaving Odysseus behind in the hall with Arete and godlike Alcinous, while the serving women cleared away the supper things. Arete was the first to speak, for she had recognized the cloak and tunic and the fine clothes Odysseus was wearing — she and her women had woven them with their own hands. So she spoke to him with winged words: “Stranger, there is one thing I will ask you first, myself. Who are you, and where are your people? And who gave you these clothes? Didn’t you say you came here drifting over the sea?”

And Odysseus, full of schemes, answered her: “It would be a long story, my queen, to tell the whole tale of my troubles end to end, for the gods of heaven have given me many. But I’ll tell you what you ask. Far off in the sea there is an island called Ogygia, where Calypso lives — daughter of Atlas, a goddess cunning and dangerous, with her lovely braided hair. She lives apart, and no one comes to her, god or mortal. But some god brought me alone to her hearth, broken, after Zeus split my swift ship with his bright bolt and shattered it in the middle of the wine-dark sea. All my brave companions drowned, every one; I alone caught hold of the keel and was carried nine days adrift, until on the tenth black night the gods washed me ashore on Ogygia, where Calypso lives. She took me in, and treated me with all kindness, and tended me, and would have made me deathless and ageless all my days — but she could never win over the heart in my breast.

“There I stayed with her seven full years, and I soaked with my tears the immortal clothes she gave me, the whole time through. But when the eighth year came round at last, she herself ordered me off and urged me to go — whether some word came from Zeus, or her own heart had turned. She sent me away on a raft of bound timbers, well stocked with bread and sweet wine, and put good clothes on my back, and sent a warm, fair wind behind me. Seventeen days I sailed across the open sea, and on the eighteenth the shadowy mountains of your land rose into view — and my heart leaped, unlucky as I was. Yet there was still much suffering ahead of me. For now Poseidon the earth-shaker raised a great storm against me and barred my way; he stirred the sea so high that I could no longer hold to my raft, and it broke apart under the fury of the wind, and I had to swim for it, until at last the wind and the current drove me to your shore.

“There I tried to come to land, but the surf hurled me against the rocks — it was a cruel place — so I drew back and swam on, until I came to a river that looked the kindest landing of all: no rocks, and sheltered from the wind. I dragged myself out and lay there, gathering my strength again, while the holy night came on. I climbed up from the river, into a thicket, and heaped the leaves over me, and a god poured a deep sleep over my eyes. There among the leaves, sick at heart, I slept the whole night through and on past dawn, until the sun was sinking toward the west and woke me. And I saw your daughter’s serving women playing on the sand, and the girl herself among them, like a goddess. To her I made my prayer — and she showed a wisdom you would never look for in one so young, for the young are usually thoughtless. She gave me plenty of bread and sparkling wine, and had me bathed in the river, and gave me these clothes. That is the whole of it — the truth, told plainly, for all the pain in telling it.”

Then Alcinous answered him: “Stranger, my daughter did fall short in one thing — she should have brought you straight here to our house with her women, since you had turned to her first for help.”

“Don’t blame her for it, my lord,” Odysseus answered. “The fault is not hers. She did tell me to follow along with the women — but I held back, out of shame, and out of fear that the sight of me might anger you. We mortals are quick to take offense, every one of us.”

“Stranger,” said Alcinous, “I’m not the kind of man to flare up over nothing. In all things the right measure is the better way. By Father Zeus, and Athena, and Apollo — seeing the man you are, and how your mind runs with my own, I could wish you would stay here, and take my daughter to wife, and be called my son-in-law. I would give you a house and lands of your own, if you chose to stay. But against your will, no Phaeacian shall keep you — Zeus forbid it. And to set your mind at rest, I name the day now: tomorrow I will see to your passage home. You may sleep the whole way over, while my men row you across smooth water to your own country, or wherever you please — even somewhere far beyond Euboea, which those of my people who saw it, the day they carried fair-haired Rhadamanthus to visit Tityus the son of Earth, call the farthest land of all. Even there they went and came back in a single day, and felt no strain. You’ll see for yourself how my ships are the best there are, and what splendid oarsmen my young men make.”

At that godlike Odysseus rejoiced, and lifted up a prayer: “Father Zeus, grant that Alcinous may do all he has promised. Then his name will never die among men on the grain-giving earth — and I will come home to my own land.”

So they spoke together. Then white-armed Arete told her women to set a bed up under the gateway gallery, to lay fine purple rugs on it and spread coverlets over them, with thick woolen blankets to throw on top. The women went out of the hall with torches in their hands, and when they had made the bed snug, they came to Odysseus and roused him: “Come now, stranger, and sleep — your bed is ready.” And glad indeed he was to lie down to his rest.

So Odysseus, long-suffering and brilliant, slept there on the corded bed under the echoing gallery; but Alcinous lay down in the inner room of the high house, and the queen his wife beside him.
