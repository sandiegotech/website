# The Odyssey, Book IX

The Cicones, the Lotus-Eaters, and the Cyclops

Then resourceful Odysseus answered him: “King Alcinous, most renowned of men, it is a fine thing indeed to listen to a bard with such a god-given voice as this man has. To my mind there is nothing better or sweeter than when a whole people keeps holiday together, the feasters sitting in good order through the hall to hear the singer, the tables piled with bread and meat, and the steward drawing wine from the bowl and carrying it round to fill every cup. That, I think, is as fair a sight as a man can see. But you have set your heart on asking me about my own bitter troubles, and so wake fresh grief in me as I tell them. Where, then, shall I begin, and where leave off? For the gods have given me sorrows beyond counting.

“First of all, I will tell you my name, so that you too may know it, and so that, if I live out this time of suffering, I may be your guest-friend in days to come, far off as my home lies. I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, known among all men for my cunning, and my fame reaches the heavens. I live in Ithaca, where there is a high mountain, Neriton, shaking its forests in the wind; and round it lie other islands, close to one another — Dulichium, Same, and wooded Zacynthus. Ithaca itself lies low, the farthest out to sea of them all, off toward the dark, while the rest face the dawn and the rising sun. It is a rugged island, but it raises good men, and for myself I know of no sweeter sight than my own country. The goddess Calypso kept me with her in her hollow cave, longing to make me her husband; and so too did Circe of Aeaea, full of guile, who wanted me for hers. But neither of them could win my heart. There is nothing dearer to a man than his own land and his own parents; let him live in the richest house in the world, far away, cut off from his father and mother, and it gives him no joy. But come — let me tell you of the hard and bitter journey home that Zeus laid on me when I sailed from Troy.

“The wind carried me first from Troy to Ismarus, the city of the Cicones. There I sacked the town and killed the men. We carried off their wives and a great store of plunder, and shared it out so fairly that no man went without his portion. Then I urged my men to be off, and quickly — but the fools would not listen. They stayed there on the beach, drinking deep of the wine and slaughtering whole flocks of sheep and herds of cattle by the sea. Meanwhile the Cicones who had escaped cried out to other Cicones, their neighbors inland, who were more in number and braver, men skilled in fighting from chariots and, when they had to, on foot as well. In the morning they came down on us thick as the leaves and flowers of spring, and the hand of Zeus was heavy on us; doom had marked us out for a hard day. They drew up their line of battle by the swift ships, and we hurled our bronze-tipped spears at one another. All morning, while the holy daylight gathered, we held our ground against them, outnumbered though we were; but when the sun wheeled down toward the hour the ox is loosed from the plow, the Cicones broke us and turned us back, and we lost six strong-greaved men from every ship. The rest of us got away, glad to be alive.

“From there we sailed on with grief in our hearts, glad to have come through with our own lives but mourning the dear comrades we had lost. And I would not let the curved ships go on until we had called out three times, each by name, to every one of our friends who had died there on the field, cut down by the Cicones. Then Zeus drove the North Wind against us, a screaming gale, and hid land and sea alike under his clouds, and night came rushing down out of heaven. The ships ran headlong before it, and the force of the wind tore our sails to shreds, three and four ways across. In terror of going under we hauled them down and rowed hard for the shore, and there we lay two days and two nights, our strength worn out, our hearts eaten with grief. But when fair-haired Dawn brought the third day, we stepped the masts again, spread the white sails, and sat back while the wind and the steersmen held our course. And I would have come home safe and sound, but as I was rounding Cape Malea the swell and the current and the North Wind beat me back and drove me wide of Cythera.

“For nine days the deadly winds carried me across the teeming sea, and on the tenth we made the land of the Lotus-Eaters, a people who live on a flowering food. We went ashore there and drew fresh water, and the crews took their meal beside the swift ships. When we had eaten and drunk, I sent two of my men ahead, with a third for a runner, to find out what kind of people lived in that country. They went off and mixed with the Lotus-Eaters, who meant them no harm but gave them the lotus to taste — and any man who ate that honey-sweet fruit lost all wish to bring back word or to come home again. They wanted only to stay there among the Lotus-Eaters, grazing on the lotus, and let all thought of home slip away. I dragged them back to the ships by force, weeping as they went, and bound them fast beneath the benches. Then I ordered the rest of my trusty men to hurry aboard, before any of them should taste the lotus and forget the way home. They came at once and took their places, and beat the gray sea white with their oars.

“From there we sailed on, sick at heart, and came to the country of the Cyclopes — a lawless, brutal people, who leave everything to the gods. They never plant with their own hands and never plow; yet the earth, untilled and unsown, brings up everything for them — wheat, barley, and vines heavy with great clusters of grapes that the rain of Zeus swells into wine. They have no councils to make laws and no meeting-places; they live on the peaks of high mountains, in hollow caves, and each one lays down the law for his own wife and children and cares nothing for his neighbors.

“Now off the harbor of that country, neither close in nor far out, there lies a wooded island, overrun with countless wild goats. No man’s foot ever scares them, for no hunters trudge through its forests or toil over its ridges. It is never grazed by flocks or broken by the plow; year after year it lies empty of men, unsown and untilled, with nothing on it but the bleating goats. For the Cyclopes have no red-cheeked ships, and no shipwrights among them to build the kind of vessel that could carry them from town to town, the way other men cross the sea to one another. With ships they could have made that island fine and rich; it is no poor land, and would bear fruit in its season. Along the shore of the gray sea run soft, well-watered meadows, where the vines would never fail; there is level ground for plowing, and a man would reap a heavy harvest in due time, for the soil beneath is deep and dark. And the harbor is so snug that there is no need of moorings — no anchor-stones to throw, no cables to make fast; a man can simply run his ship ashore and wait as long as he likes, until his crew is willing and the wind turns fair. At the head of the harbor a spring of bright water runs out from under a cave, with poplars growing all around.

“It was there we put in, and some god must have guided us through the dark, for there was nothing to be seen: a thick fog lay close around the ships, and no moon shone in the sky, but lay smothered in cloud. Not one of us could make out the island with his eyes, or the long rollers breaking on the beach, until our ships ran up onto the sand itself. Then we struck the sails, stepped ashore, and lay down to sleep on the edge of the sea, waiting for the holy light of day.

“When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, we walked the island in wonder. And the nymphs, the daughters of Zeus who holds the storm-shield, started up the wild mountain goats, so that my men might have their meal. At once we ran to the ships for our curved bows and our long spears, split into three bands, and let fly; and the god gave us game in plenty. I had twelve ships in my company, and to each one nine goats fell by lot, and ten to mine alone. So all that long day, until the sun went down, we sat and feasted on the rich meat and the sweet wine; for the red wine in the ships had not yet run dry — each crew had carried off a great store of it in jars when we sacked the holy city of the Cicones. And as we feasted we looked across to the land of the Cyclopes, so near at hand, and saw the smoke of their fires and heard the voices of the men and the bleating of their sheep and goats. Then, when the sun went down and darkness came on, we lay down to sleep along the breaking surf; and when the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, I called my men together and spoke to them all:

“‘The rest of you, my loyal friends, wait here. I am going across with my own ship and crew to learn what kind of men live over there — whether they are savage and lawless and cruel, or whether they fear the gods and treat a stranger kindly.’

“With that I went aboard and told my men to follow and cast off the cables. They took their places and beat the gray sea with their oars. When we reached the land — it was no long crossing — we saw a great cave near the water’s edge, high on a headland and shaded over with laurels. There many flocks of sheep and goats spent the night, and around it ran a high outer wall, built of quarried stones sunk deep, and of tall pines and oaks with their crowns still on them. It was the den of a monstrous man, who pastured his flocks far off and alone, keeping clear of all others, with no law in his heart, a savage thing. He was a marvel of dread to look at — not like a man who lives on bread, but like a wooded crag that stands out on its own against the sky, high among the mountains.

“I told the rest of my trusty men to stay there and guard the ship, and chose the twelve best of them to come with me. I took along a goatskin of dark, sweet wine, which Maron, son of Euanthes, had given me — the priest of Apollo, the god who watches over Ismarus, for Maron lived within the shaded grove of the temple. When we sacked the town we had spared him, out of reverence, along with his wife and child, since he lived in the holy precinct of Phoebus Apollo; and in return he gave me splendid gifts: seven talents of well-wrought gold, a mixing-bowl all of silver, and, besides, twelve jars of wine, sweet and unmixed, a drink fit for the gods. Not one of the servants in his house knew of it, none of the maids, but only he himself, his own wife, and a single housekeeper. Whenever they drank that honey-sweet red wine, he would pour one cup of it into twenty of water, and even so a fragrance rose from the bowl so sweet and strange that it was hard to hold back from drinking. I filled a great skin with this wine and packed a leather bag with food; for something deep in me warned, even then, that I was about to meet a man of monstrous strength, a wild thing that knew neither right nor law.

“We soon came to the cave, but found him gone — he was out pasturing his fat flocks. So we went inside and looked over everything. The drying-racks were heavy with cheeses; the pens were crowded with lambs and kids, each age penned apart — the firstlings in one fold, the middle ones in another, the newborn in a third — and all the vessels swam with whey, the pails and the bowls he milked into. At once my men began to beg me: let us take some of the cheeses and be gone, then come back quickly and drive the kids and lambs down to the swift ship and sail away. But I would not listen — though it would have been far better if I had — for I wanted to see the man himself, and find out whether he would give me a guest-gift. As it turned out, his coming was no joy to my companions.

“We lit a fire, made an offering of some of the cheeses, ate of the rest, and sat down inside to wait until he should come back with his flocks. And come he did, carrying a huge load of dry wood to make his supper fire, and he flung it down inside the cave with a crash that sent us scrambling in terror to the far corner. Then he drove his fat flocks in under the roof, all the ewes he meant to milk, leaving the rams and the he-goats outside in the deep yard. After that he heaved up a great doorstone and set it in the mouth of the cave — such a stone that two and twenty good four-wheeled wagons could not have pried it from the ground, so vast was the crag he jammed against the doorway. Then he sat down and milked his ewes and bleating goats, each in turn, and set her young one under each. Half the white milk he curdled and pressed and laid by in woven baskets, and half he kept standing in the pails to drink with his supper. When he had finished all his work in haste, he kindled the fire — and only then did his eye fall on us, and he said:

“‘Strangers, who are you? Where have you sailed from, over the watery ways? Are you on some trading run, or do you wander the sea at random, like raiders who risk their lives to bring trouble to other men?’

“Our hearts cracked within us at the deep boom of his voice and the sheer size of the monster. Still, I found words to answer him: ‘We are Greeks, blown off our course on the way home from Troy, driven over the great gulf of the sea by every kind of wind, far from the path we meant to keep. So it has fallen out, by the will of Zeus. We are proud to be the men of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, whose fame is the greatest under heaven now, so mighty a city did he sack, so many people did he bring down. And we have come to you as suppliants, begging that you treat us with kindness, or even give us the gift a guest may rightly expect. Respect the gods, great sir; we are at your mercy. Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers — Zeus the god of guests, who walks at the side of every honest traveler.’

“That was my plea, but his answer was without mercy: ‘Stranger, you are a fool, or you have come from a long way off, to tell me to fear the gods and shrink from their anger. We Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus who holds the storm-shield, or for any of the blessed gods; we are far stronger than they. I would never spare you or your men to keep out of Zeus’s way — not unless my own heart moved me to it. But tell me now: where did you leave your good ship when you came ashore? Far down the coast, or close by? I should like to know.’

“He was fishing to trap me, but I knew the world too well to be caught, and I answered him with a lie of my own: ‘My ship? Poseidon the earth-shaker smashed it on the rocks at the far edge of your country; he drove it against the headland, and the wind carried it in from the open sea. Only I and these few here got away from sudden death.’

“To that the cruel creature gave no answer at all. He simply lunged, shot out his hands, snatched up two of my men together, and dashed them to the ground like a pair of puppies. Their brains spilled out and wetted the earth. He hacked them limb from limb and made his supper of them, and like a mountain lion he devoured them — flesh, innards, bones, marrow — and left nothing. We could only weep and lift our hands to Zeus at the horror before our eyes, helpless to do anything else. When the Cyclops had filled his great belly with human meat and washed it down with raw milk, he stretched himself full length among his flocks inside the cave and slept. My first thought was to draw the sharp sword at my side, steal up, and drive it into his chest where the liver is cradled against the midriff, feeling for the spot with my hand. But a second thought held me back: we too would have died there, every one of us, for our own hands could never have shifted that monstrous stone he had wedged against the high doorway. So we lay there groaning, and waited for the holy dawn.

“When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, he kindled his fire again and milked his fine flocks, each in turn, and set her young one under each. As soon as he had finished his work in haste, he snatched up two more of my men and made his morning meal of them. Then, easily, he rolled the great stone away from the door, drove his fat flocks out, and set the stone back in its place, as lightly as a man fits the lid on a quiver. With a great whistling and shouting he turned his flocks toward the mountain, and left me there behind him, brooding on revenge — praying that Athena would grant it, and that I might pay him back.

“And of all the plans my heart turned over, this seemed the best. There lay beside one of the pens a great club of the Cyclops, green olive-wood, which he had cut to carry once it dried. To us it looked as big as the mast of a broad black ship of twenty oars, the kind that crosses the wide gulf of the sea — that long it was, and that thick. I went up to it and cut off a length the height of a man, handed it to my crew, and told them to plane it smooth; and they did. Then I sharpened the end to a point myself and hardened it in the blazing fire, and hid it away under the heaps of dung that lay thick across the cave floor. After that I had the others draw lots: which of them would dare to lift the stake with me and grind it into the giant’s eye when sweet sleep had taken him. The lot fell on the very four I would have chosen, and I made a fifth. That evening he came back driving his fleecy flocks, and this time he brought the whole flock in, leaving none in the deep yard outside — whether some thought struck him, or a god put it in his mind. He heaved the great stone up and set it in the doorway, then sat and milked his ewes and bleating goats, each in turn, and set her young one under each. When he had finished his work in haste, again he seized two of my men and made his supper of them. And then I came up close to the Cyclops, holding out an ivy-wood bowl brimful of the dark wine, and said:

“‘Here, Cyclops, drink some wine, now that you have feasted on the flesh of men, and learn what kind of vintage our ship was carrying. I was bringing it as an offering to you, hoping you might pity me and send me on my way home. But your savagery is past all bearing. You hard heart — how can you think any other man on earth will ever come to you again, when you do such lawless things?’

“He took it and drank it off, and the sweet drink delighted him so much that he begged me for another bowl. ‘Be a good fellow and give me more,’ he said, ‘and tell me your name, here and now, so that I may give you a guest-gift to make your heart glad. Our own rich soil bears the Cyclopes wine, too, from great clusters that the rain of Zeus swells full; but this is a draught of pure nectar and ambrosia.’

“So I filled the bowl for him again. Three times I poured it, and three times the fool drank it down. And when at last the wine had clouded his wits, I spoke to him in soft and winning words: ‘Cyclops, you ask my honored name, and I will tell it — and you give me the guest-gift you promised. Nobody is my name. Nobody my mother and father call me, and all my friends.’

“And the cruel creature answered me at once: ‘Then Nobody I will eat last of all his company, and the others first. That is the gift I give you.’

“As he spoke he toppled backward and fell sprawling on his back, his thick neck lolling sideways, and sleep, the tamer of all, came over him. Out of his gullet poured wine and gobbets of human flesh, for the drink had heaved him up. Then I drove the stake deep under the ashes to heat it, and put heart into my men with steadying words, so that not one would shrink back in fear. When the olive stake, green as it was, was on the very point of catching fire and glowing terribly, I drew it from the embers, and my men stood close around me, and a god breathed great courage into us all. They took the sharpened olive stake and drove it into his eye, while I leaned my weight on it from above and turned it — the way a man bores a ship’s timber with a drill, while the others below keep it spinning with a strap, and the drill runs on and on without stopping. So we took the fire-pointed stake and spun it in his eye, and the blood came boiling up around the burning point. The searing heat of the eyeball singed off all his brows and lashes, and the roots of the eye crackled in the flame. As a smith plunges a great axe or an adze into cold water and it shrieks aloud — for that is the secret of the iron’s strength — so his eye hissed around the olive stake. He let out a hideous howl, and the rock walls rang with it, and we shrank back in terror. He wrenched the stake from his eye, all fouled and slick with blood, and hurled it from him, flailing his arms, and bellowed to the other Cyclopes who lived around him in their caves along the windy heights. They heard his cry and came crowding from every side to stand about his cave, and called out, asking what was wrong:

“‘Polyphemus, what is it? Why such a terrible cry in the still of the holy night, robbing us of our sleep? Surely no man is driving off your flocks against your will? Surely no man is killing you, by trickery or by force?’

“And from inside the cave mighty Polyphemus called back to them: ‘My friends — Nobody is killing me, by trickery, not by force!’

“And they answered him with winged words: ‘Well, if nobody is doing you violence, and you are all alone, there is no help for the sickness that great Zeus sends a man — pray, then, to your father, Lord Poseidon.’

“With that they went off, and the heart in me laughed to think how my name and my flawless cunning had fooled them. But the Cyclops, groaning and writhing in his pain, groped along the wall with his hands until he found the stone and dragged it from the doorway, and then sat himself down in the entrance with his arms spread wide, to catch anyone trying to slip out among the sheep — for he must have thought me fool enough to try it that way.

“But I was turning over plan after plan, searching for the one that would save the lives of my men and my own; for death stood close, and the danger was great. And of them all, this was the plan I judged the best. The rams were broad and well grown, with thick fleeces of dark wool. These I lashed together silently in threes, with the twisted withies the lawless monster slept on; the middle ram of each three would carry a man, and the two on either side would walk beside him and shield him. So three rams bore each man across. For myself, there was one ram, far the finest of the whole flock; I gripped him by the back, swung myself in under his shaggy belly, and lay there face up, both hands twisted tight in the marvelous fleece, holding on with a steady heart. So, sighing, we waited for the holy dawn.

“When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, the rams went butting out to pasture, while the unmilked ewes bleated about the pens, their udders near to bursting. Their master, racked with pain, ran his hands along the backs of all the rams as they passed him standing upright — never guessing, the fool, that my men were lashed beneath the woolly bellies. Last of all the great ram went to the door, heavy with his own fleece and with the weight of me and my scheming. And mighty Polyphemus laid his hands on him and said:

“‘My good ram, why are you the last of all the flock to leave the cave? You were never one to lag behind the others. No, you would always be first, striding out ahead to graze the soft grass and the running streams, first to reach the fold again at evening — and now you come last of all. Is it that you grieve for your master’s eye, which a vile man and his wretched crew have put out, after they fuddled my wits with wine — Nobody, who has not yet escaped his death, I swear it! If only you could think and speak like me, and tell me where he is skulking from my rage, I would dash his brains across the floor of the cave and ease the misery this good-for-nothing Nobody has brought on me.’

“With that he sent the ram out through the door. When we had got a little way from the cave and the yard, I first slipped out from under the ram, and then untied my men. And quickly, often glancing back, we drove the long-shanked, fat-thighed sheep down to the ship. My dear companions were glad indeed to see us — those of us who had escaped death — but they broke into weeping and wailing for the rest. I would not let them: I frowned and shook my head at each of them to silence their grief, and ordered them to fling the fine fleecy sheep aboard at once and put out over the salt sea. They climbed in, took their places, and beat the gray sea white with their oars. But when I was as far off as a man’s shout will carry, I called back to the Cyclops, taunting him:

“‘Cyclops! So he was no weakling after all, the man whose comrades you murdered and ate in your hollow cave! Your evil deeds were bound to fall back on your own head, you brute, who had no shame to devour your own guests in your own house. Now Zeus and the other gods have paid you out.’

“That set him blazing with fury. He tore loose the peak of a great mountain and flung it, and it crashed down just ahead of the dark-prowed ship, barely clearing the steering-oar’s tip. The sea heaved up where the rock plunged in, and the backwash of the swell drove us in toward the land again, the wave sweeping us hard against the shore. But I caught up a long pole and shoved us off, and with a jerk of my head I signaled the crew to throw their backs into the oars and save their lives; and they bent to it with a will. When we had pulled out twice as far as before, I was ready to taunt the Cyclops again, but the men crowded round me on every side, begging me to stop:

“‘Are you mad? Why provoke this wild thing further? Already the rock he hurled into the sea drove the ship back to land, and we were sure we were finished. If he hears so much as a sound from one of us, he will let fly another jagged crag and smash our heads and the ship’s timbers to pulp — his throw carries that far.’

“But they could not bend my stubborn heart, and I shouted back at him a second time, in my anger: ‘Cyclops, if ever any mortal man asks you who put out your eye and shamed you, tell him it was Odysseus, sacker of cities, the son of Laertes, whose home is in Ithaca!’

“At that he gave a great groan and cried out: ‘No — so the old prophecy has come home to me at last! There was a seer here once, a tall and noble man, Telemus son of Eurymus, the best of prophets, who grew old foretelling among the Cyclopes. He told me all this would come to pass in time — that I would lose my sight at the hands of one called Odysseus. But I always looked for some tall and handsome man to come, armed in great strength. And now a little nothing of a man, a feeble weakling, has blinded my eye — but only after he downed me with his wine. Come back, Odysseus, and let me give you the gifts a guest is owed, and beg the great earth-shaker to send you safely home. For I am his son, and he calls himself my father. He, if he wishes — and no other, neither god nor man — can heal me.’

“And I answered him: ‘I only wish I could be as certain of robbing you of your life and your breath and sending you down to the house of Hades, as I am that not even the earth-shaker will ever heal that eye of yours.’

“At that he stretched out his hands toward the starry sky and prayed to Lord Poseidon: ‘Hear me, Poseidon, dark-haired holder of the earth. If I am truly yours, and you are truly my father, grant that Odysseus, sacker of cities, the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca, never reaches his home again. But if it is his fate to see his own people and come back to his high-roofed house and his native land, then let him come late, and broken, having lost every last one of his companions, in a ship that is not his own — and let him find nothing but trouble in his house.’

“So he prayed, and the dark-haired god heard him. Then the Cyclops heaved up a stone far greater than the first, swung it, and hurled it with measureless force. It fell just astern of the dark-prowed ship, barely missing the tip of the steering-oar. The sea heaved up where the rock plunged in, and the wave drove us forward, sweeping us on toward the far island.

“And so we came back to the island where the rest of our well-benched ships lay waiting all together, and our companions sat around them, grieving, watching the sea for us. We ran the ship up onto the sand and waded ashore, and unloaded the Cyclops’s flocks from the deep hull and shared them out, so fairly that no man went without his portion. As for the great ram, when the sheep were divided my dear companions gave him to me alone, beyond my share; and there on the beach I sacrificed him to Zeus, the dark-clouded son of Cronus, who is lord of all, and burned the thigh-pieces in his honor. But he took no heed of my offering; even then he was laying his plans to destroy all my well-benched ships and my faithful men.

“So all that long day, until the sun went down, we sat and feasted on the rich meat and the sweet wine. And when the sun had set and darkness came on, we lay down to sleep along the breaking surf. Then, when the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, I roused my men and ordered them aboard to cast off the cables. They climbed in, took their places, and beat the gray sea white with their oars. So we sailed on, sick at heart, glad to be alive but grieving for the dear companions we had lost.”
