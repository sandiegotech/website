# The Odyssey, Book XX

The Last Night Before the Reckoning

Odysseus lay down to sleep in the forecourt. He spread an undressed ox-hide on the floor, heaped over it a pile of fleeces from the sheep the suitors were forever slaughtering, and Eurynome threw a cloak across him once he had settled. There he lay, wide awake, turning over in his mind how he would bring death to the suitors. And as he lay there the women of the house who had been sleeping with those men came slipping out, laughing and joking with one another as they went. The sight set his heart boiling. He weighed it hard — whether to rush in and kill every one of them on the spot, or to let them go to the suitors this one last time. His heart growled inside him. As a bitch standing over her pups will growl and bare her teeth at a stranger, ready to fight, so his heart growled within him at the wickedness he was watching. But he struck his chest and rebuked his own heart: “Endure, my heart. You have borne worse than this. You bore it the day the Cyclops, that savage with no restraint in him, devoured your brave companions one by one — and you held on, in silence, until your cunning brought you out of that cave where you were sure you would die.”

So he spoke, calming the heart in his breast, and it obeyed him and held steady. But his body would not be still. As a man turns a sausage thick with blood and fat over a blazing fire, this way and that, hungry to have it roasted through, so Odysseus tossed from side to side, racking his mind to see how he, one man alone, could ever lay hands on so great a crowd of them. Then Athena came down from heaven to him, in the likeness of a woman, and stood over his head and spoke: “Why are you still awake, you most unlucky of men? This is your own house. Your wife is safe inside it, and your son too — a young man any father would be proud of.”

“All of that is true, goddess,” Odysseus answered. “But here is what gnaws at me. How am I, one man, to lay hands on these shameless suitors, who are never here in less than a crowd? And beyond that — a harder thing still — even if I do kill them, with Zeus’s help and yours, where am I to escape to afterward, with all their kinsmen out for my blood? I ask you to think on that.”

“You have no faith,” said Athena. “A man will lean on a weaker friend than I am — a mortal, with none of my wisdom. And I am a goddess, who has watched over you through every trouble you have had. Hear me plainly: if fifty companies of fighting men stood around the two of us, hot to kill, you would still drive off their cattle and their fat sheep and walk away. Now sleep. To lie awake the whole night through, on guard, is a wearing thing. You are nearly clear of your troubles.”

As she spoke she poured sleep over his eyes, and went back to Olympus.

While sleep was loosening the cares from Odysseus’s limbs, his faithful wife woke. She sat up in her soft bed and wept; and when she had wept until the grief eased, she prayed to Artemis: “Artemis, great goddess, daughter of Zeus — drive an arrow into my breast and take my life now, this moment. Or let a storm-wind catch me up and carry me down the paths of darkness and drop me where the streams of Ocean pour back into themselves — as the winds once carried off the daughters of Pandareus. The gods killed their father and mother and left them orphans in their house; but Aphrodite fed them on cheese and honey and sweet wine, and Hera made them lovelier and wiser than other women, and holy Artemis gave them height and bearing, and Athena taught them every fine handiwork. Then, while Aphrodite had gone up to high Olympus to ask Zeus — who knows all things, what is fated and what is not — to grant the girls a happy marriage, the storm-winds snatched them away and handed them over to be servants to the hateful Furies. Let the gods on Olympus blot me out the same way. Or let fair Artemis strike me, so that I might pass under the dreadful earth still looking on Odysseus, and never gladden the heart of a lesser man. A grief is bearable, however a person weeps by day, so long as sleep comes at night — for sleep closes the eyes and wipes out everything, the good and the bad alike. But some god sends me even evil dreams. This very night one lay beside me who was the image of Odysseus as he looked the day he sailed; and my heart leapt, for I thought it no dream but the waking truth at last.”

So she spoke, and at once golden Dawn came on. Odysseus caught the sound of her weeping, and it troubled him; half-dreaming, it seemed to him that she already knew him and was standing at his side. He gathered up the cloak and the fleeces he had slept on and laid them on a chair in the hall, and carried the ox-hide outside. Then he lifted his hands to heaven and prayed: “Father Zeus — if it was truly your will to bring me home over land and sea, after all the suffering you laid on me, then let one of those now waking in the house speak a word of good omen for me, and send me some other sign as well, from outside.”

So he prayed, and Zeus the counselor heard him. At once he thundered out of shining Olympus, high above the clouds, and Odysseus’s heart lifted. And inside the house a woman grinding grain spoke the word of omen, close by, from the room where the mills of the master stood. Twelve women in all worked there, grinding barley and wheat, the marrow of men’s strength. The others had finished their measure and lain down to rest, but this one had not yet stopped, for she was the frailest of them. Now she let her millstone rest and spoke the words that were a sign for her master: “Father Zeus, lord of gods and men — how loud you have thundered out of a clear sky, with not one cloud in it. That is a sign for someone. Then grant this too, to me, poor wretch that I am: let this be the last day, the very last, that the suitors take their pleasant feast in the house of Odysseus. They have broken my knees with the bitter grinding of their meal. Let this be the last meal they ever eat.”

So she spoke, and Odysseus rejoiced at the word of omen and at the thunder of Zeus; for he knew now that he would have his revenge on the guilty.

The other women of the house were rising now and kindling the fire on the hearth, the flame that would not die. And Telemachus got up from his bed and dressed, a godlike man. He slung his sharp sword over his shoulder, bound the fine sandals on his feet, and took up his strong bronze-tipped spear; then he came and stood in the doorway and said to Eurycleia: “Nurse, did you see the stranger looked after in the house — a bed, a meal — or has he been left to fend for himself? That would be like my mother: shrewd as she is, she will fuss over some second-rate man and turn a far better one out the door.”

“Do not blame her, child, when there is no blame to give,” the wise Eurycleia answered. “The stranger sat and drank his wine as long as he wished. He had no more appetite for bread — your mother asked him. And when he thought of sleep, she told the maids to make up a bed; but he is so battered and luckless a man that he would not lie on a bed under blankets. He slept in the forecourt, on an undressed ox-hide with some fleeces over it, and I laid a cloak across him myself.”

So Telemachus went out through the hall, his spear in his hand and his two quick hounds trotting beside him, on his way to the place of assembly where the Greeks were gathering. And Eurycleia, that good woman, daughter of Ops son of Peisenor, called out to the maids: “Up with you, and to work. Sweep the hall and sprinkle it down to lay the dust; throw the purple covers over the chairs; sponge the tables clean; rinse out the mixing-bowls and the fine cups. Some of you, off to the spring for water, and be quick. The suitors will not stay away long this morning — they will come early, for today is a feast for all the people.”

So she spoke, and they jumped to it. Twenty of them went off to the deep-running spring, and the rest got busy through the house. The serving-men of the suitors came in and set to splitting firewood, deft hands at it. The women came back from the spring; and after them came the swineherd, driving in three fat hogs, the best of his herd. He left them to root about the good courtyard, and said to Odysseus, kindly enough: “Stranger, are the suitors treating you any better now, or are they riding you as hard as ever?”

“May the gods pay them back, Eumaeus,” Odysseus answered, “for the outrages they pile up so insolently, in another man’s house, with no shame in them at all.”

So the two of them talked. And now Melanthius the goatherd came up, driving in the best of his goats for the suitors’ dinner, with two herdsmen at his side. He tied the goats under the echoing gateway, and then he turned on Odysseus with his sneering tongue: “Still here, are you, stranger — still pestering the house with your begging? Still in the way? I can see the two of us are not going to part before we have given each other a taste of the fist. There is no decency in the way you beg. Are there no other feasts among the Greeks, that you have to glue yourself to ours?”

Odysseus said nothing in answer. He only bent his head and brooded, turning his dark thoughts over inside him. Then a third man came up — Philoetius, the chief of the herdsmen — leading in a barren heifer and some fat goats. The ferrymen had brought him across, the men who carry over anyone who comes to them. He tied his beasts carefully under the echoing gateway, and went to the swineherd and asked: “Swineherd, who is this newcomer, this stranger in our house? Where does he claim his people are, where is he from? Poor fellow — and yet for all his rags he carries himself like a king. But the gods send hard days even to wandering men, and spin out trouble even for kings, when it is their will.”

And as he spoke he stepped over to Odysseus and took his right hand in greeting, and said: “Good day to you, father stranger. Things go badly for you now, but may better days come. Father Zeus — there is no god crueler than you. You feel no pity for the men you fathered yourself; you let them fall into hard living and into bitter pain. The sweat broke out on me and my eyes filled the moment I saw this man, for he put me in mind of Odysseus. I cannot help thinking my master too is dressed in rags like these, knocking about the world somewhere — if he is even still in the light of the sun. But if he is dead already and gone down to the house of Hades — then bitter grief for the good Odysseus, who set me over his cattle while I was still a boy, among the Cephallenians. The herds have grown past counting; no man could have bred them better; broad-browed cattle thick as standing grain. And now strangers order me to drive them in for their own eating. They take no thought for the son of the house, though he is right here; they have no fear of the gods’ anger; they are itching already to share out the goods of a master gone so long away. I have turned it over and over in my mind — only it would be a wrong thing while his son lives — to drive the cattle off to some other country, to strangers. But that is a hard road too. So I stay here and put up with it, minding cattle for other men’s pleasure, and it has become more than I can bear. I would have run off long ago and put myself under some other great lord, for life here is past enduring — but I keep thinking of my unlucky master, that he might yet come home and scatter these suitors through the house.”

“Herdsman,” said Odysseus, “you strike me as no fool and no bad man, and I can see for myself you have sense in you. So I will tell you something, and swear a great oath on it. Witness it, Zeus before all the gods, and this table of guest-friendship, and the hearth of noble Odysseus that I have come to: while you are still here, in this very place, Odysseus will come home. And if you have a mind to it, you will see with your own eyes the killing of the suitors who lord it here.”

“Stranger, if only Zeus would bring that to pass,” the herdsman answered, “you would see what strength is in these hands, and how I would use it.” And Eumaeus prayed in the same way, to all the gods, that wise Odysseus might come home again.

So the three of them talked. Meanwhile the suitors were busy hatching death and doom for Telemachus. But a bird came to them on the left — a high-flying eagle with a trembling dove in its talons. And Amphinomus rose and spoke: “My friends, this plan of ours, this killing of Telemachus, will never come off cleanly. Let us turn our minds to the feast instead.” So said Amphinomus, and his words pleased them. They went into the house of noble Odysseus, laid their cloaks over the chairs and benches, and set to slaughtering the great sheep and the fat goats, the well-fed hogs and the heifer of the herd. They roasted the inner meats and shared them round, mixed the wine in the bowls, and the swineherd handed out the cups. Philoetius, chief of the herdsmen, passed the bread in fine baskets, and Melanthius poured the wine. And they all reached out to the good things laid ready before them.

Telemachus, working a plan of his own, seated Odysseus inside the strong-built hall, by the threshold of stone, with a mean little table and a poor chair. He set a portion of the inner meats before him and poured his wine into a gold cup, and said: “Sit there now, and drink your wine among your betters. I will keep the gibes and the fists of every suitor off you myself. This house is not common property. It is the house of Odysseus, and he won it for me. So you suitors — hold your tempers, keep your hands and your tongues to yourselves, or there will be a quarrel and a fight.”

So he spoke, and they all bit their lips and marveled that Telemachus had the nerve to speak so. Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, said: “Hard as it is, Greeks, let us swallow what Telemachus says, threats and all. Zeus would not let us, or we would have shut his ringing mouth long ago, here in this hall.” So Antinous spoke, but Telemachus paid him no mind.

Now the heralds were leading the sacred offering, the hundred beasts, up through the city, and the long-haired Greeks were gathering under the shady grove of Apollo the archer. When the suitors had roasted the outer meat and drawn it off the spits, they shared it out and feasted in splendor; and those who served set a portion before Odysseus equal to their own, for so Telemachus, the dear son of godlike Odysseus, had commanded.

But Athena would not let the proud suitors hold back from their galling insolence — she wanted the pain to bite still deeper into the heart of Odysseus. There was among them a man of lawless mind named Ctesippus, whose home was in Same. Trusting in his great wealth, he had come courting the wife of the long-absent Odysseus; and now he spoke up to the arrogant suitors: “Hear me, you proud suitors, while I say a word. The stranger has had his fair share all along, as he should — there is no decency in slighting a guest of Telemachus, whoever comes to this house. But come, let me give him a guest-gift of my own — something he can pass on to the bath-girl, or to any other servant in the house of godlike Odysseus.”

And with that he snatched up a cow’s hoof from the basket where it lay and flung it with a strong hand. But Odysseus tipped his head just aside and dodged it, and inside himself he smiled — a grim, bitter smile — while the hoof struck the solid wall. Then Telemachus rounded on Ctesippus: “It is well for your own skin, Ctesippus, that you missed the stranger — that he dodged your throw. Had you hit him, I would have run you through the middle with my spear, and your father would be busying himself with your funeral here, not your wedding. So let me see no more loutishness from any of you in this house. I see now, and understand, both the good and the bad of things; I am no longer the child I was. I have borne the sight of all this long enough — my sheep slaughtered, my wine drunk, my bread eaten — for one man cannot hold off many. But let there be no more violence done to me out of spite. And if it is your pleasure to cut me down with the bronze, then I would rather die — far rather — than go on watching these foul deeds day after day: guests knocked about, and the serving-women dragged through this fine house in shame.”

So he spoke, and they all fell dead silent. At last Agelaus, son of Damastor, said: “Friends, when a thing is fairly said, no one should round on it in anger or answer it with hard words. Leave off mishandling the stranger, and any other servant in the house of godlike Odysseus. But to Telemachus and his mother I would speak a gentle word, if it might find a welcome in both their hearts. As long as you could still hope in your hearts that wise Odysseus would come home, there was no fault in waiting and holding the suitors off in your halls. That would have been the better way, had he come back. But by now it is plain enough he never will. So sit down beside your mother and tell her this: let her marry the best man, the one who offers the most, so that you may keep all your father’s goods to yourself, to eat and drink in peace, while she keeps another man’s house.”

“No, Agelaus,” Telemachus answered. “I swear it by Zeus, and by the sufferings of my father, who is dead or wandering far from Ithaca — I put no obstacle in the way of my mother’s marriage. I urge her, in fact, to wed whom she will, and I will give her gifts beyond counting. But to drive her from this house against her will, with a hard word — that I cannot bring myself to do. Heaven forbid I should.”

So spoke Telemachus. But Pallas Athena set the suitors laughing — laughing until they could not stop, until their wits went wandering. They were laughing now with jaws that were not their own. The meat they ate ran with blood; their eyes brimmed over with tears; and their hearts foreboded grief. Then Theoclymenus, the godlike seer, spoke out among them: “You poor doomed men — what is this horror coming over you? Night has wound itself around your heads, your faces, your knees. The hall is loud with the sound of wailing; your cheeks are wet with tears. The walls are spattered with blood, and the fair crossbeams above. The porch is crowded with ghosts, and the courtyard too — ghosts hurrying down into the dark of Hades. The sun has perished out of the sky, and an evil fog has rolled over everything.”

So he spoke, and they all laughed at him, easy and loud. Eurymachus, son of Polybus, took the lead: “This stranger who has just blown in from somewhere has lost his mind. Quick, you servants — show him out the doors, out into the marketplace, since the hall seems as dark as night to him.”

But the godlike Theoclymenus answered: “Eurymachus, I have no need of anyone to guide me out. I have eyes, and ears, and two good feet of my own, and a sound mind in my breast besides — nothing wrong with any of them. By these I will take myself outside. For I see the evil coming on you, and not one of you suitors will dodge it or escape it — not one of you who, in the house of godlike Odysseus, do violence to men and plot your wicked deeds.”

So he spoke, and walked out of the well-built hall, and came to Piraeus, who took him in gladly. But the suitors only looked at one another, and went on baiting Telemachus by jeering at his guests. One of the high-handed young men would say: “Telemachus, no man is worse served in his guests than you. First there is this filthy tramp, good for nothing but begging his bread and his wine, with no strength in his hands and no stomach for a fight — a dead weight on the ground. And now up gets this other one, to play the prophet. Take my advice, for it is the better course: let us throw the pair of them aboard a ship of many oars and sell them off to the Sicels. That would fetch a decent price.” So the suitors talked. But Telemachus paid them no mind. He sat in silence, watching his father, waiting always for the moment he would lay his hands on the shameless crowd.

And the daughter of Icarius, wise Penelope, had set her lovely chair facing them, just across the way, so that she heard every word each man spoke in the hall. For all the laughter, they had made a fine, rich dinner of it, with all those beasts slaughtered. But never was there a supper less to be envied than the one a goddess and a strong man were about to set before them — for it was they themselves who had begun the foul work first.
